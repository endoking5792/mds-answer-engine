## 1. Definition, incidence and aetiology

### Explanation

**Definitions.** The AAE Glossary defines a perforation as "the mechanical or pathologic communication between the root canal system and the external tooth surface" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Endodontology quotes the earlier (2003) Glossary wording, which names the supporting periodontal apparatus rather than the external tooth surface (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC], and Ingle's defines perforations as "pathologic or iatrogenic communications between the root canal space and the attachment apparatus" (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. The wording matters: the damage that decides prognosis is to the attachment apparatus, not to the tooth.

**Incidence.** The sources disagree. Endodontology gives a reported rate of 1% to 3% (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer's figure appears in the OCR text as "3-1096", most likely 3-10% (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]; the OCR is unreliable, so this reading is [Guessing]. Both are reported figures from older studies whose definitions and populations differ; write "about 1-3% (up to 10% in some reports)".

**Aetiology.** Endodontology cites Kvinnsland and co-workers (1989): of 55 perforations seen in a Norwegian dental school, almost half came from endodontic treatment and slightly more than half from prosthodontic treatment (post preparation), and buccal, mesial and midroot surfaces were most often perforated (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer give the same split: about half during root canal treatment (searching for orifices, treating calcified canals, instrumenting) and half during restorative procedures, plus non-iatrogenic causes (resorption, caries) (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. They state the aim of repair: to eliminate microbes and restore the integrity of the root "by means of a tight, biocompatible seal", so that the periodontium can regenerate (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** A perforation is a mechanical or pathologic communication between the root canal system and the external tooth surface (American Association of Endodontists, 2012), that is, between the root canal space and the attachment apparatus (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Incidence.** About 1-3% of treated teeth (Baumann and Beer, 2010); some reports give higher figures (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

**Aetiology**
A. **Iatrogenic** (about half endodontic, half prosthodontic; Kvinnsland et al, 1989, cited by Baumann and Beer, 2010):
1. Access cavity: bur not parallel to the long axis (tilted, rotated, crowned teeth), calcified chamber, searching for orifices: crown, cervical and furcal floor perforations.
2. Cleaning and shaping: ledge, artificial canal and apical perforation; over-flaring of the inner wall of curved roots with Gates-Glidden drills, Peeso reamers or files (strip perforation); overinstrumentation through the foramen.
3. Post space preparation: drills not following the canal, removing gutta-percha with drills.
4. Retreatment: removal of posts and old fillings.
B. **Pathologic:** internal or external (cervical) resorption, caries of the pulp chamber floor.

Buccal, mesial and midroot surfaces are most often perforated (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Significance.** Every perforation is an injury to the attachment apparatus; it becomes a pathway for infection from the canal to the periodontium and back, and is one of the main reasons for failure of endodontic and post-retained restorations. Most iatrogenic perforations are not recognised at once or are not treated adequately (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

**Aim of repair.** Eliminate infection and restore the integrity of the root with a tight, biocompatible seal so that the periodontium can regenerate (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

Several classifications coexist and each answers a different clinical question.

- **By level of the root** (coronal, middle, apical third; furcal floor treated with the coronal third). Ingle's notes that "the more apical the perforation, the more favorable the prognosis", and Cohen's adds that the difficulty of repair runs the opposite way (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].
- **By relation to the crestal bone and epithelial attachment.** Endodontology considers this "probably the most important factor" in prognosis, because a perforation close to the epithelial attachment is open to oral bacteria and epithelium migrates apically if the defect is not closed at once (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].
- **Fuss and Trope (1996)**, as reproduced in Endodontology: good prognosis if fresh, small and apical or coronal (away from the crest); poor prognosis if old, large and at the level of the alveolar crest ("high alveolar ridge") (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].
- **Furcal: direct vs stripping.** Torabinejad's chapter separates the punched-out "direct" perforation made by a bur, which is accessible and has walls, from the "stripping" perforation of the furcal side of the root from over-flaring, which is usually inaccessible (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].
- **Ingle's "four dimensions"** (level, location, size, time), which always occur together (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Location around the root (buccal, lingual, mesial, distal) matters little for nonsurgical repair but decides whether a surgical approach is possible.
- **By time**: fresh (immediate, usually uninfected) vs old (delayed, infected, with granulation tissue) (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. By site**
- Crown (supragingival, supracrestal).
- Furcal floor (multirooted teeth).
- Root: coronal third, middle third (lateral, strip), apical third (through the foramen or along a new canal).

**2. By relation to the crestal bone (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Baumann and Beer, 2010):** supracrestal; crestal (at the epithelial attachment, the "critical zone"); subcrestal.

**3. By mechanism (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002)**
- Direct (punched-out) perforation: bur searching for an orifice; round, accessible, has walls.
- Stripping perforation: over-flaring of the inner (furcal) wall of a curved root; ovoid, inaccessible.
- Strip perforation (American Association of Endodontists, 2012): complete penetration of the canal wall from excessive lateral removal of tooth structure, usually in curved roots or roots with concavities.

**4. Fuss and Trope (1996) prognostic classification (Baumann and Beer, 2010)**

| Factor | Good prognosis | Poor prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| Time | Fresh (repaired at once) | Old (delayed, infected) |
| Size | Small | Large |
| Location | Apical or coronal, away from the crest | Crestal ("high alveolar ridge"), furcal |

**5. Four dimensions (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)**
1. Level: coronal, middle, apical third; the more apical, the better the prognosis.
2. Location: buccal, lingual, mesial, distal; decides surgical access.
3. Size: area = πr², so doubling the diameter quadruples the area to seal.
4. Time: repair as soon as possible to prevent loss of attachment.

## 3. Pathogenesis: why a perforation fails

### Explanation

Hülsmann and Schäfer describe the biology. A perforation in a tooth with an infected canal infects the adjacent tissue directly, causing osteolysis and a non-specific reaction with epithelial proliferation by chemotaxis; near the crown this soon creates a communication with the mouth through the gingival sulcus, which re-infects the perforation (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. The reverse also happens: the canal is infected from the mouth through the periodontal ligament space. Torabinejad's chapter notes that an untreated furcal perforation communicates with the gingival sulcus "within weeks or sometimes days" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Endodontology explains why furcal perforations are treated as crestal: they lie close to the epithelial attachment and may communicate with the sulcus (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer conclude that prognosis "depends on the quality of infection control" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

1. Mechanical injury to periodontal ligament and bone, with bleeding and inflammation.
2. Bacteria from the canal (or from the mouth through the sulcus) infect the site; osteolysis follows (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).
3. Epithelium proliferates and migrates apically along the root to the defect.
4. A periodontal pocket forms and links the perforation with the oral cavity, which re-infects the repair; furcal perforations reach the sulcus within weeks or days (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
5. Result: attachment loss, furcation involvement, endo-perio lesion, tooth loss.

**Fresh vs old perforation.** A fresh mechanical perforation made during instrumentation of a clean canal is usually sterile and causes only bleeding and acute inflammation; if sealed at once, the periodontal ligament can regenerate over a biocompatible material. An old perforation, unnoticed or left unsealed, is infected, filled with granulation tissue and often already linked to a pocket; the repair must first remove infection and granulation tissue, and periodontal reattachment is less predictable (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Effect of level.** Near the crest, epithelium reaches the defect quickly and oral bacteria enter through the sulcus; apical defects are surrounded by bone and are protected from the mouth, so they heal better once infection is controlled (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

Hence the three rules: diagnose early, seal at once, control infection. Furcal perforations behave as crestal perforations because of their closeness to the epithelial attachment (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 4. Diagnosis

### Explanation

**During treatment.** Torabinejad's chapter lists early signs: sudden pain during working length determination when anaesthesia was adequate during access; sudden bleeding; burning pain or a bad taste during sodium hypochlorite irrigation; a malpositioned file on the radiograph; or an apex locator reading of the periodontal ligament short of the expected length (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Bleeding may be slight, because bone is relatively avascular. Hülsmann and Schäfer explain how an apex locator shows a perforation (an over-instrumentation warning while the file is still well short of length) and that the position of the blood spot on a paper point localises a small perforation (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. Grossman's says an apex locator distinguishes a bleeding canal from a perforation, and that strip perforation is recognised by bleeding after the pulp has been removed (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Apical perforation through the foramen is suggested by fresh bleeding, new pain and sudden loss of the apical stop (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. On the pulp chamber floor it is not always clear whether a bleeding spot is a perforation or a vital canal orifice; an apex locator and a radiograph with an instrument in place decide (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].

**Delayed (old) perforations.** Endodontology: radiographs from different angles including bitewings; a sinus tract, pocket or furcation involvement may indicate a perforation (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer warn that subgingival and subcrestal perforations cannot be found clinically alone, and that radiographic detection is "a rare exception"; indirect signs are radiopaque material beside the root, fillings or posts deviating from the root axis, lesions in the furcation or on the convex side of a curved root (strip perforation) (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. CBCT shows the location, size and surgical accessibility of the defect (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. During treatment (fresh perforation)**
1. Sudden, persistent bleeding (paper point bleeds again); strip perforation: bleeding after pulp removal (Gopikrishna, 2021).
2. Sudden pain with an instrument when anaesthesia was adequate, or pain in a non-vital tooth well short of length (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).
3. Burning pain or bad taste during NaOCl irrigation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
4. Electronic apex locator: "apex" or over-instrumentation reading far short of the working length; it distinguishes a bleeding orifice from a perforation (Gopikrishna, 2021; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).
5. Paper points: position of the blood spot locates the defect (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).
6. Radiograph with a file in place, at two angles: file outside the root outline.
7. Apical perforation: fresh bleeding, new pain, loss of apical stop, file beyond the apex (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
8. Direct view under the operating microscope.

**B. Delayed (old) perforation**
- History of previous treatment, post placement; pain, swelling.
- Sinus tract (trace with gutta-percha), isolated narrow pocket, furcation involvement (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Radiographs at different angles and bitewings: posts or fillings off the root axis, extruded material beside the root, lateral or furcal radiolucency, lesion on the convex side of a curved root (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).
- CBCT: exact site, size and surgical access (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Differential diagnosis:** lateral canal lesion, vertical root fracture, periodontal pocket, external resorption.

## 5. Prevention

### Explanation

Grossman's prevention for access perforations: study crown-root angulation, follow the "stay lingual rule" in maxillary lateral incisors and mandibular first premolars, check bur depth and angle, remove crowns and old restorations if needed, and use magnification (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; haemorrhage control with 1:50,000 epinephrine and MTA repair for non-furcal cervical perforations follow from the same chapter. Torabinejad's chapter adds that failure to achieve straight-line access is the main cause of intracanal accidents, and allows access without the rubber dam in difficult orientation cases, provided instruments are tied with floss (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. For shaping, Hülsmann and Schäfer blame Hedström files and Gates-Glidden drills (and even more Peeso reamers) for strip perforations of the curved mesial roots of lower molars; prevention is pre-bent stainless steel, balanced-force NiTi and selective preparation of canal segments (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. Torabinejad's 6th edition adds that coronal root perforations come from over-enlarging the cervical canal with files, GG drills, orifice openers or Peeso reamers, and that shaping shortens the working length by up to 1-2 mm, so the length must be re-checked (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

For post space, Torabinejad's chapter says removing gutta-percha with a drill "can only result in perforation"; heated instruments should be used (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer go further: in the age of adhesive dentistry the best prevention is to avoid posts; take care with kidney-shaped roots (palatal roots of maxillary molars, distal roots of mandibular molars); remaining dentin walls after post preparation were a mean 0.5 mm thinner than the radiograph suggested (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Access (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002)**
- Read the preoperative radiograph (angled views): chamber size, calcification, root angulation.
- Relate the bur to the long axis of the tooth and adjacent teeth; check depth and angle repeatedly; "stay lingual" in maxillary laterals and mandibular first premolars.
- Remove crowns or old restorations when orientation is uncertain; start access without the dam if needed (instruments tied with floss).
- Magnification and illumination; radiograph with a bur in place to check direction.

**Cleaning and shaping**
- Straight-line access; precurved small files; sequential sizes; patency; avoid forcing files past ledges.
- Anticurvature filing away from the furcation; limited use of Gates-Glidden drills and Peeso reamers in curved mesial roots of mandibular molars; NiTi with balanced force (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).
- Re-check working length: shaping shortens it by 1-2 mm (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Post space**
- Avoid posts where adhesive cores suffice (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).
- Remove gutta-percha with heated instruments, not drills (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Know root anatomy: concavities and kidney-shaped roots; dentin walls are about 0.5 mm thinner than they appear on radiographs (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).
- Sequential drills, check bur direction frequently.

## 6. Principles of management and treatment planning

### Explanation

Torabinejad's chapter states that a perforation into the periodontal ligament anywhere worsens long-term prognosis, and that the decision rests on the strategic value of the tooth, the site and size of the defect and the potential for repair; the patient must be told of the questionable prognosis and of the signs of failure (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology gives the primary goal: to arrest inflammation and loss of attachment by preserving healthy tissue at the site; nonsurgical repair is preferred whenever possible, and surgery is chosen for defects only accessible surgically, defects with a periodontal component, failed nonsurgical repairs and large extrusions of material; a costly restoration that would be risky to dismantle can also justify surgery (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Ingle's adds the sequence: if the canal has not been prepared, repair the perforation first, because this controls bleeding into the canal and confines irrigants, but prepare the canal enough beforehand to reach the defect; keep the canal pathway open with a gutta-percha segment or a collagen plug (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's says the same: locate and partly prepare the canals and protect them from blockage by the repair material (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. The operating microscope "has become the standard of vision" for these repairs (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Assessment before treatment (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)**
- Strategic value and restorability of the tooth.
- Level, location, size and age of the perforation; periodontal probing (is the attachment intact?).
- Access to the defect (internal or surgical); infection; patient's wishes, cost.

**Options**
1. Nonsurgical (internal) repair: first choice (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
2. Surgical repair, or combined nonsurgical and surgical.
3. Orthodontic extrusion or crown lengthening (crestal defects).
4. Root resection, hemisection, bicuspidization, intentional replantation.
5. Extraction and implant or bridge.

**Indications for surgery (Baumann and Beer, 2010):** defect only accessible surgically; concomitant periodontal component; failed nonsurgical repair; gross extrusion of material; restoration that would be risky or costly to remove.

**Sequence of nonsurgical repair (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021)**
1. Rubber dam, operating microscope.
2. Locate and partly shape all canals; protect them with gutta-percha cones, a collagen plug or Cavit.
3. Haemostasis.
4. Clean the defect (old perforations: ultrasonics, NaOCl).
5. Place a barrier if needed.
6. Seal with MTA or another material.
7. Confirm set, complete root canal treatment, restore with an adhesive coronal seal.
8. Inform the patient; recall.

**Repair first or obturate first?** When the canal has not yet been prepared, the perforation is repaired first: this stops bleeding into the canal, confines the irrigant and allows a controlled obturation. The canal should still be enlarged enough beforehand to give access to the defect and to limit instrumentation after repair. In a failing case, the old root filling can be left in place to hold the canal position while the perforation is repaired, taking care not to disturb the repair during later retreatment (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Patient communication.** Explain that a perforation has occurred, what will be done, the alternatives (surgery, extraction and implant) and that the prognosis is guarded; record everything (Gopikrishna, 2021; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 7. Haemostasis

### Explanation

Ingle's recommends calcium hydroxide syringed into the canal and defect for 4-5 minutes and flushed with NaOCl; two or three applications usually control bleeding, and it can be left until the next visit if they do not; collagen, calcium sulfate, freeze-dried bone and MTA also act as haemostats (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's warns that some of the best haemostatics, like ferric sulfate, leave a coagulum that may promote bacterial growth and compromise the seal (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer agree: stop bleeding with saline or aqueous calcium hydroxide and avoid a "porous blood clot potentially contaminated" with medicaments, which is why ferric sulfate and aluminium chloride must not be used (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. Grossman's, by contrast, gives 1:50,000 epinephrine for cervical perforations (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The positions are compatible: epinephrine leaves no coagulum; ferric sulfate does.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Aim:** a dry, clean field for placing the repair material (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Agents**
- Sterile saline irrigation and paper points.
- Calcium hydroxide: syringed into the defect for 4-5 minutes and flushed with NaOCl, two or three times; leave until the next visit if bleeding persists (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).
- Epinephrine 1:50,000 on a cotton pellet (cervical perforations) (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Collagen (CollaCote), calcium sulfate, MTA itself.
- **Avoid** ferric sulfate and aluminium chloride: they leave a coagulum that harbours bacteria and spoils the seal (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

**Why haemostasis matters.** Most repair materials need a dry field (amalgam, glass ionomer, composite, resin-ionomer); even MTA, which sets in blood, needs a visible, controlled site for placement. A porous clot left under the material becomes a pathway for bacteria and for leakage, and medicaments trapped in it irritate the tissue (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009). With marked hyperaemia in an old perforation, cover the site with calcium hydroxide paste for a few hours or until the next visit before repairing (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

## 8. Barriers and the internal matrix

### Explanation

Barriers solve the two main problems of perforation repair: haemostasis and controlled placement of the material, by providing a "backstop" against which to condense (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer credit the internal matrix concept to Lemon (1992): hydroxyapatite is pushed through the perforation to displace tissue and recreate the root contour, then amalgam is packed against it; the hydroxyapatite is not resorbed, so new periodontal tissue is unlikely (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. Calcium sulfate sets into a solid wall within minutes and resorbs quickly, allowing new periodontal fibres; resorbable collagen pieces can also be placed, especially when the granulation tissue is unstable (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. Ingle's gives the details: CollaCote resorbs in 10-14 days and is left in situ but is contraindicated with adhesive restoratives because it absorbs moisture; calcium sulfate (Capset) creates a tamponade by plugging vascular channels, resorbs in 2-4 weeks, sets hard and is the barrier of choice with wet bonding (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Endodontology lists tricalcium phosphate, hydroxyapatite, demineralized freeze-dried bone, collagen and calcium sulfate as matrix materials, and adds a caution: a matrix gives better control of the material, but "whether this will result in a better treatment outcome remains to be demonstrated" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. With MTA, Hülsmann and Schäfer say no stable support is strictly needed if the granulation tissue itself is stable; check it with thick paper points or a plugger (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Purpose:** haemostasis; prevent extrusion of repair material; provide a backstop to condense against; recreate the external root contour (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Internal matrix concept:** Lemon (1992) (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

| Barrier | Properties |
|---|---|
| Hydroxyapatite | Stable base for amalgam; not resorbed, so little periodontal regeneration |
| Calcium sulfate (Capset) | Tamponade effect; sets hard in minutes; resorbs in 2-4 weeks; barrier of choice for adhesive (wet-bonding) repairs |
| Collagen (CollaCote) | Haemostasis in 2-5 minutes; resorbs in 10-14 days; contraindicated with adhesive materials (absorbs moisture) |
| Tricalcium phosphate, freeze-dried bone | Resorbable matrices |
| MTA | Non-resorbable barrier and restorative; sets in moisture |

*(Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010)*

Matrices improve control of the material, but a better outcome has not been demonstrated (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Technique.** After cleaning and haemostasis, small increments of the barrier are carried through the access and the perforation into the bony defect with a plugger until a firm wall is formed at the external root surface; calcium sulfate is syringed in, sets in minutes and can be trimmed flush with the root with an ultrasonic tip; MTA is then placed against it from inside the canal (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010). Barriers are placed in the bone and must not be left inside the tooth. When MTA is used alone, a stable granulation-tissue bed is enough as a base; test it with thick paper points or a plugger (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

## 9. Materials used for perforation repair

### Explanation

**Requirements.** Hülsmann and Schäfer say the repair material should be biocompatible or at least inert, easy to handle, adherent to the root walls, moisture resistant, dimensionally stable and non-resorbing, ideally allowing cementum or periodontal tissue to grow over it; these are the same requirements as for a root-end filling (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].

**History.** Amalgam, gutta-percha, zinc oxide-eugenol, Cavit, calcium hydroxide, freeze-dried bone and indium foil were used (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and Endodontology adds super-EBA, glass ionomer and composite, with variable success (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Harty's attributes the high failure rates of amalgam and gutta-percha to microleakage (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Ingle's adds that traditional restoratives discoloured teeth and tattooed soft tissue (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

**MTA.** Endodontology: "substantial evidence" makes MTA the material of choice; it is biocompatible, seals tightly and promotes regeneration of cementum; white MTA contains no iron; MTA is Portland cement with added bismuth oxide (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Cohen's: MTA seals even when contaminated with blood, and cementum-like tissue grows on it (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's 6th edition widens the recommendation to "bioactive materials (i.e., MTA and MTA-like materials)" (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Atav and co-workers recommend at least 3 mm thickness (Atav, Arıcan and Olcay, 2024) [SRC].

**The setting-time problem, and a source error.** Endodontology states "The main advantage of MTA is its long setting time; however, this makes the material unsuitable for the repair of supracrestal perforations" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The second half shows this is a slip for "disadvantage": Cohen's calls the long setting time "the main disadvantage", because MTA washes out before it sets in defects that cross the gingival margin, for which a resin-ionomer such as Geristore is recommended (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Write "disadvantage".

**Biodentine and fast-setting cements.** Ingle's: Biodentine is a calcium silicate dentine substitute with no gypsum and less water than MTA, self-adhesive, with no shrinkage, better antibacterial action and a setting time of 12-15 minutes (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Atav and co-workers give 9-12 minutes for the initial set, from calcium chloride in the liquid, but the final set can take up to 45 minutes (Atav, Arıcan and Olcay, 2024) [SRC]. Fast-set putties used for perforation repair should be covered with glass ionomer in single-visit treatment (Atav, Arıcan and Olcay, 2024) [SRC].

**Choice by situation (Ingle's).** MTA is the barrier of choice where moisture contamination is likely or access and visibility are limited; for coronal-third defects in the aesthetic zone a calcium sulfate barrier with adhesive restoration is generally used; Biodentine is a good alternative to MTA (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Ideal requirements (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009):** biocompatible; seals tightly and adheres to dentin; sets in moisture (blood); dimensionally stable; non-resorbable; radiopaque; easy to handle; non-staining; bacteriostatic; allows cementum and periodontal ligament regeneration (osteo- and cementoconductive).

**Materials**
| Material | Comment |
|---|---|
| Amalgam | Historical; needs a dry field and a matrix; leakage, staining, tattoos |
| Gutta-percha, ZOE, Cavit, calcium hydroxide, indium foil | Historical; poor seal or resorbable |
| IRM, Super-EBA | Better tissue tolerance; moisture sensitive |
| Glass ionomer, resin-modified GI (Geristore), composite, compomer | Need a dry field; adhesion; Geristore for defects crossing the gingival margin, where MTA washes out |
| **MTA (grey, white)** | Material of choice: seals in the presence of blood, biocompatible, cementum grows on it; white MTA has no iron (less staining); disadvantages: long setting time (about 3-4 hours), difficult handling, grey MTA stains, cost |
| **Biodentine** | Calcium silicate; setting 12-15 minutes (final up to 45); self-adhesive, no shrinkage, antibacterial; good alternative to MTA |
| Bioceramic putties (e.g. fast-set putty) | Premixed; cover with glass ionomer if repair and restoration in one visit |
| Calcium phosphate cement | Listed by Ingle's |

*(Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Baumann and Beer, 2010; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Atav, Arıcan and Olcay, 2024)*

**Selection:** moisture or poor access: MTA; aesthetic coronal defect: calcium sulfate barrier with adhesive material; defect reaching the gingival sulcus: resin-ionomer or composite (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). Place MTA at least 3 mm thick (Atav, Arıcan and Olcay, 2024).

**Why MTA replaced older materials.** Amalgam and gutta-percha repairs failed mostly through microleakage (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017); amalgam and Super-EBA need a dry field and a firm matrix to condense against, and traditional materials stained the tooth and tattooed the gingiva (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019). MTA, introduced at Loma Linda as a perforation and root-end material, sets in blood and moisture, seals well (its alkalinity and antibacterial effect while setting are [RECALL]), and cementum and periodontal ligament form directly on it in animal and clinical studies (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010). Its drawbacks are the long setting time (washout at the gingival margin), difficult handling, discoloration with grey MTA and cost. Biodentine and premixed bioceramic putties keep MTA's biocompatibility with shorter setting and easier handling (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Atav, Arıcan and Olcay, 2024).

## 10. Technique of repair by site

### Explanation

**MTA handling.** Endodontology: mix to the consistency of wet sand; deliver with a carrier (e.g. Dovgan carriers) or small spatula; indirect ultrasonic activation (touching the plugger with an ultrasonic tip) gives a denser fill than hand compaction; blot excess moisture with thick paper points; MTA needs at least 3 hours to set, so place a moist pellet and a temporary seal and confirm the set at the next visit, then restore adhesively as soon as possible (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Cohen's suggests the butt end of paper points as a condenser (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer prefer a cellulose swab over cotton, whose fibres become incorporated in the set MTA and wick through the temporary seal (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC; OCR passage, read with care].

**Fresh vs old.** Fresh perforations from overzealous instrumentation are sterile and need no further preparation (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Old perforations are infected and filled with granulation tissue: NaOCl dissolves the invading tissue and disinfects; chlorhexidine can be used if the aim is to disinfect without dissolving tissue beyond the defect (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. If immediate sealing is impossible, place calcium hydroxide and seal (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].

**Crown and supracrestal.** Exteriorise and restore with amalgam, glass ionomer or composite, or include the defect in a crown margin; Endodontology adds cast restorations (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

**Crestal or subcrestal coronal third.** Poorest prognosis because attachment recedes and a pocket forms; aim to bring the defect above the crest by orthodontic extrusion (aesthetic zone) or crown lengthening, or repair internally with MTA, which seals adequately (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. After pocket formation the prognosis is very poor, though a case report suggests calcium silicate materials may still induce healing (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Furcal floor.** Ingle's: mechanical furcal perforations are generally round and those on the lateral root ovoid; a fresh clean defect can be repaired at once if haemostasis is achieved; a chronic one is first cleaned with ultrasonics (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Direct (punched-out) perforations are repaired immediately with MTA (or glass ionomer or composite if dry); stripping perforations are harder (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Endodontology's case shows an 8 mm buccal pocket reduced to 4 mm six months after MTA repair over a calcium sulfate matrix (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

**Middle third (strip).** Caused by files, Gates-Glidden drills or misdirected posts; ovoid, large areas to seal (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. If small and dry, it can be sealed during three-dimensional obturation; if large or wet, it must be repaired first, with MTA as the material of choice for poor access and moisture (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter: canals apical to a strip perforation are filled with MTA first, then the defect with the same material (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; MTA can also obturate the canal when there is a midroot perforation (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Apical third.** Usually blocked and ledged canals; first try to negotiate the true canal. Ingle's "holding file" technique: a precurved file is placed apical to the perforation to keep the true canal open while MTA is placed; it is moved in 1-2 mm strokes so it is not frozen in the setting MTA, then sectioned (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Perforation through the foramen: establish a new working length 1-2 mm short of the perforation, create an apical seat and obturate, or use an MTA apical barrier (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Prognosis depends on the size, shape and location of the defect and the material; apical repairs are easier in anterior teeth (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Post perforation.** Fresh blood during post preparation indicates a perforation; if the post cannot be removed, management is generally surgical; if it can, nonsurgical repair is preferred (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**MTA technique (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021)**
1. Haemostasis; canals protected.
2. Mix MTA to wet-sand consistency; carry with an MTA carrier or small spatula.
3. Condense with pluggers or the butt end of paper points; indirect ultrasonic activation for a denser fill; at least 3 mm thick.
4. Blot excess water with thick paper points.
5. Moist pellet (or cellulose swab) and temporary seal; MTA needs at least 3 hours.
6. Next visit: confirm hard set; complete treatment; adhesive coronal restoration.

**Fresh vs old perforation**
- Fresh: clean and usually sterile; seal immediately (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).
- Old: infected granulation tissue; dissolve with NaOCl, clean with ultrasonics; calcium hydroxide dressing; then repair (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

**By site**
| Site | Management |
|---|---|
| Crown / supracrestal | Exteriorise; restore with amalgam, GIC, composite or crown margin (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| Crestal / subcrestal coronal third | Immediate internal MTA repair; or orthodontic extrusion (aesthetic zone) or crown lengthening to bring it above bone; aesthetic: calcium sulfate barrier + adhesive material (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) |
| Furcal floor, direct | Immediate MTA (GIC or composite if dry); prognosis good if immediate |
| Furcal, stripping / middle third | Prepare canal; protect canals; MTA (canals apical filled with MTA first); small dry defects sealed during obturation (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) |
| Apical third, new canal | Negotiate true canal; holding file; MTA into the defect; obturate the true canal |
| Through the foramen | New working length 1-2 mm short; apical seat or MTA apical barrier (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) |
| Post perforation | Remove post and repair internally; if the post cannot be removed, surgery |

**Holding-file technique for apical-third perforations (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)**
1. Negotiate the true canal past the perforation with a small precurved file; enlarge it sequentially.
2. Leave a precurved "holding file" in the true canal apical to the perforation.
3. Place MTA into the perforation around the file; move the file in short 1-2 mm strokes so it is not locked in the setting MTA.
4. Section the file below the occlusal surface; radiograph; moist pellet and temporary seal.
5. Next visit: remove the file, irrigate, shape and obturate the true canal.

**Middle-third strip perforation (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021):** prepare the canal first; protect the canal apical to the defect (gutta-percha cone or collagen plug); repair with MTA under the microscope; or fill the canal apical to the defect and the defect itself with MTA. Small, dry strip perforations can be sealed during warm vertical obturation.

## 11. Surgical and adjunctive management

### Explanation

Torabinejad's chapter: if accessible, the perforation can be repaired surgically with MTA after locating it with CBCT; if not repairable, consider hemisection, bicuspidization, root amputation or intentional replantation; divergent roots and adequate bone allow hemisection or bicuspidization; replantation suits inaccessible defects or combined problems such as a perforation with a separated instrument; surgically altered teeth have a questionable prognosis because of difficult restorations and hygiene, and the remaining roots are prone to caries, periodontal disease and fracture (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Endodontology calls intentional replantation with perforation repair a last resort (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Surgical repair:** flap, osteotomy, curettage of granulation tissue, preparation of the defect, MTA (or resin-ionomer/composite for defects near the gingival margin); combine with guided tissue regeneration if bone loss is extensive [RECALL] (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Periodontal-orthodontic adjuncts:** orthodontic extrusion (aesthetic zone) or crown lengthening for crestal defects (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Resective procedures (multirooted teeth):** root amputation, hemisection, bicuspidization (divergent roots, adequate bone); prognosis questionable (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Intentional replantation:** inaccessible defects or combined problems; last resort (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Extraction** with implant or bridge when the prognosis is hopeless.

**Choosing surgery over internal repair (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021)**
- The defect can only be reached surgically (e.g. post that cannot be removed, apical-third defect in a curved root).
- A periodontal defect accompanies the perforation and needs direct treatment.
- Nonsurgical repair has failed.
- Large amounts of filling or repair material have been extruded.
- Removing a bridge or crown would be risky or costly while the defect is surgically accessible.

Surgical access depends on location around the root: location matters little for internal repair but may preclude a surgical approach (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019); palatal and lingual defects of posterior teeth are often hard to reach [RECALL]. CBCT is used to plan the approach (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021). Surgically altered teeth need demanding restorations and hygiene; remaining roots are prone to caries, periodontitis and vertical fracture (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

## 12. Prognosis and outcome

### Explanation

**Prognostic factors.** Torabinejad's chapter lists: location relative to the crestal bone, root trunk length, access, size, periodontal communication, time to repair, sealing ability of the material, operator skill and patient hygiene; a defect under 1 mm causes less destruction (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Cohen's lists location, time delay, seal and previous microbial contamination (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer add contamination with foreign material in the defect and whether the defect can be seen through the access cavity (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].

**Outcome evidence.** Hülsmann and Schäfer report a 1989 study of 55 perforations (mostly repaired with gutta-percha and chloropercha) in which 56% of treated perforations were successful after a mean of 3.4 years, and a later MTA series of 16 cases in which all showed normal radiographic tissue (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. Endodontology: Fuss and Trope considered furcal repairs to have a low success rate, but Main and co-workers (2004) found normal tissue next to MTA-repaired infected furcal perforations at 1 year, so "the availability of MTA may require re-evaluation of previous guidelines regarding prognosis of perforated teeth" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. These are case series, not controlled trials [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Factors affecting prognosis (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009)**
1. Time: immediate repair is better; delay leads to sulcular breakdown.
2. Level: relation to crestal bone and epithelial attachment; crestal and furcal worst; apical heals best but is hardest to repair.
3. Size: under 1 mm best.
4. Infection and foreign material in the defect.
5. Periodontal communication (pocket) before repair.
6. Sealing ability and biocompatibility of the material.
7. Access (visible through the access cavity?), root trunk length.
8. Operator skill, magnification; patient's oral hygiene.

**Evidence:** older repairs (gutta-percha, amalgam): about 56% success at 3.4 years (Kvinnsland 1989, cited by Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009). MTA case series: normal tissue at the repair site, including infected furcal perforations at 1 year (Main et al 2004, cited by Baumann and Beer, 2010), so the traditionally poor prognosis of furcal perforations is being revised.

**Follow-up:** clinical, radiographic and periodontal probing at recall; failure needs surgery, resection or extraction (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Criteria of success:** no symptoms; no sinus tract; normal probing depth at the perforation site (no pocket); radiographic resolution of any lateral or furcal radiolucency with a continuous lamina dura; tooth functional. **Failure:** persistent or new pocket, sinus tract, enlarging radiolucency, pain [RECALL for the exact list; the sources describe these signs under recall without a formal list].

**Why repairs fail:** leakage of the repair material; re-infection through a pocket communicating with the sulcus; infection left in the canal or defect; extruded material; root fracture (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

## 13. Complications and conclusion

### Explanation

Endodontology describes sodium hypochlorite penetrating through a perforation: burning, severe pain, facial swelling and later fistula, ulceration and nerve complications; management is informing the patient, analgesics, cold compresses and antibiotics only if infection risk is high (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Ingle's adds that when the attachment apparatus is still intact, timing is critical and treatment should aim at immediate nonsurgical repair (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's: inform the patient that an error has occurred and communicate a guarded prognosis (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Complications:** NaOCl extrusion through the perforation (pain, swelling, necrosis, paraesthesia: analgesics, cold compresses, antibiotics if infection risk) (Baumann and Beer, 2010); extrusion of repair material; discoloration (grey MTA); periodontal pocket and endo-perio lesion; root fracture after resection.

**NaOCl through a perforation:** irrigating a bleeding site mistaken for pulp tissue forces hypochlorite into bone and soft tissue. Prevention: confirm the perforation (apex locator, radiograph) before irrigating; once confirmed, irrigate with saline or low pressure, keeping the needle away from the defect (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Medicolegal:** inform the patient that an error occurred, the plan and a guarded prognosis; document; refer when beyond one's skill (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Conclusion.** Perforations are largely preventable by careful access, controlled shaping and conservative post preparation. When one occurs, early diagnosis (apex locator, microscope, CBCT), immediate haemostasis and sealing with a biocompatible calcium silicate material such as MTA or Biodentine, preservation of the canal pathway and follow-up of periodontal health give a favourable prognosis to most teeth; crestal, large and delayed perforations remain the difficult cases.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Furcal perforations have a poor prognosis."**
*Textbook:* Fuss and Trope (1996), as cited in Endodontology, rate furcal repairs as low-success because they behave as crestal perforations; Torabinejad's chapter says repair after pocket formation has a very poor prognosis (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].
*Refinement:* Main and co-workers' MTA cases showed normal tissue at 1 year in infected furcal perforations; Endodontology's own case had an 8 mm pocket reduce to 4 mm; Torabinejad cites a case report of healing after pocket formation (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].
*Strength:* case series and case reports only; the sources do not report a controlled comparison. [Certain]
*Conclusion:* write that MTA has improved the prognosis and that the old guideline is being re-evaluated, not that furcal perforations now have a good prognosis.

**Claim 2: "The more apical the perforation, the better the prognosis."**
*Textbook:* Ingle's and Cohen's state it; Torabinejad's chapter calls subcrestal coronal-third defects the poorest; Hülsmann and Schäfer warn that supracrestal defects risk communication with the mouth (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC].
*Interpretation:* consistent. The worst zone is the crestal one, at the epithelial attachment. Supracrestal defects can be restored externally; apical ones heal well but are hardest to reach, so Cohen's notes the repair difficulty runs the other way. [Certain]

**Claim 3: "MTA's long setting time is its main advantage."**
*Textbook:* Endodontology says so; Cohen's says it is the main disadvantage (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].
*Interpretation:* Endodontology's next clause (the long set makes MTA unsuitable for supracrestal defects because it washes out) shows "advantage" is a wording error. Biodentine's 12-15 minute set is presented by Ingle's as an improvement (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. [Likely]

**Claim 4: Incidence.** Endodontology gives 1-3%; Hülsmann and Schäfer's figure is illegible in the OCR (probably 3-10%) (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. The difference cannot be resolved from the sources read. [Guessing]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- AAE definition: mechanical or pathologic communication between root canal system and external tooth surface.
- Half endodontic, half prosthodontic (post) in origin; buccal, mesial, midroot most often.
- Fuss and Trope: fresh, small, away from the crest = good; old, large, crestal = poor. Ingle's four dimensions: level, location, size (double diameter = 4x area), time.
- Diagnosis: bleeding, pain, apex locator reading short, paper-point blood spot, angled radiographs, CBCT.
- Haemostasis: calcium hydroxide, saline, epinephrine; avoid ferric sulfate (coagulum).
- Barriers: calcium sulfate (2-4 weeks, adhesive repairs), collagen (10-14 days, not with adhesives), hydroxyapatite (Lemon 1992 internal matrix, not resorbed).
- MTA material of choice; long set (3-4 h) is a disadvantage; Geristore at gingival margin; Biodentine sets in 12-15 min.
- Ingle's "holding file" for apical-third repair; new working length 1-2 mm short for perforation through the foramen.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling ferric sulfate the haemostatic of choice for perforation repair; it leaves a coagulum that spoils the seal.
- Writing that MTA's long setting time is an advantage, or using MTA for a defect at the gingival margin.
- Treating all furcal perforations as hopeless; immediately repaired direct perforations do well.
- Forgetting to protect the canal orifices before placing MTA, so the canals are blocked.
- Omitting prevention of post-space perforations, which cause about half of all perforations.

## MNEMONIC
**"Fresh, Small, Far = Fine"**: fresh, small perforations far from the crest have a good prognosis (Fuss and Trope). Four dimensions: **"LLST"** (Level, Location, Size, Time).
