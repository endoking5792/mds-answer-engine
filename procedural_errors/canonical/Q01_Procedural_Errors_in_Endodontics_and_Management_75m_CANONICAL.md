## 1. Definition and classification

### Explanation

Walton and Torabinejad define the subject as a group: unwanted or unforeseen circumstances during root canal therapy that can affect prognosis, which "are collectively termed procedural accidents" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Grossman's opens with the point that procedural errors occur not only with students and beginners but also with skilled clinicians (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], which is why examiners expect prevention to be written alongside management. Three ways of classifying them appear in the sources, and they answer different questions:

- **By stage of treatment.** Grossman's uses three groups: errors in access opening, in canal shaping and cleaning, and in obturation (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad use the same idea with post space preparation as a fourth stage (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. This is the most useful frame for an essay because management changes with the stage.
- **By whether the error can be undone.** Attar, Chogle and Hsu call blockage and separated instruments "reversible" (recovery is often possible if the error is noticed in time), and ledge, zip, transportation, gouging, over-instrumentation and flare-ups "irreversible". Irreversible errors "often are the product of reversible errors that were ignored or unidentified", a "domino effect" (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC]. This explains why early recognition dominates every management section.
- **By the AAE Glossary terms** (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC], which give the formal definitions examiners mark: ledge, perforation, strip perforation, transportation, zip and elbow (quoted in the exam core).

The sources differ slightly on wording, not on substance. Grossman's ledge definition ("an artificially created deviation of the root canal wall that prevents the passage of an instrument to the apex of an otherwise patent canal") is close to the AAE's (Gopikrishna, 2021; American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; Walton and Torabinejad define the ledge by its effect: the working length "can no longer be negotiated" and patency is lost (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Procedural errors (procedural accidents, endodontic mishaps) are unwanted or unforeseen events during root canal treatment that can affect prognosis; they occur with experienced clinicians as well as beginners (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Gopikrishna, 2021). Most are preventable by following the basic biological and mechanical principles of access, cleaning and shaping and obturation.

**Definitions (American Association of Endodontists, 2012)**
- **Ledge:** an artificial irregularity created on the surface of the root canal wall that impedes placement of instruments to the apex of an otherwise patent canal.
- **Perforation:** a mechanical or pathologic communication between the root canal system and the external tooth surface.
- **Strip perforation:** complete penetration of a canal wall due to excessive lateral removal of tooth structure, usually in curved roots or roots with surface invaginations.
- **Transportation:** removal of canal wall structure on the outside curve in the apical half of the canal, because files tend to return to their original straight shape.
- **Zip:** a tear-drop shape formed at the apical foramen when a file extends through it and transports the outer wall.
- **Elbow:** the narrow portion of a curved canal immediately coronal to a transportation or zip.

**Classification**

| A. By stage (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) | Errors |
|---|---|
| I. Access | Wrong tooth; incomplete caries removal; missed canals; crown/cervical/furcal perforation; gouging |
| II. Cleaning and shaping | Blockage; ledge; artificial canal; transportation, zip, elbow; strip and apical perforation; separated instrument; irrigant extrusion |
| III. Obturation | Underfilling; overfilling; extrusion into nerve or sinus; vertical root fracture |
| IV. Post space | Perforation; loss of seal |
| Any stage | Aspiration/ingestion of instruments; flare-up; heat damage |

**B. By reversibility (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025):** reversible (blockage, separated instrument) and irreversible (ledge, zip, transportation, gouging, perforation, over-instrumentation); irreversible errors usually follow reversible ones that were not recognised ("domino effect").

## 2. Prevention: general principles

### Explanation

Grossman's lists clinical guidelines that prevent most errors (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]: rapport and informed consent; full history and examination; assessment of periodontal status, restorability and prognosis before starting; rubber dam with high-power suction as the standard of care; a periapical radiograph at each step (preoperative, working length, master cone, postobturation); working length "estimated by more than one" method; recapitulation at every phase; copious irrigation; and care with medicaments. Grossman's adds a medicolegal point dating from 1955: if an instrument is swallowed when no rubber dam was used, the dentist is likely to face a lawsuit, and the court needs no expert opinion to find negligence (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Walton and Torabinejad set out what to tell the patient when an accident has happened: the incident, the procedures needed to correct it, the alternatives, and the effect on prognosis (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. The 6th edition of Torabinejad's chapter adds three-dimensional imaging: CBCT "helps the clinician to prevent most of the procedural accidents"; axial slices show the number of canals and wall thickness (preventing perforation), sagittal and coronal slices show curvature (preventing ledges and instrument fracture) (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's adds that a clinician should assess their own capability honestly and call in a consultant or refer when a case is beyond it (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad say correction of cleaning and shaping accidents "is usually difficult and the patient should be referred to an endodontist" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**General principles of prevention (Gopikrishna, 2021)**
1. Case selection: history, examination, periodontal status, restorability and prognosis assessed before starting; refer cases beyond one's skill.
2. Rubber dam and high-power suction: standard of care; they prevent aspiration and ingestion of instruments.
3. Radiographs at each step (preoperative, working length, master cone, postobturation), studied under magnification; angled views for extra canals.
4. Working length by more than one method (radiograph and electronic apex locator).
5. Straight-line access; precurved small stainless steel files; sequential sizes; recapitulation; copious irrigation and lubrication.
6. Magnification (dental operating microscope); CBCT in complex cases to show curvature, canal number and wall thickness (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
7. Knowledge of canal anatomy and its variations, apical anatomy (foramen, constriction, anatomic apex) and the age of root completion.
8. Training in the instruments, materials and techniques used; single-use shaping instruments.
9. Shaping and cleaning of the apical third, and obturation, without damaging periradicular tissues.

**Anticipation.** The clinician should assess and explain prognosis before starting, recognise difficult cases (calcification, curvature, crowns, previous treatment) and identify an error the moment it occurs (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Communication.** When an accident occurs, inform the patient of the incident, the corrective procedure, alternative treatments and the effect on prognosis, and record it (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

## 3. Errors during access cavity preparation

### Explanation

**Why access errors start the chain.** Walton and Torabinejad state that failure to achieve straight-line access "is often the main etiologic factor for other types of intracanal accidents" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; Grossman's puts the same idea as "access is success" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Bur misdirection happens when the axial inclination of the tooth is ignored, when the operator works from a mirror image (the tendency is to angle the bur away from the long axis), when a cast crown is not aligned with the root, and when a flattened, disk-like chamber in a multirooted tooth is passed through without the drop being felt (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Wrong tooth and caries.** Grossman's recommends marking the tooth on the radiograph and in the mouth before the dam goes on, or cutting the initial access before dam placement; old proximal restorations should be removed in full so that secondary caries is not left behind (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Missed canals.** Causes listed by Grossman's include failure to "externalize" the internal anatomy from the radiograph and incomplete deroofing of the chamber; prevention is angled radiographs, the DG16 explorer or size 06-10 K-files, and magnification (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's adds that a crown with a satisfactory marginal seal may be left in place and accessed through, because magnification and axial light eliminate most access problems, while a deficient crown should be removed (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

**Access without the dam.** Walton and Torabinejad allow initial access without a rubber dam where orientation is difficult (tilted teeth, misaligned castings, calcified chambers), on the condition that no file, reamer or broach is used "unless they are secured by a piece of floss" and a throat pack is placed; a "split dam" or a radiograph with a bur in the access hole are alternatives (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Grossman's, by contrast, presents the dam as the standard of care without this exception (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]: the two positions are about the early access stage only.

**Recognising a perforation.** Walton and Torabinejad list early signs: sudden pain during working length determination when anaesthesia was adequate during access; sudden haemorrhage; burning pain or a bad taste during sodium hypochlorite irrigation; a malpositioned file on the radiograph; or a periodontal reading on the apex locator well short of the expected length (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Grossman's adds that an apex locator distinguishes a bleeding canal from a perforation (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Management by level.** For non-furcal cervical perforations Grossman's gives haemorrhage control with 1:50,000 epinephrine, repair with tricalcium silicate cement/MTA, and informing the patient of a guarded prognosis (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad divide lateral perforations by the bone crest: at or above it the prognosis is favourable and the defect can be "exteriorized" and restored with amalgam, glass ionomer or composite (or covered by a crown margin); below the crest in the coronal third the prognosis is poorest, because a pocket forms, and the aim is to bring the defect above bone, by orthodontic extrusion in the aesthetic zone or by crown lengthening (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Causes (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Gopikrishna, 2021):** bur not parallel to the long axis (tilted, rotated or crowded teeth, crown-root angulation, misaligned crowns, working from the mirror image); calcified or flattened chamber; underprepared access; searching for orifices through a small opening.

| Error | Prevention | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong tooth | Mark tooth on radiograph and in mouth before dam; begin access before dam | Restore the opened tooth; treat the correct tooth; inform patient |
| Incomplete caries removal | Remove old restorations in full | Remove caries before continuing |
| Missed canals | Read the radiograph, angled views, full deroofing, DG16 explorer, 06-10 K-files, microscope | Locate and treat; retreatment if found later |
| Crown/cervical perforation | Study crown-root angulation; "stay lingual" in maxillary laterals and mandibular first premolars; remove crowns if needed; check bur depth and angle | Haemostasis (1:50,000 epinephrine), seal with MTA; guarded prognosis |

*(Gopikrishna, 2021; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017)*

**Recognition of perforation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002):** sudden pain when anaesthesia was adequate; sudden persistent bleeding; burning pain or bad taste with NaOCl; malpositioned file on radiograph; apex locator reading of the periodontal ligament far short of length. An apex locator distinguishes a bleeding canal from a perforation (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Lateral (cervical) perforation, by level (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002):**
- At or above crestal bone: favourable; exteriorise and restore with amalgam, glass ionomer or composite, or cover with the crown margin.
- Below crestal bone, coronal third: poorest prognosis (pocket formation); bring the defect above bone by orthodontic extrusion (aesthetic zone) or crown lengthening, or repair internally.

Furcal perforations are discussed with root perforations in section 6.

## 4. Errors in cleaning and shaping: blockage, ledge, artificial canal

### Explanation

**Blockage.** Grossman's attributes blockage to dentinal debris packed into the middle and apical thirds (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Attar, Chogle and Hsu add restorative fragments, pulp stones and separated instruments, and recommend recapitulation with a patency file, which Goldberg and co-workers define as a small K-file passed passively through the constriction without widening it (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC]. Harty's notes that ledges and blockages "often occur simultaneously": debris builds up, the file loses its path and cuts a ledge (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

**Ledge: causes.** Walton and Torabinejad give four main causes: inadequate straight-line access, inadequate irrigation or lubrication, excessive enlargement of a curved canal with files, and packing debris apically; the canals most prone are "small, curved, and long" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Grossman's cites the longer list of Jafarzadeh and Abbott, which adds working through a proximal cavity, wrong length or curvature assessment, forcing files, non-curved large stainless steel files, skipping sizes, rotating the file at length, over-reliance on chelators, and attempts to remove broken instruments or old fillings or to prepare calcified canals (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The 6th edition of Torabinejad's chapter quantifies the risk: ledging rises significantly above 20 degrees of curvature, and above 30 degrees more than half of canals were ledged; it adds failure to precurve and forcing large files as causes, and warns that overusing EDTA-based lubricants and instruments with pyramidal (cutting) tips increase ledging (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's adds the mechanism: ledges form mainly on the outside of the curve, from non-precurved end-cutting instruments, and they "feel like a hard brick wall" short of length (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Ledge: management.** All three sources agree on the technique, with small differences of detail. Walton and Torabinejad use a No. 10 steel file with the apical 2-3 mm sharply curved, worked toward the curve with a "picking" motion to feel the catch of the original canal (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; Grossman's says to precurve or overcurve the apical 3-4 mm and to "tease" the file past (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's uses a #08 or #10 file with a small bend at the tip and the rubber stop turned to show the bend's direction, pointed away from the ledge (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Once bypassed, the file is worked with short up-and-down movements and the ledge is smoothed or blended into the canal wall. Grossman's points out that ledges made by small instruments are easier to bypass, "while larger instruments tend to create a table" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The 6th edition gives a stepwise protocol (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]: locate the original canal and the ledge under the microscope and on CBCT; if the ledge cannot be seen, enlarge the canal to the ledge in an anticurvature direction with a No. 3 Gates-Glidden bur, because ledges form in a straight line; then bypass with (a) hand files (No. 10 K-file, 2-3 mm bend, "pecking" motion; a micro-Opener #10/.06 under the microscope), (b) small precurved ultrasonic tips, which save dentin, or (c) a precurved .02 taper rotary file (precurved 30-45 degrees, 100 rpm or slower, withdrawn about 1 mm and reinserted until the tip finds the canal); if all fail and a lesion persists, periapical surgery or intentional replantation. If bypass fails, clean, shape and fill to the ledge; Grossman's suggests thermoplastic obturation if the ledge is near the terminus (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Prognosis depends on how much infected debris is left beyond the ledge; "short and cleaned apical ledges have good prognoses" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; teeth with vital tissue apical to the ledge do better than those with necrotic, infected tissue beyond it (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. In retreatment, Cohen's reports that 74% of teeth with short root fillings could be negotiated to adequate length, so a short fill is not in itself a contraindication to retreatment (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Artificial canal.** If the operator, trying to regain length, "bores" apically past a ledge, a new canal is created and may perforate; perforation is checked by apex locator, bleeding on paper points and a radiograph with a file in place; without perforation the canal is filled with warm gutta-percha, and with one the defect is repaired (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. The 6th edition names aggressive use of stainless steel files as the commonest cause, prevents it by a radiograph with the file in place as soon as length is lost, and seals a perforation larger than 0.5 mm with MTA before renegotiating the original canal (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Canal blockage.** Dentinal debris (also restorative fragments, pulp stones, separated instruments) packed into the apical canal, with loss of working length (Gopikrishna, 2021; Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025).
- Prevention: small files first (08, 10); sequential sizes; precurved files; stable stops; copious irrigation; recapitulation with a patency file (a small K-file passed passively through the constriction).
- Management: confirm by radiograph and apex locator; small precurved file with lubricant and irrigant to loosen and flush debris.

**B. Ledge**
- Causes (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Gopikrishna, 2021; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021): curvature over 20-30 degrees (over half of canals above 30 degrees ledged); lack of straight-line access; overuse of EDTA lubricants; inadequate irrigation; large uncurved stainless steel files in curved canals; skipping sizes; rotating files at length; forcing instruments; incorrect working length; packing debris; retrieving broken instruments or old fillings. Most prone: small, curved, long canals. Ledges form on the outer wall of the curve (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- Recognition: file stops short with a solid feel ("hard brick wall"); loss of tactile sense of the canal; radiograph shows the file pointing away from the canal.
- Prevention: preoperative radiograph for curvature; maintain patency; recapitulate; precurve; work passively; sequential sizes; NiTi files.
- Management (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Gopikrishna, 2021; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021):
  0. Locate ledge and original canal (microscope, CBCT); if needed, flare to the ledge in an anticurvature direction with a No. 3 Gates-Glidden drill (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
  1. No. 08 or 10 file, apical 2-4 mm sharply curved, stop marking the bend's direction; alternatives: precurved ultrasonic tip, or precurved .02 rotary file at 100 rpm or less (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
  2. Lubricant; tip directed toward the canal curvature, away from the ledge; picking motion to find the original canal.
  3. Once engaged, short up-and-down strokes; do not withdraw; enlarge gradually and blend the ledge into the wall.
  4. If not bypassed: clean, shape and obturate to the ledge (thermoplastic technique if near the apex); recall; periapical surgery or intentional replantation if disease persists.
- Prognosis: depends on debris left apical to the ledge; short, clean apical ledges do well (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**C. Artificial canal.** Boring apically past a ledge creates a new canal, which may perforate the root (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002). Check for perforation (apex locator, bleeding on paper points, radiograph with file); without perforation fill with warm gutta-percha; with perforation repair it (section 6); seal perforations over 0.5 mm with MTA before renegotiating (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 5. Transportation, zipping and elbow

### Explanation

**Mechanism.** The AAE attributes transportation to "the tendency of files to restore themselves to their original linear shape" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Grossman's distinguishes internal transportation (instrument within the canal, elliptic preparation) from external transportation of the foramen (instrument outside the canal) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Rotating a file in a curved canal at the apex produces the zip and the elbow: less flexible instruments overcut the outer wall apically and the inner wall coronally, giving an hourglass shape whose narrowest point, at the maximum curvature, is the elbow (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Attar, Chogle and Hsu credit the term "apical zip" to Weine and Kelly (1975) (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC]. The definitions of elbow differ slightly: Grossman's calls it "the narrowest portion of the zipped canal", where obturation usually stops; the AAE places it "immediately coronal" to the zip; both describe the same narrowing (Gopikrishna, 2021; American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC].

**Risk factors and prevention.** Harty's: the more coronal the curvature, the harder the canal; prepare a glide path before engine-driven NiTi; reciprocal (balanced-force) hand techniques do better than filing; flexible instruments; in S-shaped canals prepare the coronal curve first (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Grossman's: stainless steel instruments above ISO 20 used aggressively are the usual cause, and NiTi in the right protocol is the safest way to shape complex canals (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Ingle's three types of apical transportation.** Ingle's classifies foraminal transportation by severity (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Type I is a minor shift: if enough dentin remains, shape is created coronal to the foramen and the canal is filled normally. Type II is moderate: canals are often wet and more shaping would weaken or perforate the root, so a barrier (MTA) is placed to control bleeding and give a backstop. Type III is severe: a barrier is not feasible, three-dimensional obturation is impossible, and the tooth is filled as well as possible and corrected surgically, or extracted.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Transportation:** removal of canal wall on the outer curve in the apical half, because files tend to straighten; internal (within the canal) or external (foramen moved onto the root surface) (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Gopikrishna, 2021).
**Zip:** apical transportation of a curved canal into a tear-drop shape with reverse apical taper (term introduced by Weine and Kelly, 1975) (Gopikrishna, 2021; Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025).
**Elbow:** narrowest part of the zipped canal at the point of maximum curvature, coronal to the zip; obturation usually stops there and the zip beyond is underfilled (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Causes:** stiff, large stainless steel files (above ISO 20) taken to length in curved canals; rotating files at the apex; skipping sizes; no glide path; coronal curvature not reduced (Gopikrishna, 2021; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).

**Prevention:** precurve small hand files; never rotate in curved canals; do not skip sizes; balanced-force technique; glide path before NiTi; NiTi instruments; coronal flaring (Gopikrishna, 2021; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).

**Management by severity (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)**
| Type | Description | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| I | Minor movement of foramen | Create shape coronal to the foramen; conventional obturation |
| II | Moderate movement; wet canal | MTA apical barrier, then backfill |
| III | Severe; foramen ripped | Fill as well as possible, then surgery; or extraction |

Thermoplasticised obturation is preferred for zipped or transported canals (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 6. Root perforations and their repair

### Explanation

**What decides prognosis.** Ingle's describes "four dimensions of a perforation" that always occur together: level (the more apical, the better the prognosis), location around the root (it matters mainly for surgical access), size (doubling the diameter quadruples the area to seal) and time (repair as soon as possible to prevent loss of attachment) (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's lists the same factors plus previous microbial contamination, and makes a point examiners like: "the more apical the perforation site, the more favorable the prognosis; however, the converse is true for the repair procedure itself" (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Furcal or coronal-third defects are easy to reach; middle-third (strip or post) defects are harder; apical-third defects often need surgery. Walton and Torabinejad add that a defect under 1 mm causes less destruction and that an untreated furcal perforation communicates with the sulcus "within weeks or sometimes days" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Furcal perforations: two types.** Walton and Torabinejad separate the "direct" type (a punched-out defect made by a bur while searching for an orifice, usually accessible, sealed immediately with MTA with a good prognosis) from the "stripping" type (excessive flaring of the inner wall of a curved root, usually inaccessible, followed by inflammation and a pocket) (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Grossman's and Harty's describe strip perforation the same way and agree that it is recognised by bleeding after the pulp has been removed, and that it needs magnification and is best referred (Gopikrishna, 2021; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

**Repair material.** Walton and Torabinejad list older materials (amalgam, gutta-percha, zinc oxide-eugenol, Cavit, calcium hydroxide, freeze-dried bone, indium foil) and conclude that immediate MTA repair "offers the best results" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Harty's attributes the failures of amalgam and gutta-percha to microleakage and reports good MTA results provided there is no infection and no communication with the oral cavity (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's gives MTA's advantages: it seals even when contaminated with blood, is biocompatible, and a cementum-like tissue grows on it; its long setting time makes it unsuitable for defects that cross the gingival margin, for which a resin-ionomer such as Geristore is preferred (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. The canal orifices must be protected from blockage by the repair material (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Surgical options.** When nonsurgical repair is not possible: hemisection, bicuspidization, root amputation or intentional replantation; the latter when the defect is inaccessible or problems are combined (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** a pathologic or iatrogenic communication between the root canal space and the attachment apparatus (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Types and causes**
| Level | Usual cause |
|---|---|
| Crown/cervical | Misdirected bur during access |
| Furcal, direct | Bur searching for an orifice ("punched-out", accessible) |
| Furcal/middle third, stripping | Over-flaring of the inner (furcal) wall of a curved root |
| Apical | Instrumentation through the foramen or along an artificial canal |
| Post space | Drill not following the canal |

*(Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Gopikrishna, 2021; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017)*

**Recognition:** sudden bleeding (strip perforation: bleeding after pulp removal), pain, apex locator reading short of length, radiograph with file in place (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Prognostic factors, "four dimensions" (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021):**
1. Level: the more apical, the better the prognosis, but the harder the repair.
2. Location: matters mainly for surgical access.
3. Size: doubling the diameter quadruples the area to seal; under 1 mm repairs best (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
4. Time: immediate repair; delay allows sulcular breakdown and an endo-perio lesion.
Plus contamination and the sealing ability of the material.

**Management**
1. Haemostasis (saline, 1:50,000 epinephrine pellet) under the microscope; protect canal orifices.
2. Seal immediately with MTA (seals in the presence of blood, biocompatible, cementum grows on it). Use a resin-ionomer (Geristore) for defects reaching the gingival margin, as MTA washes out before setting (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
3. Supracrestal defects: restore externally or include in the crown margin; subcrestal coronal-third defects: orthodontic extrusion or crown lengthening (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
4. Surgical: repair through a flap; hemisection, bicuspidization, root amputation, intentional replantation; extraction if hopeless (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
5. Inform the patient; guarded prognosis; review periodontal probing at recall.

## 7. Separated (broken) instruments

### Explanation

**Incidence and causes.** Cohen's reports separation in 0.25% of cases with hand instruments and 1.68-2.4% with rotary instruments (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's says it is more common in inexperienced hands (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. NiTi instruments fail by cyclic fatigue (rotation in a curve for too long, or too many uses) or torsional failure (tip locked while the shank turns, from apical force) (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. For hand files, Attar and co-workers note that "overturning a hand file is what usually breaks the file, not the pull strokes", and that 0.06 taper files resist fracture less than 0.04 (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC]. The 6th edition of Torabinejad's chapter adds corrosion by NaOCl and manufacturing defects as causes, notes that NiTi fractures at lower force than stainless steel despite its flexibility, that fatigue life is longer in liquid than in air (irrigant acts as a heat sink), and that reciprocation extends instrument life; recognition is a shortened file with a blunt tip and loss of patency, confirmed by radiograph (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's recommends single use (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's notes UK guidance that files are single-patient use (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

**Can it be removed?** Ingle's: if one-third of the fragment's length can be exposed it can usually be removed; fragments in the straight part of the canal usually come out; those apical to the curve usually cannot. Stainless steel fragments are easier because NiTi may "break again, albeit deeper within the canal" under ultrasonics, presumably from heat (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. The microscope is central: "If you can see it, you can probably do it" (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Technique (Harty's): a staging platform is made at the fragment's head, then a slender ultrasonic tip is worked anticlockwise around it, because most files have clockwise flutes (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Devices named in the sources: Steiglitz forceps, Masserann extractor, Instrument Removal System, Terauchi File Retrieval Kit (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017; Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC]. The 6th edition (written partly by Terauchi) gives a detailed retrieval technique (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]: plan with periapical radiographs and CBCT (length of fragment, curvature, wall thickness); most NiTi fragments are in the apical third; enlarge to the fragment with a #3 Gates-Glidden bur without its pilot tip (or a NiTi file three sizes larger if the fragment is around the curve); a microtrephine rotated counterclockwise at 600 rpm exposes the coronal 1 mm; a sharp ultrasonic tip cuts a thin space on the inner wall, because oscillation from the inner wall moves the fragment coronally (from the outer wall it is pushed apically), over at least one-third of its length and more than 180 degrees around it, at the lowest power, intermittently, with EDTA in the canal; the loosened fragment should come out within about 10 seconds, or with a loop device (needs a space 0.4 mm wide and 0.5 mm long). A staging platform must not be cut beyond the curve, because it causes ledging, perforation or loss of dentin. Attar and co-workers give a decision table: if it can be bypassed, continue; if not, do not attempt removal in the apical third; in the coronal or middle third attempt removal only with straight-line access, weighing risk against benefit (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC].

**Does it affect prognosis?** Here the sources agree on the principle: Harty's states the fragment is not itself the problem, being a noncorrodible alloy, but it blocks disinfection apical to it (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Cohen's states the instrument "in itself does not predispose the case to posttreatment disease", the outcome is better if separation occurs late in preparation, and in a vital tooth without apical periodontitis it should not affect prognosis (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. The 6th edition concurs: separation affects outcome only where microbial control is compromised or a lesion already exists, prognosis is best when a large instrument separates late near working length, and the presence of a periapical lesion is the most important prognostic factor (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. If it cannot be removed or bypassed, Ingle's says "Failing is not always failure": clean and seal to the fragment and follow up, with surgery or extraction if disease appears (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Incidence:** hand 0.25%; rotary 1.68-2.4% (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). Most NiTi fragments lie in the apical third (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Recognition:** shortened file with a blunt tip, loss of patency; confirm by radiograph; inform and document (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Causes:** cyclic fatigue (rotation in a curve, repeated use); torsional failure (tip binding with apical force); corrosion by NaOCl; manufacturing defects; overturning hand files; defective (unwound) instruments; lack of glide path and straight-line access; skipped sizes; dry canals; acute or coronal curvatures; inexperience (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).

**Prevention:** inspect and discard instruments (single use); straight-line access; hand glide path before rotary; manufacturer's speed and torque; do not force or keep rotating in curves; sequential sizes; irrigate and lubricate; balanced-force motion for hand files (Gopikrishna, 2021; Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025).

**Factors in removal (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019):** length, diameter and position of the fragment; canal curvature; root thickness and external concavity; material (NiTi may re-fracture); access to the head (if one-third of its length can be exposed it can usually be removed).

**Management options**
1. **Removal** (coronal and middle thirds, visible fragment), planned with CBCT: modified #3 Gates-Glidden drill (no pilot tip) to the fragment; microtrephine to expose its head; thin ultrasonic tip on the inner wall, more than 180 degrees around and one-third of its length, lowest power, EDTA in the canal, until the fragment moves (Terauchi technique; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021); ultrasonic tips worked anticlockwise; Masserann kit, IRS, Terauchi kit, Steiglitz forceps for loose or exposed fragments (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).
2. **Bypass** with small precurved files and incorporate the fragment in the filling; no evidence of worse prognosis in selected cases (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).
3. **Leave and seal** to the fragment if apical to a curve; review (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).
4. **Surgery** (root-end resection, removal) or extraction if symptoms or a lesion persist.

**Prognosis:** best when a large instrument separates late, near working length; the presence of a periapical lesion is the most important factor (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021); outcome depends on the stage of preparation, preoperative pulp and periapical status, and whether the canal beyond is disinfected; the fragment itself does not cause failure (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).

## 8. Irrigation and medicament mishaps

### Explanation

Harty's lists the factors behind sodium hypochlorite extrusion: overpreparation of the apical foramen, an open apex, poor working length control, not using a dedicated endodontic needle and syringe, and even poor access design; tissue contact causes acute pain, swelling, bruising, necrosis and nerve damage, and one case of life-threatening airway obstruction has been reported (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Treatment depends on severity (saline irrigation, antibiotics, analgesics, cold compresses, antihistamines, rarely extraction or surgery) (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. The 6th edition of Torabinejad's chapter differs on antibiotics: initially there is "no reason to prescribe antibiotics"; visible swelling may need incision and drainage to reduce pressure; treatment is palliative with analgesics and steroids (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. The two positions are shown side by side; neither source gives trial data. The full answer is in the Root Canal Irrigation topic. The preventive details in the exam core (needle loose in the canal, 1-2 mm short of length, side-vented tip, cold then warm compresses) are standard teaching from that topic, not from the chapters read here [RECALL]. Walton and Torabinejad list burning pain or a bad taste during irrigation as a sign of a perforation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], which links this section to section 6. Calcium hydroxide extrusion generally heals, except into the inferior alveolar canal, where it can cause permanent nerve damage; Harty's advises urgent maxillofacial referral and caution with spiral fillers in mandibular premolars and molars (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sodium hypochlorite accident (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017)**
- Causes: overpreparation of the foramen, open apex, poor working length control, needle binding, not using a side-vented endodontic needle, poor access.
- Signs: immediate severe pain, swelling, bruising (ecchymosis), bleeding through the canal; later necrosis, paraesthesia; rarely airway obstruction.
- Management: stop; reassure; aspirate and irrigate with saline; analgesics and local anaesthesia; cold then warm compresses; steroids; incision and drainage for visible swelling; antibiotics only if infection develops (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017 give antibiotics; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021 see no initial reason for them); refer to hospital if the airway is threatened.
- Prevention: needle loose in the canal, 1-2 mm short of length, gentle pressure, side-vented needle.

**Irrigant through a perforation:** burning pain or a bad taste during NaOCl irrigation suggests a perforation; stop irrigating and confirm with an apex locator and radiograph (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Calcium hydroxide extrusion:** usually harmless, but into the inferior alveolar canal it may cause permanent nerve damage; refer to a maxillofacial surgeon at once; avoid spiral fillers in mandibular posterior teeth (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).

## 9. Errors during obturation

### Explanation

**Underfilling.** Grossman's attributes failure to seat the master cone mainly to loss of working length from packed dentinal mud; the remedy is small files with frequent sodium hypochlorite to dislodge it, a check radiograph and reseating the cone (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

The 6th edition of Torabinejad's chapter lists causes (natural barrier, ledge, insufficient flaring, poorly adapted master cone, inadequate condensation) and prefers removal and retreatment that addresses the cause, warning that forcing gutta-percha apically with more spreader or plugger pressure can fracture the root (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Overfilling.** The 6th edition: usually from overinstrumentation through the constriction or lack of taper; an open apex leaves no matrix to condense against; if suspected, radiograph before cutting off the excess, and remove the mass if the sealer has not set; if failure follows, apical surgery removes the material (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's prevention list: a continuous tapered preparation, accurate working length checked frequently by radiograph and apex locator, an apical stop (or an artificial one if absent), more than one master cone radiograph, and in wide canals placing the master cone slightly short so that thermally compacted gutta-percha flows into the constriction (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Plotino and co-workers report a meta-analysis showing that warm gutta-percha overextends more often than cold lateral condensation (Plotino, Venturi and Grande, 2025) [SRC]. Overextension away from vital structures causes little lasting harm, "unless the obturation materials contain paraformaldehyde"; near the inferior alveolar nerve or the maxillary sinus it can cause serious, possibly permanent damage (Plotino, Venturi and Grande, 2025) [SRC]. Nerve injury is mostly reported with mandibular second molars; eugenol-containing pastes are not recommended at all; recovery depends on the extent of the damage and how fast the cause is removed (Plotino, Venturi and Grande, 2025) [SRC]. Extrusion into the antrum has been reported with silver points, thermoplasticised and conventional gutta-percha, and pastes (Plotino, Venturi and Grande, 2025) [SRC].

**Vertical root fracture.** The 6th edition gives post cementation as the main cause and overzealous condensation of an under- or over-prepared canal as the second; signs are a narrow deep pocket, sinus tract and a lateral or J-shaped radiolucency; diagnosis needs visualisation (surgery, removing the restoration) or CBCT, which is limited by scatter; treatment is root removal in multirooted teeth or extraction (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Not from the chapters read [RECALL]:** CBCT for suspected nerve contact as a routine step.

**Irregular canals.** Harty's: ledges and elbows found in retreatment are best filled with a warm gutta-percha technique (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Underfilling**
- Causes: loss of working length (dentinal mud, ledge, blockage, natural barrier), insufficient flaring, poorly adapted master cone, inadequate condensation (Gopikrishna, 2021; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Management: small files and NaOCl to remove debris, check radiograph, reseat the cone; if found after obturation, remove and retreat, correcting the cause; do not force gutta-percha with extra condensation pressure, which can fracture the root (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**B. Overfilling / overextension**
- Causes: overinstrumentation, loss of apical stop, zipped or open apex, inaccurate length, excess condensation force, injected thermoplastic gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021; Plotino, Venturi and Grande, 2025).
- Prevention: tapered preparation with an apical stop (artificial stop or MTA barrier if absent); working length by radiograph and apex locator; master cone radiograph; cone slightly short with warm techniques (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Consequences: little harm if away from vital structures and the canal is well sealed; serious with paraformaldehyde pastes, or near the inferior alveolar canal (paraesthesia, mostly mandibular second molars) or maxillary sinus (sinusitis, aspergillosis) (Plotino, Venturi and Grande, 2025).
- Management: radiograph before cutting off excess; remove the gutta-percha mass if the sealer has not set (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021); small overextensions are observed; apical surgery if failure follows; inferior alveolar nerve involvement needs CBCT and immediate referral for surgical removal, since recovery depends on how fast the cause is removed; sinus involvement may need surgical removal.

**C. Vertical root fracture:** main cause post cementation, then excessive condensation force; narrow deep pocket, sinus tract, J-shaped radiolucency; hopeless: root resection (multirooted) or extraction (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 10. Other mishaps: aspiration and ingestion, heat damage, flare-ups

### Explanation

**Aspiration and ingestion.** Grossman's states that aspiration of a hand instrument happens only when the rubber dam is not in place, that it is a preventable error, and that it can end in life-threatening situations or major surgery (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The 6th edition of Torabinejad's chapter: signs are disappearance of an instrument followed by violent coughing or gagging, confirmed radiographically; immediate medical referral; in Grossman's survey 87% of instruments were swallowed and the rest aspirated; some swallowed and nearly all aspirated instruments need surgical removal (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Accidental injections.** Clear solutions (NaOCl, chloroform, formalin) loaded into empty anaesthetic cartridges have been injected; all caused immediate severe pain, and consequences were worse after an inferior alveolar nerve block than after infiltration (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Heat.** Cohen's: heat to soften gutta-percha and ultrasonics to remove posts or separated instruments can raise the root surface temperature by 10°C or more, and periodontal ligament temperatures more than 10°C above normal damage the attachment apparatus; without coolant, ultrasonic post removal approached that rise in 15 seconds in vitro (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. This is in-vitro and case-report evidence (Cohen's says so), not a clinical threshold study [Likely].

**Flare-ups.** Attar and co-workers: incidence 1.8-3.2% in prospective studies and an average of 8.4% in a meta-analysis of 1966-2007; causes are mechanical, chemical and microbial; antibiotic prophylaxis does not reduce flare-ups, whereas NSAIDs or paracetamol before and after treatment, and long-acting anaesthesia, reduce postoperative pain (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Aspiration/ingestion of instruments:** occurs only without a rubber dam; potentially life-threatening; a medicolegal liability. Prevention: rubber dam always; if access is started without a dam, floss-tied instruments and a throat pack (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002). Signs: lost instrument, coughing or gagging. Management: stop, refer immediately for chest and abdominal radiographs and medical or surgical removal; 87% are swallowed, the rest aspirated; nearly all aspirated ones need surgery (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Accidental injection** of NaOCl, chloroform or formalin from refilled anaesthetic cartridges: severe pain, worse after nerve blocks; prevent by never refilling cartridges (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Heat damage:** heated pluggers and ultrasonics (post or instrument removal) can raise the root surface temperature more than 10°C, which damages the periodontal ligament; without coolant this can happen within about 15 seconds of ultrasonic post removal (in vitro). Severe cases have led to loss of the tooth and bone. Use water coolant, short bursts and the lowest effective power (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

**Flare-up:** incidence 1.8-3.2% (prospective studies), average 8.4% (meta-analysis) (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025). Causes: overinstrumentation, extruded debris, irrigants or medicaments, hyperocclusion, microbial factors. Management: re-diagnose, complete debridement if incomplete, drainage if needed, NSAIDs, occlusal relief; antibiotics only for spreading infection, as prophylaxis does not prevent flare-ups.

## 11. Prognosis and conclusion

### Explanation

The common thread across Cohen's, Harty's and Walton and Torabinejad is that the error does not cause failure by itself; failure comes from the infected canal it leaves uncleaned or the periodontal breach it opens. Cohen's says so directly about ledges and separated instruments: "It is not the complication itself, however that results in persistent disease" but the bacteria left behind (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad say the same for ledges and artificial canals (prognosis depends on the uninstrumented and unfilled portion) and for perforations (prognosis depends on level, size, time and periodontal communication) (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. This is why the recall plan in every section checks for apical healing and periodontal probing. The recall intervals in the exam core (6 months, 1 year, annually to 4 years) follow the usual outcome-assessment schedule and are not stated in the chapters read [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prognosis.** The error itself rarely causes failure; failure comes from infected tissue left beyond a ledge, block or fragment, or from a periodontal breach at a perforation (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002). Prognosis is therefore better when the error happens late in preparation, in a vital tooth without apical periodontitis, is recognised early and is managed at once.

**Follow-up.** Recall at 6 months, 1 year and annually for up to 4 years: symptoms, probing depths at perforation sites, radiographic healing; surgery or extraction if disease persists.

**Conclusion.** Procedural errors are best managed by prevention: case assessment, rubber dam, straight-line access, accurate working length, precurved and sequential instrumentation with copious irrigation, magnification, and knowing when to refer. When an error occurs, early recognition, honest communication with the patient, prompt correction (MTA repair, bypass or removal) and documented follow-up preserve most teeth.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "A separated instrument reduces the prognosis."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's says the fragment "in itself does not predispose the case to posttreatment disease"; outcome depends on stage of preparation and preoperative pulp and periapical status (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's says the same and adds that bypassing does not worsen prognosis in selected cases (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Attar and co-workers state that necrotic cases with lesions and fragments short of length have a lower success rate (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC].
*Agreement:* all three agree; the apparent disagreement is about what is measured. The fragment marks an undisinfected canal; it is the infection, not the metal, that lowers success. [Certain]
*Strength:* the sources cite clinical cohort data indirectly; the clinical studies were not read this session. [Likely]
*Conclusion:* write "prognosis depends on infection status and stage, not on the fragment". Removal that weakens the root or perforates it is worse than leaving the fragment (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Claim 2: "The more apical the perforation, the better the prognosis."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's and Ingle's both state it; Cohen's adds that repair is hardest apically (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad say subcrestal coronal-third perforations have the poorest prognosis, while supracrestal ones are favourable (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].
*Refinement:* the two statements fit together. Supracrestal defects do well because they can be repaired externally; the worst zone is just below the crest, where a pocket forms. Apical defects heal better but are harder to repair from inside and often need surgery. [Certain]
*Conclusion:* prognosis and ease of repair run in opposite directions along the root; both should be written.

**Claim 3: "Ledged or zipped canals should be filled with thermoplasticised gutta-percha."**
*Textbook:* Grossman's prefers thermoplastic obturation for zips and near-apical ledges (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's prefers warm gutta-percha for irregular canals (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Plotino and co-workers report a meta-analysis showing warm gutta-percha overextends more often than cold lateral condensation (Plotino, Venturi and Grande, 2025) [SRC]. A zipped canal has lost its apical constriction, so the risk is highest exactly where the technique is recommended.
*What follows:* use warm techniques for the irregular shape, but with an apical stop or MTA barrier first (Ingle's Type II approach; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC] and the master cone checked short. The sources do not test this combination directly. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Three stages: access, cleaning and shaping, obturation (plus post space); reversible vs irreversible ("domino effect").
- AAE definitions of ledge, perforation, strip perforation, transportation, zip, elbow.
- Ledge: precurved No. 08-10 file, tip toward the curve, picking motion; small ledges bypass, large ones "table".
- Ingle's Type I/II/III apical transportation: reshape / MTA barrier / surgery.
- Perforation "four dimensions": level, location, size (double diameter = 4x area), time; MTA (Geristore at the gingival margin).
- Separation: hand 0.25%, rotary 1.68-2.4%; cyclic vs torsional; one-third exposed = removable; fragment itself not the cause of failure.
- PDL heat damage above 10°C rise; flare-ups 1.8-3.2%, antibiotics do not prevent them.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Listing errors without the stage-wise classification, or forgetting obturation and post-space errors.
- Saying "elbow" and "zip" are the same: the elbow is the narrowing coronal to the zip.
- Writing that a separated instrument always causes failure, or that it must always be removed.
- Saying "apical perforations have the worst prognosis": they repair worst, but heal best.
- Recommending amalgam or Cavit as the perforation repair of choice; MTA is, and Geristore for gingival-margin defects.
