## 1. Definition and related errors

### Explanation

**Definitions compared.** The AAE Glossary: "an artificial irregularity created on the surface of the root canal wall that impedes the placement of instruments to the apex of an otherwise patent canal" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC], which Cohen's Ch.20 quotes (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's: "an artificially created deviation of the root canal wall that prevents the passage of an instrument to the apex of an otherwise patent canal" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter defines it by effect: the working length "can no longer be negotiated" and patency is lost (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Berutti and Castellucci stress what distinguishes it from a perforation: it straightens the curvature "without communication with the periodontium" (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. Ingle's calls a ledge "an internal transportation of the canal" (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]; Attar and co-workers describe "an artificial step within the root canal wall", usually on the outer wall of the curve (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC]. All agree on three elements: iatrogenic, on the canal wall, blocks instruments short of the apex in a canal that is otherwise patent.

**The sequence of errors.** Cohen's Ch.20 places ledging among the results of transportation, with zipping and perforation (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Attar and co-workers describe the progression: unidentified ledges proceed to gouging or an elbow and finally to apical perforation (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter describes the next step: the operator, trying to regain length, "bores" apically and creates an artificial canal, which may perforate (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; Berutti and Castellucci call it a "false canal", tangential to the original pathway (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** A ledge is an artificial irregularity created on the surface of the root canal wall that impedes the placement of instruments to the apex of an otherwise patent canal (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Terauchi and Renton, 2021). It is an internal transportation of the canal (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019): an artificial step, usually on the outer wall of a curve, which straightens the canal without communicating with the periodontium (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025; Berutti and Castellucci, 2025). Clinically, a ledge exists when the working length can no longer be negotiated and patency is lost (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Related errors (American Association of Endodontists, 2012)**
- Transportation: removal of canal wall on the outside of the curve in the apical half, because files tend to straighten.
- Zip: tear-drop widening of the apical foramen from transportation of the outer wall.
- Elbow: narrow part of the canal just coronal to a zip or transportation.
- Artificial (false) canal: a new path bored past the ledge (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Perforation: the artificial canal reaches the root surface.

**Sequence.** Transportation → ledge → artificial canal → perforation; unrecognised ledges progress to gouging, elbow and apical perforation (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025).

| Feature | Ledge | Perforation |
|---|---|---|
| Communication with PDL | No | Yes |
| Bleeding | No | Yes |
| Apex locator | Reading short, no PDL signal | PDL reading short of length |
| Treatment aim | Bypass, reduce, clean beyond | Seal the defect |

## 2. Incidence and clinical significance

### Explanation

Torabinejad's 6th edition and Cohen's Ch.20 report the same study: ledging increases significantly when canal curvature exceeds 20 degrees, and above 30 degrees more than half of the canals were ledged (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021; Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.20 explains why it matters: the ledge prevents shaping and cleaning of the canal beyond it, so there is "a possible causal relationship between ledge formation and an unfavorable endodontic treatment outcome" (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Berutti and Castellucci state that ledging makes it impossible to shape, clean and fill the entire canal and frequently leads to failure (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. Note the hedge in Cohen's ("possible"): see the critical synthesis.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Incidence.** Ledges are among the commonest instrumentation errors. Their frequency rises sharply with curvature: significant above 20 degrees, and over 50% of canals ledged when curvature exceeds 30 degrees (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021; Terauchi and Renton, 2021).

**Significance**
1. The canal apical to the ledge cannot be cleaned, shaped or filled; infected tissue and debris remain (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).
2. Loss of working length leads to underfilling.
3. Continued forcing leads to an artificial canal and perforation.
4. A possible causal link with treatment failure (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
5. In retreatment, ledges are a common reason why old fillings stop short.

**Why curvature matters.** The stiffer and larger the file, the more it resists bending; in a curve it straightens and presses on the outer wall. The coronal third is most important: a severe coronal curve forces the file to approach the apical curve at an angle, so the apical canal is ledged; coronal flaring reduces this. Longer and narrower canals give more chances to lose control (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021). Radiographs show only the buccolingual curve; most canals also curve faciolingually, so true curvature is underestimated without angled views or CBCT (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 3. Aetiology

### Explanation

**Factors.** Cohen's Ch.20 lists instrumentation technique, instrument type, root canal curvature, tooth type, working length, master apical file size, the clinician's expertise and canal location; failure to precurve, inability to achieve a proper glide path and forcing large files into curved canals are "perhaps the most common reasons" (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC].

**Jafarzadeh and Abbott's 14 causes** are reproduced in both Grossman's and Cohen's (Gopikrishna, 2021; Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's 6th edition gives five major causes (straight-line access, precurving, forcing large files, irrigation/lubrication, packing debris) and adds that pyramidal (cutting) tips form ledges and that overusing EDTA lubricants increases the risk (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Berutti and Castellucci name a short working length as "the usual cause", and add aggressive instrumentation with cutting-tip instruments, large rigid stainless steel files (#25, 30), and rotary NiTi without a glide path (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.10: a ledge results from "placing non-precurved, end-cutting instruments into curved canals" (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Canal factors.** The canals most prone are "small, curved, and long"; severe coronal curvature predisposes the apical canal to ledging (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Debris.** Harty's: ledges and blockages "often occur simultaneously"; debris builds up, the file loses its path and cuts a ledge (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Grossman's: blockage is dentinal debris packed into the middle and apical thirds (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Jafarzadeh and Abbott's 14 causes (Gopikrishna, 2021; Terauchi and Renton, 2021)**
1. Access cavity not extended enough for straight-line access.
2. Loss of instrument control when treating through a proximal cavity or restoration.
3. Incorrect assessment of canal direction (curvature).
4. Incorrect working length (working short).
5. Forcing the instrument into the canal wall.
6. Non-curved stainless steel instrument too large for a curved canal.
7. Not using instruments in sequential order.
8. Rotating the file excessively at working length (reaming).
9. Inadequate irrigation or lubrication.
10. Over-reliance on chelating agents.
11. Attempting to retrieve separated instruments.
12. Removing root filling materials in retreatment.
13. Attempting to negotiate calcified canals.
14. Packing debris in the apical canal (blockage).

**B. Grouped by factor**
- Canal: small, curved (over 20-30 degrees), long canals; coronal curvatures; S-shaped and calcified canals (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Instrument: large, rigid stainless steel files (#25 and above) that straighten; cutting (pyramidal) tips; greater taper (less flexible); worn instruments (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Technique: no glide path before rotary NiTi; failure to precurve; skipping sizes; short working length; filing with too much force; lack of recapitulation; overuse of EDTA (Terauchi and Renton, 2021; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Operator: inexperience; impatience.

**Key causes explained**
- *Working short of length:* the file repeatedly cuts at the same point short of the curve and forms the step; the usual cause (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).
- *No glide path:* a rotary or large file entering an unprepared narrow canal cuts its own path (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- *Large stainless steel files:* stiffness rises steeply above ISO 20-25; even precurved they straighten (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- *Rotating at length (reaming) and filing with force:* the tip bores into the outer wall instead of following the canal.
- *Debris and blockage:* packed dentin stops the file, which then deflects into the wall (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).
- *Retreatment and calcified canals:* searching for the canal through old filling or calcification with force (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- *EDTA overuse:* softened dentin is cut more easily by the file tip (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 4. Mechanism and location of a ledge

### Explanation

Berutti and Castellucci: the ledge is always at a curve, on the outer surface, made by a rigid instrument that straightens even when precurved; they add a three-dimensional argument. A mesial molar canal curves both in the buccolingual view (seen on the radiograph) and in the proximal view (not seen); the file straightens both curves, so the sum of the two vectors places the ledge: "In an MB canal, the ledge is always mesio-buccal"; mesiolingual in an ML canal; the canal entrance is exactly opposite (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. Ingle's adds that canals are often more curved than their roots (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's: ledges made by small instruments are easy to bypass, "while larger instruments tend to create a table" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Berutti and Castellucci: a ledge cut with a #20-25 file is easily bypassed, one cut with #40-50 "will become a problem" (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

1. A stainless steel file has elastic memory; in a curved canal it presses hardest on the outer wall apically (and the inner wall coronally) (American Association of Endodontists, 2012).
2. Working short of length, with filing or rotation, concentrates cutting at one point on the outer wall.
3. A step forms; the next, larger file catches on it and deepens it; debris packs apically and blocks the true canal (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).
4. Location: always on the outer wall of the curve (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). In three dimensions the ledge in a mesiobuccal canal of a mandibular molar is mesiobuccal, in a mesiolingual canal mesiolingual; the original canal lies directly opposite (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).
5. Size: the larger the file that made it, the wider the ledge ("table") and the harder the bypass; #20-25 easy, #40-50 difficult (Gopikrishna, 2021; Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).


## 5. Recognition and diagnosis

### Explanation

Cohen's Ch.20 gives the clinical signs: the canal is "straightened" where the file stops following the curve, and there is "suddenly a looser feeling of the file within the canal", with no tip binding; on the radiograph the file tip deviates from the curve, and a file in a single canal should appear at the midpoint of the root; if a root holds more than one canal, CBCT is needed before deciding (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.10 describes ledges as feeling "like a hard brick wall" short of length (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. In retreatment, Berutti and Castellucci: suspect a ledge when the old filling stops before a curve, does not follow the canal anatomy, or stops short of the radiographic root outline (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.20: CBCT, compared with 2D radiography, shows the location and direction of both the ledge and the original pathway (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Clinical (tactile)**
- Loss of working length: a file that previously reached length stops short.
- Solid, "brick wall" stop rather than the springy binding of a narrow canal (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- Sudden looser feel, loss of tip binding; the file "straightens" at the curve (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- Precurved small file passes when turned in one direction only.
- Loss of patency; the apex locator does not reach apex reading.

**Radiographic**
- File tip deviates from the canal curvature toward the outer wall, away from the centre of the root (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- In retreatment: old filling stops short, before a curve, or deviates from the canal (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).
- Angled views (both curvatures).

**CBCT:** shows the ledge, the original pathway and their relationship in sagittal, coronal and axial views; distinguishes ledge from a second canal in the root (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).

**Differential diagnosis:** blockage by debris (often coexists), calcification, sharp natural curvature, separated instrument, perforation (bleeding, PDL reading).

**Blockage vs ledge.** A blocked canal gives a soft or "sticky" stop and a small file may stick in the debris; a ledge gives a hard stop and the file tip slides off it. Both cause loss of length and often coexist (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). If repeated gentle pecking produces stickiness, it is a block; if no sticky spot is found after a reasonable time, suspect a ledge (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

## 6. Prevention

### Explanation

Cohen's Ch.20: glide path preparation prevents ledging and transportation; precurving and not forcing instruments is among the most important rules; NiTi reciprocating instrumentation produces fewer ledges and fractures even with novices; non-cutting tips slip along the wall; heat-treated NiTi (controlled memory, Gold) shows less transportation and better centring (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer: instruments with non-cutting tips and frequent recapitulation prevent ledges and blockages (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009) [SRC]. Grossman's: preoperative radiograph, patency, recapitulation, passive instrumentation, sequential sizes (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's: glide path before engine-driven NiTi; balanced force rather than filing (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Torabinejad's 6th edition: radiographs from different angles and CBCT for curvature; flexible, non-cutting-tip NiTi; passive step-back and balanced force with circumferential filing; lubricants, but not overuse of EDTA (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Attar and co-workers: recapitulation with a patency file, defined by Goldberg as a small K-file passed passively through the constriction (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Assessment:** preoperative radiographs at different angles; CBCT for complex curvature; note curvature over 20 degrees, coronal curves, long and narrow canals (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Access:** straight-line access; remove proximal restorations and coronal interferences; flare the coronal third (crown-down) to reduce the curvature the instrument must follow.

**Instruments**
- Precurve stainless steel hand files to the canal curvature; never force (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- Flexible NiTi with non-cutting (rounded) tips; heat-treated (controlled memory, Gold) alloys centre better; reciprocation reduces ledges even for beginners (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- Avoid large stainless steel files in curves; sequential sizes, no skipping.

**Technique**
- Accurate working length (apex locator and radiograph), maintained throughout; working short invites ledging.
- Glide path (to size 15-20) before rotary instruments (Terauchi and Renton, 2021; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).
- Balanced force or passive step-back with circumferential filing; avoid reaming at length.
- Recapitulation with a patency file; copious NaOCl; lubricants, avoiding EDTA overuse (Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Stop and radiograph as soon as length is lost.

**Crown-down and coronal flaring.** Removing coronal interferences early lets the file reach the apical third with less bending and better tactile sense, and lets irrigant reach deeper so debris does not pack apically (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025). Each file should be worked until loose before the next size; rotary instruments should not go further than hand files have already reached (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Attar, Chogle and Hsu, 2025).

## 7. Principles of management

### Explanation

Berutti and Castellucci's three steps: locate, bypass, eliminate the ledge (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.20: success depends on whether the ledge and the orifice of the original pathway can be seen, reached or felt; visible ledges are less complex; select the shortest file that reaches length, because shorter files are stiffer and bring the fingers closer to the tip (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's agrees and adds patience, perseverance and 21 mm stainless steel files sizes 06-10 with NaOCl or a lubricant (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's 6th edition: if the ledge cannot be seen, enlarge to it with a No. 3 Gates-Glidden in an anticurvature brushing motion, because a ledge forms in a straight line (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Berutti and Castellucci: pre-enlarge to about 1-1.5 mm from the ledge so the file keeps its curve as it approaches (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. Ingle's: "Not all ledges can or should be removed"; weigh risk against benefit to preserve dentin (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Aims:** regain the original canal to working length; clean, shape and fill beyond the ledge; reduce the ledge only as far as safe; preserve dentin.

**Steps (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025)**
1. **Locate** the ledge and the original canal: radiographs, CBCT, microscope; think in three dimensions.
2. **Bypass** the ledge to regain working length.
3. **Eliminate** (reduce, blend) the ledge so that instruments and gutta-percha pass easily.

**General rules (Terauchi and Renton, 2021; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021)**
- Stop forcing files; radiograph with the file in place.
- Flood the canal with NaOCl or a lubricant (glycerine, EDTA gel used sparingly).
- Flare the canal coronal to the ledge in an anticurvature direction (No. 3 Gates-Glidden or NiTi orifice shapers) to within about 1-1.5 mm of it, so a precurved file keeps its bend.
- Use the shortest file that reaches length (21 mm): stiffer, better tactile control.
- Small files (06, 08, 10), new for each attempt as tips dull.
- Magnification and good light; patience.
- Not all ledges should be removed: stop if further work risks weakening or perforating the root (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Why anticurvature flaring helps.** A ledge forms in a straight line from the orifice, so the original canal leaves at an angle on the inner side. Widening the canal coronal to the ledge away from the furcation gives a straighter approach to the inner wall, makes the ledge and the orifice visible under the microscope, and allows a precurved file to swing into the original canal (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021; Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).

## 8. Bypassing the ledge with hand instruments

### Explanation

**Classic technique.** The sources agree on the method and differ on the bend. Torabinejad's chapter: No. 10 steel file, apical 2-3 mm "sharply curved", worked in the direction of the curve with a "picking" motion to feel the catch of the original canal slightly short of the ledge's apical extent (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.20: precurving a #8 or #10 file is critical; a rubber stopper with a directional indicator points toward the original pathway; slight rotation with pecking advances the file (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.10: the directional stop is oriented to the bend and turned away from the ledge, toward the suspected curvature (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's: precurve or overcurve the apical 3-4 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Berutti and Castellucci: the last 3-4 mm of a #10 or #15 file "precurved, gently but sharply, at about 90°", stopper tear toward the tip, introduced from the side opposite the ledge, then rotated to explore about 60 degrees on the side opposite; re-bend after one or two attempts; if it fails, a false path may exist, so probe at 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 mm shorter (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. They recommend C+ files, which are stiffer and hold their bend, and K-FlexoFile Golden Mediums (sizes 12, 17 etc.) to bridge the gaps between ISO sizes (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. Ingle's block technique: precurved 06 or 08 file, single back-and-forth reciprocation with light pressure, irrigate, three repetitions per file, then a new file; if no progress in two to three minutes, change to a viscous chelator (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

**Microscope-guided micro-instruments.** Cohen's Ch.20: guided by CBCT, the original pathway is opened under the microscope; caries detector or methylene blue dye stains its orifice; micro-Opener (#10/.06, only 16 mm long), TFRK microexplorer (0.1 mm biconical tip), and the diamond-coated ELES instrument widen the orifice; ProTaper S1 and S2 used as precurved hand files then shape and reduce the ledge (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's 6th edition also recommends the micro-Opener (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Conventional technique (no microscope)**
1. Select a 21 mm, size 08 or 10 stainless steel K-file (or C+ file, which holds its bend) (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).
2. Bend the apical 2-4 mm sharply (Berutti and Castellucci: about 90 degrees over 3-4 mm; Walton and Torabinejad: 2-3 mm sharply curved).
3. Set a directional rubber stop pointing to the bend (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
4. Insert with the tip turned away from the ledge, toward the inner wall and the expected curvature.
5. At the ledge depth, rotate gently through about 60 degrees on the side opposite the ledge, with a light "picking"/"pecking" motion, to feel the "catch" (sticky spot) of the original canal (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).
6. Withdraw and re-bend after one or two attempts; use a new file when the tip dulls.
7. If unsuccessful, probe 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 mm shorter in case the entrance lies on a false path (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).
8. Once engaged, do not withdraw: short in-and-out movements (1-2 mm) staying apical to the ledge; confirm length with apex locator and radiograph.
9. Progress to 15 and 20 (precurved); intermediate sizes (12, 17) bridge the gaps (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).

**B. Microscope-guided (Terauchi and Renton, 2021)**
- CBCT to measure where the original canal opens relative to the ledge.
- Stain the orifice with methylene blue or caries detector dye.
- Micro-Opener #10/.06 (16 mm), TFRK microexplorer (biconical 0.1 mm tip), diamond-coated ELES instrument in push-pull strokes to open and widen the entrance.
- Then ProTaper S1/S2 precurved as hand files.

**C. Ingle's technique for blocks and ledges (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)**
1. Locate the level of the obstruction; preshape the canal coronal to it without touching it.
2. Fill the canal with full-strength NaOCl or a lubricant.
3. Precurve a size 06 or 08 file abruptly at the tip (stiff, heat-hardened C-Pilot files hold the bend).
4. Touch the obstruction; one back-and-forth reciprocating motion with light apical pressure; remove; irrigate 1 ml NaOCl; repeat three times; discard the file and use a new one, since the tip dulls.
5. Continue until a light "catch" is felt; if no progress in 2-3 minutes, replace NaOCl with a viscous chelator and repeat.
6. Once patent, use the file in a "slip, slide and glide" manner to smooth the glide path.

## 9. Bypassing with ultrasonic and rotary instruments

### Explanation

**Ultrasonics.** Torabinejad's 6th edition: small ultrasonic tips improve visibility and reduce the dentin sacrificed; sharpened and precurved, they enlarge the entrance to the original canal (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.20 adds the drawback that thin tips fracture more easily, so they are activated "in a pulsing and pecking fashion at the lowest possible power setting" to avoid fracture, extending the ledge and heat (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC].

**Rotary.** Torabinejad's 6th edition and Cohen's Ch.20 describe the same technique: a small .02 taper file (PathFile 13 or Race 10) with the apical 2-3 mm precurved 30-45 degrees, rotated at 100 rpm or slower, or with 90 degrees clockwise/30-60 degrees counterclockwise reciprocation at 150 rpm; on meeting the ledge, withdraw about 1 mm and reinsert so the curved tip turns to a new orientation, then follow with larger files (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021; Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's figure shows that at 300 rpm the file went into the ledge, at 100 rpm into the original canal (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC; figure legend].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Ultrasonic tips (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021; Terauchi and Renton, 2021)**
- Indication: entrance of the original canal visible under the microscope or felt as a sticky spot.
- Thin tip (e.g. TFRK-S, ET25), sharpened and precurved toward the original canal.
- Placed in the orifice and activated in short pecking push-pull motions, pulsed, at the lowest power.
- Advantages: visibility, minimal dentin removal. Risks: tip fracture, extending the ledge, heat.
- Follow with negotiating files to establish patency and smooth the walls, then NiTi shaping.

**Rotary NiTi (same sources)**
- Enlarge the canal down to the ledge first, so the orifice of the original path is visible.
- Small, low-taper file (.02; PathFile 13, Race 10), apical 2-3 mm precurved 30-45 degrees in the direction shown on CBCT.
- Slow rotation, 100 rpm or less, or reciprocation (90° clockwise/30-60° counterclockwise at 150 rpm); higher speeds follow the ledge.
- If it stops, withdraw about 1 mm and reinsert: the bent tip rotates to a new orientation; repeat once or twice.
- Then the next larger rotary files to enlarge the original path and reduce the ledge.

**Choice of method.** Hand files remain first choice because of tactile feedback. Ultrasonics are best when the entrance to the original canal can be seen. Slow precurved rotary files help when the canal beyond is long and curved. All work best when CBCT shows the direction of the original canal (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).

## 10. Reducing and eliminating the ledge

### Explanation

Ingle's: once the tip is apical to the ledge, very short push-pull strokes staying apical to it, then longer strokes; if the file slides easily, turn it clockwise on withdrawal, which straightens the apical third of the steel file so it rasps and smooths the ledge on the outer wall; keep short of length to protect the foramen; a size 10 file passed 1 mm through the foramen enlarges it to 0.12 mm and paves the way for a 15 (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Berutti and Castellucci describe the same for sizes 25-40, extracted with clockwise rotation (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. NiTi of greater taper: ProTaper hand files have tapers "three to six times larger" than .02 files and remove the ledge with few files; they are used only after the ledge is bypassed and a glide path to size 15-20 exists; NiTi must be precurved by pulling it through 180-270 degrees in Bird Beak pliers because of shape memory, with the stop oriented to the bend; S1 and S2 to length, then finishing files (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Berutti and Castellucci: "The increased taper of the ProTaper will rapidly eliminate the ledge", using balanced force, but only when the ledge is more than 3-4 mm from the foramen; nearer the foramen, stop rotary shaping 1 mm short of the ledge and "link in the apical 1-2 mm using precurved manual files" (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Hand files (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Berutti and Castellucci, 2025)**
1. Keep the file tip beyond the ledge; very short push-pull strokes, then longer ones as it frees.
2. Turn clockwise on withdrawal: this straightens the apical part of a steel file so it rasps the ledge on the outer wall.
3. Work slightly short of the foramen to keep it small; establish patency with a size 10 file 1 mm through the foramen (enlarges it to 0.12 mm).
4. Increase to 15, 20, 25-40, each precurved, taken past the ledge and withdrawn with clockwise rotation.
5. Irrigate continuously; recapitulate with a size 10 file.

**Greater-taper NiTi (ProTaper S1, S2, F1) (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Berutti and Castellucci, 2025)**
- Only after the ledge is bypassed and a glide path to 15-20 exists.
- Precurve with Bird Beak pliers (pull through 180-270 degrees, since NiTi has shape memory); orient the stop.
- Balanced force; tapers 3-6 times larger than .02 files remove the ledge quickly.
- Canal must be enlarged coronal to the ledge so the file touches only beyond it.

**Limit:** do not use these movements within 3-4 mm of the foramen (risk of external transportation); instead stop rotary shaping 1 mm short of the ledge and connect the apical 1-2 mm with precurved hand files (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025).

**End point:** a size 10 file slides past without catching; the ledge is reduced or blended; finish shaping to the planned size.

## 11. When the ledge cannot be bypassed

### Explanation

Torabinejad's chapter: if the original canal cannot be found, complete cleaning and shaping to the new length; flaring may later allow the ledge to be bypassed (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Grossman's: if the ledge is near the terminus, finish and obturate with injectable thermoplastic gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's: if the patient is truly asymptomatic (not masked by drugs), the periodontium healthy and there is no lesion, prepare and fill to the obstruction and inform the patient of recall and possible surgery; if there are symptoms or a lesion, still pack three-dimensionally (hydraulics may carry material to length) and advise recall, surgery, replantation or extraction (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's cone-fit tip: when the ledge remains apically, trim the master cone to the diameter of the file snug at length, precurve it, dip it in 70% isopropyl alcohol to stiffen it, and notch it to show direction (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.20 adds GentleWave, which cleans the canal system beyond the ledge by fluid dynamics without shaping, and surgery or intentional replantation (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's 6th edition: periapical surgery or intentional replantation with retrograde treatment if the lesion persists (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

1. **Clean, shape and fill to the ledge**: thorough irrigation (activated irrigation to reach beyond the ledge); warm/thermoplastic gutta-percha so material may flow past the ledge (Gopikrishna, 2021).
2. **Master cone fit past a residual apical ledge** (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019): trim the cone to the size of the last file at length, precurve it, stiffen it in 70% isopropyl alcohol for a few seconds, notch the butt end to show direction.
3. **Decision by status (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019):**
   - Asymptomatic, no lesion, healthy periodontium: fill to the ledge, inform, recall.
   - Symptoms or lesion: fill three-dimensionally, review; surgery if it persists.
4. **GentleWave** (multisonic fluid cleaning without shaping) to disinfect beyond the ledge (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
5. **Surgery:** periapical surgery with root-end resection and filling (removes the untreated apical segment), or intentional replantation with retrograde treatment (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).
6. **Extraction** if the tooth is unrestorable or the patient declines.

**Irrigation beyond the ledge.** Since files cannot reach the apical canal, chemical disinfection carries the burden: copious NaOCl, activated irrigation and longer contact time; calcium hydroxide dressing between visits [RECALL for activation and dressing in this context]. The prognosis depends on how much infected tissue remains beyond the ledge (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Documentation and consent:** inform the patient of the ledge, its effect on prognosis and the need for recall (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

## 12. Complications of ledge management

### Explanation

Cohen's Ch.20: root fracture, root perforation, worsening the ledge and instrument separation; because ledges lie at curves, bypassing or removing them sacrifices dentin, weakening the root (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's 6th edition: aggressive stainless steel use is the commonest cause of the artificial canal; take a radiograph with the file as soon as length is lost; seal a perforation over 0.5 mm with MTA before renegotiating the original canal (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Thin ultrasonic tips fracture (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Complication | Prevention / management |
|---|---|
| Artificial (false) canal | Radiograph with file as soon as length is lost; stop boring; locate original canal under microscope and CBCT (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) |
| Perforation | Check by apex locator, bleeding, radiograph; seal over 0.5 mm with MTA before renegotiating (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) |
| Worsening the ledge | Small files, slow rotation, no force |
| Instrument separation | New files; low speed; no force; inspect instruments |
| Root weakening/fracture | Minimal dentin removal; ultrasonics at lowest power; accept "not all ledges should be removed" |
| Apical transportation/zip | Keep instruments short of the foramen when reducing apical ledges |
| Heat damage | Pulsed ultrasonics with irrigation |

*(Terauchi and Renton, 2021; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)*

## 13. Ledges in retreatment and prognosis

### Explanation

**Retreatment.** Cohen's Ch.10: ledges and blocks are often visible as short root fillings; warn the patient they may prove impenetrable, but this should not deter retreatment: in one study 74% of teeth with short fillings were negotiated to adequate length (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Removing old fillings is itself one of the 14 causes of ledging (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Prognosis.** Torabinejad's chapter: failure depends on the debris left beyond the ledge; "short and cleaned apical ledges have good prognoses" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; the 6th edition adds that vital tissue beyond a ledge carries a better prognosis than necrotic, infected tissue (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.20: a non-negotiated ledge may have a prognosis similar to a retained separated instrument; if bypassed and cleaned without excessive enlargement or perforation, prognosis "should not be that greatly reduced"; outcome depends on disinfection and the host response to residual biofilm (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Ledges in retreatment:** suspect when the old filling is short, stops at a curve or deviates; remove the old filling carefully (itself a cause of ledging); 74% of short-filled canals can be negotiated to length, so a short fill is not a contraindication to retreatment (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

**Prognosis depends on (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021; Terauchi and Renton, 2021):**
1. Whether the ledge is bypassed and the canal beyond cleaned and filled.
2. Pulp status beyond the ledge: vital tissue better than necrotic infected tissue.
3. Stage of preparation when it occurred: short, late, apical ledges in a cleaned canal do well.
4. Presence of periapical lesion.
5. Complications: perforation or excessive enlargement worsens prognosis.
6. Host response to residual biofilm.

**Summary:** a bypassed, cleaned ledge has little effect on prognosis; a non-negotiated ledge behaves like a retained separated instrument: acceptable in vital, uninfected canals, guarded in infected ones. **Recall** at 6 and 12 months and then yearly: symptoms, sinus tract, radiographic healing; surgery if a lesion persists or enlarges [RECALL for intervals].

**Effect of when the ledge forms.** A ledge formed late, near the working length, after most of the canal has been cleaned, leaves little debris and has a good prognosis. A ledge formed early, short of a long uninstrumented, infected canal, carries a poorer prognosis, and the untreated segment may need surgical resection (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Medicolegal:** inform the patient of the ledge, its effect on prognosis, the importance of recall and the signs of failure; record in the notes (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 14. Conclusion

#### ✎ Exam core

A ledge is an iatrogenic step on the outer wall of a curved canal caused mainly by working short with large, uncurved, stiff instruments without a glide path. It is prevented by straight-line access, precurved small files, glide path, flexible non-cutting NiTi, accurate working length, recapitulation and irrigation. Management follows three steps: locate (radiographs, CBCT, microscope), bypass (precurved small hand files with directional stops, micro-instruments, ultrasonic tips or slow precurved rotary files) and reduce the ledge (short push-pull strokes, clockwise withdrawal, precurved greater-taper NiTi), stopping before the root is weakened. When bypass fails, clean and fill to the ledge, recall, and use surgery or replantation if disease persists. Prognosis depends on whether the canal beyond the ledge is disinfected.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "A ledge causes treatment failure."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's Ch.20 writes of a "possible causal relationship" (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]; Berutti and Castellucci say ledging "frequently leads to treatment failure" (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC].
*Refinement:* the same Cohen's chapter and Torabinejad's say a bypassed, cleaned ledge barely changes prognosis, and that vital tissue beyond a ledge does better than infected tissue (Terauchi and Renton, 2021; Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. As with separated instruments, the ledge is a marker of undisinfected canal, not the cause of disease itself. [Likely]
*Strength:* the sources do not cite outcome studies of ledged canals specifically; "possible causal relationship" is the most the evidence allows. [Certain]

**Claim 2: How much should the bypass file be bent?**
| Source | Bend |
|---|---|
| Walton and Torabinejad, 2002 | apical 2-3 mm, "sharply curved" |
| Gopikrishna, 2021 | apical 3-4 mm, precurve or overcurve |
| Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021 | small bend at about 45 degrees to the shaft, stop oriented to the bend |
| Berutti and Castellucci, 2025 | last 3-4 mm at about 90 degrees |
| Nosrat et al, 2021 (rotary) | apical 2-3 mm at 30-45 degrees |
The sources do not disagree on principle; the bend depends on the canal and the instrument (steeper for hand files at a sharp ledge, gentler for rotary). Write "a sharp bend in the apical 2-4 mm" and name the variation. [Certain]

**Claim 3: "Every ledge should be removed."**
*Textbook:* most sources aim to bypass and blend the ledge; Ingle's states "Not all ledges can or should be removed" and Berutti and Castellucci forbid elimination movements within 3-4 mm of the foramen (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]. Cohen's lists root fracture and perforation as complications of removal (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC].
*Conclusion:* bypass and disinfect beyond the ledge is the goal; removing the ledge is secondary and limited by remaining dentin.

**Claim 4: "Fill ledged canals with thermoplastic gutta-percha."**
*Textbook:* Grossman's recommends it near the apex (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. *Challenge:* warm gutta-percha overextends more often than cold lateral condensation in a meta-analysis (Plotino, Venturi and Grande, 2025) [SRC]. Where the ledge has been bypassed and the foramen enlarged, the risk of overextension rises; use a well-fitted cone and controlled heat. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- AAE: artificial irregularity on the canal wall impeding instruments to the apex of an otherwise patent canal; no communication with the PDL (unlike perforation).
- Over 20 degrees curvature raises risk; over 30 degrees more than half of canals ledged.
- Jafarzadeh and Abbott: 14 causes; commonest: no precurve, no glide path, forcing large files, working short.
- Always on the outer wall of the curve; mesiobuccal in MB canals; original canal opposite (Berutti and Castellucci).
- Signs: brick-wall stop, looser feel, file tip off the root centre on radiograph; CBCT locates ledge and canal.
- Bypass: shortest file, 08-10, sharp bend, directional stop, rotate about 60 degrees with picking; micro-Opener, ultrasonics (lowest power), precurved .02 rotary at 100 rpm, withdraw 1 mm and reinsert.
- Reduce: short push-pull, clockwise withdrawal; ProTaper S1/S2 precurved 180-270° in Bird Beak pliers; not within 3-4 mm of the foramen.
- Cannot bypass: fill to ledge, recall; GentleWave; surgery or replantation.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Confusing a ledge with a perforation: a ledge does not communicate with the periodontium and does not bleed.
- Writing that the ledge is on the inner wall; it is on the outer wall (strip perforation is on the inner wall).
- Recommending large files or rotary instruments at full speed to "break through" the ledge; this deepens it or creates an artificial canal.
- Omitting prevention, which carries marks in every version of this question.
- Stating that every ledged canal fails; prognosis depends on disinfection beyond the ledge.
