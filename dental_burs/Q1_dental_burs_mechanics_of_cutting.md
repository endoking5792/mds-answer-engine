## STUDY MODE

A dental bur is a rotary cutting instrument consisting of three parts — the **shank** (fits the handpiece: straight for straight handpiece, latch-type for contra-angle at slow speed, friction-grip/FG for the high-speed air-turbine handpiece), the **neck** (tapering connector, the weakest part, also the point where overheating is most likely to cause fracture), and the **head** (the working/cutting part, bearing the blades or flutes). [RECALL] [Certain]

Cutting occurs by a **shearing/chip-formation mechanism**, not by abrasion in the strict sense (abrasion is diamond burs' mechanism — see below). Each blade on a steel or tungsten-carbide bur has a defined edge geometry described by the **rake angle** (angle of the cutting face to a radius drawn through the cutting edge) and the **clearance angle** (angle relieving the trailing face so it does not rub the cut surface). A more positive rake angle gives a sharper, more aggressive edge that removes tooth structure faster but is mechanically weaker and dulls sooner; a more negative/neutral rake angle sacrifices some cutting speed for blade strength and longer service life (Mr Bur Education, mrbur.com; general bur-design consensus). [LIT] [Likely]

As the rotating blade engages enamel or dentin, it shears off a **chip** ahead of the cutting edge — the same basic metal-cutting principle as a lathe tool — provided the bur is rotating fast enough and engaging the tooth with enough pressure to exceed the tissue's shear strength; too little speed/pressure causes rubbing/burnishing rather than cutting, and too much causes chatter, heat generation and bur/tooth damage. [RECALL] [Certain]

**Diamond burs** cut by a fundamentally different mechanism: **abrasion**. Diamond chips (industrial diamond grit) are bonded to a steel blank by electroplating or sintering; there is no defined cutting edge/rake angle, and material is removed by multiple random abrasive particles scratching/grinding the surface rather than by discrete shearing blades. Diamond burs are therefore efficient on enamel and ceramic (hard, brittle substrates) but relatively inefficient and more heat-generating on dentin compared with tungsten-carbide burs. [RECALL] [Certain]

Heat generation is the central clinical concern in bur mechanics: inefficient cutting (dull blades, excessive pressure, inadequate coolant, high rotational speed without water spray) converts mechanical energy to heat at the bur-tooth interface, risking pulpal injury (Zach and Cohen's classic finding that a pulpal temperature rise of ~5.5°C can cause irreversible damage is the standard cited threshold). Air-water spray coolant, intermittent/light brush-stroke cutting technique, and a sharp bur are the standard countermeasures. [RECALL] [Certain]

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: ~7 minutes)*

**Parts of a bur:** Shank – Neck – Head.

**Cutting mechanisms:**
1. **Shearing (steel/tungsten carbide burs):** defined blades with a rake angle (cutting edge sharpness) and clearance angle; chip is sheared off ahead of the blade, analogous to a lathe cutting tool.
2. **Abrasion (diamond burs):** randomly bonded diamond chips grind/scratch the surface; no discrete cutting edge.

**Factors governing cutting efficiency:** bur sharpness (rake angle), rotational speed, applied pressure, blade/grit design (number of blades, crosscuts), and adequate air-water coolant.

**Clinical significance:** inefficient cutting → heat generation → pulpal damage (>5.5°C rise = irreversible injury); hence intermittent light-pressure technique with coolant spray is mandatory, especially at high speed.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Bur = Shank + Neck + Head.
- Steel/carbide burs cut by shearing (defined blade, rake + clearance angle); diamond burs cut by abrasion (random grit, no blade).
- Rake angle: more positive = sharper/faster cutting but weaker edge; more negative = stronger, slower-cutting, longer-lasting.
- Heat generation is the key clinical hazard — >5.5°C pulpal rise = irreversible damage (Zach & Cohen).
- Countermeasures: sharp bur, light intermittent pressure, air-water coolant.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling diamond-bur cutting "shearing" — it is abrasion, a distinct mechanism.
- Omitting the neck as the weak point prone to fracture.
- Forgetting coolant/heat-generation as the clinical tie-in (examiners expect this link even in a 5m note).
