## STUDY MODE

### Tungsten carbide burs — design
Manufactured by **sintering tungsten carbide particles with a cobalt binder** under heat and pressure, producing a material far harder than stainless or carbon steel while remaining tough enough to machine discrete cutting blades. The head carries a defined number of **milled blades (typically 6–8 for cutting burs, up to 12+ for finishing burs)**, each with a specific **rake angle** (cutting-edge sharpness) and **clearance angle** (relief behind the edge). Blades may be **plain-cut** (continuous blade, aggressive, rougher finish) or **cross-cut** (transverse grooves segmenting the blade, improving chip clearance and cutting speed at the cost of surface smoothness). Autoclavable and far more wear-resistant than steel, which is why it has replaced steel as the routine cavity/crown-prep bur. [RECALL] [Certain]

### Tungsten carbide burs — function/cutting mechanism
Cuts by **shearing**: each blade engages the tooth surface and shears off a discrete chip, analogous to a lathe tool cutting metal — provided speed and pressure are adequate to exceed the shear strength of enamel/dentin. This gives tungsten-carbide burs a **smoother surface finish** than diamond burs (the tissue is cut/chipped away cleanly rather than ground), and they are generally regarded as more efficient and controllable on **dentin** and on metal (trimming amalgam, cutting crown preparations in alloy) because the discrete-blade geometry handles a softer, more homogeneous substrate well. ### Diamond burs — design
A steel blank onto which **industrial diamond chips/grit** are bonded, either by **electroplating** (single layer, less durable, cheaper) or **sintering/powder metallurgy** (multiple layers, more durable, used in surgical-length/long-life instruments). There is **no discrete blade, rake angle or clearance angle** — the cutting surface is an irregular field of randomly oriented abrasive particles. Grit size is graded by colour-coded bands (coarse/black or green, medium/blue, fine/red, extra-fine/yellow, super-fine/white, in commonly used colour conventions), determining aggressiveness versus surface finish. [RECALL] [Certain]

### Diamond burs — function/cutting mechanism
Cuts by **abrasion**: the bonded grit grinds/scratches the surface rather than shearing a defined chip. This makes diamond burs particularly effective on **hard, brittle substrates — enamel and ceramic** — where a shearing blade would tend to chip or fracture the material unpredictably, whereas multi-point abrasive contact removes it more controllably. Diamond burs are the standard choice for **crown/veneer preparation finishing margins in enamel**, for cutting **all-ceramic/porcelain restorations**, and for gross reduction where surface roughness of the cut itself is less important than control on a hard substrate. [RECALL] [Likely]

### Comparative summary
| Feature | Tungsten carbide | Diamond |
|---|---|---|
| Cutting mechanism | Shearing (defined blade) | Abrasion (random grit) |
| Surface finish | Smoother | Rougher |
| Best substrate | Dentin, metal | Enamel, ceramic |
| Edge geometry | Rake + clearance angle | None (grit-dependent) |
| Grading | Blade number/cross-cut pattern | Grit size (colour-coded) |
| Durability | Good, re-sharpenable in theory | Wears as grit dislodges; not re-sharpenable |

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
Textbook teaching generally frames diamond burs as categorically better on enamel and carbide burs as categorically better on dentin, treating this as settled fact. Retrieved commercial/clinical-summary evidence complicates this cleanly: one summarized British-Dental-Journal-linked handpiece study found diamond burs generated only *slightly* more heat than carbide burs under high- and low-torque conditions, and attributed the (small) difference to the diamond bur's larger surface-contact area rather than to the cutting mechanism per se — and the same summary found that modern handpiece water-spray systems kept pulp-chamber temperature well below the injury threshold (<35°C) regardless of bur type, concluding that **coolant delivery matters more than bur-type choice for heat control**. ## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes)*

**I. Design**
- Tungsten carbide: sintered WC + cobalt binder; milled blades (plain-cut/cross-cut), defined rake + clearance angle; autoclavable.
- Diamond: steel blank + bonded diamond grit (electroplated or sintered); no discrete blade; graded by grit size (colour-coded).

**II. Function/mechanism**
- Tungsten carbide = shearing (discrete chip removal) → smoother finish, preferred for dentin/metal.
- Diamond = abrasion (multi-point grinding) → preferred for enamel/ceramic/hard brittle substrates.

**III. Comparison table** (as above).

**IV. Clinical selection logic:** carbide for bulk tooth-structure/metal removal and a smooth cavity wall; diamond for enamel margins, ceramic cutting, and crown-prep finish lines where chip-free control on a brittle substrate matters more than surface smoothness.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Carbide = sintered WC/cobalt, milled blades, shears; diamond = bonded grit on steel blank, abrades.
- Carbide → smoother finish, preferred for dentin/metal; diamond → preferred for enamel/ceramic.
- Diamond grit graded by colour-coded size (coarse → super-fine); carbide graded by blade count/cross-cut.
- Comparative heat-generation evidence shows only a small difference between the two, with coolant delivery mattering more than bur material.
- Diamond cannot be "resharpened" — once grit is lost the bur must be discarded; carbide blades at least retain a defined edge geometry.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Treating the enamel/dentin substrate preference as an absolute rule rather than a general clinical guideline (evidence is genuinely mixed).
- Forgetting cobalt as carbide's binder metal.
- Omitting that diamond burs cannot be re-sharpened once grit is lost — a frequently tested design-consequence point.
