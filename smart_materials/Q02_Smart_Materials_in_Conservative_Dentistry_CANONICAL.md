## 1. Introduction and the conceptual shift

Conservative dentistry has long relied on bioinert, passive restorative materials: amalgam, conventional composite and cements. These were chosen to survive in the oral cavity with minimal interaction with the surrounding tissue (Maloo et al, 2022; Badami & Ahuja, 2014) `[LIT]`.

The current paradigm asks for functional materials, meaning materials that perform an active task and change in a purposeful way as part of how they work (Badami & Ahuja, 2014) `[LIT]`. Such materials are called **smart** or **intelligent** materials.

### 1.1 Definition

Smart materials are materials whose properties can be altered in a controlled, usually reversible, way by stimuli such as stress, temperature, moisture, pH, or electric and magnetic fields (McCabe et al, 2011, cited in Loya et al, 2023) `[LIT]`. A material behaves "smartly" when it senses a stimulus and responds in a useful, reliable, reproducible and usually reversible manner. The key feature is that it returns to its original state once the stimulus is withdrawn (Badami & Ahuja, 2014) `[LIT]`.

### 1.2 What "smart" means in restorative dentistry

In restorative and caries-related practice, a material earns the label "smart" if it can:
- sustain remaining tooth structure;
- permit minimally invasive cavity preparation;
- actively take part in the curing or caries-control process, for example through bacteriostatic or bactericidal action; and
- reverse to baseline once the triggering condition resolves (Maloo et al, 2022) `[LIT]`.


#### ✎ Exam core

### Introduction
Restorative materials were traditionally chosen to be passive and inert, so that they could survive in the mouth without interacting with it; amalgam, conventional composite and cements are examples (Maloo et al, 2022; Badami and Ahuja, 2014). The present philosophy asks materials to be functional: to perform a task and change in a purposeful way. The first sign of this shift was the realisation that fluoride release from a material is beneficial (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).

### Why conventional materials are not enough
- Secondary caries at the margins is the leading cause of restoration failure, driven by microleakage and the lack of antimicrobial action in conventional materials (Sun et al, 2025).
- Resin composites last on average 6–10 years; they fail by fatigue, hydrolytic breakdown and interface degradation, and each replacement is larger than the original (Khan, 2025).
- Conventional materials differ from tooth in modulus, hardness and thermal expansion (risking microleakage), lack the enamel–dentine gradient, and may release unreacted monomers (Sun et al, 2025).
- Smart materials aim to answer these problems actively: release ions when pH falls, kill bacteria at the interface, and repair microcracks.

### Definitions
- **Smart material:** a material with one or more properties that can be changed in a controlled fashion by external stimuli such as stress, temperature, moisture, pH, and electric or magnetic fields (Badami and Ahuja, 2014; McCabe et al, 2011).
- **Smart behaviour:** sensing a stimulus and reacting to it in a "useful, reliable, reproducible, and usually reversible manner", with return to the original state when the stimulus is removed (Badami and Ahuja, 2014; Maloo et al, 2022).
- **Smart material in conservative dentistry:** one that helps preserve remaining tooth structure, permits minimally invasive preparation and takes an active part in healing, for example by bacteriostatic or bactericidal action (Maloo et al, 2022).
- **Smart versus intelligent:** some authors hold that no material is truly smart, only responsive; a truly intelligent material would sense, process the information and decide on a response (Takagi, 1990, as cited by Ved et al, 2025). No artificial material is yet intelligent in that sense (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- **Three functions of material intelligence:** detecting a change in the environment, processing the sensed information, and responding to it (Ved et al, 2025).
- **Smart versus bioactive versus biocompatible:** biocompatibility means coexisting with tissues without harm; bioactivity means a measurable beneficial biological effect (ion release with apatite formation and a cellular response); ion release alone does not make a material bioactive (Abozaid et al, 2026; Dascanio et al, 2026). The 2023 FDI policy statement asks for a clear mechanism, a documented effect, its duration, absence of adverse effects and preservation of the restorative function (Abozaid et al, 2026).

## 2. Classification

Three independent, complementary classification axes exist in the literature. A complete answer should state all three.

### 2.1 By stimulus or property (McCabe et al, 2011; Loya et al, 2023; Bruni et al, 2019) `[LIT]`

Material types in this axis are:
- thermochromic;
- piezoelectric;
- magnetorheological;
- shape-memory;
- photochromic;
- pH-sensitive; and
- ion-release-and-recharge.

Bruni et al (2019) group the stimuli by origin:
- **physically dependent**: temperature, electric field, specific wavelength, ultrasound, magnetic field, mechanical deformation;
- **chemically dependent**: pH, ionic strength, redox state, solvent; and
- **biologically dependent**: glucose, glutathione, enzymes, inflammatory metabolites.

### 2.2 By mechanism: passive versus active (Maloo et al, 2022) `[LIT]`

- **Passive** smart materials react to the environment with no control over the reaction. Examples are conventional composite, glass-ionomer cement (GIC), resin-modified GIC (RMGI) and compomer.
- **Active** smart materials use a feedback-loop mechanism. Examples are smart composites, smart GIC, smart ceramics, ACP-releasing pit-and-fissure sealants, self-healing composites and SmartPrep burs.

The distinction matters clinically. An active material does more than degrade predictably; it senses and intervenes.

### 2.3 By material category (Sun et al, 2025) `[LIT]`

The text names seven categories (nanocomposite, hydrogel, biomaterials, chemical materials, composite resins, smart carriers, ceramics), grouped under six section headings:
1. nano-restoration materials;
2. hydrogel restorative materials;
3. natural agents and extracts;
4. chemical and resin-based smart materials;
5. intelligent carrier materials; and
6. ceramic and ceramic-based smart repair materials.

This is the most materials-science-complete framework and underlies the material account in Section 3.

Sun et al add that incomplete understanding of mechanisms still hinders systematic classification (Sun et al, 2025).

**6. By material source** (Sun et al, 2025): nanocomposites, hydrogels, natural agents (antimicrobial peptides, autologous bone), chemical materials, composite resins, intelligent carriers, ceramics (seven categories in the text, grouped under six headings).

**7. By degree of bioactivity** (Abozaid et al, 2026): high (45S5 Bioglass, some calcium phosphate cements), moderate (some RMGI, newer bioactive composites), low (some GIC and ion-releasing resins that release ions but do not readily form apatite).

### Mechanisms of smart behaviour
- **pH-triggered ion release:** ACP stays amorphous at neutral pH; at or below pH 5.8 it forms a gel within seconds and amorphous crystals in under 2 minutes, depositing apatite (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- **Apatite formation by bioactive glass** (Hench): surface Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange for H⁺ with pH rise; Si–O–Si hydrolysis; silanol formation; Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻ accumulation; apatite nucleation and crystallisation (Dascanio et al, 2026).
- **Water exchange:** GIC loses and regains loosely bound water, balancing thermal dimensional change (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- **Phase transformation:** stress-induced austenite to martensite in NiTi; tetragonal to monoclinic in zirconia at a crack tip (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
  - *Superelasticity:* under stress austenite becomes martensite and springs back when the stress is released; *shape memory:* deformed martensite returns to its original shape when heated above the transformation temperature (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- **Crack-triggered healing:** microcapsules rupture and release monomer that polymerises (Khan, 2025).
- **Contact killing and triggered release:** quaternary ammonium groups disrupt bacterial membranes; chitosan swells and releases agents below pH 5.5 (Yao et al, 2023; Vasluianu et al, 2025).
  - The positively charged quaternary nitrogen disturbs the charge balance of the negatively charged bacterial membrane, which ruptures (Yao et al, 2023). In acid, protonated amine groups of chitosan make it swell and become soluble, releasing loaded fluoride, zinc or drugs (Vasluianu et al, 2025).

## 3. Material-by-material account

### 3.1 Smart (pH-triggered, ACP-releasing) composites

A nano-filled, light-activated alkaline composite, exemplified by Ariston pH Control (Ivoclar Vivadent), stays stable at neutral or high pH. When intraoral pH falls below the critical threshold of 5.5, it releases calcium, fluoride and hydroxyl ions to arrest demineralisation and promote remineralisation (Loya et al, 2023; Ved et al, 2025) `[LIT]`.

- Bulk cure to 4 mm is possible.
- It is indicated for Class I and II lesions in both primary and permanent dentitions (Loya et al, 2023) `[LIT]`.
- Amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) is the most soluble physiologically important calcium phosphate. It converts fastest to crystalline hydroxyapatite, so it works as a demand-triggered mineral reservoir within the resin (Ved et al, 2025, citing Skrtic et al) `[LIT]`.

### 3.2 Self-healing composites and resins

Two mechanisms exist (Khan, 2025) `[LIT]`:
- **Extrinsic** systems embed microcapsules of 10 to 300 μm containing a healing monomer. When a crack forms, the capsule ruptures and the monomer polymerises in situ with an embedded catalyst.
- **Intrinsic** systems build reversible chemistry into the polymer network itself. Examples are Diels-Alder and retro-Diels-Alder bonds, disulfide-bond exchange, hydrogen-bonded supramolecular networks, and shape-memory polymers.

Laboratory fracture-toughness recovery of about 65 to 77% has been shown at 10 to 20 wt% microcapsule loading (Khan, 2025) `[LIT]`.

**Original experimental data.** Yao et al (2023) report a multifunctional dental resin containing nanoparticle-modified self-healing microcapsules. At 10% microcapsule mass fraction it achieved:
- 80.3% bacteriostasis; and
- 66.1% self-healing efficiency,

with hydrophilicity, mechanical properties and cell toxicity unaffected `[LIT]`. The full text adds important limits: the base was an unfilled BisGMA/TEGDMA (1:1) resin, not a filled composite; only S. mutans was tested; flexural strength fell from 56.0 to 49.1 MPa at 10% capsules (not statistically significant) and fell significantly at 15–20%; and only static mechanical tests were done (Yao et al, 2023) `[LIT]`. So the study shows that both functions can coexist in an experimental resin without a significant loss at 10% loading; it does not show that a clinical composite behaves the same way. Review-level sources discuss this question only in the abstract, so this is the most direct answer to it in the supplied set.

Short-term water ageing (6 months) did not significantly compromise self-healing or bactericidal performance in microcapsule-modified luting cements (Khan, 2025) `[LIT]`.

### 3.3 Smart and bioactive antimicrobial or antibiofilm materials

Vasluianu et al (2025) document three platforms with quantified performance `[LIT]`:
1. **Bioactive glass ceramics (BGCs)** reduce biofilm by more than 90% while promoting bone integration.
2. **Silver-nanoparticle (AgNP)-doped polymers** are effective against *S. mutans* and *C. albicans*. They must be dosed below 0.3 wt% in PMMA to avoid cytotoxicity. This is a real safety-efficacy trade-off, not a case where more is better.
3. **pH-responsive chitosan coatings** release drug in response to acidity, which disrupts acidic cariogenic biofilms.

The same review names emerging approaches: quaternary ammonium compounds, graphene oxide hybrids and 4D-printed hydrogels offering on-demand antimicrobial and regenerative function `[LIT]`.

Its stated barriers to clinical translation are:
- cytotoxicity must be addressed;
- antibiofilm testing needs standardisation, with a proposed benchmark of at least 3-log CFU/mL reduction, which is a suggestion and not an adopted standard; and
- long-term efficacy is unproven (Vasluianu et al, 2025) `[LIT]`.

### 3.4 Bioactive-particle platforms (the substrate beneath 3.1 to 3.3)

Dascanio et al (2026) catalogue the structural diversity behind bioactive and smart ion-releasing behaviour `[LIT]`:
- silicate-, phosphate- and borate-based bioglasses;
- bioactive glass-ceramics;
- amorphous calcium phosphate;
- hydroxyapatite;
- mesoporous bioactive glasses (MBGs); and
- hybrid composites.

Emerging systems include dendrimers, functional peptides, enzymes, micelles and phytoderivatives.

Their conclusion is that bioactivity shifts the function of dental materials from structural restoration to therapeutic and regenerative performance. Clinical consolidation, however, requires robust validation, regulatory standardisation and long-term functional stability. In their words, bioactivity "must be recognised as a measurable and clinically relevant therapeutic property" rather than a marketing concept (Dascanio et al, 2026) `[LIT]`.

### 3.5 Smart glass-ionomer cement

Davidson (1998) proposed that GIC is intrinsically "intelligent." Its hydrogel structure reversibly absorbs and releases water in response to temperature and pH, loosely mimicking dentine fluid dynamics. It also gives a long-term, rechargeable, low-level fluoride release rather than a single depleting burst (Davidson, 1998, cited in Loya et al, 2023) `[LIT]`.

This is the field's oldest and most clinically embedded example of a smart restorative material.

### 3.6 Smart ceramics

Zirconia's smart behaviour is a stress-induced phase transformation. Zirconia is tetragonal at firing temperature and monoclinic at room temperature, the monoclinic unit cell occupying about 4.4% more volume. Stabilised with yttrium, calcium or cerium, the tetragonal form is retained at room temperature but is metastable: the stress ahead of a crack makes nearby grains transform to monoclinic, and their expansion produces a crack-closing compressive stress. This response to stress is what makes zirconia a smart material (Badami and Ahuja, 2014) `[LIT]`. Glass-based ceramics, with their disordered atomic network, have no such mechanism.

> **SOURCE CONFLICT, zirconia transformation temperature (corrected 2026-10-22).** Maloo et al (2022) and Loya et al (2023) place the monoclinic-to-tetragonal change at about 95°C `[LIT]`. Craig's textbook states that the monoclinic form is stable at all temperatures below 1170°C and the tetragonal form between 1170°C and 2370°C (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) `[SRC]`. The 95°C figure should not be reproduced; the clinically relevant smart behaviour is the stress-induced change at the crack tip, not a temperature change in the mouth. The earlier version of this answer repeated the 95°C figure.

CERCON zirconium is named as a clinically marketed metal-free, high-strength, crack-resistant example used for bridges, crowns and implants (Ved et al, 2025) `[LIT]`.

### 3.7 SmartPrep burs

SmartPrep burs are polymer burs with a hardness between that of sound and carious dentine. They allow minimally invasive, self-limiting caries excavation: the cutting edges wear on contact with harder sound tissue, so the bur mechanically "senses" when to stop (Loya et al, 2023; Ved et al, 2025) `[LIT]`.

This is an instrument-level smart adjunct rather than a restorative material, but the literature classifies it with restorative smart materials.

### 3.8 Smart bonding systems and impression materials

- **Bonding agents** enhanced with ACP, silver nanoparticles or DMADDM (dimethylaminododecyl methacrylate) combine antimicrobial action with remineralisation at the adhesive interface (Ved et al, 2025) `[LIT]`.
- **Vinyl polysiloxane (VPS) smart impression materials** are hydrophilic, which reduces void formation. They resist tearing and show shape memory during elastic recovery from undercuts, giving accurate, distortion-free impressions. Working and setting time is reduced by at least 33% (Ved et al, 2025) `[LIT]`.

### 1. Smart glass ionomer cement
- Described by Davidson as an "intelligent material" (Ved et al, 2025).
- **Thermal behaviour:** in wet conditions little dimensional change between 20°C and 50°C; in dry conditions marked contraction above 50°C. Expansion is balanced by fluid movement, as in dentine with its tubules, so GIC mimics dentine (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- Porosity, and therefore this behaviour, is partly operator-controlled: hand mixing reduces porosity in low-viscosity cement (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- The trigger is the initial water loss on heating: "loosely" bound water is lost and regained reversibly, and the water gained compensates for contraction of the matrix, so the net change in wet conditions is minimal (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- **Fluoride release and recharge:** fluoride released when pH falls below 5.5 inhibits demineralisation (Maloo et al, 2022).
- **Modifications:** CPP-ACP raises flexural strength and remineralisation (Maloo et al, 2022); chitosan improves antibacterial action against S. mutans, strength and fluoride release (Vasluianu et al, 2025).
- Fluoride-loaded chitosan nanoparticles in GIC reduced secondary caries depth by 9% in one passage and dentinal demineralisation depth by 14% in another passage of the same review (Vasluianu et al, 2025).
- Limitation: bacterial adhesion on GIC is still a concern (Maloo et al, 2022).

### 2. Smart (ACP-releasing) composites
- ACP fillers in a polymer binder (Skrtic) release calcium and phosphate ions in a sustained way, especially in acid from plaque (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- Described as an alkaline, nano-filled, light-cured material releasing calcium, fluoride and hydroxyl ions below pH 5.5; cured in bulk to 4 mm; for Class I and II lesions (Maloo et al, 2022).
- Nano-ACP release depends on filler level and pH, which makes it "a representative smart material" (Sun et al, 2025).
- ACP was first described by Posner in the mid-1960s; it is used in liners, orthodontic adhesives, endodontic sealers and pit and fissure sealants (Badami and Ahuja, 2014); with casein phosphopeptide (CPP-ACP) it is used in remineralising dentifrices (Ved et al, 2025).
- ACP combined with chlorhexidine (CHX/ACP fillers) or with DMAHDM gives remineralising composites that are also antibacterial or self-healing (Sun et al, 2025).

### 2A. Smart pit and fissure sealants
- Fluoride-releasing and ACP-releasing sealants are classed as active smart materials (Maloo et al, 2022).
- Sealants placed on early non-cavitated lesions can act as secondary prevention, not only primary prevention; GIC and resin sealants are the two main types (Ved et al, 2025).

### 3. Bioactive glass and ion-releasing restoratives
- **45S5 Bioglass** (Hench, 1969): 45% SiO₂, 24.5% Na₂O, 24.5% CaO, 6% P₂O₅; forms apatite in under 48 h in simulated body fluid, an in-vitro, not clinical, benchmark (Dascanio et al, 2026). Derivatives: S53P4 (slower, antimicrobial), 58S, NovaMin desensitising paste.
- S53P4 has more SiO₂ (53%) and less Na₂O, so it dissolves more slowly and is antimicrobial by raising pH and osmotic pressure; 58S has no Na₂O and about 36% CaO; 13-93B1 is stronger but slower (Dascanio et al, 2026).
- Bioactive glass is wholly amorphous; bioactive glass-ceramics contain at least one crystalline phase in a glassy matrix, which raises strength and toughness (Vasluianu et al, 2025).
- **Giomer (S-PRG fillers):** fluoroaluminosilicate glass pre-reacted with polyacrylic acid; releases F⁻, Sr²⁺, Na⁺, Al³⁺, borate and silicate, and can be recharged with fluoride (Beautifil II re-release up to 14 days in the laboratory) (Dascanio et al, 2026).
- **ACTIVA (Pulpdent):** marketed as a "bioactive composite"; its fluoride claim has been challenged and some view it as a reinforced RMGI (Abozaid et al, 2026).
- **Cention N (Ivoclar):** an "alkasite", a self-adhesive composite with an active calcium fluorosilicate filler (Abozaid et al, 2026).

| Feature | Cention N | ACTIVA |
|---|---|---|
| Category | Alkasite (resin-based composite) | Bioactive composite, RMGI-like |
| Setting | Light cure, optional self-cure | Dual cure |
| Ion release | High fluoride and calcium | Moderate fluoride and calcium |
| Mineralisation | Potential apatite-like phase | Limited evidence |
*(Abozaid et al, 2026)*
- Giomers recharge more fluoride than compomers and composites, but recharge of Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺ and Ag⁺ is limited and the clinical value of recharge under biofilm and brushing is unproven (Dascanio et al, 2026).

### 4. Self-healing composites, adhesives and cements
- **Extrinsic (autonomous):** microcapsules (10–300 µm) release a healing monomer when a crack ruptures them; in dentistry TEGDMA in poly(urea-formaldehyde) shells. Heals once (Khan, 2025).
- **Intrinsic (non-autonomous):** reversible Diels–Alder bonds (need about 80–120°C), disulfide exchange, supramolecular hydrogen bonds, shape-memory polymers; can heal repeatedly but need a trigger (Khan, 2025).
- **Results:** fracture-toughness recovery 65–77%; a luting cement with 7.5% capsules recovered 68–77% and kept dentine bond strength after 6 months' water ageing (Khan, 2025). Yao et al found 80.3% bacteriostasis and 66.1% healing efficiency at 10% capsules in an experimental resin (Yao et al, 2023).
- **Origin:** the first self-healing synthetic material was an epoxy with resin-filled microcapsules and a dispersed Grubbs catalyst (White et al, 2001, as cited by Badami and Ahuja, 2014); a dental model resin with encapsulated dicyclopentadiene kept fracture toughness similar to the control (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- **Other approaches:** a prototype "healing powder plus encapsulated liquid" composite reacts like a glass ionomer to reseal a crack; dynamic covalent bonds at the resin–filler interface cut shrinkage stress by 30% and raised modulus by 60% and flexural strength by 33% (Khan, 2025). Heat treatment restored up to 85% of fracture toughness in some systems (Sun et al, 2025).
- **Multifunctional adhesives:** microcapsules with DMAHDM and nano-ACP for healing, antibacterial and remineralising functions (Sun et al, 2025).

### 5. Smart antimicrobial materials
- **Quaternary ammonium monomers** (DMAHDM, DMADDM, MDPB): contact killing; MDPB also inhibits MMPs at the resin–dentine interface (Sun et al, 2025; Ved et al, 2025).
- **Silver nanoparticles:** up to 90% less biofilm in PMMA, but cytotoxic above about 0.3 wt% (Vasluianu et al, 2025).
- **Bioactive glass ceramics:** alkaline pH plus Zn²⁺/Ag⁺, more than 90% reduction in viability (Vasluianu et al, 2025).
- **Chitosan:** pH-responsive release in acidic biofilm (Vasluianu et al, 2025).
- **Antimicrobial peptides:** GH12 targets acidic microenvironments; pheromone-guided peptides target S. mutans (Sun et al, 2025; Ved et al, 2025).
- **pH-responsive chlorhexidine carriers:** release in acidic biofilm with less cytotoxicity than free chlorhexidine; Fe₃O₄ carriers allow release controlled by a magnetic field (Sun et al, 2025).
- **Biodegradation:** S. mutans can hydrolyse UDMA; silver–quaternary ammonium additives are used to counter this (Vasluianu et al, 2025).
- **Smart bonding systems:** silver nanoparticles, ACP and DMADDM added to adhesives give antimicrobial and remineralising action (Ved et al, 2025).
- **Graphene oxide:** sharp nanosheets puncture bacterial membranes and also reinforce polymers (Vasluianu et al, 2025).
- **Trade-offs by class:** natural antimicrobials are biocompatible but unstable and weak; organic agents are effective but may be toxic, volatile and induce resistance; inorganic agents are broad-spectrum and long-lasting, with little resistance, but costly to produce (Sun et al, 2025).

### 6. Smart ceramics
- Zirconia is tetragonal at firing temperature and monoclinic at room temperature; the monoclinic cell is about 4.4% larger. Stabilised (yttria) tetragonal zirconia is metastable: stress at a crack tip transforms grains to monoclinic, and the expansion closes the crack. This stress response makes zirconia a smart material (Badami and Ahuja, 2014). Monoclinic is stable below 1170°C (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Cercon is a CAD/CAM zirconia system cited as a smart ceramic (Maloo et al, 2022).
- Mica-based machinable glass-ceramics resist crack propagation because cracks branch along the irregular mica–glass interface (Maloo et al, 2022).

### 7. Other smart products
- **Smart prep burs:** polymer burs whose edges blunt on sound dentine, so they remove only infected dentine (Ved et al, 2025).
- **Smart impression materials:** hydrophilic, shape-memory, snap-set VPS and vinylsiloxanether (Maloo et al, 2022; Ved et al, 2025).
- **NiTi:** superelasticity recovers up to 8% strain against under 1% for stainless steel (Badami and Ahuja, 2014).
- **Smart impression materials, details:** vinylsiloxanether combines the hydrophilicity of polyether with the handling of polyvinylsiloxane, for moist fields and narrow sulci; fast-set versions cut working and setting time by at least 33% (Maloo et al, 2022; Ved et al, 2025).
- **Shape-memory polymers:** thermo-responsive shape-memory polymers are useful only if their transition can be exploited at oral temperature; restorative and prosthodontic uses (such as provisional fixed prostheses) exist mainly as patents (Bruni et al, 2019).

## 4. Critical evidence synthesis

Three claims deserve full appraisal. For each, the evidence, the interpretation and the limitation are kept separate.

### Claim 1: "Self-healing composite resins are now a clinically viable solution to secondary caries and bulk fracture."

**Evidence.** Laboratory data are genuine and reproducible. Yao et al (2023) achieved 80.3% bacteriostasis and 66.1% self-healing efficiency at 10% microcapsule loading in an unfilled experimental resin, with no statistically significant loss of flexural strength (56.0 to 49.1 MPa) or cytotoxicity. Microcapsule-based systems broadly recover 65 to 77% of fracture toughness (Khan, 2025) `[LIT]`. The evidence is laboratory and in vitro, and includes original experimental synthesis as well as review-level citation.

**Interpretation.** Review-level framing often implies an imminent clinical solution. The evidence does not support that.

**Limitation.** Most dental self-healing chemistry is adapted from non-dental polymer science. No standardised dental testing protocol or long-term biocompatibility and regulatory pathway yet exists. The source states that "full clinical translation has not yet been realised" (Khan, 2025) `[LIT]`.

> **SOURCE CONFLICT, is a self-healing composite already in clinical use?** *Sun et al (2025)*: 3M's Filtek Supreme Ultra, "with its superior antimicrobial and self-healing capabilities, is already widely used in clinical practice" `[LIT]`. *Khan (2025)*: "full clinical translation has not yet been realised" `[LIT]`. No other paper in the supplied set describes a commercially available self-healing composite, and Sun gives no study for the claim. Treat Sun's statement as unsupported until a source is found `[Likely]`.

**Clinical implication.** The science is well grounded, and Yao's data suggest antimicrobial and self-repair functions can be combined without an obvious trade-off. The material remains a research-stage option, not a clinically available restoration `[Certain]` on current availability. The translation timeline is `[Guessing]`.

### Claim 2: "Giomer (S-PRG) and glass-ionomer cement are clinically interchangeable as smart caries-control restoratives."

**Evidence.** A 2026 meta-analysis of 40 RCTs (5,506 restorations, at least 12 months' follow-up) found that bioactive and smart restorative materials reduced secondary caries risk by 45% overall against conventional controls (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.46 to 0.65, p<0.001). The effect is not uniform:
- GIC gave the strongest effect (RR 0.36, p=0.002); and
- giomer showed no significant benefit (RR 1.09, p=0.87) (Zailai et al, 2026) `[LIT]`.

**Interpretation.** The evidence strongly supports GIC's long-recognised status as a clinically load-bearing intelligent material (Davidson, 1998). It does not support the claim that all bioactive or smart ion-releasing materials share this advantage. Giomer's real, mechanistically genuine ion-release-and-recharge capability does not currently translate into a demonstrated secondary-caries benefit over conventional restoratives.

**Clinical implication.** "Smart" by passive-versus-active classification (Maloo et al, 2022) is a mechanistic claim. It is not an automatic claim of clinical superiority. The two must be evaluated separately. The material-by-material account in Section 3 should not be read as implying uniform clinical superiority `[Certain]`.

### Claim 3: "Antimicrobial nanoparticle incorporation (silver, chitosan, quaternary ammonium) is straightforwardly beneficial."

**Evidence.** BGC materials achieve more than 90% biofilm reduction with a bone-integration benefit. AgNP-doped polymers are effective against *S. mutans* and *C. albicans* (Vasluianu et al, 2025) `[LIT]`.

**Limitation.** The same review says efficacy is conditional. AgNP polymers "require controlled dosing (<0.3 wt% in PMMA) to avoid cytotoxicity." Antimicrobial action and biocompatibility are in direct tension, not simply additive (Vasluianu et al, 2025) `[LIT]`. Standardised antibiofilm testing is still an open methodological problem. The proposed 3-log benchmark is a suggestion, not an adopted standard `[LIT]`.

**Position.** There is no genuine disagreement that nanoparticle antimicrobial action works mechanistically. The live tension is the dose-response window between efficacy and cytotoxicity, and whether a given formulation has been independently validated against a standardised biofilm benchmark.

**Clinical implication.** Nanoparticle-doped antimicrobial materials should be selected and dosed against published safety thresholds, not assumed safe at whatever concentration maximises antibacterial effect. The trade-off is established `[Certain]`. The 0.3 wt% PMMA figure is reported for one matrix, so generalising it to other resin systems is `[Likely]` but not proven.


#### ✎ Exam core

### Critical evaluation
**1. Definitions are loose.** Many products are called bioactive only because they contain calcium, phosphate or fluoride particles; the label is often used as a marketing tool (Dascanio et al, 2026). Some researchers argue that no current restorative material fully meets the criteria for bioactivity (Abozaid et al, 2026).

**2. Therapeutic effect versus strength.** More ion release brings more solubility, lower strength and poorer marginal integrity; ACP and hydroxyapatite systems absorb more water and lose hardness and colour stability (Dascanio et al, 2026). ACTIVA and Cention N lost fracture toughness after 30 days' water storage and wore more than conventional composites (Abozaid et al, 2026).
- **Matching particle to product** (Dascanio et al, 2026): in adhesives, low-solubility particles (nano-hydroxyapatite, ACP, silica, less soluble glasses) that do not impair monomer infiltration or conversion; in composites, silanised bioactive glass, S-PRG and stabilised calcium phosphates that keep strength, wear resistance and polish; highly reactive 45S5-like glasses suit topical agents and cements rather than load-bearing resins.

**3. Self-healing is laboratory evidence.**
- Yao's result came from an unfilled BisGMA/TEGDMA resin tested against one organism; flexural strength fell from 56.0 to 49.1 MPa at 10% capsules (not significant) and fell significantly at 15–20% (Yao et al, 2023).
- Healing efficiency above 80% in the laboratory may fall below 50% in the mouth; repeated healing degrades; heat triggers of 60–90°C would harm the pulp; dicyclopentadiene, Grubbs catalyst and urea-formaldehyde shells are cytotoxic (Khan, 2025).
- **Source conflict:** Sun et al state that Filtek Supreme Ultra, with antimicrobial and self-healing capabilities, "is already widely used" (Sun et al, 2025); Khan states that full clinical translation has not yet been realised (Khan, 2025). No other source read supports a clinically available self-healing composite.

**4. Clinical outcomes differ by material.**
- Bioactive and smart restoratives reduced secondary caries by 45% overall in a meta-analysis of 40 trials, but the effect was strongest for GIC (RR 0.36) and not significant for giomer (RR 1.09) (Zailai et al, 2026).
- Ion-releasing restorations and composite showed no significant clinical difference in one comparison (Abozaid et al, 2026).
- In Class II cavities bulk-fill and conventional composites outperformed reinforced GIC (Sun et al, 2025).

**5. Antimicrobial safety window.** AgNP above 0.3 wt% in PMMA, or 0.1–0.5 ppm in solution, can cause fibroblast apoptosis; Cu²⁺ at 10 wt% or more inhibits fibroblasts; C. albicans can resist silver (Vasluianu et al, 2025).

**6. No standard tests or regulation.** A ≥3-log CFU reduction and 12-month clinical follow-up have been proposed as benchmarks (Vasluianu et al, 2025). Smart materials may fall under FDA 510(k), De Novo or PMA routes and need ISO 10993 biocompatibility testing (Sun et al, 2025). Shape-memory polymer evidence was rated low quality (Bruni et al, 2019).
- Regulators place materials that combine structural and therapeutic roles in an unclear position between medical devices and drugs (Dascanio et al, 2026).
- There is a "stark paucity of long-term human clinical data" for many promising materials (Vasluianu et al, 2025); clinical safety needs a three-tier evidence chain from in vitro to animal to clinical follow-up (Sun et al, 2025).
- Microcapsule manufacture at industrial scale is costly and technically demanding (Khan, 2025).

**7. Factual traps.****8. Applying the FDI criteria.** Before calling a product smart or bioactive, ask: is the mechanism clear, is the effect documented, how long does it last, are there side effects, and is the restorative function preserved (Abozaid et al, 2026)?

 One review places zirconia's monoclinic–tetragonal change at about 95°C (Maloo et al, 2022); Craig's textbook gives 1170°C (Sakaguchi et al, 2019), and the smart behaviour is the stress-induced change at the crack tip (Badami and Ahuja, 2014). Sources give 5.5 or 5.8 as the critical pH for ACP release (Maloo et al, 2022; Badami and Ahuja, 2014).

## 5. Conclusion

Smart materials represent conservative dentistry's move from passive tissue replacement toward restoratives that sense and respond to the oral environment. They buffer pH, release therapeutic ions, repair microcracks and resist biofilm.

The evidence is uneven across this category:
- glass-ionomer ion release and zirconia's stress-induced transformation toughening are mature, clinically embedded technologies;
- antimicrobial nanoparticle systems work, but need careful, published dosing; and
- self-healing composite, despite compelling laboratory data including original synthesis work, remains preclinical.

"Smart materials" should therefore not be presented as one uniformly ready category. The maturity of each material should be stated.


#### ✎ Exam core

### Future directions
- Multi-stimulus systems (pH, enzyme, redox), combining immediate capsule healing, intrinsic re-healing and ion-releasing fillers (Khan, 2025; Vasluianu et al, 2025).
- "Living" materials with dormant Bacillus subtilis spores that mineralise cracks (Khan, 2025).
- 3D/4D printing, artificial intelligence and closed-loop material discovery (Sun et al, 2025).
- Nanotechnology combined with sensors may allow real-time monitoring of oral conditions and controlled release of agents (Sun et al, 2025).
- Standardised fatigue, thermocycling and biofilm test models, then randomised clinical trials (Khan, 2025; Dascanio et al, 2026).
- **Sustainability:** an estimated 800 million composite restorations were placed in 2015, with about 40 million replacements a year; doubling restoration lifespan could nearly halve the environmental impact, which makes durable, self-repairing materials part of "green dentistry" (Khan, 2025).

### Conclusion
Smart materials mark the move from passive replacement to restoratives that sense and respond: they buffer pH, release ions, repair microcracks and resist biofilm. Their maturity differs. Glass ionomer and zirconia behaviour is clinically established; ACP, giomer and alkasite materials have mixed clinical evidence; self-healing and most nanoparticle systems remain largely preclinical. The term "smart" should therefore be used with the evidence for each material, not as a blanket claim.
In practice, the strongest clinical evidence for caries control among these materials currently belongs to glass ionomer (Zailai et al, 2026), while the newer resin-based smart systems should be chosen on their own published evidence.

## 6. High-yield summary

- Smart material: senses a stimulus (stress, temperature, moisture, pH, electric or magnetic field) and responds in a controlled, reversible way (McCabe, 2011; Badami & Ahuja, 2014).
- Classification axes: by interaction (bioinert, bioactive, bioresponsive); passive versus active (Maloo 2022); by property and by stimulus origin (Bruni 2019); by degree of intelligence, inert/active/reactive/autonomous (Sun 2025); by material source (Sun 2025); by degree of bioactivity, high/moderate/low (Abozaid 2026).
- Smart composite exemplar: Ariston pH Control, ACP release triggered at pH 5.5 or below.
- Self-healing resin (Yao 2023): 10% microcapsules gave 80.3% bacteriostasis and 66.1% self-healing in an unfilled experimental resin; reviews report 65–77% toughness recovery in the laboratory (Khan 2025).
- Antimicrobial platforms: BGC gives more than 90% biofilm reduction. AgNP is effective but capped below 0.3 wt% in PMMA because of cytotoxicity. pH-responsive chitosan coatings are a further option.
- Davidson (1998): GIC is the original intelligent restorative material.
- Zirconia is smart because stress at a crack tip transforms metastable tetragonal grains to monoclinic (about 4.4% expansion), closing the crack; do not quote 95°C (Craig's: monoclinic below 1170°C).
- 2026 meta-analysis (40 RCTs): 45% overall secondary-caries reduction, but GIC (RR 0.36) against giomer (RR 1.09, not significant). Smart does not automatically mean clinically superior.

## 7. Common errors to avoid

- Quoting 95°C as zirconia's transformation temperature; the smart behaviour is stress-induced transformation toughening (Craig's: monoclinic below 1170°C).
- Calling every ion-releasing product "bioactive" or "smart"; ion release alone does not meet the definition (Abozaid et al, 2026; Dascanio et al, 2026).

1. Defining smart materials only through GIC's fluoride release. This collapses the stimulus-response concept into one example.
2. Presenting self-healing composite as a marketed restorative rather than a research-stage direction.
3. Treating "smart" or "bioactive" as one undifferentiated category of caries-control success. The GIC-versus-giomer split must be stated.
4. Citing antimicrobial nanoparticle efficacy without the dose-dependent cytotoxicity ceiling the same literature reports.
5. Omitting SmartPrep burs or smart bonding and impression materials. The topic spans the whole conservative armamentarium, not only resins.
6. Failing to state the classification frameworks. A complete answer covers stimulus, passive versus active, and material category.

## 8. Mnemonic

**PACS-GB**: **P**H-triggered composites; **A**ntimicrobial nanoparticles; **C**eramics (zirconia phase change); **S**elf-healing resins; **G**IC, the original smart material; **B**onding and impression systems. The **G** carries the strongest clinical evidence.
