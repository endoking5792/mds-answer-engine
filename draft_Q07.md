## STUDY MODE

### S1. How to organise the uses [orientation]
Use follows type: Type I luts, Type II.1 restores aesthetically, Type II.2 restores where strength matters more than colour, Type III lines and bases; fissure sealing, orthodontic cementation and core build-up are extra uses in Sikri's wider listing [SRC:A187] [SRC:A188] [SRC:A189] [SRC:A190] [SRC:G10] [SRC:G11].

### S2. Uses by type [clinical decision-making]
- **Type I luting.** Crowns, bridges, inlays, orthodontic appliances. Low powder:liquid ratio (about 1.5:1), fast set, thin film, and low solubility make it suitable; its adhesion improves marginal seal but does not add retention, so retention must come from preparation design [SRC:A187] [SRC:A309]. On vital teeth avoid conditioning with 10% polyacrylic acid because of hydraulic pressure; use a mineralising solution or tannic acid [SRC:A311].
- **Type II.1 restorative aesthetic.** Class III, Class V, root caries, erosion lesions, and primary teeth; properties are adequate if the cement is supported and not overloaded [SRC:A312] [SRC:G49] [SRC:G51].
- **Type II.2 reinforced restorative.** Fast set and higher strength where colour match is unimportant: posterior sites, tunnel restorations, transitional restorations, repairs of margins, and limited cores [SRC:A313] [SRC:A317] [SRC:A372].
- **Type III lining and base.** Lining (1.5:1) is a thin layer for a deficiency or thermal protection; a base (3:1 or more) replaces dentine; GIC gives the most effective long-term dentine seal [SRC:A190] [SRC:A320]. Under amalgam, minimal cavities need no lining; for deeper ones a small amount of low powder:liquid lining is used [SRC:A321].
- **Other products by Sikri's classification.** Type IV fissure sealant, Type V orthodontic cement, Type VI core build-up [SRC:G10].

### S3. What a bi-layered restoration is [classification]
A bi-layered (sandwich, laminate) restoration uses GIC as the dentine replacement and composite as the enamel replacement, so each material is used where its properties suit [SRC:B50] [SRC:A392]. It is **open** if the GIC forms part of the axial wall and is exposed to the mouth, and **closed** if composite covers the GIC completely [SRC:B316].

### S4. Why do it [rationale]
Composite shrinks 1-5% by volume and the dentine bond is hard to keep over time; GIC bonds to dentine by ion exchange and bonds to composite, giving a near-monolithic reconstruction [SRC:A386] [SRC:A387]. Practical gains: less composite, fewer increments, less total shrinkage, time saved [SRC:A394]. Lamination is preferred in any cavity with at least one margin on dentine [SRC:A393]. A GIC base in larger restorations gives a much better chance of keeping oral microflora from reaching dentine and so reduces pulpal irritation [SRC:A400].

### S5. Technique [treatment]
Mount's principles: use the strongest GIC in quantity as the dentine substitute, with no sub-base of calcium hydroxide; condition for 10 seconds; let it set fully; cut back about 2 mm to expose all enamel margins; leave GIC exposed at a subgingival margin without enamel; build the contact in composite; always use a radiopaque GIC [SRC:A395] [SRC:A415]. With auto-cure GIC, etch cement and enamel together for 15 seconds, apply thin low-viscosity enamel resin, then incremental composite [SRC:A396]; with resin-modified GIC no etching of cement is needed [SRC:A397]. The cement must cover all tubules, be at least 1 mm thick, and use the highest powder:liquid ratio [SRC:A385].

### S6. Limits and cautions
Early sandwich studies showed more bulk fractures, though those used Black-style cavities and early bases [SRC:A401]. Pickard says closed sandwich restorations are more prone to long-term failure at interfaces [SRC:C2121]. Etching GIC too long or too early damages it: early etching harms the immature matrix, and composite shrinkage can pull glass particles from etched cement [SRC:B315] [SRC:B316]. Lining-grade cement is not designed to be exposed [SRC:B51]. Restorative GIC is radiolucent, so a radiopaque composite over cut-back GIC can mimic recurrent decay [SRC:B51].

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement is classified by Wilson and McLean into Type I (luting), Type II (restorative: II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced) and Type III (lining and base) [SRC:A187] [SRC:A188] [SRC:A189] [SRC:A190]. Each type has uses that follow from its powder:liquid ratio and strength.

### 2. Uses of each type
| Type | Powder:liquid | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| I luting | about 1.5:1 | crowns, bridges, inlays, onlays, posts, orthodontic bands and brackets [SRC:A187] [SRC:G11] |
| II.1 aesthetic restorative | 2.8:1 to 6.8:1 | Class III and V, erosion, abrasion and abfraction lesions, root caries, primary teeth [SRC:A188] [SRC:G49] |
| II.2 reinforced restorative | 3:1 to 4:1 | posterior sites, tunnel restorations, transitional restorations, limited cores, margin repairs [SRC:A189] [SRC:A317] |
| III lining and base | lining 1.5:1; base 3:1 or more | thermal protection, dentine substitute, dentine seal, undercut block-out [SRC:A190] [SRC:A320] [SRC:C185] |
| IV (Sikri) | high | fissure sealant [SRC:G10] |
| V (Sikri) | low | orthodontic cement [SRC:G10] |
| VI (Sikri) | high | core build-up [SRC:G10] |

### 3. Advantages that justify these uses
Chemical adhesion, fluoride release, thermal expansion close to dentine, biocompatibility, and fast set in Type I and II.2 [SRC:G14] [SRC:G17].

### 4. Special uses
- Transitional restoration and ART, with maintenance care [SRC:C1135] [SRC:C2123].
- Fissure sealing: GIC sealants bond to enamel by ion exchange; a weak low-powder mix is contraindicated because of wear, and even when the sealant is lost, fluoride uptake into enamel reduces caries activity [SRC:A327] [SRC:A328].
- Cervical and root lesions [SRC:C1118].
- Repair of perforation and resorption; endodontic sub-seal [SRC:G11] [SRC:C2163].

### 4A. Choosing the type by site (Mount and Hume)
- **Minor occlusal lesions (Site 1, Size 1):** GIC is preferred, and remaining fissures are sealed with GIC or composite [SRC:A368].
- **Occlusal lesions needing more strength:** GIC is the best initial restoration; laminate with composite or amalgam if the occlusal load is heavy; use the strongest, preferably auto-cure, high powder:liquid cement [SRC:A336].
- **Proximal tunnel:** Type II.2 high-strength auto-cure radiopaque cement, preferably capsulated, placed in two increments under a lightly wedged Mylar strip [SRC:A372].
- **Cervical lesions (Site 3, Size 2):** resin-modified or auto-cure GIC depending on light access, always conditioned with 10% polyacrylic acid [SRC:A378].
- **Large cavities:** a high-strength GIC base on the axial wall gives a thermal barrier and bioactivity [SRC:A376].

### 5. Bi-layered (sandwich) restoration: definition
GIC as dentine replacement plus composite as enamel replacement, also called laminate restoration [SRC:B50] [SRC:A392].

### 6. Types of sandwich
- **Open:** GIC forms part of the axial or gingival wall and is exposed in the mouth, used in Class II and Class V extending below the CEJ [SRC:B316] [SRC:C164].
- **Closed:** GIC completely covered by composite [SRC:B316].

### 7. Indications
- Cavities with a margin on dentine or cementum, especially subgingival Class II [SRC:A393] [SRC:G54].
- Erosive and cervical lesions, bonding composite to enamel margins only [SRC:G54].
- Deep Class II with amalgam: GIC in the gingival half, amalgam above [SRC:A391].
- Where composite-dentine bonding is in doubt [SRC:A393].

### 8. Rationale
Composite shrinks 1-5% and loses dentine bond over time; GIC bonds to dentine and to composite; the combination uses less composite and fewer increments [SRC:A386] [SRC:A394].

### 9. Technique
1. Isolate; remove caries; condition dentine 10 seconds [SRC:A395].
2. Place the strongest radiopaque GIC under a matrix, covering all tubules, at least 1 mm thick [SRC:A385].
3. Let it set; cut back about 2 mm to expose all enamel margins [SRC:A415].
4. Etch enamel and auto-cure GIC for 15 seconds only; wash; dry lightly [SRC:A396].
5. Apply thin enamel bonding resin and light cure; for resin-modified GIC etch enamel only [SRC:A397].
6. Place composite in increments; build the contact in composite [SRC:A395].
7. Finish and polish; leave GIC exposed only at a subgingival margin without enamel [SRC:A395].

### 10. Advantages and limits
- **Advantages:** maximum GIC exposure for fluoride release, less composite, interproximal GIC tolerated by tissue and resists plaque [SRC:A384].
- **Limits:** closed type more prone to interface failure [SRC:C2121]; early studies reported more bulk fractures [SRC:A401]; lining-grade GIC is too weak to support composite and must not be exposed [SRC:G52] [SRC:B51].
- **Evidence:** retention improved with the sandwich technique in cervical lesions in a meta-analysis of 4 studies at unclear risk of bias (Q1, claim 3) [LIT:dePaula2019].

### 10A. Sandwich restoration in other settings
- **Amalgam lamination:** the strongest GIC is used as a base, cut back generously to give mechanical interlocks; amalgam protects the cement from occlusal load [SRC:A398] [SRC:A391].
- **Cermet or amalgam lamination in Class II:** the cement is tamped in, left to set for 6 minutes, then cut back below the contact area [SRC:A390].
- **Anterior and trauma cases:** a strong GIC replaces dentine, and enamel margins are bevelled for composite [SRC:A413].
- **Aesthetics:** wait up to a week for GIC to mature and reach its final colour before deciding on a composite laminate for appearance [SRC:A414].

### 11. Conclusion
Each GIC type has a defined clinical niche set by strength and ratio; the sandwich restoration is the way to combine GIC's bond and fluoride with composite's wear resistance, and works best as a planned technique in subgingival or dentine-margin cavities.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Types: I luting, II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced, III lining and base; P/L from 1.5:1 to over 3:1 [SRC:A187] [SRC:A190].
- Sandwich = GIC as dentine, composite as enamel; open vs closed [SRC:B316].
- Etch GIC and enamel 15 s only (auto-cure); RMGIC needs no etching [SRC:A396] [SRC:A397].
- Cover all tubules, at least 1 mm [SRC:A385].
- Closed sandwich: more interface failure [SRC:C2121].

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing uses without linking them to the type.
2. Acid-etching GIC for too long or etching a freshly set cement.
3. Writing that lining-grade cement can support composite.
4. Forgetting that restorative GIC is radiolucent.
