T1|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.406 | ANCHOR: "developed by Wilson and Kent"] GIC developed by Wilson and Kent (1972); popularised early as ASPA (aluminosilicate polyacrylate); hybrid of silicate and polycarboxylate cement made to overcome silicate's deficiencies; ISO name polyalkenoate cement.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.406 | ANCHOR: "man made"] Called 'man made dentin' and 'dentin substitute' because of use as a dentine replacement; greatest advantages are adhesion to enamel and dentine and fluoride release for an anticariogenic effect; most useful in erosion lesions without cavity preparation and pit and fissure sealing.
T2|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.406 | ANCHOR: "tartaric acid, maleic acid, itaconic acid,"] Tartaric, maleic and itaconic acid decrease the viscosity of the polyacrylic acid liquid; variants exist for restoration, luting, base-liner, core build-up, geriatric and paediatric restorations, ART and orthodontic band cementation.
T2|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.406 | ANCHOR: "about 1,100 to 1,500°C"] Powder (silica, alumina, fluorides of calcium, sodium and aluminium; fluoride is a flux) is fused at about 1,100-1,500 C, shock cooled and ground; restorative particle size about 50 micrometre, luting about 20 micrometre.
T2|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.406 | ANCHOR: "calcium-fluoro-alumino-silicate glass"] Traditional powder composition (% weight): SiO2 35-50, Al2O3 20-30, AlF3 1.5-2.5, CaF2 15-20, NaF 3-6, AlPO4 4-12, traces of lanthanum, strontium, barium for radiopacity; called 'ion leachable glass'.
T8|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.407 | ANCHOR: "Silver-tin alloy (Miracle Mix)."] Powder modifications: dried polyacrylic acid (anhydrous GIC), silver-tin alloy (Miracle Mix), silver-palladium/titanium (cermet), BisGMA/TEGDMA/HEMA (light or dual cure GIC); liquid modifications: water and tartaric acid only (anhydrous), HEMA.
T2|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.407 | ANCHOR: "The liquid is 45% solution of copolymer of"] Liquid is 45% copolymer of polyacrylic acid, 5% modifiers (itaconic, maleic, tricarballylic, tartaric acid) and 50% water; itaconic acid lowers viscosity and inhibits gelation by hydrogen bonding (longer shelf life); tartaric acid improves working time, controls pH and glass dissolution and gives a snap set.
T7|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.407 | ANCHOR: "A. According to Wilson and McLean"] Classifications given: (A) Wilson and McLean: Type I luting, Type II restorative (aesthetic, reinforced); (B) McLean: traditional GIC, resin-modified GIC, polyacid-modified composite; (C) by application: I luting, II restorative, III lining, IV fissure sealant, V orthodontic cement, VI core build-up; (D) by manufacturer: I luting, II restorative, III base and liner (weak low-acid; stronger more acidic; strong light-cure like Vitrebond), IV admixtures (Ketac Silver, Miracle Mix); (E) newer: traditional, metal-modified, light-cure, hybrid/RMGIC.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.408 | ANCHOR: "1. Restoration of permanent teeth"] Uses listed: permanent teeth Class V and III, abrasion/erosion, root caries; deciduous Class I-VI, rampant and nursing bottle caries; luting of metal and non-metal restorations, veneers, pins and posts, orthodontic bands; preventive restorations (tunnel, sealants); liner under composite and amalgam; bonding agent; dentine substitute; core build-up; splinting of periodontally weak teeth; glazing; sandwich, ART, bonded restorations; endodontic repair of resorption and perforation, retrograde filling.
T3|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.408 | ANCHOR: "slowly degrades the outer layer of glass particles"] Acid slowly degrades the glass outer layer; calcium ions released early form calcium polysalts (initial set); water is the transfer medium; early contact with moisture lets aluminium leach out (weak, soluble cement) and drying stops the reaction (weak, crumbly, dull white).
T3|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.408 | ANCHOR: "'secondary reaction stage’."] Aluminium ions released more slowly give a secondary reaction stage, replace calcium and form tight cross-links; set cement is unreacted glass cores in a cross-linked polyacid matrix; looks hard in 5 minutes but needs at least 24 hours, full reaction up to 7 days, after which solubility falls.
T14|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.408 | ANCHOR: "Advantages (conventional glass-ionomer cement)"] Conventional GIC advantages: chemical adhesion, fluoride release, biocompatible, low oral solubility after complete set, easy manipulation; disadvantages: white opaque surface, poor aesthetics, surface cracks, debonds readily, technique sensitive, high initial solubility (24 h), poor strength, unsuccessful in larger lesions.
T3|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.409 | ANCHOR: "also described in three"] Setting reaction also described as three overlapping stages: dissolution, gelation and hardening; silica forms a covalent silica network around glass particles; fluoride shows an initial high release then a constant level and is re-taken up when environmental fluoride rises.
T8|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.409 | ANCHOR: "2. Light-Activated Polymerization"] Light-cure GIC sets in two stages: light polymerisation of methacrylate groups (initiators in powder, HEMA in liquid) then acid-base reaction ('dual cure'); tri-cure RMGIC adds a chemical redox 'dark cure' useful where light penetration is poor, so no free resin remains.
T6|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.409 | ANCHOR: "The working time of traditional glass-ionomer cement"] Working time of traditional GIC 2-3 minutes; high solubility in first 24 h; thermal expansion close to dentine; inhibits bacterial growth through fluoride; initial pH about 2 rising to 6-7.
T16|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.409 | ANCHOR: "The biocompatibility of the cement is due to the large"] Mild pulp irritant over dentine; biocompatibility attributed to large molecular size that does not travel through narrow dentinal tubules; not to be placed on direct pulp exposure; if remaining dentine under 0.5 mm protect with calcium hydroxide.
T6|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.409 | ANCHOR: "1. Sensitivity to Moisture"] Moisture sensitivity: water uptake weakens the cement and over-drying cracks it, so protection during and after placement is mandatory; postoperative sensitivity linked to poor manipulation, wrong powder/liquid ratio and moisture contamination (hydraulic effect), less with light-cure GIC and compomer.
T6|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.410 | ANCHOR: "Traditional GIC"] Table 20.1 (traditional GIC / glass cermet / light-cure GIC / compomer): compressive strength 16,000-22,000 / 27,500 / 23,200 / 29,000 psi; tensile 900-960 / 950-1,000 / 2,000-3,000 / 2,000-3,000 psi; solubility 0.3-0.5 / 0.1 / 0.08 / 0.08 %; hardness KHN 48 / 39 / 40 / 38; thermal expansion 10-11 / 15 / 18 / 18 x10-6 per C; opacity 90 / 95 / 85 / 80 %.
T4|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.410 | ANCHOR: "diffusion-based adhesion"] Adhesion mechanism: polyacrylic acid penetrates tooth releasing phosphate ions, which take up calcium from the tooth and combine with the cement surface layer to form an intermediate layer, described as diffusion-based adhesion; adhesion to dentine also by hydrogen bonding or metallic-ion bridging between carboxyl groups and collagen.
T4|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.410 | ANCHOR: "Micromechanical (polyacid components of glass-"] Adhesion has two inter-related parts: micromechanical (the polyacid makes GIC self-etching) and chemical (ionic bonds between carboxyl groups and tooth calcium); an ion-exchange layer forms as the cement matures; failure is cohesive within the cement; adhesion aids retention and reduces marginal leakage.
T5|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.410 | ANCHOR: "continue for years after"] Fluoride release continues for years: initial rise for about a week then a gradual fall over months; cement takes up fluoride from the environment and acts as a reservoir (recharge); zinc sulfate added to GIC inhibited S. mutans and raised fluoride release.
T5|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.410 | ANCHOR: "acid resistant calcium fluorapatite"] Fluoride converts hydroxyapatite to acid-resistant fluorapatite and reduces the metabolic activity of cariogenic flora; a continuous low fluoride supply can inhibit dentine demineralisation and may reduce hypersensitivity.
T18|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.410 | ANCHOR: "A few authors have questioned the validity of"] A few authors question the anticariogenic value of fluoride release (a survey found GIC restorations repeatedly replaced for secondary caries); release into salivary components not authenticated.
T5|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.411 | ANCHOR: "Soon after placement, there is sudden"] Greater fluoride release means a more open, weaker structure; denser strong materials release less; after placement there is a burst then rapid decline; a two-part 'fluoride recharge' GIC (restorative part plus gel 'charge part') restores release by ion exchange without replacing the material; release affected by P/L ratio, mixing, temperature, solubility, finishing and storage medium.
T15|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.411 | ANCHOR: "Recently, hydroxy apatite nanoparticles (8.0% by"] Hydroxyapatite nanoparticles (8% by weight) added to enhance fluoride release, mechanical properties and inhibit residual bacteria at margins; cellulose nanocrystals also tried.
T15|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.411 | ANCHOR: "5. Bioactvity"] GIC is a natural bioactive material releasing fluoride, sodium and phosphate ions (more in acid) and taking up calcium and phosphate from saliva to harden its surface as it matures.
T6|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.411 | ANCHOR: "6. Esthetics"] Aesthetics: pigments (metal oxides) give shades but GIC lacks translucency, is opaque and dulls with time; compomers are aesthetically better than light-cure and traditional GIC.
T8|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.411 | ANCHOR: "Silver cermet was prepared by incorporation of silver-"] Miracle Mix (silver cermet): silver-tin alloy mixed into the powder improves toughness and abrasion resistance but other strengths unchanged, grey-black colour and metal-carboxylate interface failure; Glass cermet: glass and metal sintered then reacted with polyacrylic acid, better wear resistance and flexural strength with aesthetics kept (about 5% titanium oxide whitener), used in small Class I of deciduous teeth, core, Class V, erosion/abrasion and rampant caries, and can be polished immediately.
T14|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.412 | ANCHOR: "Advantages of metal-modified glass-ionomers"] Metal-modified GIC: stronger, nearly insoluble, fluoride release, radiopaque, prevents secondary caries as a liner at the cervical wall of Class II, biocompatible; disadvantages: unaesthetic black colour, long-term metal-carboxylate interface failure, small lesions only.
T8|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.412 | ANCHOR: "A monomer, hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) is"] RMGIC: HEMA grafted onto polyacrylic acid, light polymerises methacrylate groups then acid-base reaction occurs; still water-based so acid-base is the main reaction; bonds to tooth through carboxyl groups; Fuji II LC example.
T8|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.412 | ANCHOR: "Advantages of resin modified (light cure) glass-ionomer"] RMGIC advantages: stronger, nearly insoluble, fluoride release, radiopaque, easy manipulation, satisfactory wear, better aesthetics, early water resistance, chemical and micromechanical bonding, excellent bond strength, minimal postoperative sensitivity, condensable, long working time with snap set, immediate finishing, repairable, better adhesion to composite as a base.
T8|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.412 | ANCHOR: "Compomer is the combination of composites ('comp') and"] Compomer: dimethacrylate monomer plus carboxylic groups plus ion-leachable glass, no water, glass partly silanated; sets by free-radical polymerisation and does not bond to tooth; properties inferior to hybrid composites; used in Class V, Class II and bilayered technique (compomer base under composite).
T8|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.413 | ANCHOR: "Giomers are available as one paste form."] Giomers: one-paste, light-cured, need bonding agents; Reactmer and Beautifil; contain pre-reacted glass-ionomer filler so acid-base reaction has already occurred; fluoride release and recharge, smooth finish, harder than compomers and RMGIC; disadvantages: little benefit in high recurrent-caries risk, rapid extensive expansion so avoid under thin unsupported enamel.
T15|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.413 | ANCHOR: "hollow glass fiber filler partials: 5.0–10% by wt"] Hollow glass fibre filler (5-10% by weight) in the powder improved mechanical properties of conventional GIC.
T15|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.414 | ANCHOR: "i. Chlorhexidine: Antimicrobial activity of chlor-"] Antibacterial GIC: chlorhexidine (raises fluoride release but high concentration lowers bonding and prolongs setting; CHX hexametaphosphate gives long-term release without harming mechanical properties), 2.5% triclosan (better than 2% chlorhexidine), quaternary ammonium salts (shorter chains keep strength; effect falls in saliva), EGCG (effective only 3-4 h), silver/zinc/titanium dioxide nanoparticles (silver darkens), bioactive glass (short-term effect, weakens), antibiotics, miswak, propolis (cytotoxic).
T15|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.415 | ANCHOR: "Glass carbomer is a modified glass-ionomer cement,"] Glass carbomer: glass acid-washed to deplete surface calcium, silicone oil (polymethylsiloxane) coating, hydroxyapatite secondary filler, can be mixed at high P/L; improved properties but long-term studies could not confirm superior efficacy.
T15|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.415 | ANCHOR: "7. Zirconomer (Bioactive Nanoceramics)"] Zirconomer: zirconium oxide nanoparticles in GIC powder for strength, aesthetics and colour stability; more than 12% (vol) hydroxyapatite/zirconia worsens properties; drawbacks poor working consistency, longer setting time, rough surface, cracks at weak zirconium-glass interface; nanoparticles give limited long-term advantage.
T15|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.415 | ANCHOR: "8. Low pH Smart Materials"] Low-pH 'smart' materials release fluoride episodically at low oral pH when caries attack threatens, prolonging usefulness of the material.
T9|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.416 | ANCHOR: "I. Isolation"] Placement steps: isolation, tooth preparation (mechanical, then conditioning), manipulation, restoration, finishing and polishing, surface protection; GIC is very sensitive to water loss and contamination, so rubber dam, retraction cord, cotton rolls and saliva ejector are mandatory near the gingival margin.
T11|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.416 | ANCHOR: "In Class III cavities, the access is gained through the"] Class III: access through the lingual marginal ridge with a small inverted cone bur, labial enamel preserved; Class V: outline dictated by caries, discolouration and aesthetics only, retention grooves on occlusal/gingival (or all four) walls if lesion wide; proximal lesions: do not break the contact, keep unsupported enamel, gingival extent dictated by lesion and sulcular fluid controlled.
T9|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.416 | ANCHOR: "50% citric acid,  25% tannic acid and 20% polyacrylic acid"] Surface conditioning after prophylaxis: 50% citric acid, 25% tannic acid or 20% polyacrylic acid, polyacrylic acid usually 10-20 seconds then washed; light-cure GIC has an added priming step (two or more coats, thin layer, light 20 s).
T9|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.416 | ANCHOR: "thinned down by immersing the bottle in"] Dispensing: fluff the powder, level the spoon, powder onto slab in two halves, liquid dispensed without air bubbles; viscous liquid can be thinned in water at 70 C for 15 minutes.
T9|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.417 | ANCHOR: "merely wet the surface of the powder particles with"] Mixing: paper pad or cooled glass slab, plastic spatula preferred, aim only to wet the powder particles, first half all wet in 10-15 s, mix complete in about 30 s; slump test: cement breaks and slumps back until a point where it breaks without slumping; working time 60-90 s traditional, about 3 minutes RMGIC.
T9|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.417 | ANCHOR: "A syringe dispenser, like the Centrix syringe,"] Restoration: matrix reduces voids and porosity; syringe (Centrix) placement avoids air bubbles, tip near cavity floor; light-cure restoration given longer exposure than suggested; a fully cured RMGIC resists water uptake but can still dehydrate.
T13|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.418 | ANCHOR: "The best finish is necessarily the matrix finish."] Best finish is the matrix finish; initial contouring with sharp instruments or diamond points, often after 24 hours; final finishing with Sof-lex discs, fine diamonds, rubber abrasives and strips in moist conditions (dry finishing dehydrates and gives porous mottled surface); final polishing delayed 24 hours; protect with resinous glaze.
T13|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.419 | ANCHOR: "The newly placed cement should be sealed"] Surface protection: resin enamel bonding agent protects best (varnish, cocoa butter and petroleum jelly wash away; protection needed 24 h); seal immediately after matrix removal; unprotected maturation causes surface cracks; low-viscosity light-cured resin bond as sealant preserves translucency.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.419 | ANCHOR: "The ion"] Cervical abrasion-erosion and abfraction lesions restored with GIC: ion-exchange adhesion is superior and the cement is flexible enough to withstand tooth flexure.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.420 | ANCHOR: "If a root surface lesion"] Root-surface lesions rarely occur alone: examine the rest of the dentition for other root lesions; Class V cervical caries restored with GIC under cord isolation.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.421 | ANCHOR: "Class III cavities (Fig. 20.20A) are best restored with"] Class III best restored with light-cure GIC for aesthetics; only hidden lesions with conventional cement; autocure cements release more fluoride than light-cure; T-shaped matrix strip, cure 20 s through the matrix then remove matrix and cure further.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.422 | ANCHOR: "In conservative Class I preparations, glass-ionomer"] Conservative Class I preparations in high caries-index patients can be restored with GIC; as a lining it gives thermal protection under metallic restorations and fills undercuts for inlay/crown preparations; Type III lining cement is too weak to be the base for composite in the lamination technique (Type II or light-cure preferred).
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.422 | ANCHOR: "Glass-ionomer cement is used for luting crowns"] Luting: crowns, bridges, orthodontic appliances; advantages anticariogenic, low oral solubility, pulp compatible, thixotropic flow, low film thickness, adhesion, tooth colour; tensile strength inadequate to add retention; low P/L for flow; fast set so water-safe within 5 minutes; string test about 3 cm; no elastic memory unlike zinc phosphate; radiopaque; seating sensitivity from hydraulic displacement of dentinal fluid, so seal tubules with low-viscosity resin or remineralising solution.
T12|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.423 | ANCHOR: "Traditonal type-II and light-cure glass-ionomer"] Bilayered/sandwich: Type II or light-cure GIC as base under composite; allows bonding to dentine in erosive lesions leaving small enamel area for composite; also used to bond amalgam where retention form is lost; preferred base in Class II with subgingival cervical extension because easier than technique-sensitive composite.
T14|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 28_20_Glass_Ionomer_Cement.md | PDF p.423 | ANCHOR: "• Dissolution of the immature cement"] Failure of GIC restoration: dissolution of immature cement (first 24 h; varnish helps), long-term erosion, abrasion loss, fracture from flexure in cervical areas, often cohesive within the body; the cement often leaves an acid-resistant hybrid surface layer on the tooth when it fractures.
## FILE 36_28_Minimal_Intervention_Dentistry.md (Ch.28; marker N follows page N; printed = N-19)
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 36_28_Minimal_Intervention_Dentistry.md | PDF p.553 | ANCHOR: "‘extension for"] Minimal intervention dentistry deals with the cause of disease and rests on prevention and control; minimal invasive dentistry is only its conservative restorative part; 'extension for prevention' has become 'prevention for extension'; adhesive biomaterials are preferred and sound plus remineralisable tissue is preserved.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 36_28_Minimal_Intervention_Dentistry.md | PDF p.553 | ANCHOR: "i. Recognizing risk factors"] Core principles: recognise risk factors, minimise them (diet, fluid balance, pH), and regenerate or remineralise early lesions with fluoride or CPP-ACP.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 36_28_Minimal_Intervention_Dentistry.md | PDF p.559 | ANCHOR: "The ART approach to manage caries lesions was first"] ART removes affected tissue with hand instruments and restores with a specially designed GIC (Fuji IX); first tried in the mid 1980s; single-surface restorations succeed better than multisurface; marked operator effects on survival.
T9|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 36_28_Minimal_Intervention_Dentistry.md | PDF p.559 | ANCHOR: "The steps followed in atraumatic restorative treatment"] ART steps: examine and isolate with cotton rolls, access with hatchet or sharp excavator, break undermined enamel and excavate soft dentine, clean, condition with weak polyacrylic acid, fill GIC into the cavity and confluent pits and fissures, then press with a petroleum-jelly-coated finger and cover with varnish or petroleum jelly; undermined dentine becomes 'supported' by GIC and routine ART needs no lining.
T14|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 36_28_Minimal_Intervention_Dentistry.md | PDF p.560 | ANCHOR: "• No sophisticated dental equipment is needed (can be"] ART advantages: no sophisticated equipment or electricity, minimal discomfort, little training, cariostatic fluoride, low cost, less time, and a more radical approach remains possible if it fails.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 36_28_Minimal_Intervention_Dentistry.md | PDF p.560 | ANCHOR: "Hall technique is a newer method for managing"] Hall technique: preformed metal crowns cemented with GIC luting cement over carious primary molars with no caries removal or tooth preparation.
T10|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 36_28_Minimal_Intervention_Dentistry.md | PDF p.567 | ANCHOR: "Adhesive dental materials make it possible to conserve"] Adhesive materials conserve tooth because no mechanical retention features are needed: GIC, resin-modified GIC, composite with bonding agents, and bonded amalgam.
T14|[BOOK: Sikri Textbook | FILE: 36_28_Minimal_Intervention_Dentistry.md | PDF p.567 | ANCHOR: "The advantages of glass-ionomer cement (GIC)"] In minimal intervention dentistry GIC offers adhesion and fluoride release, performs well in low-stress areas and can be recharged from fluoride varnishes and toothpastes; disadvantages are technique sensitivity and opacity, which resin modification improves (easier placement, light cure, better aesthetics).
