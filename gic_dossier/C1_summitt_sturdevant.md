T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.752 | ANCHOR: "interference with ionic bonding during pellicle and plaque formation"] Fluoride roles in caries prevention: fluorapatite formation (more acid resistant than hydroxyapatite), enhanced remineralization, interference with ionic bonding during pellicle/plaque formation, inhibition of microbial growth and metabolism. High concentrations (e.g. tray fluoride at 12,300 ppm) are bactericidal, lower concentrations enhance remineralization; all levels produce fluorapatite crystals. Even about 1 μmol/L (approx. 0.02 ppm F) leaves oral fluids supersaturated with fluorapatite.
T10|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.753 | ANCHOR: "fluoride-releasing materials are essential in treating the high-caries-risk patient"] Source states fluoride-releasing materials are essential in treating the high-caries-risk patient (caries risk assessment: high, moderate, low).
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.753 | ANCHOR: "10 calcium ions to 6 phosphate ions to 2"] Remineralization requires a ratio of 10 calcium : 6 phosphate : 2 fluoride or hydroxyl ions; calcium may be the dominant factor; fluoride most effective at remineralizing smooth surfaces.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.753 | ANCHOR: "begins at a higher pH (6.4, compared with 5.5 for enamel)"] Dentin demineralization begins at pH 6.4 (enamel 5.5) and proceeds about twice as rapidly because dentin has half the mineral content of enamel. Low fluoride levels are insufficient to initiate dentin remineralization but adequate for enamel.
T10|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.754 | ANCHOR: "Restorative materials that release fluoride are often recommended for caries"] Fluoride-releasing restoratives are often recommended for root-surface caries because root is mainly dentin and such lesions need significantly more fluoride than enamel lesions to remineralize.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.754 | ANCHOR: "averaging about 0.03 ppm (1.6 μmol/L) in the normal subject"] Salivary fluoride averages about 0.03 ppm (1.6 μmol/L); unstimulated salivary flow about 0.3 mL/min, stimulated 1.5 to 2.5 mL/min. Fluorapatite critical pH 4.5 versus hydroxyapatite 5.5.
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.754 | ANCHOR: "patient compliance in caries-control programs requiring home fluoride applications"] Rationale: patient compliance with home fluoride is often poor; fluoride-releasing materials supply fluoride without relying on compliance.
T1|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.754 | ANCHOR: "The first popular fluoride-releasing tooth-colored restorative material was silicate"] First popular fluoride-releasing tooth-colored material was silicate cement: poor adhesion, high intraoral solubility, poor mechanical properties, did not survive well, but recurrent caries around it was rare, attributed to released fluoride.
T7|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.755 | ANCHOR: "Fluoride-releasing materials may be classified into five categories"] Five categories (continuum): (1) resin composite, (2) compomer, (3) giomer, (4) resin-modified glass ionomer (RMGI), (5) conventional glass ionomer. Composites release least fluoride, conventional GIC the highest; compomers are more like composites, RMGIs more like conventional GIC; giomers lie between RMGIs and compomers. Fluoride release and recharge increase from composite toward GIC.

T4|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.757 | ANCHOR: "Glass ionomers chemically bond to dentin by an"] Glass ionomers bond chemically to dentin by an ionic bond with hydroxyapatite; composites, compomers, giomers bond micromechanically via hybrid layer with a dentin adhesive. RMGIs bond both by ionic bond (polyacrylic acid-hydroxyapatite) and mechanical interlocking with collagen and resin monomer.
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.757 | ANCHOR: "tensile shrinkage stresses exceed the bond strength"] Setting contraction pulls material from the wall; when tensile shrinkage stress exceeds bond strength adhesion is lost, compromising marginal seal, increasing staining, postoperative cold sensitivity and potentially marginal caries.
T17|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.757 | ANCHOR: "RMGI > compomer > Ormocer > resin composite"] Gerdolle et al in vitro polymerization shrinkage ranking: RMGI > compomer > Ormocer > resin composite. Attin et al: most of six RMGIs shrank more than hybrid composite or autocured GIC.
T17|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.758 | ANCHOR: "glass ionomers generate less polymerization shrinkage"] Glass ionomers generate less polymerization shrinkage stress than composites; this plus water sorption by GIC can lessen shrinkage effects and reduce marginal gap probability.
T6|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.758 | ANCHOR: "The CTE of glass ionomers is similar to that of"] CTE of tooth structure given as 8.3 × 10–6/°C to "1¼ × 10–6/°C" for dentin and enamel respectively (second figure OCR-garbled; exact value unreadable); GIC CTE similar to enamel and dentin, composites approximately twice.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.758 | ANCHOR: "This property, known as recharge, has the potential to"] Fluoride release diminishes with time, but GIC can be replenished by topical high-fluoride solutions (recharge) giving continuous low salivary fluoride; recharge depends on fluoride at the material surface where concentration is maximum. Fluoride release may mitigate imperfect marginal adaptation.
T8|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.758 | ANCHOR: "Fluoride can be incorporated into resin composites through three mechanisms"] Fluoride into composites by (1) adding NaF or SnF2, (2) fluoride-releasing fillers, (3) fluoride in resin matrix. They have best mechanical properties and wear resistance, higher CTE, no inherent adhesion, but lowest fluoride release and recharge.
T2|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.759 | ANCHOR: "Carboxylic acid chains on the"] GIC = polyalkenoic acid matrix plus ion-leachable fluoroaluminosilicate glass filler; carboxylic chains form ionic bonds with calcium and aluminum ions leached from glass, cross-linking polymer into rigid structure; carboxylic ions also chelate Ca2+ in hydroxyapatite giving chemical bond to tooth.
T3|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.759 | ANCHOR: "form ionic bonds with calcium and aluminum ions leached from"] (see above) Setting of chemically cured GIC shown schematically in Fig 14-3.
T8|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.759 | ANCHOR: "A newer generation of high-viscosity glass ionomers"] High-viscosity GICs (Ketac Molar, Fuji IX) have improved mechanical properties and higher fluoride release than traditional GICs; wear resistance improved but still inferior to composite and should not be used for load-bearing areas in the permanent dentition.
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.759 | ANCHOR: "reported a 1-year wear rate of 73 μm"] Walls and Mather: 1-year wear rate 73 μm for GIC versus <10 μm per year for resin composites. Frankenberger et al: 8% and 40% failure rates for Class 1 and Class 2 glass-ionomer restorations respectively at 24 months.
T8|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.760 | ANCHOR: "RMGIs contain elements of conventional glass ionomers and light-cured resins"] RMGI matrix: polyalkenoic acids modified with unsaturated carbon bonds and/or mixed with (di)methacrylate monomers; cure by acid-base ionic bonding plus free-radical polymerization. Resin content varies widely; increased in recent years to improve handling and strength. Bond mainly chemical to hydroxyapatite, some evidence of hybrid layer especially with higher resin content. Smaller filler particles give smoother surface and increased fluoride release.
T8|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.760 | ANCHOR: "Recently, paste-paste RMGIs (those supplied as two"] Paste-paste RMGIs marketed as luting cements and recently restoratives; easier to mix and place, but one study showed lower crown retention strength than powder-liquid.
T10|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.760 | ANCHOR: "RMGIs may be an acceptable short-term alternative to amalgam"] RMGIs, like conventional GIC, should not be used in occlusal load-bearing areas of permanent dentition; a 1-year clinical study suggests RMGIs may be an acceptable short-term alternative to amalgam in pediatric restorations regarding failure rate.
T8|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.761 | ANCHOR: "Compomers (polyacid-modified resin composites) are also blends of resin"] Compomers: primarily composite in composition; carboxylic groups added to dimethacrylate monomers; fluoroaluminosilicate filler partly silanized; require dentin bonding system and acid etching; release more fluoride than composites but less than GIC/RMGI; abrasion resistance between RMGI and composite; early problem of expansion from water sorption less severe now.
T8|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.761 | ANCHOR: "(glass-ionomer and polymer). Giomers use prereacted glass-ionomer (PRG)"] Giomers (glass-ionomer + polymer): prereacted glass-ionomer (PRG) filler made by reacting fluoride glass with polyalkenoic acid in water, freeze-dried, milled, silanized, added to resin matrix; fluoride more accessible than in resin-matrix materials.
T8|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.762 | ANCHOR: "surface-reacted PRG filler (S-PRG filler) and (2) fully reacted PRG filler"] Two PRG types: S-PRG (thin glass-ionomer outer layer around unreacted glass core) and F-PRG (reacted throughout). Giomers popular for root caries, NCCLs, Class 5, primary teeth lesions; Class 1 and 2 giomer trial had no failures of 41 restorations at 8-year recall.
T17|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.762 | ANCHOR: "the glass-ionomer cement had the greatest fluoride release and recharge"] In vitro 43-day study (deionized water pH 6.5 and lactic acid pH 4.0; recharge with 5,000-ppm NaF for 5 minutes, weekly for 3 weeks): giomer Beautifil II released/recharged substantially more than two fluoride composites, but the GIC Fuji IX Extra had greatest fluoride release and recharge of all.
T1|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.762 | ANCHOR: "Phillips and Swartz56 measured the enamel solubility around"] History: Hals and Norderval found fewer caries lesions around silicate restorations (Class 5); in 1957 Phillips and Swartz showed fluoride from silicate cements reduced adjacent enamel solubility.
T1|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.763 | ANCHOR: "Silicates released large amounts of fluoride for the first 24 hours"] Norman et al 1960: silicates released high fluoride first 24 h, enamel fluoride uptake increase 495%; only 18% of available fluoride absorbed from high-fluoride solution; smaller amounts give larger percentage uptake; sustained low-level exposure matters more than short high bursts. Silicates failed from solubility and poor mechanics; this research led to development of glass-ionomer cement.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.763 | ANCHOR: "greatest release occurred on the first day; subsequently, it decreased"] Four GICs in vitro: greatest release day 1, sharp decrease day 2, gradual decline over 3 weeks to low-level long-term release; after 1 year all still releasing at least 0.5 ppm daily. Other studies report burst for 1-2 days then rapid decline. Burst attributed to fluoride at surface released during glass-polyalkenoate reaction and soluble fluoride added to powder; prolonged release by dissolution from glass in acidified hydrogel and diffusion through matrix.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.764 | ANCHOR: "0.04 ppm before placement of"] Hatibovic-Kofman and Koch: salivary fluoride 0.04 ppm before, 0.8 ppm 3 weeks after, up to 0.3 ppm 1 year after GIC restorations. Hattab et al in situ appliance with four Ketac Fil restorations raised salivary fluoride overnight.
T17|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.764 | ANCHOR: "negative (inverse) correlation between fluoride release and compressive strength"] Xu and Burgess (15 materials): inverse correlation between fluoride release and compressive strength. Fig 14-6 compares Fuji IX, Ketac Molar, Miracle Mix (metal-reinforced), Ketac Silver, Fuji II LC, Vitremer, Photac Fil over 21 days.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.765 | ANCHOR: "should be recharged with external neutral NaF daily"] Hsu et al (flow 20 mL/h): Vitremer released more than Fuji IX over 8 hours; release after recharge declined rapidly, most within 6 hours; so materials should be recharged daily with external neutral NaF.
T17|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.765 | ANCHOR: "greatest from Fuji VII, followed by Fuji IX Extra"] Mousavinasab and Meyers 21-day cumulative fluoride order: Fuji VII > Fuji IX Extra > Fuji II LC > Fuji IX > Dyract Extra > Beautifil; resin-matrix compomer/giomer significantly lower than GICs, showing importance of the GIC matrix.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.765 | ANCHOR: "a lower pH producing higher fluoride release"] Lower pH gives higher fluoride release (probably surface erosion); Moreau and Xu: increased release at cariogenic pH.
T9|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.766 | ANCHOR: "can be decreased and inhibited by a layer of adhesive"] Adhesive layer beneath RMGI can decrease/inhibit fluoride release and incorporation into tooth; adhesive on surface (to reduce moisture loss) initially reduces surface release but resin coat is lost with brushing and mastication so no significant long-term effect.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.766 | ANCHOR: "Perhaps the most important variable in fluoride release"] Most important variable is ability to be recharged, not initial release. Forsten: constant release level about 3 weeks; about 0.5 to 1.0 μg/mL during second year for all GICs except cermets (silver-containing).
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.766 | ANCHOR: "demonstrate the greatest fluoride-recharge capacity"] RMGIs (Photac Fil, Vitremer, Fuji II LC) and conventional GICs (Fuji IX, Ketac Molar) show greatest recharge; fluoride composites (Heliomolar, Tetric Ceram) little; compomers (Dyract AP, Compoglass) between RMGI and composite. Xu and Burgess: RMGIs greatest recharge, then compomers, GIC intermediate, composites least (as stated in text). Raised release lasts less than a day. Recharge capability may be due to microporosities.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.767 | ANCHOR: "High-concentration fluoride solutions such as gels, when applied to"] Reservoir ability depends on type/concentration of agent, frequency, solubility and material type; gels recharge more than low-fluoride toothpastes; effect short-lived so recharge daily; release by washout from surface/pores.
T9|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.767 | ANCHOR: "Acidic topical fluoride solutions, such as acidulated phosphate fluoride"] Acidic topical fluorides (APF, other acidified preparations) degrade GIC and should be avoided; RMGIs more resistant but still degrade; composites also degraded by frequent acidic fluoride.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.768 | ANCHOR: "Fluoride is bactericidal in concentrations significantly greater than"] Fluoride effects on bacteria: inhibits metabolism, growth, causes death; bactericidal only at concentrations well above released; so direct bactericidal effect probably does not occur from restoratives but metabolism affected. Plaque fluoride on adjacent/distant teeth not increased; effect very local, zone about 1 mm from margins. Caries around restorations may be new lesions, not residual caries.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.769 | ANCHOR: "a pH of 2.6 was produced with freshly mixed glass ionomer"] McComb and Erickson: freshly mixed GIC pH 2.6, rising to 7.4 during setting; antibacterial effect attributed to low pH plus fluoride. DeSchepper et al (11 GICs): polyacid liquids inhibit bacteria; inhibition zones correlated with fluoride levels. Later study with light-cured GICs (Vitrebond, XR Ionomer) and neutral NaF control (no inhibition zone): when pH raised to 5 with NaOH none had antibacterial effect; mechanism probably combined fluoride and low pH. Palenik et al: six GICs inhibited growth/adherence of five plaque bacteria, inhibition directly related to fluoride release.
T18|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.769 | ANCHOR: "despite releasing the most fluoride, had the lowest antibacterial properties"] A recent study found GIC, despite releasing the most fluoride, had the lowest antibacterial properties among GIC, RMGI, compomer, giomer, composite; source says fluoride release may not be the dominant antibacterial mechanism or its concentration too low.
T18|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.770 | ANCHOR: "The difference between findings in these studies may be due to"] Antibacterial effect declines as restoration ages; up to 1 month strong positive correlation between fluoride release and reduced S mutans counts; data on older restorations equivocal (Svanberg vs Van Dijken; differences due to silver-containing GIC, sampling site, oral hygiene stopped 1 week).
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.770 | ANCHOR: "enhancement of remineralization is the most important mechanism"] Source states enhancement of remineralization is likely the most important anticaries mechanism of fluoride from restoratives; evidence from caries next to orthodontic bands/brackets, direct fillings in high-risk patients, and ART restorations.
T10|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.771 | ANCHOR: "Bands cemented with glass-ionomer cements have increased"] White spot occurrence during orthodontics 11% to 12%; daily 0.2% NaF rinse retards but does not completely inhibit; bands cemented with GIC show increased retention and less demineralization than zinc phosphate.
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.771 | ANCHOR: "Wood et al 123 placed 54 pairs of Class 5"] Wood et al: 54 pairs of Class 5 Ketac Fil and amalgam in irradiated xerostomic patients: without topical fluoride GIC survived longer than amalgam; with topical NaF (pH 5.8) amalgam survived longer than GIC but recurrent caries reduced in both; shows acidic fluoride degrades GIC.
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.771 | ANCHOR: "recurrent caries lesions had occurred at the margins of 15%"] Haveman et al, 2-year recall, Class 5 in high-risk patients: recurrent caries at margins of 15% conventional GIC (Ketac Fil), 12% RMGI (Vitremer), 44% amalgam (Tytin).
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.772 | ANCHOR: "Nonusers had an 80% reduction in recurrent caries"] McComb et al, 45 head-and-neck-irradiated patients, 2-year trial, Class 5 GIC (Ketac Fil), RMGI (Vitremer), composite: tray fluoride users (more than 50% of the time) had no recurrent caries; non-users had 80% reduction adjacent to fluoride-releasing materials vs non-fluoride-releasing. Caries still developed around fluoride-releasing materials in each study; fluoride is one factor.
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.772 | ANCHOR: "65% lower chance of developing"] ADA Center for Evidence-Based Dentistry systematic review (10 clinical studies, meta-analysis): 65% lower chance of recurrent caries around single-surface GIC than amalgam in permanent teeth; no difference in primary teeth.
T18|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.772 | ANCHOR: "Tyas127 reported no significant difference in recurrent caries"] Contrary evidence: Tyas found no significant difference in recurrent caries between composite (Silux) and GIC (Fuji II) in low-caries-risk population.
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.773 | ANCHOR: "Recurrent caries lesions led to the replacement of almost"] Two clinical reports (almost 7,000 restorations): recurrent caries developed within 10 years for amalgam, 8 years for composite, 5 years for GIC; recurrent caries led to replacement of almost half of GIC restorations. Mjör et al: 9,805 replacements by 243 Norwegian dentists, GIC gave little protection against recurrent caries. Source cautions these retrospective studies lacked randomization and standard recurrent caries definition; fluoride-releasing materials do not eliminate recurrent caries, only one part of a complete programme.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.773 | ANCHOR: "A minimum inhibitory concentration of 100 to 200"] Minimum inhibitory concentration 100 to 200 μg/mL NaF for oral streptococci, 30 times that for bactericidal effect; naturally occurring fluoride up to 21 μg/mL has no obvious effect on supragingival plaque. A study extrapolated that a composite releasing 200 to 300 μg/cm2 over 1 month would completely inhibit secondary caries, approximately 40 to 50 times more than current fluoride composites release. Eichmiller and Marjenhoff: in vitro caries inhibition/remineralization at release rates approx 1 μg/mL.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.773 | ANCHOR: "fluoride concentrations of 0.03 to 0.08 ppm can shift the equilibrium"] Fluoride concentrations of 0.03 to 0.08 ppm shift equilibrium from net demineralization to net remineralization. Dentin remineralization more complex: collagen matrix devoid of mineral does not support remineralization; needs more fluoride plus calcium and phosphate; extent controlled by remaining mineral.
T5|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.774 | ANCHOR: "fluoride release rate of at least 2 to 3 μg/mL/day"] Recommendation: materials with long-term fluoride release of at least 2 to 3 μg/mL/day; with present materials only achievable with supplemental fluoride recharge. Saliva quantity/quality may be the greatest caries-risk factor (statherin, acid proline-rich proteins, histatins stabilise calcium phosphate).
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.774 | ANCHOR: "the mean wear of Dyract was 190 μm"] Even in primary teeth fluoride-releasing materials should be used selectively, weighing expected survival (tooth lifespan) against strength/wear. Dyract compomer: mean wear 190 μm at 1 year (91 Class 1/2 primary restorations) vs approx 10 μm for wear-resistant composite.
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.775 | ANCHOR: "evaluation of posterior compomer restorations revealed a 16% failure"] Compomers showed poor Class 2 marginal adaptation at 6 months; 4-year posterior compomer evaluation 16% failure; compomers should not be used in Class 1 or 2 load-bearing areas of permanent dentition, but can be used in Class 5 and Class 2 open sandwich restorations; Dyract Class 5 NCCL clinically acceptable at 3 years and superior to RMGIs in two studies; more clinical testing needed.
T10|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.775 | ANCHOR: "RMGIs and compomers are recommended as the esthetic restorative"] RMGIs and compomers recommended as esthetic materials of choice for Class 5 restorations in xerostomic/high-risk patients (fluoride release and recharge); resin composite should be used for load-bearing areas because of their poorer wear resistance.
T12|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.775 | ANCHOR: "the open sandwich technique (Fig 14-9)"] Open sandwich technique: for Class 2 and Class 5 restorations extending gingivally below the CEJ; fluoride-releasing material placed along gingival margin (proximal box) when margin is in cementum or dentin; RMGI is cured, then resin composite placed in increments for occlusal wear resistance.
T12|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.775 | ANCHOR: "The open sandwich technique was clinically successful at the 5-year recall140"] Evidence: open sandwich with RMGI (Vitremer) in gingival proximal box covered with composite (Z100) successful at 5-year recall in moderate-to-high caries-risk patients; another 6-year study found acceptable durability of RMGIs in open sandwich; combines composite wear resistance with RMGI fluoride release/recharge; conventional GICs less successful in open sandwich.
T12|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.776 | ANCHOR: "a 9-year clinical trial found no"] Compomers suggested for open sandwich (more fluoride than composite, better handling, likely solubility resistance in acid), but a 9-year trial found no difference in secondary caries with compomer open sandwich vs composite alone.
T12|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.776 | ANCHOR: "RMGI is placed as a thin layer, apical to proximal"] Fig 14-10 sequence: defective amalgam, cavity preparation, primer application and light curing of primer, RMGI placement, etching in preparation for adhesive and composite, resin composite placement, finishing; RMGI placed as thin layer, apical to proximal contacts only.
T14|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.777 | ANCHOR: "modest but growing evidence from clinical trials that fluoride-releasing"] Modest but growing clinical evidence that fluoride-releasing materials (esp. RMGIs and conventional GIC) reduce recurrent caries; dose-response relationship between fluoride release and decreased caries; limitations: poor wear resistance, staining/discoloration (coffee, tea, wine, daylight), loss of surface gloss, contribute to failure (Figs 14-11 to 14-13: Fuji II LC A2 stained vs composite; 2-year-old GIC Class 5 restorations visibly stained).
T10|[BOOK: Summitt | FILE: 14_14_Fluoride_Releasing_Materials.md | PDF p.777 | ANCHOR: "Guidelines for recommended use of fluoride-releasing materials"] Table 14-3 uses. GIC: provisional restorations or caries control in high-risk patients; Class 3 and Class 5; cores/buildups when half or more of tooth remains; ART; sealants for erupting teeth. RMGI: same plus patients with moderate salivary flow, open sandwich technique. Giomer/compomer: high-caries-risk with diminished salivary flow; primary teeth; permanent Class 3 and 5. T9|[BOOK: Sturdevant | FILE: 20 - Class II Cast Metal Restorations.md | PDF p.864 | ANCHOR: "The peripheral 0.5 to 1 mm of the"] Leave peripheral 0.5 to 1 mm of dentin for bonding the light-cured GIC base; large bases can be stressed during temporization/try-in/cementation; if Ca(OH)2 used or no undercuts, place retention coves with No. 1/4 carbide bur remote from pulp; apply GIC without pressure covering the Ca(OH)2 and some peripheral dentin.
T10|[BOOK: Sturdevant | FILE: 20 - Class II Cast Metal Restorations.md | PDF p.890 | ANCHOR: "After caries excavation, a glass-ionomer cement base is inserted to form"] GIC used to temporarily form missing wall portion (mesiofacial onlay margin) and later removed 1 mm deep for composite insert; GIC bases/undercut blocks should carry a light coat of water-soluble lubricant or petroleum jelly to prevent adherence to the temporary.

## FILE 04 - Biomaterials.md (additional entries)
T4|[BOOK: Sturdevant | FILE: 04 - Biomaterials.md | PDF p.199 | ANCHOR: "Cements also may be chemically adhesive (polycarboxylate or glass-ionomer), but"] Cements may be chemically adhesive (polycarboxylate or glass-ionomer) but most cement bond strength results from mechanical adhesion; luting limited by poor wetting, high viscosity and thickness of cement.
T7|[BOOK: Sturdevant | FILE: 04 - Biomaterials.md | PDF p.203 | ANCHOR: "Glass-ionomer cements have been modiﬁed with polymer-containing ﬁllers and monomer-containing matrices."] GICs modified with polymer-containing fillers and monomer-containing matrices are classified as hybrid or resin-modified glass-ionomers, but could equally be described as modified composites.
T17|[BOOK: Sturdevant | FILE: 04 - Biomaterials.md | PDF p.218 | ANCHOR: "Glass-ionomers set more slowly and seem to develop less"] Glass-ionomers set more slowly and seem to develop less interfacial stress than composites (polymerization stress theory); source says the real importance for current clinical systems may be much less.
