### T1 History
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853210000388.pdf | Ngo 2010 | PDF p.2 | ANCHOR: "introduced the GIC family of materials to the dental profession"] Wilson and McLean introduced the GIC family to the dental profession in 1988 (article cites Wilson AD, McLean JW. Glass-ionomer cement. London: Quintessence; 1988). The four families of acid-base materials used in dentistry are silicate, zinc phosphate, polycarboxylate and glass-ionomer.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853210000388.pdf | Ngo 2010 | PDF p.2 | ANCHOR: "silicate cements were introduced to dentistry as a restorative material"] Silicate cements were introduced over 100 years ago; anecdotal evidence that they were an effective anticaries agent, attributed mainly to high fluoride release; solubility was high, so the search for a replacement led to polycarboxylate cements and eventually GIC. "Currently, glass-ionomer is the only restorative material that is water-based and like silicate has an anticaries effect."
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.6 | ANCHOR: "introduced in 1969 by Wilson and Kent"] Glass-ionomer cement was introduced in 1969 by Wilson and Kent, originally ASPA (aluminosilicate polyacrylic acid); developed from the wish for a luting agent with the fluoride release/translucency of silicate cement and the tooth adhesion of polycarboxylate cement. ISO officially uses "glass polyalkenoate cement", with "glass-ionomer" generic for a larger group of cements of similar composition (review article).
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853211000292.pdf | Vaderhobli 2011 | PDF p.2 | ANCHOR: "most widely researched dental materials since their introduction in the 1970s"] Glass ionomers "have been one of the most widely researched dental materials since their introduction in the 1970s". (Differs from Hill's 1969 and Ngo's 1988 book; see T18.)
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.8 | ANCHOR: "In the 1980s, with the desire to improve toughness and resistance to"] RMGI arose in the 1980s when water-soluble polymers or polymerizable resins were added to conventional GIC; in the original material part of the water was replaced by a water-hydroxymethyl methacrylate (HMMA) mixture plus initiator/activator.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.9 | ANCHOR: "appeared in the late1990s"] Compomers (polyacid-modified composite resins) appeared in the late 1990s.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [1, p.128] | refers to: "Sakaguchi RL, Ferracane JL, Powers JM. Craig's Restorative Dental Materials. 14th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2019." | ANCHOR: "Invented in the 1970s, GICs deliberately combine the chemistry"] GICs (invented in the 1970s per the note) combine silicate cement's ion-leachable FAS glass with zinc polycarboxylate's polymeric carboxylic acids, replacing silicate's phosphoric acid liquid (the source of dissolution susceptibility).
- [NOTE: Module 1 - Conservative and Restorative/SubModule_3.1_Restorative_Material_Categories.md | [1, p.157] | refers to: "Sakaguchi RL, Ferracane JL, Powers JM, editors. Craig's Restorative Dental Materials. 14th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2019. Chapter 9, 'Restorative Materials: Resin Composites and Polymers,' p.135-166; Chapter 10, 'Restorative Materials: Metals,' p.171-178." | ANCHOR: "introduced in the late 1980s specifically to give a longer working time"] RMGI introduced in the late 1980s to give longer working time plus immediate light-cure finishing, addressing conventional GIC's slow initial maturation.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [1, p.129] | refers to: "Sakaguchi RL, Ferracane JL, Powers JM. Craig's Restorative Dental Materials. 14th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2019." | ANCHOR: "RMGI was introduced in the late 1980s specifically to solve"] RMGI introduced late 1980s to reconcile longer working time with a quick true set allowing immediate finishing.

### T2 Composition and manufacture
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853210000388.pdf | Ngo 2010 | PDF p.2 | ANCHOR: "GICs are true acid–base cements where the base is the fluoroaluminosilicate glass"] Base = fluoroaluminosilicate glass powder; liquid contains an acid from the polyalkenoic family; third major component is water (major component of the liquid). "The total water content of the set cement is somewhere between 11% and 24%." Ngo calls GIC "the only biological active restorative material that is currently available" (citing Mount, An atlas of glass-ionomer cements, 2002).
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853210000388.pdf | Ngo 2010 | PDF p.2 | ANCHOR: "the calcium has been substituted by strontium (Sr)"] Traditionally GIC powder is calcium-based; in several materials Ca is substituted by strontium (Sr) to give radiopacity; because of similar polarity and atomic size Ca and Sr are interchangeable in GIC and in hydroxyapatite; Curzon reported reduced DMFT with strontium-containing drinking water.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853210000388.pdf | Ngo 2010 | PDF p.3 | ANCHOR: "approximately 60% of the liquid is water"] For restorative GIC approximately 60% of the liquid is water, important for setting, final structure, biocompatibility and ionic exchange.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.6 | ANCHOR: "The powder is usually a calcium aluminosilicate glass"] Powder usually calcium aluminosilicate glass (some replace calcium with strontium or lanthanum) containing fluoride to help control cement formation and modify properties; liquid dilute poly(alkenoic acids) (poly(acrylic acid), itaconic acid, maleic acid plus minor organic acids); acid components may be dried into the powder and mixed with water or dilute tartaric acid.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.7 | ANCHOR: "viscosity has been shown to increase after 24 months"] Shelf life may be an issue: viscosity of GI luting cement increases after 24 months.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000183.pdf | Puckett 2007 | PDF p.10 | ANCHOR: "Compomers are basically polyacid modified resins with filler"] Compomers: polyacid-modified resins with fillers from composite and GI compositions, "a low-fluoride-releasing resin composite"; vinyl groups polymerized by visible light; the GI reaction needs oral fluids to diffuse in.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.9 | ANCHOR: "Compomers are really anhydrous resins that contain ion-leachable glass"] Compomers are anhydrous resins containing ion-leachable glass filler and dehydrated polyalkenoic acid.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [1, p.128] | refers to: "Sakaguchi RL, Ferracane JL, Powers JM. Craig's Restorative Dental Materials. 14th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2019." | ANCHOR: "share four essential components: polycarboxylic acid, FAS glass, water, and tartaric acid"] Four components: polycarboxylic acid, FAS glass, water, tartaric acid (controls working/setting). Matrix usually acrylic acid copolymer with itaconic or maleic acid; glass has calcium, strontium, in some lanthanum; heavy-metal ions make the cement radiopaque.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [3, p.247] | refers to: "McCabe JF, Walls AWG. Applied Dental Materials. 9th ed. Oxford: Blackwell Publishing; 2008." | ANCHOR: "Polymaleic acid, sometimes used instead or alongside it"] Polyacrylic acid has COOH on alternate carbons; polymaleic acid on every carbon (twice the carboxyl groups for a given chain length).
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [7, p.2] | refers to: "Mount GJ. An Atlas of Glass-Ionomer Cements: A Clinician's Guide. 2nd ed. London: Martin Dunitz; 1994." | ANCHOR: "stripping excess calcium ions from the glass particle surface during manufacture"] Fast-set cements get early water-uptake resistance by stripping excess surface calcium from the glass during manufacture so aluminium exchange starts earlier; translucency falls, so used for Type I and III, not Type II.1.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [7, p.63] | refers to: "Mount GJ. An Atlas of Glass-Ionomer Cements: A Clinician's Guide. 2nd ed. London: Martin Dunitz; 1994." | ANCHOR: "approximately 40% by weight of micro-fine silver particles"] Silver cermet (Type II.2): about 40% by weight micro-fine silver (<3.5 micrometre) sintered with the glass under pressure; up to 5% titanium dioxide for colour.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [1, p.129] | refers to: "Sakaguchi RL, Ferracane JL, Powers JM. Craig's Restorative Dental Materials. 14th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2019." | ANCHOR: "sometimes adjusted specifically to match the glass's refractive index"] RMGI glass is sometimes adjusted to match the resin's refractive index for translucency.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_4.1_Cement_Types_and_Selection.md | [2, p.318] | refers to: "Anusavice KJ, Shen C, Rawls HR, eds. Philips' Science of Dental Materials. 13th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2013." | ANCHOR: "restorative-indication GICs use larger particles (~50 μm)"] Restorative GICs use ~50 micrometre particles; cementing GICs finer ~15 micrometre (conflicts with M1 5.3 "10 μm or less"; see T18).

### T3 Setting reaction
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853210000388.pdf | Ngo 2010 | PDF p.3 | ANCHOR: "the matrix-forming cations, Ca or Sr and Al, to leach"] Acidity lets Ca or Sr and Al leach from the glass; they cross-link the polyalkenoic chains to form metal polyacrylate salts (matrix).
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853210000388.pdf | Ngo 2010 | PDF p.3 | ANCHOR: "The setting reaction of GIC is a two-phase process"] Phase 1: cross-linking by Ca/Sr right after mixing - unstable, easily affected by water loss or gain, so protect from contamination and desiccation at once; Phase 2: trivalent Al further cross-links the chains, increasing physical properties and reducing solubility.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853210000388.pdf | Ngo 2010 | PDF p.3 | ANCHOR: "water molecules can be classified as either loosely bound or tightly bound"] Set GIC water is loosely or tightly bound; the loose:tight ratio falls as it matures; loosely bound water is essential for ion release/uptake; water balance must be maintained for the life of the restoration.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.7 | ANCHOR: "divided into at least"] Setting chemistry divided into at least four overlapping stages from mixing, taking several months (Wilson and Nicholson; Mount); patients should limit heavy functional stress on GI-luted restorations for several days.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.7 | ANCHOR: "Water balance is important in the newly placed cement"] Saliva contamination must be avoided for several minutes to prevent early erosion; Wilson and Nicholson: varnish after bulk removal; Curtis: leave excess undisturbed 10 minutes in a wet field, too-dry exposure risks microcracking; petroleum jelly suggested; Mount: newer luting GICs resist water within 5 minutes, isolation beyond 10 minutes not recommended.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.8 | ANCHOR: "Resin-modified glass-ionomer is a dual-cure hybrid"] RMGI sets by slow acid-base reaction plus chemical/light polymerization; initial hardening is from resin polymerization; if too little water or too much resin only polymerization occurs and the material is a "compomer".
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000055.pdf | Pegoraro 2007 | PDF p.4 | ANCHOR: "If the material can set properly by the acid-base reaction without the need of light activa-"] A material that sets by acid-base reaction without light is RMGIC; if set is mainly by light-cured monomers it is a compomer and "does not fit into the class of glass ionomer cements".
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853218300946.pdf | Seghi 2019 | PDF p.7 | ANCHOR: "essentially high molecular weight polyacrylic acid chains that have a small proportion of functional methacrylate groups"] RMGI = polyacrylic acid chains with a small proportion of grafted methacrylate groups, hydrophilic enough to mix in water/HEMA; with acid-soluble glass sets by both ionic and free-radical reactions.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [1, p.128] | refers to: "Sakaguchi RL, Ferracane JL, Powers JM. Craig's Restorative Dental Materials. 14th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2019." | ANCHOR: "This hydrated proton is what actually attacks the glass particle surface"] COOH ionizes to COO- and H3O+; the proton attacks glass releasing Ca and Al; carboxylate-metal salt bridges give gelation/set.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [1, p.129] | refers to: "Sakaguchi RL, Ferracane JL, Powers JM. Craig's Restorative Dental Materials. 14th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2019." | ANCHOR: "calcium ions bind to the polyacrylate chains rapidly; binding to aluminum ions happens only at a later stage"] Staged Ca then Al binding; silicic acid polymerizes to silica hydrogel; fluoride release is a by-product of the same ion exchange and is sustained.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [1, p.129] | refers to: "Sakaguchi RL, Ferracane JL, Powers JM. Craig's Restorative Dental Materials. 14th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2019." | ANCHOR: "An initial set is reached within 3-4 minutes of mixing"] Initial set 3-4 minutes; reaction continues 24 hours or longer; newer formulations allow finishing at about 15 minutes.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [3, p.247] | refers to: "McCabe JF, Walls AWG. Applied Dental Materials. 9th ed. Oxford: Blackwell Publishing; 2008." | ANCHOR: "tartaric acid helps break down the glass particles' surface layers"] Tartaric acid frees Al rapidly but complexes it, preserving working time and inhibiting initial onset of setting.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [7, p.4] | refers to: "Mount GJ. An Atlas of Glass-Ionomer Cements: A Clinician's Guide. 2nd ed. London: Martin Dunitz; 1994." | ANCHOR: "The light-cured resin component acts as an \"umbrella,\""] In dual-cure GIC the light-cured resin acts as an "umbrella" against early water loss/uptake while acid-base setting continues; leftover resin "dark cures" (hence "tri-cure").
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_2.2_Ceramics_and_Glass_Materials.md | [1, p.129] | refers to: "Sakaguchi RL, Ferracane JL, Powers JM. Craig's Restorative Dental Materials. 14th ed. St. Louis: Elsevier; 2019." | ANCHOR: "Two genuinely distinct curing reactions therefore occur within a true light-cured RMGI"] RMGI = polyacid, FAS glass, water + hydrophilic methacrylate (HEMA/glycerol dimethacrylate) + initiators; pendant-methacrylate (isocyanatoethyl methacrylate) vs unmodified-polyacid approaches; light-cured 10-20 s ([1, p.140]); bonds without separate bonding agent.
- [NOTE: Module 3 - Dental Materials and Material Science/SubModule_7.1_Material_Degradation_Mechanisms.md | [3, p.251-252] | refers to: "McCabe JF, Walls AWG. Applied Dental Materials. 9th ed. Oxford: Blackwell Publishing; 2008." | ANCHOR: "a freshly placed cement bathed in a highly acidic fluid"] Erosion depends on pH and cement maturity; worst case a fresh cement in fruit juice; countermeasures varnish/resin, shorter setting time, correct powder:liquid ratio.

### T4 Adhesion
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000055.pdf | Pegoraro 2007 | PDF p.4 | ANCHOR: "Glass ionomers remain the only materials that are self-adhesive"] "Glass ionomers remain the only materials that are self-adhesive to the tooth tissue without any surface pretreatment"; a weak polyalkenoic acid conditioner significantly improves bonding/sealing (cleaning effect, partial demineralization; more important after coarse diamonds). Bond strength greater to enamel than dentin: bonding is to the mineral phase by chelation of Ca on hydroxyapatite. Major benefit: permanent adsorption to hydrophilic hard tissue sealing margins. Stated disadvantage: not always sufficient enamel/dentin bond strength.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000055.pdf | Pegoraro 2007 | PDF p.5 | ANCHOR: "glass ionomers can be considered as adhering to tooth tissue through a self-etch approach"] Chemical adhesion to partially demineralized dentin demonstrated; GIC are self-etching using a high molecular weight polycarboxyl polymer vs low molecular weight acidic monomers of resin self-etch adhesives.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853218300946.pdf | Seghi 2019 | PDF p.6 | ANCHOR: "GI is still considered the one material that self-adheres to mineralized tissues"] Polyacrylic acid removes smear layer exposing intact collagen; cement interdiffuses giving micromechanical bond via a very thin hybrid layer (printed "(1 mm)" - probable typo, see Verification); carboxylic acid groups bond calcium of hydroxyapatite; large polymer gives a shallow hybrid layer; too viscous to enter phosphoric-acid-etched dentin. Cement market valued US$784 million in 2016, dominated by GI cements, RMGI most important.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853218300946.pdf | Seghi 2019 | PDF p.7 | ANCHOR: "the interface between the RMGI and dentin has been shown to be very durable"] Despite low reported bond strengths, the RMGI-dentin interface is very durable in Class V non-carious lesions; retention comparable with or better than adhesives with much higher bond strengths.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853218300946.pdf | Seghi 2019 | PDF p.8 | ANCHOR: "form a submicron hybrid layer with a deposition of a submicron gel phase"] RMGI with conditioner: submicron hybrid layer plus gel phase; without conditioner: intimate contact, no hybrid layer; both perform well (chemical interaction plus some micromechanical interlocking); manufacturers generally do not require conditioning.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853218300946.pdf | Seghi 2019 | PDF p.9 | ANCHOR: "intimate contact without hybridization was observed"] RMGI Vitrebond: intimate contact without hybridization, clinical performance comparable to the best adhesives.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000225.pdf | Stangel 2007 | PDF p.5 | ANCHOR: "whose carboxyl functionality complexes with calcium in apatite"] Glass ionomer cements are the exception to the generic etch-and-rinse/self-etch methodology: carboxyl groups complex with calcium in apatite.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000225.pdf | Stangel 2007 | PDF p.8 | ANCHOR: "Improvement in the glass ionomer bond to enamel can be made by pretreating surfaces"] Enamel: carboxyl-calcium complexation is ionic and "may not be substantial for clinical use"; polyalkenoic acid pretreatment removes smear layer, partly demineralizes and adds a micromechanical component.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000225.pdf | Stangel 2007 | PDF p.14 | ANCHOR: "resin-modified glass ionomer performance was comparable to three-step etch-and-rinse systems"] Clinical NCCL trial review (85 publications): RMGI comparable to three-step etch-and-rinse; conventional GI loss rate somewhat greater than RMGI; one-step self-etch 0-48% restoration loss.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853220300367.pdf | Hayashi 2020 | PDF p.3 | ANCHOR: "the glass ionomers and two-step self-etch mild adhesives (2SEa-6th generation) dis-"] Peumans systematic review of NCCL trials (1950-2013): glass ionomers and two-step mild self-etch adhesives showed the most favourable, durable bonding; 3-step etch-and-rinse also favourable; strong self-etch (pH <1.5) had higher annual failure.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853211000292.pdf | Vaderhobli 2011 | PDF p.2 | ANCHOR: "One main advantage of glass ionomer materials is their chemical bonding ability"] Chemical bonding to tooth makes GIC more leak-resistant; GI bonding is more degeneration-resistant than the hydrolytically degrading resin hybrid layer.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853211000292.pdf | Vaderhobli 2011 | PDF p.3 | ANCHOR: "glass ionomer adhere to these surface better then dental adhesive-bonding agents"] Deep cervical lesions and proximal boxes with root-surface floors (wide tubules): GI adheres better than adhesives; condition with polyacrylic acid, rinse, then place GI (smear plugs retained, better seal, no postoperative sensitivity); a Fuji self-conditioner for RMGI needs no rinsing.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.4 | ANCHOR: "Molecular adhesion results from bipolar, Van der Waals forces"] Molecular adhesion = bipolar, Van der Waals forces and weak chemical bonds (polycarboxylate, GI); mechanical interlocking on parallel walls is the principal retention for any luting cement.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.7 | ANCHOR: "Chemical adhesion to tooth structure by chelation with calcium and phosphate ions"] Chelation of Ca/phosphate in dentin and enamel, translucency and slow fluoride release made GI a popular luting agent; tooth clean, dry but not dehydrated, smear layer retained; resin-based sealer recommended to reduce sensitivity and enhance retention.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853207000171.pdf | Hill 2007 | PDF p.9 | ANCHOR: "Removal of the smear layer before cementation by acid etching is not typically needed"] RMGI luting: adhesives tried gave mixed results; acid-etch smear removal not typically needed and may raise post-cementation sensitivity.
- [DCNA: 1-s2.0-S0011853210000388.pdf | Ngo 2010 | PDF p.7 | ANCHOR: "ion exchange adhesion with both enamel and dentine, with low technique sensitivity"] GIC delivers a long-lasting seal by ion exchange adhesion with enamel and dentine with low technique sensitivity.
