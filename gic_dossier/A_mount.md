## T1 History and evolution
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.155 | ANCHOR: "developed in England and first reported by Wilson and Kent"] Glass-ionomer was developed in England and first reported by Wilson and Kent in 1972; it was then recommended for restoration of cervical abrasion lesions, but early versions lacked aesthetics and translucency.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.157 | ANCHOR: "Since the introduction of the original auto‐cure glass‐ionomers in 1976"] Original auto-cure glass-ionomers were introduced in 1976; many variations followed to strengthen the restoration, increase resistance to early water uptake, modify chemical properties and enhance translucency.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.173 | ANCHOR: "It was recognized in the 1950s that silicate cement released fluoride"] Silicate cement was recognised in the 1950s to release fluoride (seen as a useful novelty); when glass-ionomers were released in 1976 they also released fluoride, and for that and other reasons (notably adhesion to enamel and dentine) rapidly replaced the silicates.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.173 | ANCHOR: "all of the restorative materials used in dentistry were biologically inert"] Before glass-ionomers, all restorative materials were biologically inert relative to the tooth structure they replaced; the profession's interest in bioactivity (apart from fluoride) was small while it concentrated on surgical elimination of caries.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.28 | ANCHOR: "As with silicate cement, fluoride is used as a flux"] As with silicate cement, fluoride is used as a flux in manufacturing the glass powder.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.18 | ANCHOR: "The following classification is adapted from"] The Type I, II (II.1, II.2) and III classification used in the Atlas is adapted from Wilson and McLean (1988) and is described as generally accepted.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.19 | ANCHOR: "ltaconic acid and maleic acid, were developed, and"] The original liquid was poly(acrylic acid) with viscosity/storage problems; copolymers of acrylic acid with other unsaturated carboxylic acids such as itaconic and maleic acid were then developed and proved more reliable, easily handled and stored (OCR renders itaconic as 'ltaconic').
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.18 | ANCHOR: "the use of glasses of lower fluoride content"] Introduction of tartaric acid to speed the set allowed glasses of lower fluoride content, which are notably more translucent and valuable for Type II.1 cements; earlier formulations used high-fluoride, rather opaque glasses for rapid set.
- [FILE: Chapter_02_type_i_luting_cements.md | PDF p.44 | ANCHOR: "cements were developed in the middle 1960s and"] Zinc polycarboxylate cements were developed in the middle 1960s, offering adhesion to tooth structure and noble metals, but their low tensile strength meant the promised adhesion was short-lived.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.36 | ANCHOR: "now used in surgery as bone replacements, and"] Derivatives of glass-ionomer cements are (at time of writing) used in surgery as bone replacements, and soft tissues adapt very intimately to the cement.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.159 | ANCHOR: "Compomers are largely no longer available on most markets"] Compomers (polyacid-modified composite resins) are largely no longer available on most markets and are mentioned for historical interest.
- [FILE: 10_10_Resin_Based_Composite_Restorative_Materials.md | PDF p.193 | ANCHOR: "only one generation of enamel bonding"] Over the period in which dentine bonding agents went through eight 'generations', there has been only one generation of enamel bonding and one generation of glass-ionomer cement chemical adhesion; both are described as strong and reliable throughout.

## T2 Composition and manufacture (powder, liquid, glass, acids, particle size)
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.156 | ANCHOR: "formed from the acid/ base reaction between a polyalkenoic acid"] Glass-ionomer is a water-based cement formed by acid/base reaction between a polyalkenoic acid (liquid) and an ion-leachable, fluoride-containing calcium or strontium aluminosilicate glass (base).
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.156 | ANCHOR: "cements’, indicates the principal components"] The official ISO terminology is 'glass polyalkenoate cements'.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.156 | ANCHOR: "Approximate composition of a calcium fluoroaluminosilicate glass."] Table 9.1, approximate calcium fluoroaluminosilicate glass composition (wt%, adapted from Prosser et al.): SiO2 29.0; Al2O3 16.6; CaF2 34.2; Na3AlF6 5.0; AlF3 5.3; AlPO4 9.9.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.156 | ANCHOR: "ground and sieved to obtain a particle size ranging between"] The glass mixture is fused, quenched, ground and sieved to particle size 4-50 µm depending on clinical application.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.156 | ANCHOR: "finer particles are used for luting cements and lining cements"] Finer particles are used for luting and lining cements; coarser for restoratives; range and distribution affect working properties, setting speed and final properties.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.156 | ANCHOR: "The reactivity of the glass is controlled by the fusion temperature"] Glass reactivity is controlled by fusion temperature and heat history (heated, held/cooled for a specified period, quenched in water); higher-temperature glasses set more quickly; annealing modifies reactivity, used to vary setting time and physical properties.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.156 | ANCHOR: "strontium is close to calcium on the periodic table"] Strontium and lanthanum can be added for radiopacity; strontium can substitute entirely for calcium (similar atomic diameter and valency) and is used in most modern materials; strontium taken up into demineralised dentine in exchange for calcium can be tracked under SEM.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.156 | ANCHOR: "matching the reflective index of the glass to that of"] Better translucency of newer materials was achieved by matching the refractive ('reflective' in the source) index of glass to that of the liquid.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.156 | ANCHOR: "powder particles are formed by sintering a metal such as"] Cermet (ceramic-metal) powder is formed by sintering a metal such as silver or gold onto glass particles (glass mixed with fine metal powder and reheated); unattached metal is washed out.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.157 | ANCHOR: "Up to 5% (weight) of titanium dioxide may then be added"] Up to 5% (weight) titanium dioxide may be added to the cermet powder to restore acceptable colour.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.157 | ANCHOR: "may improve the abrasion resistance and radiopacity but does not appear"] Adding metal flakes or spheres (e.g. amalgam alloy) may improve abrasion resistance and radiopacity but not other physical properties, and appears to reduce integrity of the ion-exchange adhesion; these technologies are no longer as popular.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.157 | ANCHOR: "its action as an oxide scavenger during the melt"] Fluoride is essential in the glass because of its effect on fusion temperature and as an oxide scavenger in the melt; it is not a physical part of the matrix but can affect handling, final properties and translucency (some products use NaF instead).
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.157 | ANCHOR: "a moderate reduction will enhance translucency without unduly reducing"] A moderate reduction in glass fluoride content enhances translucency without unduly reducing caries-control potential.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.157 | ANCHOR: "contains a 40–55% solution of 2:1 acrylic acid"] A typical liquid is a 40-55% aqueous solution of 2:1 acrylic acid-itaconic acid copolymer, or a maleic acid/acrylic acid copolymer; copolymers store better than the original polyacrylic acid solution, which became viscous quickly.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.157 | ANCHOR: "trials being conducted with polyphosphonic acids but only the polyalkenoic"] Variations such as polyphosphonic acids have been tried but only polyalkenoic acid has been clinically successful.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.157 | ANCHOR: "small quantities (5–15%) of the optically active isomers of tartaric acid"] Tartaric acid (5-15%, optically active isomers) is added to the liquid to assist ion extraction from glass, keep working time and sharpen setting time, and allow lower-fluoride, more translucent glasses.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.157 | ANCHOR: "it is a misnomer in as much as the glass"] 'Anhydrous' cements are a misnomer: polyacrylic acid is vacuum-dried and premixed in the powder and the liquid is water or dilute aqueous tartaric acid; low viscosity on mixing, suited to luting/lining; higher-molecular-weight acid can be used, moderately improving properties.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.18 | ANCHOR: "powders are of a high-fluoride variety to allow for"] Earlier formulations used high-fluoride, rather opaque glass powders for rapid set; another way to speed set is to deplete available calcium ions on the glass surface at the expense of aesthetics, giving early resistance to water uptake.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.18 | ANCHOR: "ranging up to 504m in size, while the faster-"] Slower-setting aesthetic cements have particles up to 50 µm (OCR '504m'); faster-setting luting and lining cements have finer distribution; smaller particles speed the reaction and give finer film thickness.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.19 | ANCHOR: "increases strength and accelerates setting time."] Higher molecular weight and concentration of polyacid increase strength and speed setting but raise viscosity (and viscosity rises on storage) while the polyacid is in solution, hence the trend to dehydrated polyacid in the powder with water or dilute tartaric acid as liquid.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.19 | ANCHOR: "a dehydrated form of polyacid"] Anhydrous-type cements give lower viscosity mixes, superior physical properties (higher molecular weight acids), better shelf life, easier hand-mixing and finer film thickness, particularly suitable for luting.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.41 | ANCHOR: "radiopacifiers such as barium sulphate or"] Radiopacity can come from selection of the glass or radiopacifiers such as barium sulphate or metals such as silver.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.18 | ANCHOR: "The thermal history of the glass during"] The thermal history of the glass during manufacture affects clinical handling and ultimate physical properties of the cement.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.18 | ANCHOR: "Powder particle size varies between both"] Powder particle size varies between manufacturers and cement types.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.18 | ANCHOR: "products must therefore never be interchanged."] Constituents of different manufacturers' powders and liquids differ considerably so products must never be interchanged; some differently named materials come from the same manufacturer.
- [FILE: Chapter_04_type_ii_2_restorative_reinforced_cements.md | PDF p.73 | ANCHOR: "incorporating approximately 40% by weight of"] Silver cermet: approximately 40% by weight micro-fine silver particles (<3.5 µm, OCR 'um') incorporated with powdered glass and sintered together under pressure; unreacted silver washed out; up to 5% titanium dioxide added to modify colour.
- [FILE: Chapter_04_type_ii_2_restorative_reinforced_cements.md | PDF p.74 | ANCHOR: "combined in the proportion of 8 parts cement"] Amalgam alloy admix (J Simmons): spherical amalgam alloy particles combined 8 parts cement powder to 1 part alloy by volume, mixed with poly(acrylic acid) at about 3:2 by weight; the cement is black.
- [FILE: Chapter_04_type_ii_2_restorative_reinforced_cements.md | PDF p.74 | ANCHOR: "composed of 50% silver, 22% copper and 28% tin,"] Silver alloy admix: flat broken (flake) silver-containing alloy, one company using 50% silver, 22% copper, 28% tin, powder/liquid about 2:1 by weight or 8:1 by volume; claimed higher abrasion resistance via a 'Bielby-type' smear layer (OCR spelling).
- [FILE: Chapter_05_type_iii_lining_cements.md | PDF p.87 | ANCHOR: "cements consist of a light-activated resin"] Non-glass-ionomer 'cements' marketed in the lining category consist of a light-activated resin with an ionomer-glass filler, with little or no poly(alkenoic acid), so they lack the acid/base reaction and ion exchange and do not release fluoride long term.
## T3 Setting reaction and maturation
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.19 | ANCHOR: "linking between polyacid chains, giving a rigidly"] The set is an ionic cross-linking between polyacid chains giving a rigid polyacid/salt matrix; initial cross-linking by readily available calcium ions gives early set, but these divalent linkages are unstable and readily soluble in water.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.21 | ANCHOR: "not stable and are readily soluble in water."] The reaction continues within the hard mass with cross-linking by less soluble trivalent aluminium ions, increasing physical properties and reducing solubility, giving a hard, stable, brittle, highly linked matrix.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.21 | ANCHOR: "for the fast-setting Type | and Type III cements."] Speed of calcium polyacrylate chain development (early water resistance, lower solubility) is increased at manufacture by removing excess calcium ions from the glass surface; this is the technique generally used for fast-setting Type I and Type III cements.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.16 | ANCHOR: "A chart showing the initial decline in translucency over"] Fig 1.8 caption: translucency declines over the first 24 hours after mixing, then increases over the following 7 days to a point more translucent than initially.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.38 | ANCHOR: "setting shrinkage (up to 2—3%) over a short period"] Auto cure cements show setting shrinkage up to 2-3% over a short period; being chemical it shrinks inward toward the cavity floor, not stressing the ion-exchange union; subsequent water uptake compensates and the restoration stabilises over about a week.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.41 | ANCHOR: "1%, Since the extra resins represent less than 5%"] Dual cure: light activation brings immediate setting of the resin component with shrinkage up to 1%; extra resins are under 5% of the restoration so are not expected to stress the chemical union; acid/base reaction continues at somewhat reduced rate and 'dark cure' completes.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.41 | ANCHOR: "considerable water uptake over 90 days with an"] Early dual cure lining cements showed considerable water uptake over 90 days with volume increase and reduced properties; restorative dual cure cements show very small uptake (volume increase about 3%, of no clinical significance).
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.15 | ANCHOR: "A theoretical diagram showing the progress of the"] Figs 1.4-1.5: resins light-activate to depth of light penetration and protect the acid/base reaction from water uptake/loss; 'dark cure' continues until the whole mass sets.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.15 | ANCHOR: "Note also the strength of the specimens that"] Figs 1.6-1.7: auto cure and dual cure cements are stronger at 5 days than at 2 hours (continuing setting chemistry); dual cure specimens allowed to auto cure only for 5 days show the acid/base reaction is not altered by the resins.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.16 | ANCHOR: "the resin component increases, the acid/base reaction"] Fig 1.9: as resin content rises the acid/base reaction reduces until the glass-ionomer benefits are lost and the material becomes a light-activated cement only.
- [FILE: Chapter_03_type_ii_1_restorative_aesthetic_cements.md | PDF p.55 | ANCHOR: "initial snap set at approxi-"] Auto cure Type II.1 cements have an initial snap set at approximately 4 minutes from start of mix (matrix can be removed) but remain very susceptible to water uptake and loss; reaction remains slow, occupying several days possibly months, and cannot be speeded without losing translucency.
- [FILE: Chapter_03_type_ii_1_restorative_aesthetic_cements.md | PDF p.54 | ANCHOR: "Note that water uptake is a serious problem in the early"] Fig 3.1: for auto cure Type II.1, water uptake early is serious (penetrates full depth rapidly, downgrading properties and translucency); water loss risk continues up to 6 months.
- [FILE: Chapter_03_type_ii_1_restorative_aesthetic_cements.md | PDF p.55 | ANCHOR: "will continue to mature for at lease"] Dual cure: unactivated cement matures like auto cure; glass-ionomer and auto-cure resin component probably mature at least one week or longer; avoid stressing the developing ion-exchange union.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.165 | ANCHOR: "Approximately 20–30% of the glass is decomposed"] Stage 1 (dissolution): polyacid attacks glass surface giving a diffusion-based union; approx. 20-30% of glass decomposed, releasing calcium/strontium, aluminium and fluoride ions, forming a cement sol.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.165 | ANCHOR: "within 4–10 minutes from the start of mixing"] Stage 2 (precipitation/gelation/hardening): calcium/strontium then aluminium ions bind polyanion carboxylate groups; initial clinically hard surface within 4-10 minutes from start of mix; maturation over next 24 hours as aluminium binds; fluoride and phosphate form insoluble salts.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.165 | ANCHOR: "Sodium ions contribute to the formation of an orthosilicic acid"] Sodium ions contribute to orthosilicic acid on particle surfaces which, as pH rises, converts to silica gel that helps bind powder to matrix.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.165 | ANCHOR: "progressive hydration of the matrix salts"] Stage 3: progressive hydration of matrix salts with maturation gives a sharp improvement in physical properties.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.164 | ANCHOR: "The calcium or strontium cross"] Calcium/strontium cross-links form first giving clinically acceptable early set but are fragile and vulnerable to early water contamination; aluminium chains cross-link later, strong and insoluble, giving the major physical properties.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.164 | ANCHOR: "are therefore far more tolerant of early exposure to water"] Modern auto-cure materials are based mainly on aluminium (calcium ions removed from glass surface) so are more tolerant of early water, yet waterproof sealing immediately after placement is still recommended.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.163 | ANCHOR: "The leaching of ions from the glass particles creates"] Leaching of ions creates an ion-depleted zone in glass particles; early matrix is an open aqueous network allowing diffusion of hydroxyl and fluoride and exchange of calcium for strontium.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.164 | ANCHOR: "surface hardness (30%) of Fuji IX after exposure to saliva for"] Calcium-for-strontium exchange is offered as a possible explanation for a reported 30% increase in surface hardness of Fuji IX after 40 days in saliva.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.166 | ANCHOR: "The set material contains particles of unreacted glass, surrounded and"] Set structure: unreacted glass particles in a siliceous hydrogel, embedded in a polysalt matrix of cross-linked polyalkenoic acid rich in calcium (strontium) and aluminium; contains bound and free water; ion migration needs water; the only water-based restorative material.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.167 | ANCHOR: "Approximately 24% of the set"] Approximately 24% of set glass-ionomer is water; water is loosely bound (lost readily early) or tightly bound (hydration shell of cation-polyacrylate bond, esp. aluminium, plus some silica gel water; cannot be removed).
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.167 | ANCHOR: "The cement is most susceptible to moisture contamination during Stage 2"] Cement is most susceptible to moisture contamination during Stage 2 and remains susceptible to water loss for some time after placement.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.165 | ANCHOR: "Storage of the mixing slab and the bottle of powder"] Rate of setting depends on manufacturer variables (glass composition, fusion and annealing temperatures, particle size, tartaric acid concentration, liquid composition, resin and photoinitiators) and operator variables incl. mixing temperature (refrigerated slab and powder extend working time up to 25%) and powder:liquid ratio.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.165 | ANCHOR: "it is likely to gel at lower temperatures"] Polyalkenoic acid liquid must never be refrigerated (likely to gel); beware dew on slab in humid areas.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.165 | ANCHOR: "High ambient temperature will speed the chemical reaction."] High ambient temperature speeds, low ambient temperature extends setting time; in tropical regions store powder and slab in refrigerator; in temperate regions store in air-conditioned areas; store capsules air-conditioned.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.166 | ANCHOR: "an ionomer salt hydrogel and a polyHEMA matrix"] RM glass-ionomer setting: acid/base neutralisation plus free-radical methacrylate cure; the two may form separate matrices (ionomer salt hydrogel and polyHEMA, possibly inhibiting acid/base reaction) or multiple cross-linking via pendant methacrylate groups replacing a small fraction of carboxylates.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.166 | ANCHOR: "presence of a photo initiator (camphorquinone)"] RM cross-linking can occur via acid/base reaction, photoinitiator (camphorquinone) light cure, or redox (resin auto-cure).
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.166 | ANCHOR: "full physical properties will not be achieved for some days"] After light activation RM cement hardens rapidly to depth of light penetration and is clinically set, but full properties take some days as acid/base reaction continues, probably at reduced rate.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.166 | ANCHOR: "Then continue to full set over next one month."] 'Be Aware' box: auto-cure sets within three minutes then continues to full set over next one month; RM snap-sets with the light then continues to full set over next one month.
- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.166 | ANCHOR: "It must be noted that initial light activation will lead"] Light activation hardens only to depth of penetration so incremental build-up is desirable, though the auto-cure component ensures eventual full cure (less critical than for composite).

- [FILE: 09_9_Glass_Ionomer_Materials.md | PDF p.167 | ANCHOR: "Water in is immediately critical for auto"] Water balance 'Be Aware': water-in immediately critical for auto-cure; water-out critical for 6 months for auto-cure aesthetic cements; water-in less critical for RM and others; water-out critical two weeks for all cements.

## T4 Adhesion/bonding mechanism to enamel and dentine (conditioning, bond strength figures)
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.28 | ANCHOR: "Wilson has described an ion-exchange layer that"] Wilson describes an ion-exchange layer seen under SEM representing chemical union between cement and tooth (diffusion-based adhesion).
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.28 | ANCHOR: "displacing phosphate ions. To maintain electrolytic"] Poly(alkenoic acid) attacks and penetrates tooth structure displacing phosphate ions; each phosphate takes a calcium ion with it, forming an ion-enriched layer bound to enamel and dentine.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.28 | ANCHOR: "The strength of both the jon-"] The strength of the ion-enriched layer and its union to tooth 'have yet to be measured' (Atlas); failure normally occurs within the cement because of low tensile strength, so the stronger the cement the better the adhesion, provided the interface is free of saliva, pellicle, plaque, blood etc.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.28 | ANCHOR: "demineralize remaining dentine and enamel and"] Condition with brief 10% poly(acrylic acid): dissolves smear layer within 10-15 s; left longer than 20 s likely to demineralise remaining dentine/enamel and open tubules.
- [FILE: Chapter_01_description_of_glass_ionomer_cements.md | PDF p.28 | ANCHOR: "pre-activate the calcium and phosphate jons in the"] Reasons for using 10% PAA: it is the cement's own acid so residue does not interfere with setting; it enhances wettability and pre-activates calcium and phosphate ions in dentine for exchange (Wilson and McLean, 1988).
