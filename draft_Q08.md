## STUDY MODE

### S1. Why biocompatibility is a question for GIC [basic science]
The fresh cement is acidic, resin-modified types contain HEMA, and the material is placed close to dentine and sometimes pulp. The answer must separate three issues: the acid, the monomers, and the bacteria that leak around a poorly sealed restoration.

### S2. Pulp response to conventional GIC [treatment relevance]
Fresh GIC has a very low pH (about 1-2) that rises quickly: Bhat gives 2.33 at 2 minutes, 4.18 at 20 minutes and 5.67-6.0 at one day [SRC:B168]; Sikri says near 2 rising to 6-7 [SRC:G17]. The pulp response is mild and resolves in 10-30 days [SRC:A458] [SRC:B23]. Reasons: the polyacrylic acid is weak and its large molecules do not pass down dentinal tubules, and dentine buffers the acid [SRC:B214] [SRC:G18]. The ion-enriched layer also seals the tubules [SRC:A465]. Residual dentine thickness matters more than the material [SRC:B23].

### S3. Direct pulp contact [clinical decision-making]
GIC is not for direct pulp exposure. Mount says it is quite toxic to an exposed pulp, and the pulp should first be covered with calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate material, then sealed with GIC [SRC:A467]; van Noort reports localised pulp necrosis and inhibited calcific repair in direct contact [SRC:B250]; Cohen says GIs are toxic in direct contact with pulp cells and are inferior to calcium hydroxide for direct pulp capping [SRC:D111]. In a study of 248 pulp-capped teeth, outcomes were less favourable in teeth restored with GIC [SRC:D112].

### S4. Resin-modified GIC and HEMA
HEMA is hydrophilic; polymerised HEMA placed directly on vital pulp may kill pulp tissue, so RMGIC in direct contact with pulp is contraindicated, and at least 0.2-0.5 mm of dentine is needed under an RMGIC lining [SRC:B353] [SRC:B354]. Summitt says RMGI tends to be more cytotoxic than conventional GIC in direct cell contact, though both make excellent liners and bases when not in direct pulp contact [SRC:C188]. Human teeth with mechanical exposures capped with an RMGIC liner showed moderate to intense inflammation, large necrotic zones and no dentine bridge [SRC:D126]. Within 0.5 mm of the pulp an RMGIC reduced odontoblast numbers by more than half in one study, yet in human deep dentin there was no difference in inflammation between a calcium hydroxide liner and an RMGIC liner under composite [SRC:D122]. Laboratory work links cytotoxicity to HEMA release [LIT:Agha2021], and experimental low-HEMA materials gave 89-98% cell viability against 4% for a commercial RMGIC liner [LIT:Potiprapanpong2023].

### S5. Why GIC is nonetheless a good biological material
It reduces bacterial penetration at the interface by chemical bonding, fluoride release, or initial low pH (the mechanism is uncertain) [SRC:C179]; bacterial colonies fail to thrive on its surface [SRC:A461]; soft tissue shows high tolerance, and gingival and bone-related uses exist [SRC:A131] [SRC:A22]. Bacteria rather than the cement itself are considered the main cause of pulp irritation [SRC:A453] [SRC:B273].

### S6. Recent developments and safety [link to Q3]
The developments in Q3 each raise their own safety question: antibacterial additives (chlorhexidine, triclosan, silver and zinc nanoparticles) must not harm strength or cells, and propolis was shown to be cytotoxic [SRC:G37]; nano-hydroxyapatite and bioactive fillers aim to add remineralisation [SRC:G27]; low-HEMA resin-modified formulas aim at cell safety [LIT:Potiprapanpong2023]; alkasite and bioactive resins are resin-based, with in-vitro ion release and little long-term clinical evidence [SRC:E325].

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 34 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Biocompatibility is the ability of a material to function without harming tissue. For GIC the concerns are acidity, monomer release from resin-modified types, and bacterial leakage; the cement is generally biocompatible when a layer of dentine separates it from the pulp [SRC:C178] [SRC:C188].

### 2. Pulp response to conventional GIC
- **pH:** about 1-2 when mixed, 4.18 at 20 minutes, near neutral by 24 hours [SRC:B168] [SRC:C178].
- **Response:** mild inflammation resolving in 10-30 days [SRC:A458] [SRC:B23].
- **Why mild:** weak polyacid, large molecules cannot enter tubules, dentine buffering, sealed tubules [SRC:B214] [SRC:A465].
- **Reparative dentine:** bridging seen in healthy pulp with mechanical exposure in animal and clinical reports [SRC:A459].
- **Residual dentine thickness** matters more than the cement [SRC:B23].

### 2A. How the pH changes (Bhat)
| Time after mixing | pH |
|---|---|
| mixing | about 1.7 [SRC:B168] |
| 2 minutes | 2.33 [SRC:B168] |
| 20 minutes | 4.18 [SRC:B168] |
| 1 hour | 4.55 [SRC:B168] |
| 1 day | 5.67-6.0 [SRC:B168] |

### 2B. Compared with other cements
- Less pulp reaction than zinc phosphate but more than zinc oxide-eugenol (Phillips) [SRC:B513].
- Weaker cell-culture reaction than zinc oxide-eugenol or zinc polyacrylate (Craig 11th ed.) [SRC:B114].
- Mild compared with its phosphoric-acid predecessors, though biocompatibility is described as controversial and varies by brand (Craig 14th ed.) [SRC:B448].
- Polyacid cements are considered less harmful than phosphoric-acid cements because of weaker acids and large immobile polyacid chains (McCabe) [SRC:B23].

### 2C. Cytotoxicity and dentine thickness
Freshly prepared GIC is mildly cytotoxic in cell screening, and the effect falls with time after setting [SRC:B149]. On pulp contact freshly mixed cements cause mild transient inflammation, greatly reduced when at least 1 mm of dentine is present; thin (low powder:liquid) mixes raise the risk of post-operative sensitivity [SRC:B448]. Fluoride released at high concentration may itself raise in-vitro cytotoxicity, and high fluoride can damage pulp tissue next to exposures [SRC:B149] [SRC:D111].

### 3. Direct pulp contact
- Toxic to exposed pulp: cover with calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate cement first [SRC:A467] [SRC:B250].
- Inferior to calcium hydroxide for direct pulp capping [SRC:D111].
- Protect dentine under 0.5 mm with calcium hydroxide [SRC:G18] [SRC:B168].

### 4. Resin-modified GIC and HEMA
| Concern | Finding |
|---|---|
| HEMA toxicity | hydrophilic, can release from the set cement; damaging biological properties including allergic contact dermatitis [SRC:D121] [SRC:B353] |
| Pulp | moderate to intense inflammation and no bridge when capping exposures in human teeth [SRC:D126] |
| Cytotoxicity | RMGI more cytotoxic than conventional GIC in direct cell contact [SRC:C188] |
| Dentine needed | at least 0.2-0.5 mm under an RMGIC lining [SRC:B354] |
| Staff | HEMA penetrates surgical gloves, so avoid touching [SRC:B353] |

### 4A. Precautions with resin-modified GIC
- Sub-line deep cavities with calcium hydroxide, and avoid direct contact between RMGIC and dentine in very deep cavities [SRC:D129].
- Cure thin increments, because excessive curing heat in bulk and HEMA toxicity both matter near the pulp [SRC:B354].
- RMGIC is not appropriate for direct pulp capping in human teeth [SRC:D126]; adhesives, hydrophilic resins and RMGI gave favourable results in primates but not the same biocompatibility in humans [SRC:D113].
- Use calcium silicate or calcium hydroxide on a pulp exposure, then seal with GIC [SRC:A467].

### 5. Soft tissue, bone and plaque
High tissue tolerance; plaque does not thrive; derivatives used as bone substitutes in surgery [SRC:A131] [SRC:A461] [SRC:A22].

### 6. Luting cements and post-operative sensitivity
Sensitivity after cementation was blamed on GIC luting agents, but studies show similar results with zinc phosphate; low powder:liquid mixes keep the pH acidic longer [SRC:A460] [SRC:B513] [SRC:C1180]. Sealing dentine tubules before luting reduces it [SRC:A308].

### 7. Recent developments (summary of Q3)
Resin-modified and tri-cure, high-viscosity, nano-fillers, antibacterial and remineralising additives, glass carbomer and zirconomer, and resin-based ion-releasing materials [SRC:G37] [SRC:G38] [SRC:G39] [SRC:B262].

### 8. Biocompatibility of the new materials
- **Antibacterial additives:** chlorhexidine, triclosan, quaternary ammonium compounds; propolis shown cytotoxic [SRC:G37].
- **Low-HEMA RMGIC:** experimental materials gave 89-98% cell viability against 4% for a commercial RMGIC liner (cell culture) [LIT:Potiprapanpong2023].
- **Bioactive additives:** nano-hydroxyapatite for remineralisation [SRC:G27].
- **Alkasite and bioactive resins:** behaviour over time unclear; clinical evidence limited [SRC:E325].

### 9. Clinical guidelines
Seal at once; leave dentine between cement and pulp; use calcium hydroxide or calcium silicate cement on exposures; sub-line deep cavities when using RMGIC; avoid direct pulp capping with GIC [SRC:A467] [SRC:D129] [SRC:D113].

### 10. Conclusion
Conventional GIC is among the more biocompatible restoratives because of its buffering, large polyacid molecules and seal; resin-modified types add HEMA-related risk near the pulp. New developments must be judged on both biological safety and clinical evidence.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Fresh pH 1-2, near neutral in 24 hours; response mild, resolves in 10-30 days [SRC:B168] [SRC:A458].
- Not for direct pulp contact; cover with calcium hydroxide or calcium silicate first [SRC:A467].
- RMGIC: HEMA, more cytotoxic than conventional; keep 0.2-0.5 mm dentine [SRC:C188] [SRC:B354].
- Bacteria, not the cement, are the main pulp irritant [SRC:A453].
