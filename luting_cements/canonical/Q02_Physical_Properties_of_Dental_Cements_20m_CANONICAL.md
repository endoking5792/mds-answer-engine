## 1. Introduction: why physical properties decide the fate of a cemented restoration

### Explanation

A luting cement joins two materials that are each far more durable than itself, such as gold and dentine or ceramic and dentine, so the cement lute is potentially the weak link of any indirect restoration (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; Phillips' says the cement layer is the weakest link of the prosthesis-tooth assembly (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Lack of retention is a common, even major, cause of failure of indirect restorations and fixed prostheses (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Each physical property therefore maps onto a clinical failure: a cement that is too viscous leaves the crown high; a weak or brittle one fractures; a soluble one washes out at the margin and invites secondary caries; an acidic one injures the pulp; an opaque or radiolucent one spoils aesthetics or diagnosis [Certain]. The requirements are written into standards: ISO 9917-1 for powder-liquid acid-base cements, ISO 9917-2 for resin-modified cements and ISO 4049 for polymer-based cements (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha make "the clinical importance of film thickness, working and setting times, compressive strength" an explicit learning objective of their cements chapter (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], which is the frame this question expects.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Dental cements retain indirect restorations and serve as bases and liners. The cement layer joins two more durable materials, so it is the weak link of the assembly (McCabe and Walls, 2008), and loss of retention is a common cause of failure (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Each physical property therefore governs seating, retention, marginal seal, pulpal health, aesthetics or diagnosis. Standards: ISO 9917-1/-2 (water-based) and ISO 4049 (resin) (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

## 2. Film thickness, consistency and viscosity

### Explanation

**Definition and test.** Film thickness is measured by pressing freshly mixed cement between two flat glass plates; Phillips' applies a 150-N vertical load 10 seconds before the end of the working time (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] and McCabe and Walls describe 150 N across two circular glass plates (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. It is therefore an indirect measure of viscosity during seating (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. The maximum for luting is 25 µm under ISO 9917 / ADA No. 96 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; for resin cements ISO 4049 allows up to 50 µm (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**What controls it.** Particle size and viscosity (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; Craig and Powers rank particle size first and powder concentration (consistency) second (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; large zinc oxide grains form a thick layer and stop the crown seating (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

**Film thickness is not cement thickness.** Cement thickness is the actual gap under a crown and depends also on seating force, crown design and fit; an acceptable range in the literature is 25 to 120 µm (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], and Sikri states that clinically achieved values vary from 20 to 100 µm (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Clinical importance.** A thick film leaves the restoration high, with occlusal problems and grinding, and exposes more cement at the margin, where it erodes and gathers plaque, stain and recurrent caries (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. A full-crown casting is the hardest to seat (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Seating is easier with a lower-viscosity cement, more taper and a shorter preparation (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; thinner layers mean less plaque and microleakage (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; and Sikri notes that tensile bond strength to cast alloy falls as film thickness rises (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Viscosity with time.** Several cements are pseudoplastic: thick at the end of mixing but flowing under seating pressure (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Craig and Powers, 2002; Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Viscosity rises quickly after mixing, so delay gives a larger film thickness and incomplete seating (Craig and Powers, 2002; Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]; for resin-modified glass ionomer, film thickness may increase substantially if the crown is not placed within the working time (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate that has lost its gloss becomes stringy and too thick to seat a casting (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Chairside consistency test (books disagree).** For zinc phosphate, Phillips' accepts a mix that strings 12 to 19 mm off the slab and calls a string longer than 19 mm too viscous (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; Craig and Powers describe the correct luting mix as stringing about 2 to 3 cm (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Film thickness and viscosity**
- Test: mixed cement pressed between two glass plates under 150 N; the thickness reflects viscosity during seating (Anusavice et al, undated).
- Limit: 25 µm for water-based luting cements; 50 µm for resin (ISO 4049) (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Controlled by particle size, powder/liquid ratio, time after mixing and seating force.
- Importance: a thick film prevents seating, leaving a high crown and open margin; exposed cement dissolves, allowing plaque and recurrent caries (van Noort, 2013).
- Pseudoplastic cements thin under pressure; delay raises viscosity, so seat promptly (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- Film thickness differs from actual cement thickness under a crown (25-120 µm) (Anusavice et al, undated).

## 3. Working time and setting time

### Explanation

Long working time with a short setting time is the ideal (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]: the working time keeps viscosity low while the restoration is seated (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and the setting time must not keep the patient waiting once it is seated (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Net setting time under ANSI/ADA No. 96 is 2.5 to 8 minutes at body temperature (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; McCabe and Walls give the ISO maximum as 8 minutes for luting and 6 for lining cements, the minimum being there to guarantee working time (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate has much less working time than zinc phosphate, approximately 2.5 versus 5 minutes (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; glass ionomer sets within 6 to 8 minutes from the start of mixing (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Clinically, a multi-unit prosthesis needs more working time, and a cool slab (kept above the dew point) is the most viable way to obtain it for zinc phosphate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Too short a working time is one of the commonest causes of an unseated crown [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**2. Working and setting time**
- Long working time keeps the cement fluid during seating; short setting time spares the patient (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Net setting time 2.5-8 min at 37 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Polycarboxylate gives about 2.5 min against 5 min for zinc phosphate; for multiple units use a cool slab above the dew point (Anusavice et al, undated).

## 4. Strength, fracture toughness and modulus of elasticity

### Explanation

**Strength and toughness.** The thin lute must withstand forces transmitted through it; high tensile strength, fracture toughness and fatigue strength help (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Permanent cements need high static and fatigue strength, fracture toughness and wear resistance (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and fatigue testing is considered more representative of clinical loading (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; Phillips' prefers high compressive, shear and flexural strengths (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

**Minimum compressive strength: the books print different ISO figures.**

| Source | Minimum 24-h compressive strength printed for luting cements |
|---|---|
| Craig and Powers, 2002 [SRC] | 70 MPa (ANSI/ADA No. 96, ISO 9917) |
| Sakaguchi et al, 2019 [SRC] | 70 MPa (ISO 9917, quoted for glass ionomer) |
| McCabe and Walls, 2008 [SRC] | 70 MPa (Table 28.2, zinc phosphate) |
| Anusavice et al, undated [SRC] | 50 MPa (Table 14-2, "ISO 9917" row) |
| Sikri, 2017 [SRC] | 70 MPa "considered optimum" |
| Bhat et al, 2019 [SRC] | >75 MPa for permanent cementing materials |

Craig and Powers add that clinical studies show cements of 70 MPa or more satisfactorily retain well-fitting castings (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

Cements are brittle: zinc phosphate's low tensile strength relative to its compressive strength shows this (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], and van Noort gives its tensile strength as 5-7 MPa (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Zinc phosphate reaches two thirds of its final strength in 1 hour (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], whereas glass ionomer keeps gaining compressive strength between 24 hours and 1 year (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Fracture toughness in Phillips' Table 14-9: conventional luting glass ionomer 0.27-0.37, hybrid (resin-modified) luting glass ionomer 0.79-1.08, resin cement 1.30 MPa·m½ (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Sikri adds that glass ionomers show higher creep than zinc phosphate (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Modulus of elasticity.** Craig's states that ideal values are debatable (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; Bhat et al argue it should ideally match dentine (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC], and van Noort notes that zinc phosphate, at about 12 GPa, is similar to dentine (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; Phillips' Table 14-2 prints 13.5 GPa (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Glass ionomer's modulus is about half that of zinc phosphate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], which in Phillips' view makes it less desirable under an all-ceramic crown, because greater tensile stress develops in the crown (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], and unsuitable for weaker, thinner ceramics (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Yet the same book says the lower modulus of resin cements does not appear to affect fracture resistance of resin-luted ceramics (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; the likely reason is that bonding, not stiffness alone, governs crown support [Likely]. Polycarboxylate's modulus is about one third that of zinc phosphate (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and it is more elastic (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], which makes set excess harder to remove [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**3. Strength, toughness and modulus**
- The lute must resist masticatory and fatigue loads: high compressive, tensile and flexural strength and fracture toughness (van Noort, 2013).
- Minimum compressive strength 70 MPa (Craig and Powers, 2002; McCabe and Walls, 2008); Phillips' table prints 50 MPa (Anusavice et al, undated).
- Cements are brittle (zinc phosphate tensile 5-7 MPa) (van Noort, 2013).
- Fracture toughness (MPa·m½): luting GIC 0.27-0.37, RMGI 0.79-1.08, resin 1.30 (Anusavice et al, undated).
- Modulus ideally near dentine (zinc phosphate about 12 GPa); GIC is about half as stiff, so it is less suitable under weak ceramic crowns (van Noort, 2013; Anusavice et al, undated).

## 5. Solubility, disintegration and acid erosion

### Explanation

Solubility governs the marginal integrity of the restoration and therefore plaque accumulation (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; a low solubility in both neutral and acid environments is required (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], and cements should neither dissolve nor absorb water (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

**Tests.** ISO 9917-1 uses 0.1 M lactic acid/sodium lactate at pH 2.74 to measure erosion rate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] by an impinging jet (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Maximum erosion rates, as printed:

| Cement | Craig and Powers, 2002 [SRC] | McCabe and Walls, 2008 [SRC] |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc phosphate | 0.1 mm/hr | 1.0 mm/hour |
| Polycarboxylate | 2.0 mm/hr | 2.0 mm/hour |
| Glass ionomer | (0.05 in Table 20-2) | 0.05 mm/hour |

Water solubility of zinc phosphate: 0.2% maximum weight loss after 24 hours (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]; van Noort reports 0.04 to 3.3% loss in the first 24 hours against an acceptable upper limit of 0.2% (van Noort, 2013) [SRC].

**Operator factors.** A thicker mix is less soluble (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; too low a powder/liquid ratio gives a weak, highly soluble, low-pH cement (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Glass ionomer must be protected from gain and loss of water for the first 24 hours (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and saliva must not reach it for 7 to 10 minutes (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Resin cements are the least soluble of all (Anusavice et al, undated; Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

The clinical meaning of laboratory solubility is contested; see the critical synthesis.

#### ✎ Exam core

**4. Solubility and disintegration**
- Marginal dissolution leaves a gap for plaque, recurrent caries and loss of retention (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Tested by water immersion (zinc phosphate 0.2% maximum) and lactic acid erosion (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- ISO erosion limit for zinc phosphate printed as 0.1 mm/hr (Craig and Powers, 2002) but 1.0 mm/hour (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Reduced by a high powder/liquid ratio and a dry field; resin is least soluble (Anusavice et al, undated).

## 6. Dimensional change and thermal behaviour

### Explanation

**Dimensional change.** Ideally a luting cement should not shrink, or voids form at the interface (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; low setting contraction keeps interfacial stresses down (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Zinc phosphate shrinks slightly, of the order of 0.04-0.06% in 7 days (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; polycarboxylate contracts from 1% (wet, 1 day) to 6% (dry, 14 days) (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; resin cements shrink 2-5% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. The opposite problem, expansion, matters with resin-modified glass ionomer: water uptake can cause expansion of the order of 3% (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC], which can crack low-strength ceramic crowns (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. The coefficient of thermal expansion of tooth, cement and restoration should be similar (Anusavice et al, undated; McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; Bhat et al give enamel's as about 11.4 ppm/°C (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Thermal properties.** These matter mainly when a cement is used as a base (thicker than 0.75 mm) under metallic restorations (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Thermal diffusivity predicts the response to transient hot and cold stimuli better than conductivity (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Conductivity values, as printed:

| Material | McCabe and Walls, 2008 [SRC] (W m⁻¹ °C⁻¹) | Anusavice et al, undated [SRC] (W/m·K) |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc phosphate | 1.17 | 1.26 (dry) |
| Zinc oxide-eugenol | 0.46 | 1.67 |
| Dentine | 0.63 | not given |

Zinc phosphate is an effective thermal insulator, though no better than dentine itself (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; glass ionomer's diffusivity is close to dentine's (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Moisture greatly reduces zinc phosphate's electrical insulation (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. For a 25 µm lute, thermal insulation is clinically trivial; it is the base thickness that protects the pulp [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**5. Dimensional change and thermal properties**
- Setting shrinkage opens marginal gaps; resin cements shrink 2-5% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- RMGI water uptake expands it about 3% and can crack weak ceramics (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- Thermal expansion should match the tooth.
- As bases under metal, cements must insulate, with diffusivity close to dentine (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## 7. Adhesion and bond strength

### Explanation

Zinc phosphate has no chemical bond; retention is purely mechanical interlocking (Anusavice et al, undated; Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and thermal cycling reduces its bond to enamel and dentine to zero (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate was the first cement to bond chemically (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], at 3.4-13 MPa to enamel and 2.1 MPa to dentine (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], yet clinical studies have not shown better crown retention with it (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Glass ionomer bonds at about 2-5 MPa in shear (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] or 1-3 MPa in tension (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], which seals the margin better but is not enough to increase retention significantly (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Resin-modified glass ionomer reaches 8-12 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], 10-14 MPa (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] or 14 MPa to moist dentine (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]; resin cements give 12-18 MPa immediate shear bond to dentine (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Absence of adhesion is not all loss: it makes old crowns easier to remove (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**6. Adhesion**
- Adhesion supplements preparation geometry and improves marginal seal.
- Zinc phosphate: mechanical interlocking only (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Polycarboxylate and GIC chelate calcium (GIC about 2-5 MPa): better seal, little extra retention (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- RMGI 8-14 MPa, resin 12-18 MPa to dentine (Craig and Powers, 2002; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Non-adhesive cement eases crown retrieval (Anusavice et al, undated).

## 8. Acidity (pH) and biological behaviour

### Explanation

Freshly mixed water-based cements are acidic, and the pulp is exposed over a large area of cut dentine (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; most luting agents are cytotoxic in vitro to some degree (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Zinc phosphate pH is printed differently: rising from about 3 to about 6 by 24 hours (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; reaching about 6 at 1 hour and nearly neutral at 48 hours (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; 4.2 initially, nearly neutral after 48 hours (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]; between 2 and 4 at application depending on brand and powder/liquid ratio (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. The risk is greatest where only a thin layer of dentine remains (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate is kinder because its large polyacid molecules limit penetration into the tubules (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Glass ionomer lies between zinc oxide-eugenol and zinc phosphate in pulpal reaction (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; the reaction is attenuated by at least 1 mm of dentine (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and calcium hydroxide is advised within 0.5 mm of the pulp (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Glass ionomer luting cements may cause prolonged hypersensitivity (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Resin-modified glass ionomer is less biocompatible than conventional because of HEMA (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. A thin (low powder/liquid) mix stays acidic longer (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], which links handling to pulp safety [Certain].

#### ✎ Exam core

**7. pH and biological properties**
- Acidity at placement irritates the pulp through cut tubules, worst over thin dentine; zinc phosphate approaches neutrality only after about 48 hours (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- Polycarboxylate is mild because large polyacid molecules do not penetrate tubules (Anusavice et al, undated).
- A thin mix stays acidic longer (van Noort, 2013); liner within 0.5 mm of the pulp.

## 9. Radiopacity, optical properties and fluoride release

### Explanation

**Radiopacity.** The dentist must be able to tell a luting agent from recurrent caries under a crown (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], so the cement should be more radiopaque than dentine, which also shows excess and overhangs proximally (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; Craig's and Bhat et al make the same point (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC], and resin composite cements are made radiopaque for posterior use (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Optical properties and colour stability.** Resin cements are the most aesthetic; zinc polycarboxylate, zinc oxide-eugenol and zinc phosphate are opaque (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], the latter because of unreacted zinc oxide (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Translucent ceramics such as veneers need lutes with matching colour, colour stability, translucency and surface texture (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Resin cement lines may discolour over time (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], partly because amines degrade and alter the shade (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Fluoride release.** Fluoride release makes glass ionomer the first choice for orthodontic bands (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; it minimises the demineralisation seen around zinc phosphate-cemented bands (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate releases only 15-20% of the fluoride of glass ionomer (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Under crowns the exposed cement surface is small (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], so the clinical significance of fluoride from luting cements is yet to be confirmed (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**8. Radiopacity, optics and fluoride**
- Radiopacity above dentine distinguishes cement from recurrent caries and reveals excess (van Noort, 2013).
- Translucent ceramics need shade-matched, colour-stable resin cements; zinc phosphate, polycarboxylate and ZOE are opaque (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Fluoride (GIC, RMGI) reduces demineralisation around orthodontic bands (Powers and Wataha, 2013); its benefit under crowns is unproven (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

**Conclusion.** No cement meets every requirement; choose by restoration, remaining dentine and caries risk, then preserve the properties by correct proportioning, prompt seating and moisture control.

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim: laboratory solubility predicts how a luting cement behaves in the mouth.** In distilled-water tests glass ionomer looks worst: Phillips' Table 14-2 prints 1.25 wt% for glass ionomer against 0.06 for zinc phosphate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], and Craig and Powers state that laboratory water tests make glass ionomer the most soluble (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Clinically the ranking reverses: a clinical study put glass ionomer least soluble, then zinc phosphate, EBA-ZOE and polyacrylate (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and an in-situ crown-well study found glass ionomer degraded least (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; McCabe and Walls record the same kind of reversal for phosphate versus silicate cements (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Craig and Powers say that acid-erosion rankings correlate well with clinical findings (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], whereas Phillips' concludes that the clinical relevance of in vitro solubility data is not clear and that disintegration rates bear no relationship to its tabulated solubility (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. What follows: water-solubility figures should not be used to rank luting cements, and acid-erosion data are at best a better proxy; none of these is clinical outcome evidence [Likely].

## High-yield points

- Film thickness ≤25 µm for water-based luting cements (ISO 9917), ≤50 µm for resin (ISO 4049); film thickness is not cement thickness.
- Viscosity rises within minutes: seat promptly; pseudoplastic cements thin under pressure.
- Net setting time 2.5-8 min; extend zinc phosphate working time with a cool slab above the dew point.
- ISO minimum compressive strength usually printed as 70 MPa; Phillips' table prints 50.
- Low-modulus cement (GIC) under weak ceramic raises tensile stress in the crown; RMGI expansion can crack it.
- Laboratory water solubility and clinical disintegration do not rank cements the same way.
- Radiopacity greater than dentine separates cement from recurrent caries.

## Common exam mistakes

- Writing film thickness as 25 mm (it is micrometres) or treating it as the same thing as cement thickness under a crown.
- Listing properties without saying the clinical consequence of each, which is what "importance in clinical practice" asks for.
- Quoting a single ISO compressive strength or erosion figure as if all books agree.
- Calling GIC "the most soluble cement" from water-solubility tables without noting its clinical behaviour.
- Forgetting radiopacity and optical properties, which examiners expect in a modern answer.
