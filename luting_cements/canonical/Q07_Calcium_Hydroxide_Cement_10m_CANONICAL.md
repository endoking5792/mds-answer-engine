## 1. Introduction and formulations

### Explanation

Bernhard W. Hermann introduced calcium hydroxide around 1930 to maintain pulp vitality (Dammaschke, 2024) [SRC]. The products fall into three groups, and they behave differently enough that the group should be named in any answer.

1. **Non-setting suspensions and liners.** Calcium hydroxide is suspended in an organic liquid such as methyl ethyl ketone or ethyl alcohol, or in aqueous methyl cellulose (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. They leave a thin film with no mechanical strength or useful thermal insulation (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls describe a water suspension as giving a very friable lining (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC], with compressive strength of only about 8 MPa even when thickened (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].
2. **Two-paste self-setting cements** (e.g. Dycal, Life), described below.
3. **Light-cured resin-containing cements.**

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Calcium hydroxide has been the traditional pulp-capping and lining agent since Hermann, about 1930 (Dammaschke, 2024).

**Formulations**
1. Non-setting suspensions/liners: Ca(OH)2 in water, methyl cellulose or a volatile solvent. Thin, friable film, about 8 MPa (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
2. Two-paste self-cure cements (Dycal, Life).
3. Light-cured: Ca(OH)2 in a resin (UDMA or Bis-GMA/HEMA).

## 2. Composition and setting reaction

### Explanation

In the two-paste cement, Craig's 14th edition gives the pastes as follows (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]:
- base paste: calcium tungstate, tribasic calcium phosphate and zinc oxide in glycol salicylate;
- catalyst paste: calcium hydroxide, zinc oxide and zinc stearate in ethylene toluene sulfonamide.

McCabe and Walls give a typical catalyst paste of 50% calcium hydroxide (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC] and a base paste with 40% glycol salicylate (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. The light-cured cement is calcium hydroxide and barium sulphate in urethane dimethacrylate (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls describe a Bis-GMA/HEMA type, in which HEMA makes the polymer hydrophilic enough to absorb water and release calcium hydroxide (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

The books describe the setting chemistry differently, and the two accounts are recorded side by side:
- Craig's 14th edition: calcium hydroxide and salicylate react to form amorphous calcium disalicylate (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].
- McCabe and Walls: zinc ions are thought to do most of the chelating, with calcium largely unreacted (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

Moisture speeds the set dramatically (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC], so the cement sets quickly in the cavity even though it handles slowly on the pad.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Composition (two-paste)** (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008)
- Base paste: glycol salicylate, calcium tungstate/barium sulphate (radiopacity), calcium phosphate, zinc oxide.
- Catalyst paste: Ca(OH)2 (about 50%), zinc oxide, zinc stearate, ethylene toluene sulphonamide (carrier).

**Setting:** acid-base chelation of the salicylate ester with Ca2+ (and Zn2+), forming amorphous calcium disalicylate. Moisture accelerates the set. Excess free Ca(OH)2 remains and is the active agent.

## 3. Properties

### Explanation

**Setting time and strength.** Craig's 14th edition gives setting times of 2.5 to 5.5 minutes, with strength still rising over 24 hours (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha give 2 to 7 minutes (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Compressive strength for five products rose from 6.5-14.3 MPa at 10 minutes to 9.8-26.8 MPa at 24 hours (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; McCabe and Walls give about 20 MPa (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Craig and Powers nonetheless consider the cement strong enough to withstand amalgam condensation (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**pH.** The books differ:
- 9.2 to 11.7 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC];
- 11 to 12 (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

One light-cured product has a surface pH of 11.9 and 80 MPa strength (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], but unlike the two-paste cements it showed no antibacterial activity (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Solubility.** Water solubility over 24 hours ranged from 0.4% to 7.8% (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and some products seem to disappear from the cavity over the long term (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Phillips' warns the material must not be left at the cavity margin (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Some solubility is needed for the therapeutic effect, so solubility is both the mechanism and the weakness [Likely].

**Other properties.** A layer thicker than 0.5 mm is not suggested (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], so it gives little thermal insulation. It does not interfere with composite polymerisation (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Properties**
- Setting time 2.5-5.5 min (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); 2-7 min (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- Compressive strength low: 9.8-26.8 MPa at 24 h; low modulus, so no load-bearing role (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- pH 9.2-11.7 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); 11-12 (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- Soluble (0.4-7.8% in water), so keep it off margins (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Compatible with composite.

## 4. Mechanism of action

### Explanation

**Antibacterial action.** Calcium hydroxide leaches out of the cement and creates an alkaline environment around it, which McCabe and Walls hold responsible for its antibacterial effect (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Free calcium hydroxide in excess of what forms the disalicylate is the fraction that stimulates secondary dentine and kills bacteria (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. This is why it is used in indirect pulp capping over carious dentine (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Hard-tissue (dentine-bridge) induction.** Craig and Powers describe the sequence after an aqueous paste is placed on an exposed pulp:
1. Necrosis to a depth of 1 mm or more (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].
2. Dystrophic calcification of that necrotic zone, which appears to stimulate dentine-bridge formation (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

The bridge is the pulp's own product. Calcium from the cement does not end up in the mineralised barrier (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; Marzouk reports labelled-calcium studies with the same result (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Alkalinity alone does not explain the effect either, because other equally basic compounds cause persistent pulpitis (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

**Growth factors from dentine.** McCabe and Walls suggest that calcification may be mediated by activation of TGF-β (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Fouad and Levin go further: the high pH liberates bioactive molecules from dentine (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC], including TGF-β1 and adrenomedullin (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. These molecules then drive hard-tissue formation. The current model combines all three steps: hydroxyl ions disinfect, cause superficial irritation and release dentine growth factors, and pulp cells then form the bridge [Likely].

**Setting versus non-setting forms.** The books disagree on which form works best, and the two views are recorded side by side:
- **Resin-containing setting cements better.** Craig and Powers say resin-containing calcium hydroxide is less irritating, forms a bridge faster without a necrotic zone (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and is the most effective liner for pulp exposures (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].
- **Aqueous suspensions better.** Dammaschke says setting salicylate cements release fewer hydroxyl ions, so pH is lower and the antimicrobial effect weaker (Dammaschke, 2024) [SRC]. Bogen et al and Dammaschke both say salicylate-based hard-setting cements cannot be recommended for direct pulp capping (Bogen et al, 2021; Dammaschke, 2024) [SRC], and Bogen et al find little specific calcium hydroxide effect in light-cured liners (Bogen et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Mechanism of action**
1. **Antibacterial:** released OH- ions raise the pH and kill residual bacteria (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
2. **Hard-tissue induction:** superficial coagulation necrosis (about 1 mm with aqueous paste) calcifies; new odontoblast-like cells form a dentine bridge (Craig and Powers, 2002). Bridge calcium comes from the pulp, not the cement.
3. **Growth factors:** alkaline pH liberates TGF-β1 from dentine, signalling repair (Fouad and Levin, 2021).
4. Setting cements release fewer OH- ions than aqueous suspensions (Dammaschke, 2024).

## 5. Uses

### Explanation

Craig's 14th edition limits calcium hydroxide liners to direct pulp capping and the deepest parts of a cavity, not general lining of the whole floor (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; it prefers RMGI for general lining (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips' adds its use as a dressing after vital pulpotomy in primary teeth (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. In pulp capping the cement is overlaid with a stronger base (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Dammaschke's finding that hard-setting cements give a better seal than aqueous suspensions (Dammaschke, 2024) [SRC] is the usual reason for placing a setting cement over an aqueous paste [Guessing: none of the books read prescribes this layering explicitly].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Uses:** indirect pulp capping; direct pulp capping (aqueous paste preferred); spot lining of the deepest dentine under composite, amalgam or GIC, always covered by a stronger base; dressing after pulpotomy in primary teeth (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated).

## 6. Limitations

### Explanation

Bogen et al list weak marginal adaptation, degradation and dissolution over time, and resorption in primary teeth (Bogen et al, 2021) [SRC]. The salicylate cements dissolve under the restoration over the long term (Dammaschke, 2024) [SRC]. Tunnel defects occur in bridges formed under calcium hydroxide (Bogen et al, 2021; Dammaschke, 2024) [SRC]; through them, microleakage can later reach the pulp. Resin types may break down and leave a gap (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and composite shrinkage can pull a resin calcium hydroxide liner away from the tooth (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Phillips' adds that calcium hydroxide converts to calcium carbonate and becomes inactive (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

Clinically, direct capping with calcium hydroxide succeeded in 80.1% of teeth at 1 year, 68% at 5 years and 58.7% at 9 years in one cohort (Bogen et al, 2021) [SRC]. Craig's 14th edition reports studies showing MTA gives a better outcome as a pulp-capping agent (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Medicaments Q05 covers intracanal use.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Limitations:** low strength; solubility and long-term dissolution ("disappearing Dycal"); tunnel defects in the dentine bridge permit later leakage; poor seal to dentine; conversion to calcium carbonate; resin types can be pulled away by composite shrinkage (Bogen et al, 2021; McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**Conclusion:** success declines with time; MTA is now preferred for pulp capping (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

## High-yield points
- Two-paste cement: the salicylate ester and Ca(OH)2 react by chelation to amorphous calcium disalicylate (Craig's account); McCabe and Walls credit zinc instead.
- The therapeutic agent is excess free Ca(OH)2, i.e. OH- ions and high pH.
- Necrosis, then dystrophic calcification, then dentine bridge; bridge calcium comes from the pulp.
- High pH releases TGF-β1 from dentine.
- pH 9.2-11.7 (Craig's) versus 11-12 (Powers and Wataha).
- Setting cements release fewer OH- ions than aqueous suspensions.
- Weaknesses: dissolution, tunnel defects, low strength; MTA now preferred.

## Common exam mistakes
- Saying calcium from the cement forms the dentine bridge.
- Placing calcium hydroxide over the whole cavity floor or at the margins.
- Treating Dycal and aqueous paste as equal for direct pulp capping.
- Writing intracanal medicament use, which belongs to the medicaments answer.
- Giving a single pH value without saying that books differ.
