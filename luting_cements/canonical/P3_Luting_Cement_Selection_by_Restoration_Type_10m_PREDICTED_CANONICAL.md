## 1. Principle of selection

### Explanation

Craig's is explicit that different situations need different luting agents and no one material suits every case (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri ties clinical performance to restoration type, with glass ionomers effective for base metals and resin cements promising for composite and ceramic restorations (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], and advises high-tensile-strength cement for crowns and long-span bridges (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

The decision turns on four questions [Certain]: (1) Is the restoration retentive by its own preparation geometry, or does it depend on bonding? (2) Is the restoration strong enough to survive without being bonded (metal, zirconia) or weak and needing support (glass-ceramic, composite)? (3) Can its fit surface be etched (glass-ceramic) or only primed (metal oxide, zirconia)? (4) Are aesthetics and moisture control demanding? Weak, non-retentive and translucent restorations push towards adhesive resin; strong, retentive, opaque restorations allow conventional cements.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle:** no single cement suits every restoration (Braga, 2019). Choice depends on:
1. Retention from preparation geometry vs need for bonding.
2. Strength of the restoration material (weak ceramics need adhesive support).
3. Fit-surface chemistry: etchable (glass) vs primable (metal, zirconia).
4. Aesthetics, moisture control, retrievability.

## 2. Metal and metal-ceramic restorations

### Explanation

Powers and Wataha list adhesive resin (dual-cured), glass ionomer, RMGIC, self-adhesive resin and zinc polycarboxylate for cast alloy crowns and bridges (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt's records zinc phosphate as the classic choice for non-bonded crowns (Rouse, 2013) [SRC], with glass ionomer and RMGIC as alternatives giving higher compressive strength and less marginal dissolution (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; dynamic seating improves seating of metal-margined crowns (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Resin is added where retention is short: Craig's recommends it for castings needing extra retention (Braga, 2019) [SRC], and Summitt's notes retention rises with resin cement plus surface treatment (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC].

If resin is used, the alloy is sandblasted (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; MDP and 4-META work better on base-metal than noble alloys (Braga, 2019) [SRC] because base-metal oxides bond to them (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], and noble alloys need tin plating (Braga, 2019) [SRC] or an alloy 10-MDP primer after alumina blasting (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Sikri adds chemically cured cements such as Panavia and C&B Metabond for metal (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. A prospective study of metal-ceramic crowns with self-adhesive resin reported 97.6% survival up to six years (Brondani et al, 2017) [LIT, abstract only].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Metal / metal-ceramic crowns and bridges** (Powers and Wataha, 2013; Rouse, 2013)
- Retentive preparation: zinc phosphate (classic), GIC or RMGIC; self-adhesive resin.
- Short or over-tapered preparation: adhesive resin (MDP/4-META) after alumina blasting; noble alloy needs tin plating or alloy primer (Braga, 2019).

## 3. Glass-ceramics (silica-based: feldspathic, leucite, lithium disilicate)

### Explanation

Resin cement is mandatory for low-strength ceramics (Braga, 2019) [SRC] and the luting agent of choice for all-ceramic inlays, crowns and bridges because it reduces fracture (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha list dual-cured adhesive resin for ceramic inlays and onlays (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. The fit surface is etched with hydrofluoric acid and silanated (Braga, 2019) [SRC]: HF dissolves the glass phase (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Alani and Patel's table gives 5% or 9.5% HF for 10, 20 or 60 s (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], then silane for 60 s (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; Sturdevant's gives about 5-10% HF for 20-180 s (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

RMGIC appears contraindicated for all-ceramics (van Noort, 2013) [SRC] because its hygroscopic expansion can crack them (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. For veneers use light- or dual-cure aesthetic resin (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], preferably light-cure (Rouse and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], and not self-adhesive cement (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. For thicker inlays and crowns use dual-cure (Fasbinder et al, 2013; Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Self-adhesive cements bond less well to etched glass-ceramic (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Full protocol: ceramics Q11.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Glass-ceramics** (Braga, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated)
- Adhesive resin cement mandatory: bonding strengthens the weak ceramic.
- HF etch (5-9.5%) + silane (Alani and Patel, 2025); tooth etched and bonded.
- Veneers: light-cure aesthetic resin; inlays, onlays, crowns: dual-cure resin.
- Avoid RMGIC (hygroscopic expansion cracks ceramic) (van Noort, 2013).

## 4. Zirconia and oxide ceramics

### Explanation

Zirconia has no glass phase, so HF and silane do not work (Braga, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated; Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The surface is alumina-blasted and primed with a ceramic 10-MDP primer (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], 10-MDP being the main zirconia-primer monomer (Braga, 2019) [SRC] and forming Zr-O-P bonds (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; Sturdevant's reports best adhesion after mechanical plus chemical pretreatment with an MDP-based resin cement (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Because zirconia is strong, Powers and Wataha also allow glass ionomer, RMGIC and self-adhesive resin for zirconia crowns and bridges (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], and self-adhesive cements are popular here (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Note that this conflicts with van Noort's caution on RMGIC under all-ceramics (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; whether this applies to high-strength zirconia is not stated [Guessing].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Zirconia / alumina** (Braga, 2019; Ritter et al, 2019)
- HF and silane ineffective (no glass phase).
- Retentive crowns: GIC, RMGIC or self-adhesive resin acceptable (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- Minimal-retention or resin-bonded: alumina blast + 10-MDP primer + MDP resin cement (Zr-O-P bond).

## 5. Posts

### Explanation

Metal posts are traditionally cemented with zinc phosphate or glass ionomer for ease and track record (Broome and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], retention being mainly frictional (Broome and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; RMGIC makes later post removal very difficult (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. The trend is to resin cement for metal and fibre posts (Broome and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], with air abrasion or tribochemical coating of metal posts (Broome and Robbins, 2013) [SRC].

Fibre posts need resin cement, but root canals are hard to keep dry (Braga, 2019) [SRC] and the canal C-factor may exceed 200 (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Self-etch adhesives are not indicated with self-cured cement because their acidity impedes chemical activation (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. The post is silanated (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], the canal coated with dual-cure adhesive (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], and cement injected from the apical end with an elongated tip (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Etch-and-rinse has most clinical support (Broome and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; self-adhesive cement is contested: less effective in Summitt's (Broome and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], possibly better retention in Sturdevant's in vitro review (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Details: post_core Q04, Q05.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Posts** (Broome and Robbins, 2013)
- Metal (cast or prefabricated): zinc phosphate or GIC (frictional retention); avoid RMGIC if retrieval likely (van Noort, 2013); resin after air abrasion/tribochemical coating.
- Fibre: dual-cure resin cement; silanated post; etch-and-rinse dual-cure adhesive; inject apically with elongated tip; self-adhesive resin is an option (evidence divided).

## 6. Resin-bonded bridges

### Explanation

Powers and Wataha list dual-cured adhesive resin (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], which bonds better than conventional resin to sandblasted Ni-Cr-Be and type IV gold (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Alani and Patel treat Ni/Co-Cr wings by alumina blasting and alloy 10-MDP primer (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], abutment and wing blasted with 50 µm alumina (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], use opaque cement under metal wings (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC] and accept chemical- or dual-cure resin (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Resin-bonded bridges:** adhesive MDP/4-META resin cement (dual- or chemical-cure), opaque under metal wings; wing alumina-blasted (50 µm) + alloy MDP primer (Alani and Patel, 2025; Powers and Wataha, 2013).

## 7. Summary table

### Explanation

The table condenses Powers and Wataha's summary of uses (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC] and Alani and Patel's Table 2.10 surface treatments (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; adhesive resin cements bond most alloy and ceramic restorations (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

| Restoration | Cement of choice | Fit-surface treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Metal, metal-ceramic | ZnPO4, GIC, RMGIC, self-adhesive | Alumina blast; MDP primer if resin |
| Glass-ceramic | Adhesive resin (light/dual) | HF + silane |

| Metal post | ZnPO4, GIC | Air abrasion if resin |
| Fibre post | Dual-cure resin | Silane |
| Resin-bonded bridge | Adhesive MDP resin, opaque | Alumina blast + alloy primer |

## High-yield points
- No universal cement: decide by retention, restoration strength, fit-surface chemistry, aesthetics.
- Glass-ceramic: HF + silane + resin, always.
- Zirconia: no HF/silane; alumina blast + 10-MDP; conventional cements acceptable for retentive crowns.
- Metal: conventional cements if retentive; MDP/4-META resin when retention is poor.
- RMGIC: avoid under glass-ceramics and on posts you may need to remove.
- Veneers: light-cure resin; not self-adhesive.
- Resin-bonded bridges: opaque adhesive MDP resin.

## Common exam mistakes
- Writing "HF etch and silane" for zirconia.
- Recommending RMGIC under glass-ceramic crowns.
- Using self-adhesive or dual-cure cement for porcelain veneers.
- Pairing self-etch adhesive with self-cured cement in a post space.
- Giving one cement for all restorations without the reasoning.
