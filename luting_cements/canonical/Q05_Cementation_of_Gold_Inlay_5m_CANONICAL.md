## 1. Try-in and evaluation

### Explanation

Cementation is the last step of a cast restoration, and Summitt's chapter notes that cement failure has been ranked among the most important factors in the failure of cast restorations (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. Most of that failure is decided before the cement is mixed, at try-in.

**Fit.** Adjustments start on the die. Several layers of die spacer improve both retention and fit (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. If the internal surface is air-abraded before try-in, any area that binds when the casting is seated shows as a bright, burnished mark (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; the alternative is a disclosing medium such as chloroform and rouge or a disclosing paste (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. The binding spot is relieved and the casting reseated until it fits passively; a tight fit usually means it is not fully seated (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. In the mouth the temporary is removed with a scaler or towel clamp (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], a gauze throat screen is placed in case the casting is dropped (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and the casting is never forced (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Contacts.** If the casting does not seat completely, Sturdevant's says the most likely cause is an overcontoured proximal surface (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Contact tightness is judged with floss (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Marzouk specifies unwaxed floss (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

**Occlusion.** The teeth are dried and the patient taps on articulating paper (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; heavy marks are reduced, then lateral working contacts are marked and adjusted.

**Margins.** A ball or beaver-tail burnisher improves marginal adaptation (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] until an explorer passes across the margin without jumping or catching (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Stones and disks are run from metal to tooth or parallel to the margin, never from tooth to gold (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], because running from tooth to gold peels the thin gold edge away from the tooth.

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Try-in** (temporary removed, tooth isolated, throat screen) (Ritter et al, 2019; Fan et al, 2013)
- **Fit:** never forced; binding spots (shiny marks or disclosing medium) relieved until it fits passively.
- **Contacts:** floss; an overcontoured proximal surface commonly prevents seating.
- **Occlusion:** articulating paper, centric and excursions.
- **Margins:** explorer passes smoothly.

## 2. Preparing the tooth and casting; choice of cement

### Explanation

The cement fills the gap between casting and tooth, giving retention and limiting marginal leakage (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. Temporary cement leaves debris on the dentine that must be removed (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; the tooth is then isolated and dried, but not desiccated (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Marzouk applies varnish to all preparation surfaces of vital teeth when zinc phosphate is used (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Summitt's notes that copal varnish has a long history for this purpose but limited duration (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. For glass ionomer the tooth is left clean and slightly moist and no varnish is placed (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC].

**The casting.** After polishing, its internal surface is air-abraded with aluminium oxide to a uniform matte finish (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], with the margins avoided or protected (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], and then cleaned. For resin cements, sandblasting with alumina is the most commonly used metal surface treatment (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Noble (gold) alloys bond poorly to adhesive monomers: tin plating improves their retention to resin cement (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], 10-MDP and 4-META seem more effective on base-metal than on noble alloys (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and alloy primers are an option (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC] although research results on metal primers are inconsistent (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Choice of cement** (full classification in Q1):

| Cement | Point for a gold inlay | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc phosphate | Retention entirely frictional; longest record; Marzouk calls it the most irritating to the pulp-dentine organ | (Fan et al, 2013; Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] |
| Glass ionomer | Very sensitive to early moisture | (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC] |
| Resin-modified GIC | With resin cement, better retention and physical properties than traditional cements; bond to metal increased by metal primers | (Fan et al, 2013; Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] |
| Polycarboxylate | Marzouk: most inert and least irritating | (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] |
| Resin | Chemically cured cements are indicated for cast restorations; Panavia and C&B Metabond suit metal, dual-cure not used for metal | (Fan et al, 2013; Sikri, 2017) [SRC] |
| ZOE | Temporary cementation only | (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] |

Summitt's warns that no cement will compensate for inadequate resistance and retention form in the long term (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; cement choice matters most when the preparation is compromised [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**2. Preparation:** tooth cleaned of temporary cement, isolated, dried without desiccating; inlay fitting surface air-abraded with alumina, margins protected (Fan et al, 2013).

**3. Cement:** zinc phosphate (frictional retention only), GIC or RMGI, polycarboxylate, or chemically cured resin for poorly retentive preparations, with tin plating or metal primer on the gold.

## 3. Mixing and seating

### Explanation

**Mixing.** Zinc phosphate is mixed on a chilled glass slab (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], powder added in small increments until the mix forms a 1-inch string on lifting the spatula (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], the whole mix completed within 90 seconds (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. The slow, cool mix dissipates the heat of the setting reaction and lengthens working time.

**Loading.** Summitt's half-fills the casting with all margins covered (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's applies cement generously to the preparation side of the casting (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The tooth is isolated with cotton rolls and saliva ejector (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Seating.** The casting must go home quickly while the cement is still fluid (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Summitt's: finger pressure, then the patient bites on an orangewood stick moved up and down and side to side (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], called dynamic seating, which seats the casting more completely (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; small inlays can be driven home with a sharpened stick and light mallet taps along the path of draw (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's uses a ball burnisher in the pits with firm pressure (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], then a rubber polishing disk over the casting for the patient to bite on (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Marzouk adds a vibrating condenser on metallic castings (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and holding devices from orangewood sticks to gauze pads (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

**Hydraulic pressure.** Cement trapped under a close-fitting casting resists seating. Zinc phosphate generates the greatest hydraulic pressure of the cements Sikri compares (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Remedies: die spacer, die relief or etching the casting (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] (Summitt's paints spacer to within 0.5 to 1.0 mm of the finish line (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]), venting (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], or Marzouk's perforation of the casting at a low-stress area (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], with zinc phosphate avoided in that case because it creates the highest hydraulic pressure (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Resin cements have a thicker film, so incomplete seating can be a problem (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], and Sikri says their viscosity may tilt castings (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**How long to hold the seating pressure: the books disagree.**

| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| Ritter et al, 2019 (Sturdevant's 2e SA) [SRC] | "A few seconds of this pressure is sufficient." |
| Sikri, 2017 (Indirect Restorations 2e) [SRC] | Seating force maintained at least three minutes, uninterrupted, while the cement sets |

**Burnishing during setting.** Sturdevant's burnishes all accessible margins while the cement is still soft (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and Summitt's notes some added benefit from burnishing on the tooth before the cement sets (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk restricts this to zinc phosphate: burnish during setting with zinc phosphate, not with polycarboxylate, whose setting polymer is dragged out from under the casting (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Burnishing brings the gold edge against the tooth and minimises the cement line (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], improving retention by direct coupling (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; it is always done from restoration to tooth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**4. Seating:** mix to luting consistency (zinc phosphate: chilled slab, finished within about 90 s); half-fill the inlay; seat quickly with finger pressure, then the patient bites on an orangewood stick moved about (dynamic seating) (Fan et al, 2013). Die spacer or venting reduces hydraulic pressure. Burnish accessible margins, metal to tooth, while the cement is soft.

## 4. Removing excess, finishing and review

### Explanation

**When to remove excess: the books disagree.**

| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| Fan et al, 2013 (Summitt's 4e) [SRC] | Removing excess before set drags thin strands of cement from under the margins and leaves voids; the extruded bead also protects marginal cement from moisture, so wait for set |
| Fan et al, 2013 [SRC] | Glass ionomer: leave excess at least 5 minutes after seating |
| Ritter et al, 2019 (Sturdevant's 2e SA) [SRC] | Excess cleaned off with an explorer and air-water spray after the cement has hardened |
| Sikri, 2017 [SRC] | For cements hard to clean once set, "cleaning" is done on partly set cement; once set, the probe should not touch the margins |

Summitt's directs the explorer parallel to or away from margins and passes floss with a knot tied in it through the embrasure (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; cement left subgingivally causes inflammation (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], and Marzouk curettes remnants from the crevice (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Properly done, no cement line is visible at the margins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. In one study cited by Summitt's, finishing the casting before and during cementation gave the least marginal opening for all three cements tested (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; this is laboratory data, not a clinical outcome.

**Review.** Occlusion is rechecked and the patient warned about possible postcementation sensitivity (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; Marzouk says slight thermal sensitivity is expected for a period (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and advises a post-cementation visit to catch occlusal interferences missed at the appointment (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**5. Finishing and review:** remove excess only after set (early removal drags cement from the margins), knotted floss interproximally; no cement line should remain (Ritter et al, 2019). Recheck occlusion; warn of transient sensitivity; review visit.

## High-yield points
- An overcontoured proximal contact is the commonest reason a casting will not seat.
- Air-abraded fitting surface: binding spots show as shiny marks.
- Dynamic seating (biting on an orangewood stick, moved about) seats the casting more completely.
- Zinc phosphate retains by friction only and gives the highest hydraulic pressure; die spacer and venting help.
- Resin to gold needs alumina sandblasting plus tin plating or an alloy primer; chemically cured, not dual-cure.
- Burnish while zinc phosphate is soft, from metal to tooth.
- Remove excess only after set; leave GIC alone for at least 5 minutes.

## Common exam mistakes
- Writing only a cement list: the question asks for the clinical procedure from try-in to review.
- Forcing a binding casting instead of finding the binding spot.
- Removing excess cement while soft "to save time".
- Using a dual-cure or light-cure resin under an opaque gold inlay.
- Running a disk from tooth to gold, which tears the gold margin away.
