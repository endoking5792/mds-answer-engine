## 1. Definition and requirements

### Explanation

Craig's 14th edition defines provisional (temporary) cements as those used to fix temporary restorations between the appointments needed to finish the definitive one (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Because the restoration has to come off again, the cement must have relatively low strength and be easy to handle (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and it must not irritate the pulp (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson state the built-in compromise: soft enough for easy removal, because cement left behind harms gingival health (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC], yet strong enough to hold the restoration until the fit appointment (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. The cement cannot make up for a preparation without retention (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

When the material is used as a temporary filling rather than a lute, Sikri adds that it must seal and insulate the cut tooth surface from the mouth to protect the pulp (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and keep contacts with adjacent and opposing teeth so that teeth do not drift or extrude (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

The specification is unusual in setting a ceiling on strength. ANSI/ADA Specification No. 30 (ISO 3107) allows a maximum of 35 MPa compressive strength for temporary cementation (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], a film thickness of not more than 40 µm (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and up to 2.5% disintegration in 24 hours (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. One clinical study cited by Craig and Powers judged 15 to 24 MPa the most suitable strength for luting provisional crowns, on retention, taste, ease of removal and ease of cleaning (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Cements that lute provisional restorations or fill cavities for a limited period until the definitive restoration (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

**Requirements:** low strength and easy removal yet adequate retention; seal dentine; non-irritant; maintain contacts; compatible with the provisional and the final cement. ANSI/ADA No. 30 Type I: compressive strength at most 35 MPa, film at most 40 µm (Craig and Powers, 2002).

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

Phillips' lists four ANSI/ADA No. 30 types of ZOE cement by clinical use (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Craig and Powers print the same specification with a different label for Type IV, and the two are shown side by side here without choosing between them:

| ANSI/ADA No. 30 type | Phillips' 12th ed (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] | Craig and Powers 11th ed (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] |
|---|---|---|
| I | Temporary cementation | Temporary cement |
| II | Long-term cementation of fixed prostheses | Permanent cement |
| III | Temporary fillings and thermal insulating bases | Filling materials and bases |
| IV | Intermediate fillings | Cavity liners |

Craig's 14th edition gives ZOE, non-eugenol cements and calcium hydroxide pastes as examples of temporary luting agents (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha add temporary resin cements (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], and Bonsor and Pearson describe non-hardening temporary cements for short trial cementation (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC] and calcium sulphate putties as temporary fillings (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**
- **Luting:** ZOE, zinc oxide non-eugenol, temporary resin cements.
- **Filling:** plain and reinforced ZOE (IRM), calcium sulphate putties (Cavit), resin temporaries, polycarboxylate/GIC.
- ANSI/ADA No. 30: Type I temporary cementation, III temporary fillings, IV intermediate fillings (Anusavice et al, undated).

## 3. Zinc oxide-eugenol: plain and reinforced

### Explanation

The provisional ZOE powder in Craig and Powers is mainly zinc oxide with white rosin to reduce brittleness, plus zinc stearate and zinc acetate; the liquid is eugenol with olive oil (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Setting is a chelation reaction that forms amorphous zinc eugenolate (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Excess zinc oxide is always used, so the set mass is unreacted zinc oxide held in that matrix, and moisture is essential for the reaction (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. This is why a damp cavity speeds the set.

The material's value lies in its seal and its effect on the pulp rather than its strength. Phillips' says ZOE seals the dentinal tubules surprisingly well against oral fluids (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], and Craig's 14th edition attributes its biocompatibility to its neutral pH, antibacterial action and anodyne effect (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Strength is low: McCabe and Walls give typical values of 20 MPa unreinforced and 40 MPa reinforced (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Leached eugenol is replaced by water, which can hydrolyse the zinc eugenolate (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC], so plain ZOE is not suitable for luting except on a temporary basis (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

Service life depends on the mix. Phillips' says temporary ZOE restorations last a few weeks at most (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], while a stiff putty-like mix serves as an intermediate restoration for at least a year (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha note that cotton fibres may be added to a short-term filling mix (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

**Reinforced ZOE.** IRM powder is more than 75% zinc oxide and about 20% polymethacrylate; the liquid is more than 99% eugenol with less than 1% acetic acid (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson recommend it for longer-term temporization (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC] because its wear resistance keeps temporized teeth in occlusion (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. The EBA-alumina type is covered in Q8.

**Two-paste luting form.** The two-paste form is the usual temporary luting cement, e.g. TempBond (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. A third "modifier" paste further softens it so that the provisional is easier to remove (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Phillips' warns that ZOE should never be used for temporary cementation of final fixed prostheses (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], because removal is difficult and risks the preparation; Bonsor and Pearson, by contrast, describe ZOE temporary cements for trial cementation of definitive indirect restorations (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. The two books disagree here.

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Zinc oxide-eugenol**
- Powder ZnO, rosin, zinc acetate; liquid eugenol, olive oil; two-paste form (TempBond) for luting (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Sets by chelation to zinc eugenolate; needs moisture.
- Seals well, sedative, antibacterial; but weak (about 20 MPa) and hydrolyses (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- **IRM** (ZnO with 20% polymethacrylate) and EBA-alumina: stronger, for longer temporization (Johnson et al, 2021).

## 4. Non-eugenol, resin and other temporary materials

### Explanation

**Zinc oxide non-eugenol.** Craig and Powers describe these as zinc oxide with an aromatic oil (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. The books name the reacting acid differently:
- long-chain aliphatic acids or aryl-substituted butyric acid (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC];
- carboxylic acids (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC];
- ortho-ethoxybenzoic acid in the catalyst paste (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

They are recorded side by side. These cements suit patients sensitive to eugenol (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and do not soften acrylic provisional crowns (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. However, they adhere less well to preformed metal crowns and set more slowly (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Temporary resin cements** are dimethacrylate with radiopaque glass filler, mostly self-cured (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. They avoid contaminating the tooth with eugenol (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC] and, being tooth-coloured, suit the esthetic zone (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; clear versions also exist (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Their compressive strength is 25 to 70 MPa, against 180 to 265 MPa for definitive composite cements (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Calcium sulphate putties (Cavit type).** Cavit contains zinc oxide, calcium sulfate, zinc sulfate, glycol acetate, polyvinyl acetate resin, polyvinylchloride-acetate, triethanolamine and pigment (Flake and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Calcium sulphate is hydrophilic, so the material expands as it takes up moisture, and this seals the cavity (Flake and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Flake and Johnson require at least 3.5 mm thickness to seal an access cavity (Flake and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson describe the set as hydration of calcium sulphate to gypsum (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Wear resistance is poor but the seal is good (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Baba et al add that these materials abrade easily, are unstable over time and suit only short periods in small cavities (Baba et al, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri gives Cavit a film thickness of 91 µm, unsuitable for luting (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Light-cured resin temporaries.** TERM is a light-cured composite interim material for endodontic access cavities (Flake and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson limit light-cured inlay temporaries to about a month because they degrade and wear (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

**Longer-term temporization.** Modified ZOE, hybrid ionomer or zinc polycarboxylate are used for longer-term fillings (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Baba et al add glass ionomer, zinc phosphate, polycarboxylate and RMGI, which seal acceptably against dentine (Baba et al, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri prefers a calcium hydroxide interim restoration where composite will be the final restoration (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Leakage.** Baba et al cite a study in which IRM, Cavit and TempBond all failed to prevent coronal leakage of microbial markers over 30 days (Baba et al, 2017) [SRC]. This appears to be a laboratory leakage model, not a clinical outcome [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**2. Zinc oxide non-eugenol:** ZnO with oils and an organic acid; does not soften acrylic or inhibit resins; sets slower (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**3. Temporary resin cements:** dimethacrylate with filler; tooth-coloured; low strength, 25-70 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

**4. Cavit:** ZnO, CaSO4, ZnSO4, polyvinyl acetate; premixed; sets with moisture, hygroscopic expansion seals; at least 3.5 mm thick; wears poorly (Flake and Johnson, 2021).

**5. Longer term:** polycarboxylate, zinc phosphate, GIC/RMGI (Baba et al, 2017).

## 5. Eugenol and later resin bonding

### Explanation

Eugenol is a free-radical scavenger (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Eugenol-containing temporary cement can therefore inhibit the adhesive and resin cement of a later bonded restoration. This raises the risk of debonding, or even fracture of low-strength silica-based ceramics (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. At ZOE-composite interfaces, composite properties fell up to 100 µm from the interface (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls flag the same problem when a provisional is luted with ZOE before resin adhesive luting (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Against this, several in vitro studies found dentine bond strength unaffected if the surface was thoroughly cleaned before the adhesive was applied (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. The evidence is laboratory-based. The practical reading is that cleaning may be enough, but avoiding eugenol removes the doubt when bonding is planned [Likely]. Bonsor and Pearson add that eugenol also interacts with addition silicone impression material (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Eugenol and bonding.** Eugenol, a free-radical scavenger, inhibits adhesives, resin cements and acrylic provisionals, risking debonding (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Thorough cleaning restores bond strength in vitro, but non-eugenol cement is safer.

## 6. Selection

### Explanation

Bonsor and Pearson list when non-eugenol cement should be used:
- eugenol allergy (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC];
- acrylic temporaries;
- addition silicone impressions to follow (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC];
- resin composite or resin-containing cement planned for the definitive restoration (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

The books give different service periods, shown side by side:

| Source | Period stated |
|---|---|
| Baba et al, 2017 [SRC] | ZOE cements for up to 1 or 2 weeks; stronger cements for longer |
| Powers and Wataha, 2013 [SRC] | Provisionals cemented with ZOE, non-eugenol or temporary resin cement for 4 to 8 weeks |
| Anusavice et al, undated [SRC] | Stiff-mix ZOE as an intermediate restoration for at least a year |

Part of the difference reflects use: luting versus filling, and the thickness of the mix [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Selection**

| Situation | Material |
|---|---|
| Provisional crown, conventional final cement | ZOE |
| Acrylic provisional, eugenol allergy, bonding planned | Non-eugenol or resin |
| Endodontic access | Cavit or IRM |
| Long-term, high caries | IRM, GIC, polycarboxylate |

**Conclusion:** temporary materials leak with time; place the definitive restoration promptly (Baba et al, 2017).

## High-yield points
- The specification sets a **maximum** strength (35 MPa) for temporary cements, not a minimum (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- ZOE sets by chelation to zinc eugenolate and needs moisture.
- IRM = ZnO with about 20% polymethacrylate; EBA-alumina = see Q8.
- Non-eugenol cements do not soften acrylic provisionals and do not inhibit resin bonding.
- Cavit: calcium sulphate based, hygroscopic expansion, at least 3.5 mm thick, poor wear.
- Eugenol is a free-radical scavenger: avoid it before resin bonding.
- ANSI/ADA No. 30 Type IV is "intermediate fillings" in Phillips' but "cavity liners" in Craig and Powers.

## Common exam mistakes
- Writing a minimum strength for temporary cements; the specification sets a maximum.
- Recommending ZOE under an acrylic provisional or before a bonded ceramic restoration.
- Calling Cavit a ZOE material; it sets by calcium sulphate hydration.
- Writing only luting cements and omitting temporary filling materials (Cavit, IRM, resin temporaries).
- Repeating provisional crown fabrication, which belongs to the provisional restorations answer.

## Mnemonic
Choosing non-eugenol: **"AAAR"**: **A**llergy, **A**crylic provisional, **A**ddition silicone next, **R**esin bonding planned.
