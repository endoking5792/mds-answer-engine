## 1. Definitions: soldering, brazing and welding

### Explanation

Craig and Powers define the terms by whether a third metal is added. Welding joins two pieces of metal directly (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; soldering and brazing join two pieces by adding a third metal (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Phillips' adds that both soldering and brazing are done below the solidus temperature of the parts being joined (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], whereas in welding the surfaces melt and fuse locally and a filler metal may not be needed (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Bhat defines soldering as filling the gap between the parts with a molten filler alloy of lower fusion temperature (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC], and welding as fusing parts by heat, pressure or both, with or without filler (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC].

The line between soldering and brazing is a temperature, and the books give different figures:

| Source | Soldering / brazing threshold |
|---|---|
| Craig and Powers, 2002 (11e) [SRC] | 425 °C; dental joining is done above this, so it should strictly be called brazing |
| Anusavice et al, undated (Phillips' 12e) [SRC] | 450 °C |
| McCabe and Walls, 2008 (9e) [SRC] | No figure; "soldering" and "brazing" are used interchangeably in dentistry |

**Why it is needed.** Long-span bridge frameworks distort on cooling, so they are cast in parts and joined (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Other uses include joining clasps to cast partial dentures (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Soldering/brazing:** joining two metal parts by adding a third, lower-fusing filler metal that melts and flows into the gap by capillary action, below the solidus of the parts (Craig and Powers, 2002; Anusavice et al, undated). Above 450 °C (Phillips') or 425 °C (Craig) it is strictly brazing, which is what dental "soldering" is.

**Welding:** parts joined directly by local melting, with or without filler.

**Uses:** bridge units, clasps, orthodontic wires.

## 2. Solders: requirements, gold solders, fineness

### Explanation

**Soft and hard solders.** Soft lead-tin solders lack corrosion resistance and are not used in the mouth (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Dentistry uses hard solders: gold-based for crown and bridge work (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and silver-based for orthodontic appliances (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Requirements.** Craig and Powers' ideal solder flows easily at a relatively low temperature (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and is fluid, of comparable strength, colour-matched, tarnish-resistant and resistant to pitting (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. McCabe gives the ISO-based standard: a strong, corrosion-resistant joint without changing the structure or properties of the parts (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; joint tensile strength above the 0.2% proof stress of the weaker part (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; no visible corrosion after 7 days in lactic acid and sodium chloride (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Bhat adds a coefficient of thermal expansion matching the parts (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC].

The fusion temperature must be below that of the parts, by how much the books state differently:

| Source | Fusion temperature of solder below the parts |
|---|---|
| Craig and Powers, 2002 [SRC] | At least 56 °C |
| Anusavice et al, undated [SRC] | Flow temperature at least 55.6 °C below the solidus |
| McCabe and Walls, 2008 [SRC] | Generally 50-100 °C below casting gold alloys |
| Bhat et al, 2019 [SRC] | 50-100 °C in the requirements list; at least 55 °C in the easy-flow paragraph |

**Gold solders.** Mainly gold, silver and copper with small tin, zinc and sometimes phosphorus to adjust fusion temperature and flow (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; gold varies from 45 to 81 wt% (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Carat and fineness.** Fineness is parts of gold per 1000 parts of alloy (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; 18 carat is 750 fine (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; both describe gold content only, not noble-metal content (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. The old solder "carat" number described the alloy the solder was to be used on, not the solder's own gold content (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], so fineness is now used to describe solders (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Easy flow and free flow.** An easy-flowing solder has a relatively low fusion temperature (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; free flow is its ability to spread over the parts (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], governed by surface tension. Lower-fineness solders are more fluid than higher-fineness ones (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] because zinc and tin lower surface tension and melting range.

**Fineness and corrosion: the books lean differently.** Bhat states gold solders need more than 650 fineness for adequate corrosion resistance (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]; Craig and Powers say lower-fineness solders are used extensively without serious tendencies to discolour (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Requirements of a solder:** fusion temperature 50-100 °C below the parts (Craig: at least 56 °C); free flow and wetting; strength comparable to the parts; colour match; tarnish and corrosion resistance; no pitting (Craig and Powers, 2002; McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**Gold solders:** Au-Ag-Cu with small Sn and Zn to lower fusion temperature; gold 45-81 wt%. Described by **fineness** (parts of gold per 1000: 18 carat = 750 fine); the old carat number named the alloy it was for. **Silver solders** for orthodontic work.

## 3. Fluxes and antifluxes

### Explanation

A flux dissolves surface oxides so the molten solder can wet and flow over the parts (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Phillips' classifies fluxes by purpose: Type I surface protection (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], Type II reducing agent (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], Type III solvent (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

For gold alloys the flux is borax with boric acid, sometimes with potassium fluoride (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Bhat calls borax flux protective and reducing (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Chromium-containing alloys need a fluoride flux because borax and boric acid do not dissolve chromium oxides (Craig and Powers, 2002; McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. The flux is chosen by the alloy being soldered, not by the solder (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Too much flux is as bad as too little (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]: Phillips' stresses using the minimum (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], since flux forms a glass with oxides that must be removed (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

An **antiflux** confines solder: graphite from a soft lead pencil (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], or rouge or whiting in alcohol and water for longer or hotter heating (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Phillips' names rouge in chloroform or graphite (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

**Order of application: sources differ, and Bhat contradicts itself.**

| Source | Order |
|---|---|
| Craig and Powers, 2002 [SRC] | Antiflux applied before the flux or solder |
| Bhat et al, 2019, antiflux paragraph [SRC] | Flux first, then antiflux on unwanted areas |
| Bhat et al, 2019, technical considerations [SRC] | Flux applied after applying antiflux |

#### ✎ Exam core

**Flux:** protects the metal from oxidation, reduces or dissolves oxides, and lets solder wet and flow. Types: protective, reducing, solvent (Anusavice et al, undated). Borax + boric acid for gold; fluoride flux for chromium alloys (chosen by alloy, not solder). Excess is trapped; too little burns off.

**Antiflux:** graphite (lead pencil), rouge or whiting, confines solder away from margins (Craig and Powers, 2002).

## 4. Technique

### Explanation

**Investment soldering** (bridges): parts indexed with acrylic or sticky wax and mounted in soldering investment, which holds them during heating (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; Craig and Powers call it the customary method for bridges (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Brazing investments are gypsum-bonded (Type 1) or phosphate-bonded (Type 2) (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and have lower setting and thermal expansion than casting investments (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC], so the parts do not shift. **Free-hand soldering** (orthodontic work): parts held manually while heat and solder are applied (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Gap.** Parts in contact expand and push apart; parts too far apart draw together as solder solidifies (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

| Source | Gap |
|---|---|
| Craig and Powers, 2002 [SRC] | About 0.13 mm for investment soldering (about a business card) |
| Bhat et al, 2019 [SRC] | About 0.1 to 0.3 mm |
| Anusavice et al, undated [SRC] | Never precisely defined; too wide, joint strength is that of the filler; too narrow, flux inclusions and porosity |

Bhat adds that a large gap distorts as the solder contracts (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Heat.** Phillips' rates propane as the best fuel gas (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] and uses the tip of the reducing zone (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; Bhat calls it the blue reducing flame (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. The flame is not withdrawn until soldering is complete (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and the reducing portion makes a protective envelope (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Alternatives: furnace soldering, which heats and cools uniformly and prevents distortion (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; infrared soldering units (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; laser welding in argon for titanium (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Rudolf et al describe reactive multilayer nanofoils as a local heat source (Rudolf et al, 2024) [SRC] that removes the need for a furnace (Rudolf et al, 2024) [SRC]; this is a recent laboratory technique [Likely]. Cast joining, which casts metal into an interlocking region between parts, is an alternative to soldering (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Craig and Powers end by calling soldering an art (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Pre- and post-soldering.** Joining a metal-ceramic framework before porcelain firing is presoldering (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; gold filler metals should not be used to presolder base-metal alloys (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Technique** (Craig and Powers, 2002; Anusavice et al, undated)
- **Investment soldering** (bridges): parts indexed with sticky wax, set in low-expansion soldering investment, fluxed, heated. **Free-hand** (orthodontic wires): parts held by hand.
- **Gap:** about 0.13 mm (Craig) or 0.1-0.3 mm (Bhat); too narrow traps flux and porosity, too wide weakens and distorts.
- **Heat:** propane-oxygen torch, tip of the reducing zone, not withdrawn until the solder has flowed. Also furnace, infrared, laser (titanium).

## 5. Defects of soldered joints

### Explanation

Overheating causes pits, burn-through of thin sections and loss of strength from diffusion or loss of fibrous structure (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; underheating causes pitting from unmelted flux and failure to flow (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and underheated solder "balls" (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. The mechanism of pitting: overheated tin and zinc boil or oxidise (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], or excess flux is trapped (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Diffusion in the joint reduces strength (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Pits collect debris and cause corrosion (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Porosity and flux inclusions can be shown radiographically (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Defects:** pitting (overheating boils off Zn and Sn; underheating traps flux), flux inclusions, burn-through, weakening by diffusion, "balling", distortion (Craig and Powers, 2002; Bhat et al, 2019).

## 6. Cementation of cast restorations

### Explanation

Full procedure for a gold inlay: Q5. Cement classification: Q1. The cement fills the gap between casting and tooth for retention and marginal seal (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], and cement failure is ranked among the most important causes of failure of cast restorations (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. Zinc phosphate retains by friction only (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; resin and RMGI give better retention (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], but no cement compensates for poor retention form (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. The casting's fitting surface is air-abraded (Fan et al, 2013; Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; for resin, tin plating (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] or an alloy primer (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], and a chemically cured cement (Fan et al, 2013; Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. The casting is half-filled (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC] and seated dynamically on a biting stick (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. Zinc phosphate gives the highest hydraulic pressure (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; die spacer and venting relieve it (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Margins are burnished while the cement is soft (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; excess is removed after set to avoid dragging cement from the margins (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], using knotted floss (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], leaving no visible cement line (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Books disagree on how long to hold seating pressure and when to remove excess; see Q5.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Cementation of cast restorations** (Fan et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019)
1. Try-in: fit, contacts, occlusion, margins.
2. Tooth cleaned and isolated; casting fitting surface air-abraded.
3. Cement: zinc phosphate, GIC, RMGI, polycarboxylate; chemically cured resin (with tin plating or metal primer) for poor retention.
4. Seat with finger pressure and a biting stick (dynamic seating); die spacer or venting relieves hydraulic pressure.
5. Burnish margins while soft; remove excess after set; recheck occlusion.

## High-yield points
- Soldering adds a lower-fusing third metal below the parts' solidus; welding melts the parts themselves.
- Dental "soldering" is really brazing (above 425 °C Craig, 450 °C Phillips').
- Fineness = parts of gold per 1000; 18 carat = 750 fine.
- Flux chosen by the alloy: borax for gold, fluoride for chromium alloys.
- Antiflux: graphite, rouge, whiting.
- Investment soldering gap about 0.13 mm (Craig) or 0.1-0.3 mm (Bhat).
- Reducing zone of a propane flame; never withdraw it mid-joint.
- Overheating and underheating both cause pitting.

## Common exam mistakes
- Defining soldering as melting the parts together: that is welding.
- Calling the solder's carat its gold content: the old carat number named the alloy it was for.
- Choosing flux by the solder rather than by the alloy being joined.
- Repeating casting defects (porosity in castings) instead of soldered-joint defects.
- Leaving out the cementation half altogether.
