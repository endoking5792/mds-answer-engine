## 1. Definition, luting space, film thickness and ideal requirements

### Explanation

Sikri defines luting literally as the use of a mouldable substance to seal joints and cement two substances together, and the luting space as the space between two surfaces that is filled with an adhesive film (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. van Noort traces the word: a "lute" is a cement or other material used as a protective covering or an airtight stopping (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Phillips' glossary is shorter: a luting agent is a viscous cement-like material that fills a gap between bonded materials (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson make the useful distinction that luting is essentially a grouting effect, filling the potential gap between a cast restoration and tooth, whereas bonding is adhesion (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's 14th edition gives the two objectives of cementation: to retain the restoration and to maintain the integrity of the remaining tooth structure (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Why a cement is needed at all: even accurate castings leave a gap of about 50-100 µm (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], and the cement both seals this gap and, by filling it, increases friction against sliding (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Film thickness versus cement thickness.** These are different things and examiners like the distinction. Film thickness is a standard test value: the thickness of set cement 10 minutes after a 150 N load has pressed it between flat plates (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; for luting, the maximum allowed is 25 µm (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Cement thickness is the real space between tooth and restoration (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. The limits by standard and the clinical figures differ, and the books do not agree on the clinical figure:

| Max film thickness, ZOE for temporary cementation | 40 µm | (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] |
| Max film thickness, resin cements (ISO 4049) | 50 µm | (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] |

**Ideal requirements.** Pulpal non-irritancy matters, though less than for a liner, because a luting cement usually sits on a thicker layer of residual dentine (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. The mix should have low initial viscosity or be pseudoplastic so the restoration seats fully (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. It should insulate thermally and electrically under metal restorations (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC] and have low solubility, because margins are exposed to oral fluids (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Sikri takes 70 MPa compressive strength as the optimum minimum (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and wants a modulus between that of dentine and the restorative material (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. The cement should not shrink on setting, or voids form at the interface (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; it should be more radiopaque than dentine, so that excess and recurrent caries can be told apart (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; and aesthetics matter under translucent ceramics, where resin cements are the most aesthetic and zinc phosphate, ZOE and polycarboxylate are opaque (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Adhesion is desirable because lack of retention is a major cause of failure of fixed prostheses (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. No one material meets all these requirements (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], which is why several cements remain in use.

#### ✎ Exam core
**Introduction.** Luting is the use of a mouldable substance to seal a joint and cement two surfaces together; the luting space is the gap the cement film fills (Sikri, 2017). Luting "grouts"; bonding adheres (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

**Film thickness** (test value) maximum: 25 µm water-based, 40 µm temporary ZOE, 50 µm resin. **Cement thickness** (clinical gap): 25-120 µm (Anusavice et al, undated; Craig and Powers, 2002; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

**Ideal requirements:** non-irritant; low viscosity or pseudoplastic, thin film; adequate working time, quick set; high strength (≥70 MPa compressive), modulus between dentine and restoration; low solubility and wear; no shrinkage; adhesion and seal; insulation; radiopaque; aesthetic; easy excess removal (McCabe and Walls, 2008; van Noort, 2013; Sikri, 2017). No cement meets all.

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

Craig's 14th edition gives the two classifications that are most useful in an exam. By duration of service, luting agents are provisional or definitive (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. By setting mechanism there are three groups: acid-base reaction cements (glass ionomer, RMGI, ZOE, polycarboxylate, zinc phosphate) (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; cements that set by dissolution and reprecipitation in water (calcium aluminate) (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; and polymerising cements (resin, compomer, self-adhesive resin) (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. The book notes that this classification refers to the predominant setting mechanism, because RMGIs also polymerise and compomers and self-adhesive resins may also have an acid-base reaction (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. van Noort divides luting cements into water-based and resin-based (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], and divides resin cements into three subgroups: aesthetic light/dual-cure composite cements, adhesive chemical/dual-cure resin cements and self-adhesive dual-cure resin cements (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's uses a different scheme, separating "traditional ceramic dental cements" (ZOE family, zinc phosphate, silicate) from "polymer-based dental cements" (polycarboxylate, GIC, RMGI, compomer, composite) (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The standards governing each group are ISO 3107 (ZOE), ISO 9917-1 (acid-base), ISO 9917-2 (resin-modified) and ISO 4049 (polymer-based) (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. The commonly taught "acid-base water-based / acid-base non-aqueous / polymer" split (ZOE being the non-aqueous acid-base cement) is consistent with these books but none of the files prints it under that heading [RECALL] [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Classification** (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; van Noort, 2013)
A. By setting reaction:
1. Acid-base, water-based: zinc phosphate, polycarboxylate, GIC, RMGI.
2. Acid-base chelate (non-aqueous): ZOE, EBA and polymer-reinforced ZOE.
3. Polymerising: resin cements (self, light, dual cure), self-adhesive resins, compomers.
4. Dissolution-reprecipitation: calcium aluminate/glass ionomer.
B. By duration: provisional and definitive.

## 3. Zinc phosphate cement

### Explanation

Zinc phosphate first appeared in 1879 and its long clinical record makes it the standard against which newer cements are judged (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

**Composition.** The powder is mainly zinc oxide (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; magnesium oxide is added to lower the calcination temperature (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and the powder is calcined at 1000-1300 °C for 4-8 hours to reduce its reactivity (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Phillips' gives the powder as more than 75% zinc oxide and up to 13% magnesium oxide (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. The liquid is phosphoric acid buffered with zinc or aluminium oxide (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC], about one-third water (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; buffering tempers its reactivity (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Setting.** The acid dissolves the surface of the alkaline powder in an exothermic reaction (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. What the set matrix is depends on which book you read. Craig's 11th edition describes a hydrated amorphous zinc phosphate network around unreacted zinc oxide (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], with crystalline hopeite able to grow later in excess moisture (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; van Noort says the aluminium prevents crystallisation, giving a glassy alumino-phosphate gel (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's says setting ends in tertiary zinc phosphate crystals forming a crystalline matrix (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Manipulation.** Use a cool slab, 18-24 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], but not below the dew point, because condensed moisture contaminates the mix (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. The powder is added in increments; mixing begins and ends with small ones (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], over 60-90 seconds (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Phillips' advises 15-20 seconds per increment and completion within 1.5-2 minutes (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. A cool slab is the most viable way to extend working time (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. The frozen-slab method takes 50-75% more powder (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and gives a working time of 4-11 minutes with a 20-40% shorter setting time (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Sturdevant's gives a powder/liquid ratio of about 2:1 by weight (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The luting consistency is judged by how far the mix strings from the spatula, and the books give different figures: about 2-3 cm in Craig's 11th edition (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] against 12-19 mm in Phillips' (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. The cement should be loaded on the restoration, at room temperature, rather than the tooth, at 37 °C, to keep working time (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

**Properties.** Net setting time by specification is 2.5-8 minutes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; minimum 24-hour compressive strength 70 MPa (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], two-thirds of which is reached within 1 hour (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Retention is purely mechanical interlocking (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], so a liner under it reduces retention (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] and sandblasting the fitting surface increases it (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. The initial acidity can cause a pulpal response (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Phillips' sums up the disadvantages as pulp irritation, no adhesion, no anticariogenic action and poor mechanical retention (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], but points out that lack of adhesion makes old crowns easier to remove (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Zinc phosphate** (1879; the standard for comparison) (Anusavice et al, undated; Craig and Powers, 2002)
- Powder: ZnO about 90%, MgO about 10% (Ritter et al, 2019), calcined. Liquid: buffered phosphoric acid, one-third water.
- Setting: exothermic acid-base; ZnO cores in zinc phosphate matrix.
- Manipulation: cool slab above dew point; small, large, small increments over a wide area, 60-90 s. Frozen slab extends working time to 4-11 min.
- Properties: setting 2.5-8 min; compressive about 80-110 MPa (books differ); weak in tension; film about 25 µm; initial pH about 3-4.
- Retention purely mechanical. Uses: metal crowns, inlays, bridges, posts, bands. Drawbacks: pulp irritation, no adhesion or fluoride.

## 4. Zinc oxide-eugenol, EBA and non-eugenol cements

### Explanation

ZOE has been used since the 1890s, is low in strength and has a sedative effect on exposed dentine (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. The basic powder has rosin to reduce brittleness and zinc stearate as plasticiser (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Two reinforcements make it strong enough for luting: polymer-reinforced powder of 80% zinc oxide and 20% PMMA (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and EBA-alumina cement with 70% zinc oxide and 30% alumina in the powder (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and a liquid of 62.5% ortho-EBA and 37.5% eugenol (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Craig's 14th edition describes the EBA:eugenol mixture as roughly 2:1 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and explains that EBA works by allowing very high powder/liquid ratios of 6:1 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Setting is a chelation reaction forming amorphous zinc eugenolate (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; moisture is essential (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and the reaction is autocatalytic because water is both reagent and product (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

ISO 3107 (ADA 30) has types for temporary cementation, permanent cementation, filling and bases, and liners (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Phillips' lists the same four types (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Temporary cements must have a compressive strength of at most 35 MPa (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and permanent ones at least 35 MPa (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. EBA-alumina film thickness is 25-35 µm in Craig 11e (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and 16-28 µm in Craig 14e (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Strength falls sharply with temperature: at 37 °C it may be only 20% of the value at 23 °C (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and reinforced ZOEs are the weakest permanent luting cements (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. The zinc eugenolate matrix is unstable in water (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. A clinical comparison gave fixed prosthesis success of 92% at 2.5 years for EBA-alumina (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], 95% for polycarboxylate (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and 98% for zinc phosphate (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

Eugenol inhibits free-radical polymerisation (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], so non-eugenol cements are used when a resin restoration will follow; they do not soften provisional acrylic crowns (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Several in vitro studies found that dentine bond strength is not reduced if the surface is thoroughly cleaned (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. ZOE is also chosen for implant superstructures where retrievability is wanted (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Zinc oxide-eugenol** (Craig and Powers, 2002; Sakaguchi et al, 2019)
- ZnO + rosin; eugenol liquid. Reinforced: 20% PMMA, or EBA-alumina (EBA 62.5%, eugenol 37.5%).
- Sets by chelation to zinc eugenolate; needs moisture.
- ISO 3107: type I temporary (≤35 MPa), II permanent (≥35 MPa), III base, IV liner.
- Sedative, good seal; weakest permanent cement, hydrolyses. Uses: provisional and implant crowns. Eugenol inhibits resin; use non-eugenol before bonding.

## 5. Zinc polycarboxylate cement

### Explanation

Polycarboxylate was developed in the 1960s by Smith (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and was the first cement to bond chemically to teeth (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. The liquid is a 32-42% polyacrylic acid solution (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], molecular weight 25,000-50,000 in Craig 11e (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] against 30,000-50,000 in Phillips' (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. The powder is sintered zinc oxide and magnesium oxide (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; fluoride, where added, is only 15-20% of that released by GICs (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. The set cement is a zinc polyacrylate ionic gel binding unreacted zinc oxide (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. It is mixed at 1:1 to 2:1 (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] within 30-60 seconds (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], is pseudoplastic (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], gives 2.5-6 minutes working time (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and should be used only while glossy (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. It passes through a rubbery stage, so excess should not be pulled away then (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

Bonding is by chelation of carboxylate groups to calcium (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; reported bond strength is 3.4-13 MPa to enamel and 2.1 MPa to dentine (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], but clinical studies have not shown better crown retention (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Sikri adds that retention equals zinc phosphate but the failure site differs: cement-tooth interface for zinc phosphate, cement-metal interface for polycarboxylate (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], and recommends conditioning with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 seconds (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Tensile strength is about 40% higher than zinc phosphate (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Sikri gives compressive 55 MPa and tensile 8-12 MPa (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Zinc polycarboxylate** (Smith, 1960s; first adhesive cement) (Ritter et al, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated)
- ZnO/MgO powder; 32-42% polyacrylic acid. Zinc polyacrylate gel matrix.
- P/L 1:1-2:1, mix 30-60 s; pseudoplastic; use while glossy; avoid rubbery stage.
- Chelates to calcium; compressive about 55 MPa but better tensile; kind to pulp. Uses: single metal units, sensitive teeth.

## 6. Glass ionomer and resin-modified glass ionomer luting cements

### Explanation

**Conventional GIC.** The powder is a calcium fluoroaluminosilicate glass (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], maximum particle size 15 µm for luting grades (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; the liquid is a 47.5% solution of polyacrylic-itaconic copolymer (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], with tartaric acid as accelerator (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Calcium ions react with the polyacid in the first 3 hours (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and aluminium ions for at least 48 hours (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; only 20-30% of the glass is dissolved (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Adhesion is by carboxylate groups displacing calcium and phosphate from hydroxyapatite (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; shear bond is about 2-5 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], enough to improve the seal but not to increase retention significantly (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Powder/liquid ratio is 1.3:1 to 1.35:1 for the viscous liquids (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], mixing time 30-60 seconds (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; working time is about 2 minutes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. The cement must be protected from gain or loss of water in the first 24 hours (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; margins are coated after the initial set at about 7 minutes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. A purpose-made luting grade should be used rather than a thinned restorative mix (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Fluoride release makes it the first choice for orthodontic bands (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], though the exposed area of a lute is small, so the benefit under crowns is less certain (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. It does not bond to high-strength core ceramics (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

Compressive strength differs by book: 90-230 MPa in Craig 11e (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and Sikri (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], 100-150 MPa in Craig 14e (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], 86 MPa in Phillips' table (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] and 120 MPa in Sturdevant's table (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Film thickness is 24 µm in Phillips' table (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] against 25-30 µm in Sikri (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. On postoperative sensitivity the books also part company: Craig 11e says GIC luting cements may cause prolonged hypersensitivity, mild to severe (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; van Noort says it is now generally agreed there is no significant difference from zinc phosphate (van Noort, 2013) [SRC].

**Resin-modified GIC (hybrid ionomer).** It sets by both acid-base and polymerisation reactions (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and lacks the early moisture sensitivity of conventional GIC (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Craig 14e calls it probably the most popular luting cement in clinical use (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], but less biocompatible than GIC because of HEMA (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], a known contact allergen (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Compressive strength is 90-140 MPa with a low modulus of 3-6 GPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] (Phillips' table: 85-185 MPa, film 11-22 µm (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]); film thickness 2 minutes after mixing is 9-25 µm (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and bond to conditioned dentine 8-12 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. The contested point is all-ceramic crowns. Craig 14e lists high-strength zirconia crowns among its indications (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] but notes some manufacturers advise against silica-based ceramic crowns because of fracture from cement swelling (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; Craig 11e reports delayed fracture of ceramics cemented with hybrid ionomers (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; van Noort considers RMGIC contraindicated for all-ceramic restorations until more clinical evidence exists (van Noort, 2013) [SRC] and warns that posts cemented with RMGIC are very hard to remove (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri lists hygroscopic expansion among reasons for its good marginal adaptation (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and limits it to low-stress single units (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Glass ionomer** (Craig and Powers, 2002; Sakaguchi et al, 2019)
- Fluoroaluminosilicate glass (≤15 µm); polyacrylic/itaconic acid with tartaric acid.
- Acid-base: calcium then aluminium polyacrylate; protect from water gain/loss 24 h (varnish).
- P/L about 1.3:1, mix 30-60 s, working time about 2 min.
- Chemical bond, fluoride, low acid erosion; compressive 90-230 (Craig 11e) versus 100-150 MPa (Craig 14e).
- Uses: metal, metal-ceramic and zirconia crowns, posts, orthodontic bands.

**RMGI:** acid-base plus polymerisation (HEMA). Less moisture-sensitive, bond 8-12 MPa, low sensitivity; but HEMA allergy, water sorption and expansion: avoid under silica-based ceramics (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; van Noort, 2013).

## 7. Resin cements, self-adhesive resin cements and compomers

### Explanation

**Conventional resin cements** are low-viscosity composites whose filler and initiators are adjusted for low film thickness (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. They are mandatory for luting low-strength ceramic and laboratory composite restorations (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. ISO 4049 classes them by cure: class 1 self-cured, class 2 light-cured, class 3 dual-cured (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Filler is 30-66% by volume (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Adhesive monomers such as MDP bond to tooth, ceramics and alloys through phosphate groups (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; the 4-META cement uses MMA with tri-n-butylborane catalyst (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; phosphonate cements are oxygen-sensitive, so the margins are covered with a gel (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and Sikri notes that this oxygen inhibition of Panavia is what eases removal of excess (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Film thickness is 13-20 µm (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and polymerisation shrinkage 2-5% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; shrinkage stress beyond the bond strength opens a contraction gap (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Compressive strength is reported as 180-265 MPa in Craig 11e (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], 180-300 MPa in Craig 14e (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and 70-172 MPa in Phillips' table (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Resin cements are the least soluble cements (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Amines in self-cure systems discolour with time (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and are inactivated by acidic adhesives (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; for that reason light-cured cements without tertiary amines are used under veneers (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC], and Sikri advises against auto- or dual-cure cements for veneers and translucent crowns (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Phillips' says dual-cure cement should not be relied on under light-transmitting prostheses thicker than 2.5 mm (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] and describes water-soluble try-in pastes matched to cement shades (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Overall, resin cements are mechanically better but more technique sensitive (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Self-adhesive resin cements** put etching, priming and bonding into one material (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Their acidic monomers are mostly phosphate or phosphonate based (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and the mixed cement starts at about pH 2 to etch the tooth (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Compressive strength is 200-240 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and film thickness 15-20 µm (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Postoperative sensitivity is reported as low (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], but cytotoxicity is higher than resin and acid-base cements (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and the benefit of their fluoride is unproven (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Enamel bond is weaker, so separate phosphoric-acid etching of substantial enamel margins is often recommended (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; van Noort does not recommend them for ceramic veneers (van Noort, 2013) [SRC] or orthodontic brackets (van Noort, 2013) [SRC].

**Compomer (polyacid-modified composite) cement** (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] sets by polymerisation, with an acid-base reaction once oral fluid is absorbed (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Cementation of all-ceramic restorations is, with exceptions, contraindicated (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Excess is removed at the gel stage about 1 minute after seating (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Phillips' table gives setting 4.0 minutes, film thickness 36 µm and compressive strength 165 MPa (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Calcium aluminate/glass ionomer sets partly by dissolution and reprecipitation as katoite (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Resin cements** (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated)
- Bis-GMA/UDMA, 30-66 vol% silanated filler; adhesive monomers 4-META, MDP.
- ISO 4049: self-cure, light-cure (veneers, amine-free), dual-cure (crowns, inlays, posts).
- Strongest (180-300 MPa), least soluble, film 13-20 µm, aesthetic; but shrinkage 2-5%, technique sensitive, needs etch-and-bond.
- Mandatory for glass-ceramics, veneers, indirect composite; poorly retentive preparations.

**Self-adhesive resin:** acidic phosphate monomers (pH about 2) etch and bond in one step; low sensitivity; weak enamel bond.

**Compomer:** polymerises, then slow acid-base reaction; metal-ceramic only (Craig and Powers, 2002).

## 8. Mechanisms of retention

### Explanation

Craig's 14th edition states that retention comes from friction (micromechanical interlocking), from an adhesive joint, or both (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate, GIC, RMGI, calcium aluminate and self-adhesive resins can interact chemically with hydroxyapatite (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], but in daily practice adhesion is mainly micromechanical (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. With non-adhesive cements, tags of set cement plus correct taper prevent displacement (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC], and polished surfaces are hard to lute (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. van Noort places the main burden on preparation geometry, path of insertion and mechanical keying (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri likewise lists preparation geometry first among determinants of retention (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. For the restoration side: silica-based ceramics are etched with hydrofluoric acid and silanised (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], which does not work on alumina or zirconia (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; on zirconia, MDP-type primers and self-adhesive cements form Zr-O-P bonds, particularly after alumina air abrasion (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; base metals are grit-blasted with 30-50 µm alumina (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; laboratory composites are sandblasted, not HF-etched (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Mechanisms of retention** (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008)
1. Mechanical: cement tags in surface irregularities; depends on taper and fit (zinc phosphate, ZOE).
2. Micromechanical: resin in etched enamel, hybridised dentine, HF-etched ceramic, sandblasted metal.
3. Chemical: carboxylate-calcium chelation (polycarboxylate, GIC); phosphate monomers to calcium and metal oxides (MDP); silane to silica ceramics.

## 9. Comparison and selection

### Explanation

Phillips' Table 14-2 gives typical values (setting time min; film thickness µm; 24-h compressive strength MPa; diametral tensile MPa; modulus GPa; solubility wt%; pulp response). Zinc phosphate: 5.5, 20, 104, 5.5, 13.5, 0.06, moderate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate: 6.0, 21, 55, 6.2, 5.1, 0.06, mild (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. GIC: 7.0, 24, 86, 6.2, 7.3, 1.25 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Sturdevant's Table 6.4 gives compressive strengths of 48 (reinforced ZOE), 65 (EBA), 160 (zinc phosphate), 70 (polycarboxylate), 120 (GIC), 148-180 (RMGI) and 170-190 MPa (composite) (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and film thickness 32, 18, 21 and 24 µm for R-ZOE, zinc phosphate, polycarboxylate and GIC (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Solubility rankings depend on the test and the books disagree. Phillips' (aqueous cements) ranks solubility rising GIC < zinc phosphate < polycarboxylate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Sikri ranks dissolution least for resin, then GIC, polycarboxylate and zinc phosphate (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Craig 11e reports a clinical order, least to most soluble, of GIC, zinc phosphate, EBA-ZOE, polyacrylate (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and Phillips' in situ crown study found GIC degraded least, then polycarboxylate, then zinc phosphate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Phillips' concludes the clinical relevance of in vitro solubility data is unclear (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Pulpal inflammation rises in the order polycarboxylate, zinc phosphate, GIC per Phillips' (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Sikri ranks modulus highest for zinc phosphate and lowest for RMGI (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Different situations need different cements and no single material suits every case (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; Phillips' advises keeping several (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

## 10. Cementation of indirect restorations: general steps

### Explanation

Phillips' describes luting a crown in three steps: cement placement, seating and removal of excess (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Before that, the internal surface of the restoration must be clean and free of films (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], and the casting is protected with a gauze throat screen (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The die spacer, die relief or etching of the casting reduce seating discrepancies (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Sturdevant's isolates with cotton rolls and saliva ejector (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and applies cement to the preparation side of the casting (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips' fills about half the crown volume (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson apply it sparingly to the axial walls (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls prefer loading the restoration, which is at room temperature, to preserve working time (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Seat promptly while viscosity is low (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; full crowns are hardest to seat because cement escapes least (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Seat with moderate finger pressure (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], then have the patient bite on a cotton roll or wooden stick (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; Sturdevant's has the patient bite on a rubber disk over the casting (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and burnishes accessible margins while cement is soft (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Maintain pressure or vent the crown (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; venting minimises film thickness and hydraulic pressure (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], and Sikri holds seating force for at least three minutes (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Taper aids seating: McCabe and Walls give an ideal convergence of 10° (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC], and Phillips' lists lower viscosity, more taper and lower height as making seating easier (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

Excess removal depends on cement. Zinc phosphate and ZOE do not adhere to surrounding surfaces (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] and are removed after setting, whereas adhesive cements should be removed as soon as seating is complete (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; a knotted floss is passed interproximally immediately after seating (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Sturdevant's removes hardened excess with explorer and air-water spray (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri warns against running a probe over set cement at the margins (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Finally varnish or bonding agent is painted on accessible margins (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], because the cement layer is the weakest link of the assembly (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Ceramic-specific surface treatment and resin bonding steps belong to the all-ceramic protocol in ceramics/conservative_dentistry Q11.

#### ✎ Exam core
**Cementation steps** (Anusavice et al, undated; Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2017)
1. Remove provisional; clean preparation.
2. Try-in: fit, margins, contacts, occlusion, shade (try-in paste); throat screen.
3. Treat restoration: clean; sandblast metal; HF + silane or MDP primer for ceramics (see ceramics Q11).
4. Isolate; treat tooth: varnish (zinc phosphate), conditioner (GIC), etch-and-bond (resin).
5. Mix; coat axial walls of restoration thinly.
6. Seat promptly with finger pressure; patient bites on cotton roll; hold until set.
7. Remove excess: knotted floss at once; zinc phosphate/ZOE after set, adhesive cements at gel stage.
8. Varnish margins; check occlusion; radiograph.

**Conclusion:** no luting cement is ideal; choose by restoration, retention and moisture control.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: self-adhesive resin cements make luting simpler without loss of performance.** The textbooks agree they bond to dentine without a separate adhesive and cause little postoperative sensitivity (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; van Noort, 2013) [SRC], but also that their enamel bond is weaker and enamel margins should often be etched (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Clinical data point the same way: in a split-mouth RCT of partial ceramic crowns, 6.5-year Kaplan-Meier survival was 60% without and 82% with selective enamel etching (Baader et al, 2016) [LIT], and a meta-analysis of nine RCTs found no difference in postoperative sensitivity between self-adhesive and other protocols but lower survival than selective-etch and self-etch protocols (Alghauli et al, 2022) [LIT]. What follows: self-adhesive cements are convenient for retentive preparations, but for adhesion-dependent ceramic restorations, enamel etching or a separate adhesive is the safer choice [Likely].

**Claim 2: GIC luting cement causes postoperative sensitivity.** Craig 11e reports prolonged hypersensitivity, mild to severe (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; van Noort says there is now general agreement that GIC and zinc phosphate do not differ (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; Craig 14e notes that the risk rises with faulty technique (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and that thin mixtures seem to increase it (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. One small single-blind trial (50 patients) found more sensitivity at 7 days with GIC than with a self-adhesive resin, but none at 24 hours (Shetty et al, 2012) [LIT; abstract only]. The evidence is thin and short term; technique, not the material alone, is the safer explanation [Guessing].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

- Film thickness (test, ≤25 µm water-based; ≤50 µm resin) is not the same as cement thickness (clinical, 25-120 µm).
- Classify by setting reaction: acid-base water-based, acid-base chelate (ZOE), polymerising, dissolution-reprecipitation.
- Zinc phosphate: cool slab, incremental mixing, mechanical retention only, initial pH about 3-4.
- Polycarboxylate: first adhesive cement (Smith); use while glossy; rubbery stage.
- RMGI: popular, but avoid under silica-based all-ceramic crowns (expansion).
- Resin: mandatory for glass-ceramics and indirect composite; ISO 4049 self/light/dual cure.
- Self-adhesive resin: one step, pH about 2, weak on enamel.
- Remove excess: zinc phosphate/ZOE after set; adhesive cements immediately or at gel stage.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

- Writing "film thickness of 25 µm" as the clinical cement gap; it is a test limit for water-based cements only.
- Saying zinc phosphate bonds chemically; its retention is purely mechanical.
- Recommending RMGI under feldspathic or glass-ceramic crowns.
- Using eugenol-containing temporary cement before resin bonding without mentioning cleaning or a non-eugenol alternative.
- Quoting one compressive strength for a cement as fact; the books disagree widely, so give a range.

## MNEMONIC

**"Phosphate Eugenol Carboxylate Glass Resin"**, in historical order: zinc **P**hosphate (1879), Zinc oxide-**E**ugenol, poly**C**arboxylate (1960s), **G**lass ionomer and RMGI, **R**esin and self-adhesive resin; strength and adhesion broadly rise along the list, ease of handling falls.
