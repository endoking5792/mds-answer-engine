## 1. Comparison table

### Explanation

The composition and setting rows follow Phillips' Table 14-1: zinc phosphate is zinc oxide and magnesium oxide powder with phosphoric acid and water, setting by acid-base reaction (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; polycarboxylate replaces the phosphoric acid with polyacrylic acid (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; glass ionomer is fluoroaluminosilicate glass with polyacrylic/polyprotic carboxylic acid (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; resin-modified glass ionomer adds water-soluble methacrylate monomer and an activator, setting by polymerisation plus acid-base reaction (Anusavice et al, undated; Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; resin cement is methacrylate monomers with fillers and initiators (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], setting by free-radical polymerisation (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

The numerical rows are Phillips' Table 14-2, read row by row: zinc phosphate 5.5 min setting, 20 µm film, 104 MPa compressive, 5.5 MPa diametral tensile, 13.5 GPa modulus, 0.06 wt% water solubility, moderate pulp response (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; polycarboxylate 6.0, 21, 55, 6.2, 5.1, 0.06, mild (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; glass ionomer 7.0, 24, 86, 6.2, 7.3, 1.25, mild to moderate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; resin-modified glass ionomer 4.25, 11-22, 85-185, 18-26, 2.5-7.8, 1.00, mild to moderate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; resin 2.0-4.0, <25, 70-172, 40-77, 2.1-3.1, 0-0.01, moderate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Phillips' adds that the values come from mixed sources and vary widely between brands, so the table is a typical profile, not a specification [Certain]. Its "ISO 9917" row prints 25 µm maximum film and 0.20 wt% maximum solubility (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

## 2. Composition, setting and bonding

### Explanation

Zinc phosphate bonds only mechanically (Anusavice et al, undated; Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; van Noort calls it nothing more than a space filler, a grout (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate was the first cement to bond chemically to tooth (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], and glass ionomer bonds by chelating calcium in apatite (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], at about 2-5 MPa in shear (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; this improves the seal but not enough to raise retention significantly (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Clinically, polycarboxylate has not shown better crown retention than zinc phosphate (Craig and Powers, 2002; Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; Sikri explains that it fails at the cement-metal interface whereas zinc phosphate fails at the cement-tooth interface (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Glass ionomer bonds to alloys including titanium (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Resin-modified glass ionomer bonds at 8-12 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], 10-14 MPa (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] or 14 MPa to moist dentine (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]; resin cement with an adhesive gives 12-18 MPa immediate shear bond (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. The progression from mechanical grout to adhesive lute is the main conceptual line of the comparison [Certain].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Bonding.** Zinc phosphate is a mechanical grout (van Noort, 2013). Polycarboxylate and GIC chelate calcium weakly (GIC about 2-5 MPa), improving seal more than retention (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). RMGI (8-14 MPa) and resin with a bonding agent (12-18 MPa) bond strongly, suiting short preparations and weak ceramics (Craig and Powers, 2002; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

Modulus (GPa): zinc phosphate 13.5 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] or about 12 (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; glass ionomer 7.3 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], around 15 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], 5.4 (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC] or 20 (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; resin 2.1-3.1 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], 4-10 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] or 1.2-10.7 (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Rank order is more consistent than the numbers: Craig and Powers place glass ionomer's modulus below zinc phosphate but above polycarboxylate (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], polycarboxylate being about one third of zinc phosphate (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], though its tensile strength is about 40% higher (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Phillips' reads glass ionomer's lower modulus, about half of zinc phosphate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], as a drawback under all-ceramic crowns (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], while noting that resin's lower modulus does not seem to matter (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Fracture toughness: conventional luting GIC 0.27-0.37, resin-modified luting GIC 0.79-1.08, resin 1.30 MPa·m½ (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Strength and stiffness.** Resin and RMGI are strongest and toughest (toughness: GIC 0.27-0.37, RMGI 0.79-1.08, resin 1.30 MPa·m½), polycarboxylate weakest in compression (Anusavice et al, undated). Figures differ between books: GIC compressive strength 86 MPa (Anusavice et al, undated), 100-150 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) and 90-230 (Craig and Powers, 2002); resin 70-172 against 180-300 MPa. Zinc phosphate is stiffest (about 12-13.5 GPa); GIC's lower modulus makes it a poorer support for weak ceramic crowns (Anusavice et al, undated).

## 4. Film thickness, working time and handling

### Explanation

Film thickness (µm), as printed: zinc phosphate 20 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] or "25 maximum" (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; polycarboxylate 21 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], 25-48 (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], 21-35 commercially (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC], 22-28 (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; glass ionomer 24 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], 22-24 (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], 25-30 (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; resin-modified 11-22 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], 10-22 (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], 9-25 at 2 minutes after mixing (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; resin 13-20 (Craig and Powers, 2002; Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], against an ISO 4049 ceiling of 50 µm (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate has about 2.5 minutes' working time against 5 for zinc phosphate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; once dull it becomes stringy and too thick to seat (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; its viscosity starts higher than zinc phosphate but a 2-minute delay reverses this (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Zinc phosphate's working time is best extended on a cool slab (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Glass ionomer sets in 6-8 minutes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Film thickness and handling.** All can meet the ISO limit (25 µm; resin 50 µm) if seated promptly (Craig and Powers, 2002). Polycarboxylate is reported at 21 µm (Anusavice et al, undated) to 25-48 µm (Craig and Powers, 2002) and has the shortest working time, about 2.5 against 5 min for zinc phosphate (Anusavice et al, undated).

## 5. Solubility and disintegration

### Explanation

Resin cements are the least soluble (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], virtually insoluble in oral fluids (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; resin-modified glass ionomer's water solubility is about two to four times that of resin (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], though it shows no measurable lactic acid erosion (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Conventional glass ionomer is the most soluble in water tests (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] (1.25 wt% in Phillips' table, above), yet clinically it is the least soluble, ahead of zinc phosphate, EBA-ZOE and polyacrylate (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and least degraded in situ (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; Phillips' summary credits it with greater resistance to disintegration than zinc phosphate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. ISO acid-erosion ceilings: zinc phosphate 0.1 mm/hr (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] or 1.0 mm/hour (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; polycarboxylate 2.0 mm/hr (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; glass ionomer 0.05 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Solubility.** Resin is virtually insoluble and RMGI low (Anusavice et al, undated). GIC looks most soluble in water tests (1.25 wt%) yet disintegrates least clinically, ahead of zinc phosphate and polycarboxylate (Craig and Powers, 2002). ISO erosion limits: GIC 0.05, polycarboxylate 2.0 mm/hr; zinc phosphate 0.1 (Craig and Powers, 2002) or 1.0 (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## 6. Biological behaviour, fluoride and aesthetics

### Explanation

Zinc phosphate stays acidic, its pH rising from about 3 to about 6 over 24 hours (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Polycarboxylate is gentle because its large polyacid molecules limit acid penetration into tubules (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], and it is chosen for patients with a history of postoperative sensitivity (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Glass ionomer's pulpal reaction lies between ZOE and zinc phosphate (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], but its luting form may cause prolonged hypersensitivity (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Resin-modified glass ionomer is less biocompatible than conventional GIC because of HEMA (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Fluoride: glass ionomer is first choice for orthodontic bands (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; conventional and resin-modified luting ionomers released similar amounts in vitro, 99-198 ppm over 180 days (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; polycarboxylate releases only 15-20% of glass ionomer's (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; the clinical significance of fluoride from luting cements is unconfirmed (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Aesthetics: resin is most aesthetic; zinc phosphate and polycarboxylate are opaque (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Biological and fluoride.** Zinc phosphate stays acidic for hours (pH about 3 rising to 6) and needs pulp protection over thin dentine (Anusavice et al, undated); polycarboxylate is the mildest, suited to sensitive teeth. RMGI releases HEMA (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). GIC and RMGI release fluoride and are preferred for orthodontic bands, though the benefit under crowns is unproven (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

## 7. Clinical selection

### Explanation

Zinc phosphate has long been the universal luting cement (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] but is unsuitable where aesthetic demand is high (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; its lack of adhesion eases removal of old crowns (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. One clinical report quoted in Craig's gives fixed-prosthesis success rates of 98% for zinc phosphate and 95% for polycarboxylate (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]; this is a single study reported second-hand, not a comparative trial of all five [Likely]. Resin-modified glass ionomer is now the most popular luting class (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] and is indicated for metal-ceramic and high-strength zirconia crowns (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC], but water-induced expansion of about 3% contraindicates it for unreinforced all-ceramic crowns (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Craig's and Powers and Wataha describe the same swelling risk (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Resin is mandatory for low-strength ceramic and laboratory composite restorations (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC] because it reduces ceramic fracture and offers shades (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Glass ionomer works well for orthodontic bands (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Selection.** Zinc phosphate: well-fitting metal and metal-ceramic crowns, with a long clinical record (Anusavice et al, undated). Polycarboxylate: single units in sensitive teeth. GIC: metal crowns, orthodontic bands. RMGI: metal-ceramic and zirconia crowns, posts; avoid weak all-ceramics because water uptake expands it about 3% (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Resin: mandatory for low-strength ceramics and composite inlays (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

## High-yield points

- Zinc phosphate = mechanical grout; polycarboxylate first chemically adhesive cement; GIC adds fluoride; RMGI adds polymerisation; resin bonds via adhesive.
- Resin and RMGI are toughest; polycarboxylate weakest in compression and shortest working time.
- Zinc phosphate stiffest; low-modulus GIC is a poor support for weak ceramics.
- Resin least soluble; GIC "most soluble" only in water tests.
- RMGI expansion (about 3%) contraindicates it for unreinforced all-ceramic crowns.
- Textbook strength and modulus figures disagree widely: quote the source.

## Common exam mistakes

- Writing a table without any interpretation; "compare" needs the contrasts explained.
- Quoting one book's compressive strength as fact when the books differ by a factor of two.
- Stating that polycarboxylate's chemical bond gives better crown retention than zinc phosphate; clinical studies do not show this.
- Recommending RMGI for unreinforced (low-strength) all-ceramic crowns.
- Calling GIC the most soluble luting cement without the clinical caveat.
