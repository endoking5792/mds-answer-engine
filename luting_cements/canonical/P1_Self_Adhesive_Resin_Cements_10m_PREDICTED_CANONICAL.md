## 1. Concept and definition

### Explanation

A self-adhesive resin cement puts the etching, priming and bonding chemistry into the cement itself (Braga, 2019) [SRC], so no extra clinical step is needed for bonding (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Phillips' defines "self-adhesive" as adhering to tooth without a dentine or enamel bonding agent (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. van Noort stresses that no pretreatment of the tooth is required, while bonding is also claimed to base-metal alloys and a range of ceramics (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's likewise says no pretreatment of the substrate (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and likens the chemistry to self-etching dentine primers (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

The aim is simplicity: one step, like a water-based cement, but with resin strength and adhesion. The first generation was powder-liquid, hand- or capsule-mixed (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; current products are automixed two-paste systems (Braga, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Resin luting cements that incorporate the etching, priming and bonding chemistry in one material, so they bond to tooth without separate etchant or adhesive (Braga, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated). Developed to simplify adhesive cementation and reduce operative errors (Alvarenga et al, 2024). Supplied first as powder-liquid capsules, now as automix paste-paste (e.g. RelyX Unicem, Maxcem Elite, SpeedCEM, Panavia SA).

## 2. Composition: the acidic functional monomers

### Explanation

What distinguishes them is one or more acid-functional monomer in one paste, which etches tooth while copolymerising with other monomers for cohesive strength (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Most use phosphate or phosphonate monomers (Braga, 2019) [SRC]: Phenyl-P, 10-MDP, bis(2-methacryloxyethyl) acid phosphate and Penta-P (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; some use carboxylic monomers such as 4-META and pyromellitic glycerol dimethacrylate (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. RelyX Unicem's monomer carries two phosphoric acid groups and two C=C bonds (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; MaxCem uses GPDM and SmartCem2 uses PENTA (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Phillips' describes them as dimethacrylates with acidic pendant side chains (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

The other paste holds non-acidic monomers and a small amount of acid-neutralising fluoroaluminosilicate glass (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Filler is about 70% by mass (about 50% by volume), lower than in restorative composites (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha summarise: diacrylate resins with acidic and adhesive groups plus glass filler (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Composition** (Braga, 2019; van Noort, 2013)
- Paste 1: acidic functional monomers: phosphate/phosphonate (10-MDP, Phenyl-P, Penta-P, GPDM) or carboxylic (4-META); plus Bis-GMA, UDMA, HEMA, photoinitiator.
- Paste 2: non-acidic dimethacrylates; basic fluoroaluminosilicate glass (neutraliser); inert filler.
- Filler about 70 wt% (about 50 vol%), lower than composites.
- Acid-resistant initiator system (amines fail in acid).

## 3. Setting reaction and neutralisation

### Explanation

Curing is free-radical polymerisation, self-cured or dual-cured (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. The mixed cement starts at about pH 2 so it can etch tooth mineral (Braga, 2019; van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; the acid groups etch enamel or dentine in the manner of self-etch adhesives while reacting with the filler (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Later the remaining acidity is neutralised by acid-base reaction with the alkaline glass (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; van Noort says the pH rises rapidly to neutral, with some fluoride release (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], and Sturdevant's gives a rise from 1 to 5 or 6 (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The set structure is a cross-linked polymer, with some ionic bridging between carboxyl groups and glass ions (Braga, 2019) [SRC].

The chemistry needed a new initiator: alkaline amines used for self-cure become inactive in acid (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], as Craig's also notes for amines with acidic adhesives (Braga, 2019) [SRC], so each product carries its own acid-resistant amine/peroxide system (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. This is the reason the self-cure mode lags behind light activation [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Setting** (Braga, 2019; van Noort, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019)
1. Mix: pH about 2; acidic monomers demineralise smear layer, enamel and dentine.
2. Free-radical polymerisation, light and/or chemical (dual-cure).
3. Neutralisation: acid groups react with basic glass filler; pH rises towards neutral (1 to 5-6); fluoride released.
4. Set cement: cross-linked polymer with some ionic bridging.

## 4. Bonding mechanism

### Explanation

Bonding is by micromechanical interlocking plus chemical interaction of the acid groups with hydroxyapatite (Braga, 2019) [SRC]: phosphate and carboxylate groups bind calcium (Braga, 2019) [SRC], forming ionic bonds with Ca2+ in van Noort's account (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's adds that this chemical interaction varies with the cement and curing mode (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The interaction is shallow: no significant infiltration into dentine (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] and only a superficial interaction with root dentine (Broome and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], so there is no true hybrid layer of the etch-and-rinse kind [Likely]. To the restoration, the phosphate monomers bond to metal oxides and zirconia (see P3).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Bonding mechanism**
- Micromechanical: shallow etching of smear layer and dentine (no deep hybrid layer or resin tags) (Anusavice et al, undated).
- Chemical: phosphate/carboxyl groups bond ionically to calcium of hydroxyapatite (Braga, 2019; van Noort, 2013), and to metal oxides and zirconia.

## 5. Properties

### Explanation

Lab values (not clinical evidence): flexural strength 50-100 MPa and compressive strength 200-240 MPa (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; film 15-20 µm (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Mechanical properties are somewhat lower than conventional resin cements (Braga, 2019) [SRC], with low-to-medium bond strengths (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Light activation raises the properties (Powers and Wataha, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Enamel shear bond strength is 3-15 MPa, between resin cements and glass ionomers (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; bond to metal and high-strength ceramics is good (Braga, 2019) [SRC].

Postoperative sensitivity is low (Braga, 2019) [SRC], because dentine is not etched with phosphoric acid (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Cytotoxicity is higher than conventional resin and acid-base cements (Braga, 2019) [SRC], though one study found no adverse pulp response (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Unreacted acid groups raise water sorption (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; fluoride release falls quickly (Braga, 2019) [SRC] and has no proven clinical benefit (Braga, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Properties** (Braga, 2019; Powers and Wataha, 2013)
- Compressive 200-240 MPa, flexural 50-100 MPa; film 15-20 µm.
- Somewhat weaker than conventional resin cements; higher when light-activated.
- Enamel bond only 3-15 MPa; dentine bond acceptable; good to alloys and zirconia.
- Low postoperative sensitivity (no phosphoric acid on dentine).
- Higher water sorption; short-lived fluoride release; higher cytotoxicity.

## 6. Limitations and uses

### Explanation

The main weakness is enamel: in vitro, non-self-adhesive cements bond better to enamel (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] and the enamel bond falls short of etch-and-rinse or self-etch adhesives (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], so with substantial enamel margins phosphoric acid selective etching is often recommended (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's says the weak acid makes them unsuitable for enamel preparations (Rouse, 2013) [SRC], though they reduce the contamination risk (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; bond strengths vary between systems (Rouse, 2013) [SRC] and some lose strength sharply in the first months (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Bond to etched glass-matrix ceramics is lower than with traditional resin cements (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. They are not for veneers (van Noort, 2013; Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] or orthodontic brackets (van Noort, 2013; Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

Indications: cast alloy crowns and bridges, metal-ceramic, ceramic (except veneers) and indirect composite (Braga, 2019) [SRC], posts and high-strength ceramics (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; most indirect restorations (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. They are popular for oxide (zirconia) ceramics (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Fibre posts: the books disagree.** van Noort says they have become particularly popular for posts (van Noort, 2013) [SRC] and Summitt's adhesion chapter lists them as an option (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's cites a systematic review of in vitro studies suggesting they may improve fibre-post retention (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] with better sealing (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Craig's calls laboratory results promising (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's post chapter says they are generally less effective than etch-and-rinse systems (Broome and Robbins, 2013) [SRC] and that weak bonds and no long-term clinical data limit their use (Broome and Robbins, 2013) [SRC].

**Clinical evidence (PubMed, abstracts only).** A meta-analysis of nine RCTs found no significant difference in failure of single-tooth indirect restorations between self-adhesive and conventional cements (Alvarenga et al, 2024) [LIT]. A prospective study of 152 metal-ceramic crowns reported 97.6% survival up to six years (Brondani et al, 2017) [LIT]. For CAD/CAM partial ceramic crowns, however, a split-mouth RCT found lower survival with self-adhesive cement than with a universal adhesive plus luting composite at 39 months (69% vs 88-96%) (Scholz et al, 2021) [LIT] and at 12 years (36.4% vs 60.7-80.8%) (Pfister et al, 2025) [LIT]; selective enamel etching raised 15-year survival of self-adhesive-luted partial crowns (78.1% vs 42.9%) (Pfister et al, 2023) [LIT]. Reading: acceptable for retentive full-coverage crowns, weaker for non-retentive, enamel-bordered bonded restorations [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Limitations**
- Weak enamel bond: selectively etch enamel margins with phosphoric acid (Braga, 2019).
- Lower bond to etched glass-ceramics; not for veneers (aesthetics) or orthodontic brackets (van Noort, 2013).
- Product variation; some lose strength over months (Rouse, 2013).
- Partial ceramic crowns: lower long-term survival than adhesive + resin cement in RCTs (Scholz et al, 2021; Pfister et al, 2025).

**Uses:** metal and metal-ceramic crowns and bridges, zirconia and alumina restorations, indirect composites, posts (fibre-post use is contested in textbooks) (Braga, 2019; Ritter et al, 2019).

**Conclusion:** a meta-analysis of RCTs shows failure rates similar to conventional cements for single crowns (Alvarenga et al, 2024); for bonded partial restorations conventional adhesive luting remains preferable.

## High-yield points
- All-in-one: etch + prime + bond inside the cement; no tooth pretreatment.
- Acidic phosphate monomers (10-MDP, Phenyl-P, Penta-P, GPDM; RelyX Unicem: two phosphate + two methacrylate groups).
- pH about 2 at mixing, neutralised by basic glass filler.
- Bond: shallow micromechanical + ionic to calcium; no deep hybrid layer.
- Enamel is the weak substrate: selective enamel etch.
- Low postoperative sensitivity; light-activate to maximise properties.
- Not for veneers or orthodontic brackets.

## Common exam mistakes
- Confusing self-adhesive cements (no adhesive at all) with self-etch adhesive resin cements (separate self-etch primer).
- Claiming a hybrid layer like etch-and-rinse systems.
- Recommending them for porcelain veneers or brackets.
- Ignoring the enamel-margin problem and selective enamel etching.
- Presenting in vitro post-retention data as clinical proof.
