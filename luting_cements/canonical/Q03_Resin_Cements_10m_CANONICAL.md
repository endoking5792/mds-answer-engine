## 1. Definition, history and classification

### Explanation

Resin cements are low-viscosity composites whose filler distribution and initiator content are adjusted to give a low film thickness and workable setting times (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; Phillips' calls them low-viscosity versions of restorative composites (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], and Sturdevant's notes the same components as restorative composites with slightly lower filler (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Methyl methacrylate resin cements have been available since 1952, and a composite crown-and-bridge cement appeared in the early 1970s (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Sikri records that the early chemically cured materials fell out of favour because of polymerisation shrinkage, pulpal irritation, microleakage and poor handling (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

The classification is easiest to remember on three axes, because each answers a different clinical question.

**(a) By curing mode (ISO 4049):** class 1 self-cured, class 2 light-cured, class 3 dual-cured (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Most commercial products are dual-cured (Braga, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

**(b) By adhesive approach to tooth:** Phillips' divides cements used with a separate bonding agent into etch-and-rinse and self-etch adhesive resin cements, according to the bonding system (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; self-adhesive cements need no separate step (see P1). A dentine bonding agent is otherwise needed for adhesion to dentine (Anusavice et al, undated; Braga, 2019) [SRC].

**(c) By chemistry and use (van Noort):** aesthetic light/dual-cure composite cements; adhesive chemical/dual-cure resin cements; self-adhesive dual-cure resin cements (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. The aesthetic group has no intrinsic adhesion (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; the adhesive group bonds chemically to prepared metal without a separate promoter (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], for example Panavia 21 (MDP) (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], and 4-META cements need no separate bonding agent for that purpose (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Low-viscosity resin composites with filler and initiators adjusted for low film thickness, used to lute indirect restorations (Braga, 2019). MMA resin cements date from 1952; composite cements from the early 1970s (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Classification**
- Curing mode (ISO 4049): class 1 self-cure, class 2 light-cure, class 3 dual-cure; most products are dual-cure (Braga, 2019).
- Adhesive approach: etch-and-rinse, self-etch, self-adhesive (Anusavice et al, undated).
- Chemistry (van Noort, 2013): aesthetic composite cements; adhesive cements (MDP, 4-META); self-adhesive cements.

## 2. Composition and setting

### Explanation

The matrix uses the composite monomers: high-molecular-weight Bis-GMA, UDMA and Bis-EMA (Braga, 2019) [SRC] diluted with ethylene glycol dimethacrylates such as TEGDMA to get high conversion with relatively low shrinkage (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Filler is 30% to 66% by volume (Braga, 2019) [SRC] of silanated radiopaque barium, strontium or zirconia glasses with silica (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Adhesive products add functional monomers: MDP, a polymerisable phosphoric acid ester, with Bis-GMA (Braga, 2019) [SRC], or 4-META with MMA liquid, PMMA powder and tri-n-butylborane catalyst (Braga, 2019) [SRC].

The initiators explain the two curing paths. Camphorquinone and a tertiary amine sit in one paste for light activation (Braga, 2019) [SRC], and benzoyl peroxide in the catalyst paste for self-cure (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. The amine is the weak link: it degrades and shifts the shade (Braga, 2019) [SRC], and it is inactivated by acidic adhesives (Braga, 2019) [SRC], which is why acidic simplified adhesives and self-cured cement are a poor pairing [Likely].

Setting is free-radical addition polymerisation, giving a densely cross-linked polymer around the filler (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; without light the radicals come from the amine-peroxide redox reaction (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Final conversion is about 70% (Braga, 2019) [SRC] and is higher when a dual-cure cement is light-activated than when it self-cures (Braga, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Composition** (Braga, 2019)
- Matrix: Bis-GMA, UDMA, Bis-EMA, diluted with TEGDMA.
- Filler: silanated barium/strontium/zirconia glass and silica, 30-66 vol%.
- Initiators: camphorquinone + tertiary amine (light); benzoyl peroxide + amine (self-cure); both in dual-cure two-paste systems.
- Adhesive monomers: MDP (phosphate ester), 4-META (with TBB catalyst).
- Pigments, opacifiers.

**Setting:** free-radical polymerisation (light or amine-peroxide redox) to a cross-linked matrix around filler; conversion about 70%, higher when dual-cure cement is light-activated (Braga, 2019).

## 3. Properties

### Explanation

Books disagree on the numbers, so quote them side by side and do not reconcile:

| Property | Craig's 14th ed (Braga, 2019) [SRC] | Phillips' 12th ed Table 14-2 (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC] |
|---|---|---|
| Compressive strength | 180-300 MPa (dual- and light-cured) | 70-172 MPa (24 h) |
| Flexural / diametral tensile | flexural 80-100 MPa | diametral tensile 40-77 MPa |
| Elastic modulus | 4-10 GPa | 2.1-3.1 GPa |
| Film thickness | 13-20 µm (ISO method) | <25 µm |
| Solubility | much lower than RMGIC | 0-0.01 wt% |

Sturdevant's gives an achieved clinical film of 50-100 µm for cements in general (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], a different measurement from the ISO value.

Shrinkage is 2% to 5% (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Immediate shear bond strength to dentine is 12-18 MPa (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; MDP bonds to tooth, ceramics and alloys through its phosphate group (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha give over 20 MPa to primed tooth and alloy (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Solubility is minimal: "virtually insoluble" (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC], and the lowest of all luting cements in Sikri's ranking (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Two weaknesses matter clinically: the cement line may discolour with time (Braga, 2019) [SRC], and polymerisation stress can debond the interface and open a contraction gap (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Monomers are cytotoxic (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; Phillips' advises a liner when remaining dentine is under 0.5 mm (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Microfilled cements wear less than microhybrid ones (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Properties** (Braga, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated)
- Compressive strength 180-300 MPa, superior to acid-base cements (Craig's 14e) vs 70-172 MPa (Phillips' 12e).
- Elastic modulus 4-10 GPa (Craig's) vs 2.1-3.1 GPa (Phillips').
- Film thickness 13-20 µm (Craig's), <25 µm (Phillips').
- Solubility near zero (0-0.01%), lowest of all cements.
- Bond to dentine 12-18 MPa with adhesive; MDP bonds to tooth, alloy, ceramic.
- Shrinkage 2-5%; stress may open marginal gaps.
- Cement-line discolouration with time; monomers cytotoxic, liner if dentine <0.5 mm.

## 4. Advantages, disadvantages and uses

### Explanation

Craig's sums up the trade-off: better mechanical properties than acid-base cements, but a more technique-sensitive procedure (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; Sturdevant's says the best laboratory properties of all cements but more complicated procedures (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's lists add low film thickness, high compressive and tensile strength, lowest solubility, compatibility with aesthetic restorations and controlled working time (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], against postoperative sensitivity and technique sensitivity (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. They are the most aesthetic luting materials (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri adds that their higher viscosity may tilt castings (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and that auto-polymerising resins can be less radiopaque than dentine (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

Uses range from inlays to bridges, posts and orthodontic appliances (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. They are mandatory for low-strength ceramic and laboratory composite restorations and useful on castings where extra retention is needed (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; Phillips' calls resin the material of choice for all-ceramic restorations (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Light-cured types are for veneers and brackets (Braga, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; chemically activated types such as Panavia and C&B Metabond suit metal restorations (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages:** high strength, lowest solubility, low film thickness, bond to tooth and restoration, best aesthetics, controlled working time (Sikri, 2017; Braga, 2019).

**Disadvantages:** technique sensitive, moisture control essential, polymerisation shrinkage stress, postoperative sensitivity, difficult excess removal, cement-line discolouration (Braga, 2019; Sikri, 2017).

**Uses:** all-ceramic and indirect composite inlays, onlays, crowns (mandatory for low-strength ceramics); veneers (light-cure); resin-bonded bridges and castings needing extra retention (adhesive MDP/4-META cements); fibre and metal posts; orthodontic brackets (Braga, 2019; Anusavice et al, undated).

## 5. Key handling points

### Explanation

Choose the curing mode by light access. Chemically cured cement suits all restorations (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]; dual-cure is for inaccessible margins where light cannot reach (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], but Phillips' says light-transmitting restorations thicker than 2.5 mm need chemical-cure cement (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Dual-cure cement should always be light cured, because light raises conversion (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri records lower microhardness in the first 24 hours when the light step was omitted (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], and Sturdevant's warns some "dual-cure" products barely polymerise without light (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. For veneers and translucent crowns avoid auto- and dual-cure cements (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] because amine accelerators such as para-toluidine discolour (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Summitt's agrees light-cure is more colour stable (Rouse and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Water-soluble try-in pastes guide shade (Braga, 2019) [SRC]. Eugenol temporary cement must be avoided beforehand because residual eugenol inhibits setting (Anusavice et al, undated) [SRC]. Excess must be removed before full set (Braga, 2019) [SRC]; Panavia's oxygen sensitivity eases this (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], and some adhesive resin cements need a gel barrier to exclude oxygen for complete setting (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Handling**
1. Rubber dam isolation; no eugenol provisional cement (inhibits polymerisation) (Anusavice et al, undated).
2. Treat tooth (etch/bond) and restoration (HF + silane, sandblast + MDP primer).
3. Shade with try-in paste.
4. Dual-cure for thick or opaque restorations, always light-cured; chemical cure beyond 2.5 mm; light-cure for veneers (amine discolours) (Anusavice et al, undated; Sikri, 2017).
5. Remove excess at gel stage; oxygen-barrier gel where the product requires it; final cure.

## High-yield points
- ISO 4049: class 1 self-cure, class 2 light-cure, class 3 dual-cure; most products dual-cure.
- MDP (phosphate) and 4-META (carboxylic, TBB catalyst) give chemical adhesion to base-metal oxides.
- Amines degrade (colour shift) and are inactivated by acidic adhesives.
- Strength figures differ by book: 180-300 MPa (Craig's 14e) vs 70-172 MPa (Phillips' 12e).
- Lowest solubility of all luting cements; but shrinkage stress and technique sensitivity.
- Light-cure for veneers; dual-cure always light-activated; chemical cure under thick restorations.
- Eugenol provisional cement inhibits resin set.

## Common exam mistakes
- Writing only one classification; give curing mode, adhesive approach and chemistry.
- Quoting a single compressive strength as fact when books differ by more than 100 MPa.
- Saying dual-cure cement needs no light.
- Recommending dual- or self-cure cement for thin veneers (colour instability).
- Forgetting that eugenol provisional cements inhibit resin setting.
