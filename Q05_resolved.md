## STUDY MODE

### S1. How the parts connect [basic science]
Composition decides setting, and setting decides properties and handling. The powder is an ion-leachable glass, the liquid is a polymeric acid in water; mixing starts an acid-base reaction in which the acid frees calcium (then aluminium) ions that cross-link the polyacid chains (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020). The powder:liquid ratio and the glass particle size then decide which clinical job the cement is suited to, which is the logic of classification.

### S2. Classification [classification]
The standard system is Wilson and McLean (used in Mount's work): Type I luting, Type II restorative (II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced), Type III lining and base (Mount, 1994). Sikri also lists a classification by McLean (traditional, resin-modified, polyacid-modified composite), by application (adding Type IV fissure sealant, V orthodontic, VI core build-up) and a newer one (traditional, metal-modified, light-cure, hybrid) (Sikri, 2020). Only the Wilson and McLean system is needed for full marks; the others add detail.

### S3. Composition and manufacture [basic science]
- **Powder:** calcium (or strontium) fluoroaluminosilicate glass, silica about 29%, alumina 16.6%, calcium fluoride about 34% in Mount and Hume's and Craig's tables (Mount et al, 2016; Craig, 2002); Sikri's table gives different ranges (silica 35-50%, alumina 20-30%) (Sikri, 2020). Fluoride is a flux and an oxide scavenger in the melt (Mount et al, 2016). Strontium or lanthanum give radiopacity (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Manufacture:** the ingredients are fused at about 1,100-1,500 C and the molten glass is plunged into water, which cracks it so it can be ground and sieved to 4-50 micrometres (Sikri, 2020; Bhat et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016). Reactivity depends on fusion temperature and heat history (Mount et al, 2016). Restorative cements use coarser glass; luting and lining cements finer (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Liquid:** a 40-55% aqueous solution of acrylic-itaconic or acrylic-maleic copolymer, with tartaric acid 5-15% (Mount et al, 2016). Itaconic acid keeps the liquid from gelling and lowers viscosity; tartaric acid sharpens the set and extends working time (Sikri, 2020).
- **Anhydrous (water-settable) cements:** the polyacid is vacuum-dried and mixed into the powder; the liquid is water or dilute tartaric acid (Mount et al, 2016).

### S4. Properties [treatment relevance]
Working time is 2-3 minutes, thermal expansion is close to dentine, solubility is high in the first 24 hours, and pH starts near 2 and rises to 6-7 (Sikri, 2020). Adhesion is chemical, fluoride is released and recharged, and the cement is brittle and moisture-sensitive (Q4, Q16, Q10 give detail) (Sikri, 2020). Biocompatibility is acceptable because the large polyacid molecules do not travel down dentinal tubules (Sikri, 2020).

### S5. Manipulation [treatment]
Isolate, prepare, condition, dispense, mix, place under a matrix, protect, finish after 24 hours (Sikri, 2020). The aim of mixing is only to wet the powder particles; a plastic spatula and a cooled slab help (Sikri, 2020).

### S6. Cavity preparation [treatment]
GIC needs minimal preparation because it bonds: remove caries, keep sound tooth, and do not break proximal contacts (Sikri, 2020). Preparation follows the lesion, and the outline is dictated by caries, discolouration and aesthetics (Sikri, 2020). For lesions in low-load sites, GIC is the first choice, and a stronger auto-cure cement is used if the load is heavier (Mount et al, 2016).

## EXAM MODE

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement (glass polyalkenoate cement) is a water-based cement formed by acid-base reaction between ion-leachable fluoroaluminosilicate glass powder and an aqueous polyalkenoic acid (Mount et al, 2016). It was introduced by Wilson and Kent in the early 1970s (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Classification
**A. Wilson and McLean (Mount and Hume's version):**
| Type | Use | Powder:liquid ratio | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | luting crowns, bridges, inlays, orthodontic appliances | about 1.5:1 | fast set, thin film (Mount, 1994) |
| II.1 | aesthetic restorative (auto or dual cure) | 2.8:1 to 6.8:1 | translucent, not for heavy load (Mount, 1994) |
| II.2 | reinforced restorative | 3:1 to 4:1 | fast set, immediate trimming, radiopaque (Mount, 1994) |
| III | lining and base | lining 1.5:1; base 3:1 or more | thermal barrier, dentine substitute (Mount, 1994) |
**B. By McLean:** traditional GIC, resin-modified GIC, polyacid-modified composite (compomer) (Sikri, 2020).
**C. By application:** adds Type IV fissure sealant, Type V orthodontic cement, Type VI core build-up (Sikri, 2020).
**D. By modification:** traditional, metal-modified, light-cure, hybrid (Sikri, 2020).

### 3. Composition
- **Powder:** silica, alumina, calcium fluoride, sodium fluoride, aluminium fluoride, aluminium phosphate; strontium or lanthanum for radiopacity (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Liquid:** 40-55% copolymer of polyacrylic and itaconic (or maleic) acid, tartaric acid 5-15%, water (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Function of each:** silica gives translucency and hardness, alumina forms the aluminium polyacrylate matrix, fluoride lowers fusion temperature and releases to tooth, itaconic acid prevents gelling, tartaric acid sharpens the set (Bhat et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).

### 3A. Powder constituents and what each does (Bhat)
| Constituent | Approximate weight | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Silica | 29% | hardness and translucency; does not react (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Alumina | 16.5% | forms the aluminium polyacrylate matrix (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Calcium fluoride | 34.3% | flux; fluoride release (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Aluminium fluoride | 7.3% | flux, anticariogenic (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Sodium fluoride | 3% | flux (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Aluminium phosphate | 9.9% | controls setting time (Bhat et al, 2019) |

### 4. Manufacture
Fuse the ingredients at about 1,100-1,500 C, plunge the melt into water (shock cooling), grind and sieve to 4-50 micrometres; restorative powders about 50 micrometres, luting about 20 micrometres (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Properties
- **Chemical:** adhesion by ion exchange; fluoride release and recharge (Sikri, 2020).
- **Physical:** working time 2-3 minutes; thermal expansion close to dentine; opaque (about 90%) (Sikri, 2020).
- **Mechanical:** compressive strength 16,000-22,000 psi; tensile 900-960 psi; hardness 48 KHN; brittle (Sikri, 2020).
- **Biological:** initial pH about 2 rising to 6-7; mild pulp irritant; protect very deep dentine with calcium hydroxide (Sikri, 2020).
- **Weaknesses:** moisture sensitive, high early solubility, poor aesthetics (Sikri, 2020).

### 5A. Setting in brief and advantages and disadvantages
- **Setting:** acid attacks the glass; calcium ions released first form calcium polysalts (initial set); aluminium ions follow and form a tighter network over 24 hours; full reaction can take up to 7 days (Sikri, 2020).
- **Advantages:** chemical adhesion, fluoride release, biocompatibility, low solubility after the full set, easy manipulation (Sikri, 2020).
- **Disadvantages:** opaque surface, surface cracks, debonding when poorly manipulated, technique sensitivity, high early solubility, poor strength, unsuitable for large lesions (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Manipulation
1. Isolate with rubber dam or cord (Sikri, 2020).
2. Condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds (Mount) or 10-20 seconds (Sikri); wash; dry lightly (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
3. Dispense: fluff the powder, level the scoop, dispense liquid without bubbles (Sikri, 2020).
4. Mix: plastic spatula, two halves, first half wet in 10-15 seconds, finish in about 30 seconds (Sikri, 2020).
5. Slump test: cement should break and slump back; working time 60-90 seconds (Sikri, 2020).
6. Place under a matrix, avoiding voids (Sikri, 2020).
7. Seal at once with resin bonding agent; varnish washes away (Sikri, 2020).
8. Finish after 24 hours with Sof-lex discs in moist conditions (Sikri, 2020).

### 7. Uses
Luting; Class III and V restorations; root caries; deciduous teeth; liner and base; sandwich technique; ART and transitional restorations; core build-up in limited cases; fissure sealant; orthodontic bands (Sikri, 2020).

### 8. Cavity preparation
- **Class III:** access through the lingual marginal ridge with a small inverted-cone bur; preserve labial enamel (Sikri, 2020).
- **Class V:** outline by caries, discolouration and aesthetics; retention grooves on occlusal and gingival walls if the lesion is wide (Sikri, 2020).
- **Proximal lesions:** do not break the contact; keep unsupported enamel; gingival extent set by the lesion, controlling sulcular fluid (Sikri, 2020).
- **Occlusal lesions:** minimal cavity; GIC is the best initial restoration, laminated with composite or amalgam if the load is heavy (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Surface treatment:** pumice slurry, then conditioning (Mount, 1994).

### 9. Conclusion
Classification follows use (luting, restorative, lining), composition is glass plus polyacid, and success depends on powder:liquid ratio, moisture control and surface protection.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Types: I luting (1.5:1), II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced (3-4:1), III lining and base (Mount, 1994).
- Powder: calcium fluoroaluminosilicate glass; liquid: 40-55% polyacrylic-itaconic copolymer with tartaric acid 5-15% (Mount et al, 2016).
- Fusion about 1,100-1,500 C, shock cooled, ground to 4-50 micrometres (Sikri, 2020).
- Condition 10 s with 10% polyacrylic acid; never acid-etch; seal at once; finish at 24 h (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- Minimal cavity: keep contacts, no undercuts needed (Sikri, 2020).
