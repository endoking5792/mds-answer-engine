## STUDY MODE

### S1. How the parts connect [basic science]
Composition decides setting, and setting decides properties and handling. The powder is an ion-leachable glass, the liquid is a polymeric acid in water; mixing starts an acid-base reaction in which the acid frees calcium (then aluminium) ions that cross-link the polyacid chains (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020). The powder:liquid ratio and the glass particle size then decide which clinical job the cement is suited to, which is the logic of classification.

### S2. Classification [classification]
The standard system is Wilson and McLean (used in Mount's work): Type I luting, Type II restorative (II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced), Type III lining and base (Mount, 1994). The powder:liquid ratios in the exam table come from Mount (1994); Mount and Hume (2016) give 3:1 or greater for both II.1 and II.2, 1.5:1 for Type I and for linings, and a Type I film thickness of 20 micrometres or less against 25 micrometres in the Atlas (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994). Sikri also lists a classification by McLean (traditional, resin-modified, polyacid-modified composite), by application (adding Type IV fissure sealant, V orthodontic, VI core build-up) and a newer one (traditional, metal-modified, light-cure, hybrid) (Sikri, 2020). Only the Wilson and McLean system is needed for full marks; the others add detail.

### S3. Composition and manufacture [basic science]
- **Powder:** calcium (or strontium) fluoroaluminosilicate glass, silica about 29%, alumina 16.6%, calcium fluoride about 34% in Mount and Hume's and Craig's tables (Mount et al, 2016; Craig, 2002); these tables list cryolite 5.0% and aluminium fluoride 5.3% with no sodium fluoride, whereas Bhat's table (Exam Mode 3A) lists aluminium fluoride 7.3% and sodium fluoride 3% (Mount et al, 2016; Bhat et al, 2019). Sikri's table gives different ranges (silica 35-50%, alumina 20-30%) (Sikri, 2020), and Garg and Garg give a third set (silica 41.9%, alumina 28.6%, aluminium fluoride 1.6%, calcium fluoride 15.7%, sodium fluoride 9.3%, aluminium phosphate 3.8%), with particle size 20 micrometres for luting to 50 for restorative products (Garg and Garg, 2011). Fluoride is a flux and an oxide scavenger in the melt (Mount et al, 2016). Strontium or lanthanum give radiopacity (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Manufacture:** the ingredients are fused at about 1,100-1,500 C (Sikri, 2020; Anusavice et al, 2013), though Bhat gives 1,000-1,500 C (Bhat et al, 2019) and Bonsor 1,150-1,450 C (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). The molten glass is plunged into water, which cracks it so it can be ground and sieved to 4-50 micrometres (Mount et al, 2016). Reactivity depends on fusion temperature and heat history (Mount et al, 2016). Bonsor adds that manufacturers wash the glass in acetic acid for up to 24 hours (passivation) so that fewer surface ions are available at once and the working time is usable (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Restorative cements use coarser glass; luting and lining cements finer (Mount et al, 2016); sizes differ by book (see Exam Mode 4).
- **Liquid:** a 40-55% aqueous solution of acrylic-itaconic or acrylic-maleic copolymer, with tartaric acid 5-15% (Mount et al, 2016). Itaconic acid keeps the liquid from gelling and lowers viscosity; tartaric acid sharpens the set and extends working time (Sikri, 2020).
- **Anhydrous (water-settable) cements:** the polyacid is vacuum-dried and mixed into the powder; the liquid is water or dilute tartaric acid (Mount et al, 2016).

### S4. Properties [treatment relevance]
Working time is about 1.5 minutes after a 30 s mix (Bhat et al, 2019) to 2-3 minutes (Sikri, 2020), depending on product and book; Sikri's slump-test paragraph itself gives 60-90 seconds for traditional cement (Sikri, 2020). Thermal expansion is close to dentine, solubility is high in the first 24 hours, and pH starts near 2 and rises to 6-7 (Sikri, 2020). Adhesion is chemical, fluoride is released and recharged, and the cement is moisture-sensitive (Q4, Q16, Q10 give detail) (Sikri, 2020); it is brittle (Bhat et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016). Biocompatibility is acceptable because the large polyacid molecules do not travel down dentinal tubules (Sikri, 2020).

### S5. Manipulation [treatment]
Isolate, prepare, condition, dispense, mix, place under a matrix, protect, and finish after 24 hours for conventional cement (Sikri, 2020). The aim of mixing is only to wet the powder particles; Sikri prefers a plastic spatula and says a cooled glass slab prolongs working time (Sikri, 2020). Books differ on handling. Bonsor calls for vigorous spatulation with an agate spatula on a glass slab and says a plastic spatula is inappropriate (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); Phillips advises a stiff spatula for restorative use, a slab not colder than the dew point, half the powder in 5-15 seconds and a total mix under 45 seconds (Anusavice et al, 2013); Craig (11th ed.) says cold-slab mixing (3 C) can extend working time to 9 minutes but lowers strength and is not recommended (Craig, 2002). Phillips adds that a glossy mix has free polyacid for bonding and a dull one has reacted too much (Anusavice et al, 2013). For the matrix, Phillips covers the surface for about 5 minutes and then varnishes before dismissal because it stays vulnerable to dehydration for a few weeks (Anusavice et al, 2013); Bonsor says protection from water for at least an hour is essential and a barrier can protect for up to 24 hours (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Finishing: Bonsor says finishing may start at the maker's time (3 to 6 minutes) but ideally waits 24 hours, and that a fine water spray does the least damage while petroleum jelly on the instrument is detrimental (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); Sikri advises finishing in moist conditions with Sof-lex discs (Sikri, 2020). Varnish is for the surface of the cement only: Garg and Garg say varnish is contraindicated under glass ionomers because it interferes with bonding, and Singh says it interferes with chemical adhesion and impairs fluoride uptake, so if needed it is applied only after the cement is placed (Garg and Garg, 2011; Singh, 2020).

### S6. Cavity preparation [treatment]
GIC needs minimal preparation because it bonds: remove caries, keep sound tooth, and do not break proximal contacts (Sikri, 2020). Preparation follows the lesion, and the outline is dictated by caries, discolouration and aesthetics (Sikri, 2020). For an advanced occlusal lesion, GIC is the best initial restoration, laminated with composite or amalgam if the occlusal load is heavy; the strongest cement available is used, preferably auto-cure (Mount et al, 2016). Under a liner or base, Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed (adapted from the 7th ed) limits a liner to 0.5 mm, uses calcium hydroxide only as a very thin liner over the deepest part, and says it should be overlaid with a resin-modified GIC base (Ritter et al, 2019). For core build-up (Type VI), Mount (1994) allows minor deficiencies with 2-3 mm of sound tooth, Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says reinforced glass ionomers (Equia, Fuji IX) should not be used for core build-ups but only as bases and block-out (Ritter et al, 2019), Goldstein lists conventional glass ionomer only for blocking out undercuts (Goldstein et al, 2018), and a fatigue study quoted by Badr et al had all glass ionomer core crowns fail at 20,000 cycles (Badr et al, 2016).

## EXAM MODE

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement is a water-based cement formed by acid-base reaction between ion-leachable fluoroaluminosilicate glass powder and an aqueous polyalkenoic acid (Mount et al, 2016). It was reported by Wilson and Kent in 1972 in most textbooks (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020), 1971 in Sikri Ch.1, 1969 in Hill (2007); ASPA, the first product, 1976 (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### 2. Classification
**A. Wilson and McLean (ratios from Mount, 1994):**
| Type | Use | Powder:liquid ratio | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | luting crowns, bridges, inlays, orthodontic appliances | about 1.5:1 | fast set, thin film (Mount, 1994) |
| II.1 | aesthetic restorative (auto or dual cure) | 2.8:1 to 6.8:1 (Mount, 1994); 3:1 or greater (Mount et al, 2016) | translucent, not for heavy load (Mount, 1994) |
| II.2 | reinforced restorative | 3:1 to 4:1 (Mount, 1994); 3:1 or greater (Mount et al, 2016) | fast set, immediate trimming, radiopaque (Mount, 1994) |
| III | lining and base | lining 1.5:1; base 3:1 or more | thermal barrier, dentine substitute (Mount, 1994) |
**B. By McLean:** traditional GIC, resin-modified GIC, polyacid-modified composite (compomer) (Sikri, 2020).
**C. By application:** adds Type IV fissure sealant, Type V orthodontic cement, Type VI core build-up (Sikri, 2020).
**D. By modification:** traditional, metal-modified, light-cure, hybrid (Sikri, 2020).

### 3. Composition
- **Powder:** silica, alumina, calcium fluoride, sodium fluoride, aluminium fluoride, aluminium phosphate; strontium or lanthanum for radiopacity (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Liquid:** 40-55% copolymer of polyacrylic and itaconic (or maleic) acid, tartaric acid 5-15%, water (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Function of each:** see table 3A (Bhat et al, 2019); itaconic acid prevents gelling and tartaric acid sharpens the set (Sikri, 2020).

### 3A. Powder constituents and what each does (Bhat)
| Constituent | Approximate weight | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Silica | 29% | hardness and translucency; does not react (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Alumina | 16.5% | forms the aluminium polyacrylate matrix (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Calcium fluoride | 34.3% | flux; fluoride release (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Aluminium fluoride | 7.3% | flux, anticariogenic (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Sodium fluoride | 3% | flux (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Aluminium phosphate | 9.9% | controls setting time (Bhat et al, 2019) |


### 4. Manufacture
Fuse the ingredients at about 1,100-1,500 C (Sikri, 2020), plunge the melt into water (shock cooling), grind and sieve to 4-50 micrometres (Mount et al, 2016). Luting powder is about 20 micrometres (Sikri, 2020), about 15 (Anusavice et al, 2013) or 10 or less (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Properties
- **Chemical:** adhesion by ion exchange; fluoride release and recharge (Sikri, 2020).
- **Physical:** working time about 1.5 minutes after a 30 s mix (Bhat et al, 2019) to 2-3 minutes (Sikri, 2020), depending on product and book; thermal expansion close to dentine; opaque (about 90%) (Sikri, 2020).
- **Mechanical:** compressive strength 16,000-22,000 psi (about 110-152 MPa), hardness 48 KHN (Sikri, 2020); 70-220 MPa for Type II.1 (Mount et al, 2016); Type II 196-251 MPa, 87-177 KHN (Anusavice et al, 2013); depends on product and test. Brittle (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Biological:** initial pH about 2 rising to 6-7; mild pulp irritant; protect very deep dentine with calcium hydroxide (Sikri, 2020).
- **Weaknesses:** moisture sensitive, high early solubility, poor aesthetics (Sikri, 2020).

### 5A. Setting in brief and advantages and disadvantages
- **Setting:** acid attacks the glass; calcium ions released first form calcium polysalts (initial set); aluminium ions follow and form a tighter network over 24 hours; full reaction can take up to 7 days (Sikri, 2020).
- **Advantages:** chemical adhesion, fluoride release, biocompatibility, low solubility after the full set, easy manipulation (Sikri, 2020).
- **Disadvantages:** opaque surface, surface cracks, technique sensitivity, high early solubility, poor strength (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Manipulation
1. Isolate with rubber dam or cord (Sikri, 2020).
2. Condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds (Mount et al, 2016) or 20% polyacrylic acid for 10-20 seconds (Sikri, 2020); wash and dry lightly (Mount et al, 2016).
3. Dispense: fluff the powder, level the scoop, dispense liquid without bubbles (Sikri, 2020).
4. Mix: plastic spatula, two halves, first half wet in 10-15 seconds, finish in about 30 seconds (Sikri, 2020).
5. Slump test: cement should break and slump back; Sikri's slump-test text gives 60-90 seconds working time (Sikri, 2020).
6. Place under a matrix, avoiding voids (Sikri, 2020); while glossy (Anusavice et al, 2013).
7. Seal at once: resin bonding agent (Sikri, 2020) or varnish (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; McCabe and Walls, 2008); Sikri notes varnish may allow some transmission of water (Sikri, 2020). Varnish goes over the set cement, never beneath it (Garg and Garg, 2011; Singh, 2020).
8. Finish conventional cement after 24 hours with Sof-lex discs in moist conditions (Sikri, 2020); Type II.2 immediately after initial set (Mount, 1994); light-cured resin-modified cement immediately (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### 7. Uses
Luting; Class III and V restorations; root caries; deciduous teeth; liner and base; sandwich technique; ART; core build-up (Type VI), for minor deficiencies only (Mount, 1994), though Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says reinforced glass ionomers should not be used for core build-ups (Ritter et al, 2019); fissure sealant; orthodontic bands (Sikri, 2020).

### 8. Cavity preparation
- **Class III:** access through the lingual marginal ridge with a small inverted-cone bur; preserve labial enamel (Sikri, 2020).
- **Class V:** outline by caries, discolouration and aesthetics; a groove on the occlusal or gingival wall can be given if the lesion is wide (Sikri, 2020).
- **Proximal lesions:** do not break the contact; keep unsupported enamel; gingival extent set by the lesion, controlling sulcular fluid (Sikri, 2020).
- **Occlusal lesions:** minimal cavity; GIC is the best initial restoration, laminated with composite or amalgam if the load is heavy (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Surface treatment:** pumice slurry, then conditioning (Mount, 1994).

### 9. Conclusion
Classification follows use (luting, restorative, lining), composition is glass plus polyacid, and success depends on powder:liquid ratio, moisture control and surface protection.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Types: I luting (1.5:1), II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced, III lining and base (Mount, 1994).
- Powder: calcium fluoroaluminosilicate glass; liquid: 40-55% polyacrylic-itaconic copolymer with tartaric acid 5-15% (Mount et al, 2016).
- Fusion about 1,100-1,500 C, shock cooled, ground to 4-50 micrometres (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).
- Conditioner: 10% polyacrylic acid 10 s (Mount et al, 2016) or 20% for 10-20 s (Sikri, 2020); seal at once; finish conventional cement at 24 h (Sikri, 2020).
- Minimal cavity: keep contacts; no mechanical undercut (Mount, 1994); optional groove in wide Class V (Sikri, 2020).
