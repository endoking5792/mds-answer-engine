## 1. Introduction and definitions

### Explanation

**What resorption is.** Cohen's defines dental resorption as "the loss of dental hard tissues as a result of clastic activities" and stresses that in the permanent dentition it is pathological and, untreated, may cost the tooth (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala call it a physiological or pathological process in which dentine and/or cementum are lost to hard tissue resorbing cells (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; the AAE Glossary uses the widest wording, a physiologic or pathologic loss of dentin, cementum and/or bone (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Sikri adds the idea of continuity: "the progressive loss of dentin and cementum through the continuous action of osteoclastic cells" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Levin opens by calling root resorption "a rare occurrence in permanent teeth" (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. The definitions differ only in emphasis: the Glossary includes bone and physiological loss, Sikri builds "progressive" into the definition and so excludes transient surface resorption, which other authors count as resorption. [Likely]

**Physiological versus pathological.** Shedding of primary teeth is physiological; in permanent teeth resorption is pathological (Patel et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Two qualifications matter. Endodontology says external surface resorption "is found in all teeth, in varying degrees, and is probably a normal physiologic response" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC], and the Glossary also defines surface resorption as "a physiologic process" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Levin takes the narrower view that "Only resorption of primary teeth during exfoliation is considered physiologic" (Levin, 2012) [SRC], and Sikri likewise calls resorption in the primary dentition physiological and in the permanent dentition pathological (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. So the books draw the line differently: Endodontology and the Glossary by whether the process is self-limiting, Levin and Sikri by whether the tooth is primary or permanent. [Likely]

**The two basic sites.** By location relative to the root surface, root resorption is classified as external or internal (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. The resorbing cell is the odontoclast, defined by the Glossary as "a multinucleated giant cell responsible for resorption of tooth structure", analogous to the osteoclast (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC].

**Why it matters.** Progressive resorption may remain asymptomatic until advanced (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; internal resorption "is often confused with external cervical resorption", and an inaccurate diagnosis may result in inappropriate treatment (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. The treatments differ: root canal treatment arrests some types, does nothing for others and may even harm a healing tooth. The whole answer is built on that one clinical point. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Root resorption is the loss of dental hard tissue (cementum and dentine) as a result of clastic cell activity (Patel et al, 2021). The AAE Glossary defines resorption as a physiologic or pathologic process resulting in loss of dentin, cementum and/or bone (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). Sikri describes it as a progressive loss through the continuous action of osteoclastic cells (Sikri, 2019).

**Physiological:** shedding of primary teeth; external surface resorption, small, self-repairing and probably present in all teeth (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
**Pathological:** any progressive resorption of a permanent tooth; untreated, it may lead to loss of the tooth (Patel et al, 2021).

**Site.** External (starting from the periodontium on the root surface) or internal (starting from the pulp on the canal wall) (Patel et al, 2021). The responsible cell is the odontoclast, a multinucleated giant cell analogous to the osteoclast (American Association of Endodontists, 2012).

**Clinical importance.** It is usually asymptomatic and found on a routine radiograph; internal and external types look alike on a periapical film; and the correct treatment depends on the type, so diagnosis comes before treatment (Patel et al, 2021; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

## 2. Why permanent roots resist resorption, and the mechanism

### Explanation

**The puzzle.** Ingle's points out that a permanent tooth lives its whole life inside alveolar bone full of active osteoclasts "without being approached by any of these two cell lines, under normal conditions"; the protection probably comes from intact cementoblast and odontoblast cell layers (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

**The protective layers.** Several overlapping explanations are offered, and Levin stresses that they are not mutually exclusive (Levin, 2012) [SRC].
1. *Unmineralised surface layers.* Externally the cementum is covered by cementoblasts over a zone of unmineralised cementoid; internally dentine is covered by predentine (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Osteoclasts do not adhere to nonmineralised collagen matrices (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC].
2. *Missing binding sites.* Clastic cells attach through the αvβ3 integrin to RGD-containing proteins such as osteopontin; osteopontin acts as a linker between apatite in denuded dentine and the cell (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's suggests that the lack of RGD peptides in predentine reduces odontoclast binding (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Levin adds the observation that even in extensive external resorption the circumpulpal dentine next to the predentine is spared (Levin, 2012) [SRC].
3. *Inhibitors.* Ng and Gulabivala describe an anti-invasion factor secreted by predentine, precementum and intact periodontal ligament (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; Levin names amelogenin and osteoprotegerin as intrinsic inhibitors (Levin, 2012) [SRC].
4. *Intermediate cementum.* The innermost cementum layer is a barrier between dentinal tubules and the periodontal ligament (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala call it the hyaline layer of Hopewell-Smith (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Once it is lost, bacterial products from an infected canal reach the ligament.
5. *Remnants of Hertwig's epithelial root sheath* surrounding the root "like a net" is a further hypothesis whose protective role Levin says is not established (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

**The cell.** Osteoclasts are, in Ingle's words, "a syncytium of stimulated macrophage progenitor cells" (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. The active cell seals onto the mineral with a clear zone around a ruffled border and resorbs in an acidic compartment (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Odontoclasts are smaller, have fewer nuclei and smaller sealing zones (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala say the clear zone of the dentinoclast is "minimal or non-existent" (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Mineral is dissolved by acidification through V-ATPases and the collagen matrix is degraded by cathepsin K and the matrix metalloproteinases (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; Ingle's summarises it as dissolution of inorganic material by acids with breakdown of the organic structures (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

**Signalling.** RANKL on stromal and osteoblastic cells drives clastic differentiation; OPG acts as a decoy receptor that binds RANKL and inhibits clastic function (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Inflammatory mediators are potent stimulators of osteoclast and odontoclast recruitment (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; Sikri adds that bacterial lipopolysaccharide makes leucocytes differentiate into osteoclasts (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Dentine phosphophoryn and dentine sialoprotein in gingival crevicular fluid have been suggested as markers for monitoring orthodontic resorption (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; this is research, not a clinical test. [Likely]

**Two conditions: injury plus stimulation.** Levin states the two requirements: removal of the protective layer (predentine internally, precementum externally) and a noxious stimulus causing inflammation next to the damaged surface (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Cohen's adds the consequence: once clastic cells reach cementum, resorption is self-limiting unless the cells are continually stimulated, by pressure, impacted teeth, cysts and tumours, pulpal infection or periodontal infection (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala put it the same way: without a persistent stimulus the process is transient and repairs; it becomes progressive when sustained by chronic inflammation of pulp or periodontium (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. This "injury + stimulus" model is the key to the classification and treatment that follow: treatment aims to remove the stimulus, because the injury has already happened. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Resistance of permanent roots.** Permanent roots are normally surrounded by active bone cells yet are not resorbed, probably because of intact cementoblast and odontoblast layers (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019). Proposed protective factors (not mutually exclusive; Levin, 2012):
1. Unmineralised precementum/cementoid externally and predentine internally: osteoclasts do not bind to unmineralised collagen (Patel et al, 2021).
2. Lack of RGD-protein binding sites (osteopontin linked to αvβ3 integrin) in predentine (Patel et al, 2021).
3. Anti-invasion factor from predentine, precementum and periodontal ligament (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014); amelogenin and OPG (Levin, 2012).
4. Intermediate cementum (hyaline layer of Hopewell-Smith) sealing dentinal tubules from the ligament (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Levin, 2012).
5. Epithelial rests around the root (hypothesis only; Levin, 2012).

**The clastic cell.** Multinucleated cell of monocyte-macrophage origin (Sikri, 2019). Clear zone (sealing zone) around a ruffled border; odontoclasts are smaller with fewer nuclei and smaller sealing zones than osteoclasts (Patel et al, 2021). Mechanism: proton pump (V-ATPase) acidifies the lacuna and dissolves mineral; cathepsin K and MMPs degrade collagen (Levin, 2012); Howship lacunae result (Patel et al, 2021).

**Regulation.** RANKL (from stromal cells and osteoblasts) binds RANK on precursors and drives osteoclastogenesis; OPG is a decoy receptor for RANKL and inhibits it (Patel et al, 2021). IL-1, IL-6, TNF, PGE2 and bacterial LPS promote resorption (Levin, 2012; Sikri, 2019).

**Pathogenesis: two requirements** (Levin, 2012):
1. Injury that removes the protective layer (precementum or predentine).
2. A stimulus that keeps the clastic cells active.
Without continued stimulation the resorption is self-limiting and repairs (Patel et al, 2021); with chronic inflammation it becomes progressive (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014). Stimuli: pulpal infection, sulcular/periodontal infection, pressure (orthodontic, impacted tooth, cyst, tumour).

## 3. Classification

### Explanation

No single classification is shared by all the books; each is built on a different axis. Showing them side by side earns the marks.

- **Location (Cohen's).** External or internal (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's then describes external inflammatory, external cervical and internal resorption, and divides internal resorption into inflammatory and replacement (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC].
- **Andreasen's (Ingle's, reproduced by Sikri).** Ingle's names each type by its biology: repair-related (surface), infection-related (inflammatory), ankylosis-related (replacement), spontaneous ankylosis-related, invasive cervical, multiple, internal surface, infection-related internal and internal replacement, plus the transient events of transient apical breakdown, transient marginal breakdown, transient ankylosis and transient internal surface resorption (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's Table 27.3 gives a "modified Andreasen classification" and notes that Andreasen's terminology is widely acknowledged (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
- **Site + aetiology + pathogenesis (Ng and Gulabivala).** The authors say their scheme is built on all three to help treatment planning (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Internal: transient, or progressive sustained by bacteria or by cytotoxic pulp-therapy materials. External: transient; progressive without persistent inflammation (replacement); progressive with persistent inflammation sustained by root canal infection, "pressure effect", foreign material or subgingival plaque; progressive with systemic disease; and idiopathic (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Its strength is that each heading names the stimulus that treatment must remove. [Likely]
- **Endodontology (after Ne et al, 1999).** Classified on site, nature and pattern; external subdivided into surface, invasive cervical, inflammatory, ankylosis and replacement (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. It lists ankylosis and replacement resorption separately; Ng and Gulabivala make the same distinction (ankylosis is fusion without loss of root tissue) (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].
- **Levin (Seltzer and Bender).** External resorption from injury restricted to the root surface (localised: healing with cementum; diffuse: osseous replacement) versus injury with an inflammatory component; four inflammatory stimuli: pressure, pulp-space infection, sulcular infection and bleaching (Levin, 2012) [SRC].
- **AAE Glossary.** External resorption "may be further classified as surface, inflammatory or replacement, or by location as cervical, lateral or apical" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Its internal resorption is "an inflammatory process" only, so it has no internal replacement category, unlike Cohen's and Ingle's. [Certain]
- **Sikri's composite table.** By process (physiological/pathological), location (internal, external/cervical) and initiating factor (inflammatory, transient, progressive, replacement, idiopathic) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

The schemes agree on the core biology (surface/transient, inflammatory, replacement, cervical, internal) and disagree on names and on where cervical and pressure resorption sit; this is taken up in the Critical Evidence Synthesis, claim 1.

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. By process:** physiological (primary teeth) and pathological (Sikri, 2019); surface resorption is also called physiological (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**B. By location:** external and internal (Patel et al, 2021); external further by site as cervical, lateral or apical (American Association of Endodontists, 2012).

**C. Andreasen's classification** (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019; modified by Sikri, 2019):

| External | Internal |
|---|---|
| Surface (repair-related) resorption | Internal surface resorption |
| Transient apical breakdown | Internal transient apical breakdown (Sikri only) |
| Inflammatory (infection-related) resorption | Internal inflammatory (infection-related) resorption |
| Replacement (ankylosis-related) resorption | Internal replacement resorption |
| Invasive (external) cervical resorption | |
| Multiple/idiopathic resorption | |

**D. Ng and Gulabivala (site, aetiology and pathogenesis; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014):**
1. Internal: (a) transient; (b) progressive, sustained by bacteria or by cytotoxic pulp-therapy materials.
2. External: (a) transient (surface); (b) progressive without persistent inflammation (replacement); (c) progressive with persistent inflammation sustained by (i) root canal infection, (ii) pressure, (iii) foreign material, (iv) subgingival plaque (cervical); (d) systemic disease; (e) idiopathic.

**E. Levin (by stimulus):** injury restricted to the root surface (localised: cemental healing; diffuse: osseous replacement) or injury with inflammation from pressure, pulp-space infection, sulcular infection or bleaching (Levin, 2012).

**F. Endodontology (after Ne et al):** internal; external surface, invasive cervical, inflammatory, ankylosis and replacement (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

Comment: all schemes share the same biological types; they differ in naming (invasive cervical vs external cervical vs subepithelial) and in whether internal replacement resorption exists (the AAE Glossary defines internal resorption as inflammatory only).

## 4. Aetiology and predisposing factors

### Explanation

The causes are best grouped by which protective layer they damage and which stimulus they supply.

**Damage to precementum (external).** Cohen's calls damage to the precementum "the precipitating factor in all types of external resorption" (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala's Table 11.1 lists trauma, orthodontic therapy, orthognathic and dentoalveolar surgery, periodontal therapy, bruxism and heated gutta-percha or post-space preparation (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Pressure from orthodontic treatment, impacted teeth, cysts and tumours may also denude the precementum (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Levin adds inadvertent contact of root surfaces with orthodontic miniscrew implants (Levin, 2012) [SRC], and bleaching as a chemical injury (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Foreign material, as in connective tissue grafting, bone grafting and regenerative procedures, can sustain resorption (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

**Damage to predentine (internal).** Cohen's lists trauma, caries and periodontal infection, excessive heat during restorative procedures on vital teeth, calcium hydroxide procedures, vital root resections, anachoresis, orthodontic treatment, cracked teeth and idiopathic dystrophic change (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. In one series of 25 teeth trauma was the most common predisposing factor, in 45% (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala add overheating and dehydration during restorative procedures and cytotoxic restorative or "dressing materials in pulpotomies" (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

**Sikri's grouping.** Local, systemic and iatrogenic (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], with iatrogenic causes including high temperature during cavity preparation and calcium hydroxide in vital pulp therapy (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri also reports resorption of primary roots linked to a thumb-sucking habit (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Developmental.** An ectopically erupting canine can resorb the lateral incisor; if the path normalises or the canine is removed the resorption arrests and the pulp survives, so root canal treatment "is not necessary" (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. A gap at the CEJ leaves dentine unprotected (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Stimuli that make resorption progressive.** Pulp-space infection, sulcular infection, pressure and bleaching (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; foreign material (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

**Systemic and idiopathic** causes are covered in section 10.

#### ✎ Exam core

**I. Local causes**
1. *Trauma*: luxation (especially intrusion), avulsion and replantation; contusion of the periodontal ligament (Patel et al, 2021).
2. *Pulpal infection* (caries, necrosis) acting through tubules: inflammatory resorption.
3. *Periodontal*: disease, scaling and root planing, periodontal surgery, sulcular plaque (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).
4. *Pressure*: orthodontic force, impacted or ectopic teeth (for example canine resorbing the lateral incisor; Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019), cysts, tumours, occlusal trauma.
5. *Developmental*: gap at the CEJ leaving dentine exposed (Patel et al, 2021).

**II. Iatrogenic causes**
1. Heat during cavity or crown preparation; calcium hydroxide in vital pulp therapy (Sikri, 2019).
2. Intracoronal bleaching, especially 30% hydrogen peroxide with heat (Levin, 2012).
3. Orthodontic miniscrews contacting roots (Levin, 2012); orthognathic and dentoalveolar surgery.
4. Grafts and regenerative materials (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).
5. Vital root resection, calcium hydroxide procedures (Patel et al, 2021); cytotoxic pulpotomy dressings (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

**III. Systemic causes:** hyperparathyroidism and other hormonal disorders, renal disease (oxalosis), hypophosphatasia, Paget's disease, Papillon-Lefèvre syndrome, herpes zoster, radiotherapy, genetic predisposition (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Sikri, 2019).

**IV. Idiopathic:** no local or systemic cause found.

**Predisposing to internal resorption:** trauma (most common, 45% in one series), caries, heat, calcium hydroxide procedures, cracks, anachoresis (Patel et al, 2021).

**Sustaining stimuli** (decide whether resorption progresses): pulpal infection, sulcular infection, continuing pressure, bleaching chemicals and foreign material (Levin, 2012; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014). Without one of these, an injury causes only transient resorption.

## 5. Transient (self-limiting) resorption: surface resorption and transient apical breakdown

### Explanation

**Surface resorption.** Ingle's calls it repair-related resorption, part of the healing response to acute or chronic periodontal injury: after concussion, subluxation, lateral luxation, intrusion and replantation, and with chronic injury from orthodontics, traumatic occlusion and pressure; when the trauma or pressure stops, "spontaneous healing takes place" (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. With the factors removed there is "almost 100% repair", but a resorbed apex may give mobility if the root is shorter than 9-10 mm (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Levin says localised injury heals with cementum within 14 days, needs no treatment and is mostly invisible on radiographs; if the pulp tests vital, no endodontic treatment should be done (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala note that transient internal resorption has been detected in boxers with repeated incisor injuries (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

Sikri describes "external surface resorption" differently, as mainly caused by chronic mechanical injury or continuous pressure from impacted teeth, cysts, tumours and orthodontic forces, treated by eliminating the cause and restoring the defect (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. That is the pressure resorption of other books under a different name. [Likely]

**Transient apical breakdown (TAB).** The Glossary defines it as a response to luxation with radiographic apical bone and root resorption "that resolves spontaneously" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Ingle's places it among transient healing events (with transient marginal breakdown, transient ankylosis and transient internal surface resorption) seen after luxation and root fracture while a sterile pulp revascularises, and warns that radiographic signs of osteoclastic activity "may represent an actual healing process and therefore only requires monitoring" (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri links TAB with orthodontic force and says the apex returns to normal within one year after the forces are removed (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Internal surface resorption.** Seen at root fracture lines and in the apical canal of a revascularising luxated tooth; it is "a sign of progressing pulp healing" and any endodontic intervention may arrest that healing (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

**Clinical message.** The diagnostic difficulty is that early surface resorption looks like early inflammatory resorption, so the pulp status and serial radiographs decide. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Surface (repair-related) resorption**
- Cause: localised injury to the periodontal ligament and cementum (concussion, subluxation, lateral luxation, intrusion, replantation, orthodontic force, occlusal trauma) (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).
- Course: self-limiting; repair by new cementum within about 14 days (Levin, 2012); almost 100% repair once trauma or pressure stops.
- Features: asymptomatic, pulp vital, defects usually too small to see radiographically.
- Treatment: none; monitor. No root canal treatment if the pulp tests vital (Levin, 2012).

**Transient apical breakdown**
- Radiographic apical root and bone resorption after luxation that resolves spontaneously (American Association of Endodontists, 2012); also after orthodontic movement, resolving within a year (Sikri, 2019).
- Part of revascularisation and healing, so monitor only (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).

**Internal surface resorption**
- Transient widening of the canal at a root fracture line or apical canal during revascularisation; a sign of pulp healing; endodontic intervention may arrest healing, so monitor (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).

**Variant view.** Sikri describes external surface resorption as due to chronic mechanical injury or continuous pressure (impacted teeth, cysts, orthodontic force), treated by removing the cause and restoring the defect (Sikri, 2019); other books call this pressure resorption.

**Key point:** early surface resorption and early inflammatory resorption look alike; pulp sensibility, history and follow-up radiographs separate them.

## 6. External inflammatory (infection-related) resorption: apical and lateral

### Explanation

**Frequency.** EIR is the most common external resorption after luxation and avulsion and affects 30% of replanted avulsed teeth (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; in Andreasen and Vestergaard Pedersen's luxation study it complicated 38% of intrusions (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's dates the description of infection-related resorption to 1965 (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

**Pathogenesis (lateral, post-traumatic).** The injury strips precementum, osteoclasts excavate the damaged root, and if they expose dentinal tubules, microbes and toxins (LPS, muramyl dipeptide, lipoteichoic acid) from the infected canal diffuse through the tubules "and directly stimulate the resorbing osteoclasts"; the defect fills with granulation tissue (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. It needs both a periodontal injury and an infected pulp, which is why it follows avulsion and severe luxation. An entire root can be resorbed within a few months (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Levin notes the necrotic pulp of a mature tooth becomes infected within about 3 weeks (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

**Apical inflammatory resorption.** Ng and Gulabivala report resorption around apical or lateral foramina in 87.3% of teeth with periradicular periodontitis, and suggest apical cementum may be too thin to protect (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Levin agrees that periodontal inflammation is always accompanied by slight resorption at the cementodentinal junction, usually visible only histologically (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Sikri goes further: "practically all teeth with apical periodontitis will exhibit apical resorption", more often with purulent lesions and cysts than with granulomas (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. When visible, it shows as irregular shortening, stumping or thinning of the apex (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Levin calls the treatment of choice debridement with hypochlorite and a creamy calcium hydroxide dressing for at least 7 days; if non-surgical treatment fails, apical surgery if the crown-root ratio allows (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

**Clinical and radiographic signs.** No response to sensibility tests; with time discolouration, sinus tract and tenderness (Patel et al, 2021); increased mobility and a dull percussion tone (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Bowl-shaped, ragged radiolucencies in root and adjacent bone with loss of lamina dura (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's says it is typically diagnosed 2-4 weeks after injury (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's says the first radiographic signs can be seen as early as 3-4 weeks, that if it develops it is always seen within one year, and that conventional radiography grossly underestimates its extent (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Management.** Root canal treatment arrests it (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; for replanted closed-apex teeth start at 7-10 days even without radiographic signs (Patel et al, 2021; Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Levin's protocol: if treatment starts within 10 days, a creamy calcium hydroxide dressing and obturation at about 1 month; if later than 10 days or resorption is active, long-term densely packed calcium hydroxide, recalled at 3-month intervals and obturated once adjacent bone has healed (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Calcium hydroxide raises external dentine pH to 9-10 (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's adds the caveat: long-term calcium hydroxide "should be used judiciously because of the associated risk of root fracture" (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's quantifies the risk in immature teeth as 66-72% with use beyond 30 days and says it does not exist in mature teeth (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC], and recommends an MTA apical barrier to shorten exposure (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's also calls tetracycline-cortisone combinations such as Ledermix the most promising medication for resorption-prone replanted teeth, and in a dog study the cortisone component had most effect (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri lists Ledermix, calcitonin with calcium hydroxide, and acetazolamide (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. See synthesis claim 3.

**Prevention.** Timely elective root canal treatment after severe luxation (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; in immature teeth, revascularisation may be attempted (doxycycline soak doubles its rate; laser Doppler flowmetry can confirm vitality at about 4 weeks) (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Full trauma protocols: Traumatic Dental Injuries T03 and P2 of this topic.

**Outcome.** Lost dentine is not replaced; healing is by cementum or bone and a new PDL (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; extensive EIR may convert to replacement resorption after debridement (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Progressive external resorption of root and adjacent bone sustained by microbes in the root canal and dentinal tubules (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019). The most common resorption after luxation and avulsion; about 30% of replanted avulsed teeth (Patel et al, 2021).

**Pathogenesis** (combined periodontal and pulpal injury):
1. Trauma strips precementum and cementum.
2. Osteoclasts resorb the damaged surface and expose dentinal tubules.
3. Pulp necrosis follows (vessels severed); the canal becomes infected.
4. Bacterial toxins (LPS, lipoteichoic acid) diffuse out through the tubules and keep the osteoclasts active; granulation tissue fills the defect (Patel et al, 2021).

**Types**
- *Apical*: with apical periodontitis; slight resorption at the cementodentinal junction is almost always present histologically (Levin, 2012); radiographically blunting or shortening of the apex.
- *Lateral*: after luxation or avulsion; anywhere on the root.

**Clinical:** negative sensibility test; mobility, dull percussion note, sometimes extrusion, sinus tract, discolouration.

**Radiographic:** bowl-shaped, ragged radiolucencies in root and adjacent bone, loss of lamina dura; seen from 2-4 weeks and always within one year; can destroy a root within about 3 months (Patel et al, 2021; Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).

**Management:** remove the stimulus, the infected pulp.
1. Prevention: elective root canal treatment 7-10 days after replantation of a closed-apex tooth, before the pulp becomes infected (Patel et al, 2021).
2. Established EIR: chemomechanical preparation with NaOCl, calcium hydroxide dressing; long-term densely packed calcium hydroxide changed when it washes out, 3-monthly radiographs, obturation once the bone heals (Levin, 2012).
3. Alternatives: Ledermix (corticosteroid-antibiotic) (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019; Sikri, 2019).
4. Immature teeth: limit calcium hydroxide (more than 30 days risks cervical fracture) and use an MTA apical barrier (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).
5. Apical surgery if non-surgical treatment fails (Levin, 2012); extraction if unsalvageable.

**Healing:** arrest of resorption, bony healing and a new PDL space (Patel et al, 2021); prognosis is generally good (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014), better the earlier it is treated (Patel et al, 2021).

## 7. Replacement resorption and ankylosis (brief; see Traumatic Dental Injuries P3)

### Explanation

**Mechanism.** After severe injury (intrusion, avulsion with long dry time) with diffuse damage on more than 20% of the root surface, bone precursor cells from the socket colonise the root before PDL cells; bone contacts dentine, which then enters the bone-remodelling cycle and is replaced by bone (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala also give "at least 20%" (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC] and Endodontology says reversal is possible below 20% (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Ingle's expresses the threshold as area: 1-4 mm² gives a reversible ankylosis, larger injuries a progressive one, and reversal needs functional mobility, with a nonrigid splint or none (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri gives transient below 20% and permanent at 40% or more (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri describes it as a "mistake" because bone cells cannot distinguish tooth from bone (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and Endodontology concludes it should be seen as physiological rather than pathological (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC], whereas the Glossary defines replacement resorption as "A pathologic loss" of cementum, dentin and ligament (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC].

**Ankylosis vs replacement.** Ng and Gulabivala separate them: ankylosis is fusion with no loss of root tissue, replacement resorption is loss of dentine and cementum replaced by bone (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. The Glossary treats ankylosis as the clinical end-result of replacement resorption, a tooth that can no longer move physiologically (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC].

**Signs.** High metallic percussion sound, immobility, infraocclusion in a growing child (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; no PDL space and a "moth-eaten" appearance with irregular borders (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Children lose ankylosed teeth within 1-5 years; in older patients the tooth may function for 5-20 years and stays in position like an implant (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

**Management.** Endodontic therapy cannot arrest it (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; calcium hydroxide may slow but not arrest it and may accelerate it (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Prevention is the aim: immediate replantation or storage in milk (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; Levin's five strategies (prevent injury, avoid further damage, anti-inflammatory measures, stimulate cemental healing, slow osseous replacement) (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Sikri reports fluoride solutions and Emdogain (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Endodontology calls Emdogain results variable and its use controversial (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Options in the growing patient include decoronation to maintain the alveolar process (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Spontaneous ankylosis of molars without trauma is suspected to reflect instability of the RANK-RANKL-OPG system (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's warns that endodontic intervention may aggravate that process (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Loss of cementum, dentine and PDL with replacement by bone, ending in fusion of root and bone (ankylosis) (American Association of Endodontists, 2012).

**Cause.** Severe PDL damage: avulsion with long extra-oral dry time, intrusion; involvement of more than about 20% of the root surface makes it progressive (Levin, 2012; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014). Smaller injuries may give transient, reversible ankylosis (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Mechanism.** Bone-forming cells from the socket wall repopulate the root ahead of PDL cells; the root joins the normal bone-remodelling cycle and is replaced by bone (Levin, 2012).

**Features.** Immobile tooth, high metallic percussion note, infraocclusion in a growing child; radiographically no PDL space and a moth-eaten root outline (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010). Faster in children (tooth lost in 1-5 years); slow in adults (5-20 years).

**Management**
1. Prevention: immediate replantation or storage in milk (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014); nonrigid splinting (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).
2. No treatment arrests it; endodontic treatment only for a necrotic pulp (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).
3. Adjuncts reported: fluoride, Emdogain (controversial) (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010).
4. Growing patient: decoronation to preserve the ridge; later implant, bridge or autotransplantation (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019). Detail: Traumatic Dental Injuries P3.

**Spontaneous ankylosis** without trauma affects primary molars and first and second permanent molars; it is suspected to reflect instability of the RANK-RANKL-OPG system, and endodontic treatment may aggravate it (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).

## 8. External cervical resorption (brief; see Q03)

### Explanation

**Terminology and site.** Cohen's prefers "external cervical resorption" and lists invasive cervical, supraosseous extracanal invasive, peripheral inflammatory and subepithelial external root resorption as synonyms (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. It starts immediately apical to the epithelial attachment, so it can arise more apically when the attachment has receded (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's calls the apical form invasive radicular resorption (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Levin uses "subepithelial inflammatory root resorption" and says the word "cervical" has led to confusion and misdiagnosis (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. The AAE Glossary still defines cervical resorption by site, "in the coronal third of the root" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC].

**Aetiology.** In Mavridou et al's 347 teeth, 59% had more than one factor; orthodontics was most common (45.7%), then trauma (28.5%), parafunction (23.2%) and poor oral hygiene (22.9%) (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. In Heithersay's 257 teeth, orthodontics (21.2%) and trauma (14%) led; bleaching alone was 4.5% (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala note that in 10% of people dentine is not covered by cementum at the CEJ (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC], and add subgingival scaling and root planing, orthognathic and dentoalveolar surgery and internal bleaching (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; Levin lists trauma, orthodontics, intracoronal bleaching, dentoalveolar surgery and periodontal disease or treatment (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Cohen's list (Mavridou) does not include bleaching or periodontal treatment.

**Pathogenesis: two views.** Levin presents it as inflammatory, sustained by bacteria in the sulcus, but records the alternative that it may be a benign proliferative fibrovascular or fibro-osseous disorder with bacteria only secondary (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala say it is sustained by subgingival plaque (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; Ingle's describes hyperplastic tissue from PDL precursors invading "in a destructive, invasive, and apparently uncontrolled fashion" and requiring a cementoblast defect plus RANK-RANKL-OPG breakdown (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's recent histology describes three stages, initiation, resorption and repair (with bone-like tissue), and a pericanalar resorption-resistant sheet that protects the pulp (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. The books cannot settle whether infection drives the process or only complicates it. [Likely]

**Features.** Often asymptomatic and found by chance; pulp usually vital; pink spot possible but "relatively rare" (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; profuse bleeding on probing (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. "There is no usual radiographic appearance" (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; the canal outline is intact through the lesion, sometimes bounded by a radiopaque line (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri differentiates it from subgingival caries (hard base, sharp edges, bleeding) and from cervical burnout, a band across the neck of the tooth (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Classification.** Heithersay's four classes (by depth on a 2D radiograph) and Patel et al's 3D classification by height, circumferential spread and proximity to the canal (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's and Sikri both say Heithersay's is valid only for proximal lesions (Patel et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; in an ex vivo study CBCT classified 70-71% correctly versus 32% for periapicals (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. The 3D grades commonly quoted are height 1-4, spread A-D and proximity d (dentine) or p (probable pulp involvement) [RECALL].

**Management and outcome.** Excavate, inactivate (90% trichloroacetic acid), restore (GIC, composite, Biodentine), monitor (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; root canal treatment if the pulp is perforated or for class 3 access (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; internal approach when the defect is close to the pulp (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Heithersay's success: 100% class I-II, 77.8% class III, 12.5% class IV (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology records Trope's recommendation of extraction and implant or bridge for class IV and offers, as an alternative, leaving it untreated with the patient informed, as progress without infection seems slow (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; it also suggests orthodontic extrusion before treatment to give a supragingival margin (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Ingle's says no treatment can be generally recommended for class 4 (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's statement that cervical defects are "mostly ... transient" and repair in 2-3 weeks (Sikri, 2019) [SRC] contradicts every other source and Sikri's own treatment table; it reads as surface resorption misplaced under this heading. [Likely] Full detail: Q03.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Localised, invasive external resorption starting immediately below the epithelial attachment, usually at the cervical region (Patel et al, 2021). Synonyms: invasive cervical resorption, subepithelial inflammatory root resorption (Levin, 2012), supraosseous extracanal invasive resorption.

**Predisposing factors** (often several): orthodontics (most common), trauma, intracoronal bleaching, parafunction, periodontal treatment, surgery, poor oral hygiene, CEJ gap (Patel et al, 2021; Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019; Levin, 2012; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

**Pathogenesis.** Precementum damaged below the attachment; fibrovascular tissue from the PDL invades dentine, later becoming fibro-osseous; channels encircle the canal; the pulp is protected by a predentine-dentine layer (pericanalar resorption-resistant sheet) (Patel et al, 2021; Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).

**Clinical:** asymptomatic, vital pulp; pink spot sometimes; subgingival cavity with sharp edges that bleeds profusely on probing.

**Radiographic:** irregular or mottled radiolucency at the attachment level; canal outline intact through it; lesion moves with tube shift; CBCT shows portal of entry and extent.

**Heithersay's classes (1999):** 1 small, shallow; 2 close to coronal pulp, little radicular extension; 3 into coronal third of root; 4 beyond coronal third (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019). 3D classification by height, circumferential spread and proximity to the canal (Patel et al, 2021).

**Imaging:** Heithersay's scheme is reliable only for proximal lesions on 2D films; in an ex vivo study CBCT classified lesions correctly in about 70% versus 32% with periapicals (Patel et al, 2021).

**Management:** flap, curettage, 90% trichloroacetic acid, restoration with GIC/composite/Biodentine; root canal treatment if the pulp is involved; internal approach for lesions near the pulp; orthodontic extrusion for subgingival defects; class 4: monitor or extract and replace (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019). Success: classes 1-2 100%, class 3 77.8%, class 4 12.5% (Patel et al, 2021).

## 9. Pressure resorption: orthodontic, impacted teeth, cysts and tumours; foreign material and bleaching

### Explanation

**Mechanism.** Ng and Gulabivala describe pressure resorption with impacted teeth, orthodontic treatment, occlusal trauma and growing cysts and tumours, add that it is not known whether a local rise in pressure is really the trigger ("it is likely that it is mediated by a molecular mechanism"), and note that the pulp is usually vital and the bone around the defect normal (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Removing the cause arrests it (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Ingle's gives the ectopic canine resorbing the lateral incisor, which needs no root canal treatment once the cause is removed (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; Endodontology illustrates a second molar resorbed "probably due to longstanding pressure from a third molar" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Levin notes the predentine may also be affected at the apex, so internal apical resorption is often seen during active orthodontic movement (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Bender et al (1997) called orthodontic resorption "periapical replacement resorption"; Ng and Gulabivala report that they suggested pulpal inflammation might be involved (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC], Levin that pulp neuropeptides may be (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; it is less frequent and severe in endodontically treated incisors (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala nonetheless say it is arrested by removal of the orthodontic pressure but not by root canal treatment (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; the two statements are compatible if the pulp modulates the risk but the force drives the process. [Likely]

**Orthodontic risk factors (Levin).** Treatment-related: duration, force magnitude, direction and method of movement (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; intermittent forces cause less resorption than continuous ones (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; intrusion and lingual root torque concentrate force at the apex (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Patient-related: short roots or resorption before treatment (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; IL1B allele 1 homozygotes have a 5.6-fold risk (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Sikri notes that orthodontic resorption frequently occurs in maxillary anterior teeth (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. If resorption is found, pause treatment for 2-3 months with passive archwires (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

**Bleaching.** In one follow-up 7% of teeth bleached with 30% hydrogen peroxide and heat showed cervical resorption after 1-8 years; heat made it more severe (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; a recall of 95 previously traumatised teeth bleached with sodium perborate in water showed none (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; because of complications with hydrogen peroxide "and occasionally sodium perborate", 10-37% carbamide peroxide is suggested as an alternative, though without clinical efficacy data, and follow-up is mandatory (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. A cervical barrier over the root filling before bleaching is commonly advised [RECALL]; none of the chapters read gives it.

**Foreign material.** Grafts and regenerative procedures, with root planing beforehand damaging cementum (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pressure resorption**
- Causes: orthodontic force, impacted/ectopic teeth (canine on lateral incisor, third molar on second molar), cysts, tumours, occlusal trauma (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).
- Mechanism: pressure damages cementum and keeps clastic cells active; probably molecular rather than simply mechanical.
- Features: asymptomatic, pulp vital, normal bone around the defect; blunted, shortened apex (orthodontic), or a concavity facing the pressure source.
- Treatment: remove the cause (stop force, extract impacted tooth, enucleate cyst); resorption stops (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Root canal treatment is not needed for a vital pulp.

**Orthodontically induced inflammatory root resorption: risk factors** (Levin, 2012)
- Treatment: long duration, heavy and continuous force, intrusion and lingual root torque, large distances.
- Patient: short or already resorbed roots, IL1B gene polymorphism (5.6-fold risk); maxillary anterior teeth most affected (Sikri, 2019).
- If detected: pause 2-3 months with passive archwires; stop treatment if severe.

**Bleaching-related cervical resorption:** 30% hydrogen peroxide with heat after trauma carries the highest risk; avoid heat; carbamide peroxide is a suggested alternative (sodium perborate in water caused none in one follow-up); review for years (Levin, 2012).

**Foreign material:** grafts and regenerative materials can sustain resorption (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

**Pulp and orthodontic resorption.** Bender et al (1997) called it periapical replacement resorption and proposed a role for pulpal neuropeptides; apical resorption is less frequent and less severe in endodontically treated incisors (Levin, 2012). Internal apical resorption may also appear during active movement. Even so, the resorption stops with removal of the force, not with root canal treatment (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

## 10. Systemic, multiple and idiopathic resorption

### Explanation

Ng and Gulabivala list herpes zoster, hyperoxaluria/oxalosis, hypophosphatasia, radiotherapy, hypoparathyroidism, osteogenesis imperfecta, Paget's disease and hyperparathyroidism, but warn that the evidence "consists mainly of retrospective case reports"; such patients should be referred and no local treatment is indicated (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. For Paget's disease they record replacement of dentine and cementum by coarse-fibred bone (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC], whereas Levin states that in hyperparathyroidism and Paget's disease radiographic bone resorption "is not accompanied by resorption of the roots" (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Both statements are in the books; the case-report basis cannot settle it. [Guessing] Levin adds renal dystrophy with oxalate deposition and genetic links (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Sikri lists non-dental lesions that resorb roots, such as chondrosarcoma, ossifying fibroma and giant cell reparative granuloma (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and alendronate as limiting systemic resorption (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Herpes zoster is suspected when unexplained pain and resorption affect several adjacent teeth on one side (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Multiple invasive cervical resorption has been linked with contact with cats with feline resorption and with bisphosphonate therapy (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri defines multiple idiopathic cervical resorption as at least three teeth and calls the feline virus link undocumented (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's gives the prognosis of multiple invasive cervical resorption as poor because of the aggressive cells (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Idiopathic cases can only be monitored while a prosthetic plan is made (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; Levin expects more of them to prove systemic or genetic (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Systemic causes** (usually several teeth; evidence mainly case reports; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014): hyperparathyroidism, hypoparathyroidism, hypophosphatasia, renal disease with oxalosis, Paget's disease, osteogenesis imperfecta, Papillon-Lefèvre syndrome, Turner's syndrome, radiotherapy, herpes zoster of the trigeminal nerve (Sikri, 2019).
- Herpes zoster: resorption and pain in several adjacent teeth on one side; root canal treatment with antiviral therapy (Sikri, 2019).
- Management: refer for medical diagnosis (blood chemistry); treat the disease; local dental treatment usually not indicated.

**Non-dental jaw lesions** (ossifying fibroma, giant cell granuloma, chondrosarcoma) resorb roots and must be diagnosed before any root treatment (Sikri, 2019).

**Multiple idiopathic cervical resorption:** three or more teeth; possible links with feline virus and bisphosphonates; poor prognosis (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019; Sikri, 2019).

**Idiopathic resorption:** no cause found; monitor, palliative care and prosthetic planning (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014); many such cases may later prove systemic or genetic (Levin, 2012).

**Note the disagreement:** Ng and Gulabivala list Paget's disease as replacing dentine and cementum with coarse-fibred bone, whereas Levin states that in hyperparathyroidism and Paget's disease bone resorption is not accompanied by root resorption (Levin, 2012). Alendronate has been used to limit systemic resorption (Sikri, 2019).

## 11. Internal resorption: inflammatory and replacement (see Q02)

### Explanation

**Definition and frequency.** Internal resorption starts in and affects the root canal wall (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; it is "the only type of resorption that starts within the root canal" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Prevalence is 0.1-1.6% of permanent teeth (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

**Pathogenesis.** Predentine and odontoblasts are lost; progression needs bacterial stimulation from necrotic infected coronal pulp, and a vital pulp apical to the lesion that brings clastic cells and nutrients (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology calls vital pulp a prerequisite (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala add that the resorption ceases with total necrosis (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Ingle's describes the resorbing granulation tissue as interposed between healthy and diseased pulp (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Levin offers an alternative trigger, cytokines from odontoblasts undergoing apoptosis after trauma (Levin, 2012) [SRC], and notes internal resorption has been produced experimentally with diathermy (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

**Types.** Inflammatory and replacement; islands of mineralised tissue in the canal define internal replacement resorption (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's calls it tissue metaplasia forming bone in the canal (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC] and says it can be arrested by pulp extirpation and root filling (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Levin classifies inflammatory internal resorption by location as apical or intraradicular; apical internal resorption is common with periapical lesions and probably explains inaccurate electronic length readings (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Sikri describes the inflammatory type as ovoid or fusiform enlargement of the canal (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Clinical.** Usually asymptomatic. Pulp test usually positive (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC], but a negative result does not exclude active resorption (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Pink spot possible if the chamber is involved (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC], though Cohen's says pink spots "actually are rare in cases of IRR" (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; Levin calls the old "pathognomonic" view a source of misdiagnosis (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

**Radiographic.** Uniform density, smooth outline, symmetrical (Gartner et al, cited by Patel et al, 2021) [SRC], though Cohen's adds that many cases do not show these features (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; a well-circumscribed oval radiolucency continuous with the canal (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; replacement lesions look mottled (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; bone is not affected unless the root perforates (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Resorption that starts in the pulp and affects the root canal wall (Patel et al, 2021); an inflammatory process with loss of dentine and possible invasion of cementum (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). Prevalence 0.1-1.6% (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

**Aetiology:** trauma (commonest), caries and pulpitis, heat during preparation, pulpotomy and calcium hydroxide procedures, cracks, orthodontic movement, idiopathic (Patel et al, 2021).

**Pathogenesis**
1. Loss of odontoblasts and predentine.
2. Infected necrotic coronal pulp supplies the stimulus (bacteria via tubules, cracks, caries).
3. Vital granulation tissue apical to it supplies clastic cells and blood.
4. Lesion enlarges until the pulp becomes totally necrotic (resorption stops) or the root perforates (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

**Types**
- *Internal inflammatory*: uniformly radiolucent, ballooning enlargement of the canal.
- *Internal replacement (metaplastic)*: dentine replaced by bone-like tissue; mottled radiolucency (Patel et al, 2021).
- By site: apical or intraradicular (Levin, 2012); transient internal surface resorption (section 5).

**Alternative theories:** apoptotic odontoblasts after trauma release cytokines that start resorption; heat (diathermy) has produced internal resorption experimentally (Levin, 2012).

**Clinical:** asymptomatic, found on radiographs; pulp test usually positive but may be negative (necrotic coronal pulp); pink spot when the pulp chamber is involved, though uncommon; pulpitis or apical periodontitis later; perforation and sinus in advanced cases.

**Radiographic:** well-circumscribed, symmetrical, oval radiolucency of uniform density continuous with the canal; canal outline cannot be traced through it; stays centred on tube shift; bone intact unless perforated.

## 12. Diagnosis and differential diagnosis

### Explanation

**History and clinical tests.** Trauma, orthodontics, bleaching and periodontal treatment are asked about; trauma is often forgotten by the patient (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Sensibility testing separates the types: negative in apical and lateral inflammatory resorption, usually positive in subepithelial (cervical) and internal resorption (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Endodontology says both internal and cervical lesions are likely to test positive (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Probing a cervical defect causes profuse bleeding (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Radiographic tracing of the canal.** In ECR the canal wall stays visible and intact through the defect (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC], and Ng and Gulabivala say the same of external inflammatory lesions (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. In internal resorption the canal balloons out and cannot be followed. Cohen's warns of two pitfalls: calcified tissue may hide the canal, and in multi-canal teeth an unaffected canal may be superimposed on an internal defect (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Parallax (SLOB).** A second film with horizontal tube shift: a lingual/palatal defect moves in the same direction as the tube, a buccal one opposite (Patel et al, 2021; American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; internal defects keep their position relative to the canal (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC], or in Endodontology's words "remains centered in the root" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

**Bone.** External inflammatory resorption always involves adjacent bone; internal resorption does not, unless perforated (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Levin's diagnostic box gives cervical lesions at the attachment level with a crestal bony defect and internal lesions contained in the root without a bony defect (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

**Pink spot.** Not a sign of apical or lateral EIR, because the pulp is non-vital (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; possible in both internal and cervical resorption (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri's table lists it as "a usual sign" of internal resorption and absent in external resorption (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. See synthesis claim 2.

**CBCT.** It removes the need for exploratory treatment (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; in Patel et al's clinical study CBCT was 100% accurate in diagnosing presence and type, better than periapicals (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. A small (limited) field of view is usually chosen to keep the dose down [RECALL].

**Confirm during treatment.** If bleeding persists after extirpation, especially at the second visit, the blood supply is external; failure to fill the defect from inside suggests an external lesion (Levin, 2012; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. History:** trauma (often forgotten), orthodontics, bleaching, periodontal surgery, restorations, systemic disease.

**2. Clinical:** sensibility test (negative in EIR; usually positive in cervical and internal), percussion note (dull in EIR, metallic in ankylosis), mobility, pink spot, probing (bleeding cervical cavity), sinus tract.

**3. Radiographs:** paralleled periapical plus a second view with horizontal tube shift (parallax). SLOB rule: a lingual lesion moves in the same direction as the tube, a buccal one in the opposite direction (American Association of Endodontists, 2012); internal lesions stay centred on the canal.

**4. CBCT** (small field of view): position, extent, portal of entry, perforation, restorability; avoids exploratory treatment; more accurate than periapicals (Patel et al, 2021).

**5. Confirmation during treatment:** bleeding that stops after extirpation means internal; persisting bleeding means an external blood supply (Levin, 2012).

**Differential diagnosis: internal vs external (cervical) resorption** (Patel et al, 2021; Levin, 2012; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014)

| Feature | Internal | External cervical |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | pulp, canal wall | PDL below attachment |
| Site | anywhere along canal | attachment level |
| Pulp test | usually positive (may be negative) | usually positive |
| Shape | smooth, symmetrical, oval | irregular, ragged or mottled |
| Density | uniform (mottled if replacement) | variable |
| Canal outline | distorted, ballooned, not traceable | intact, traceable through lesion |
| Tube shift | stays centred on canal | moves (SLOB) |
| Bone | intact unless perforated | crestal bony defect |
| Pink spot | possible if chamber involved | possible, cervical |
| Bleeding during RCT | stops after extirpation | persists |

**Other differentials:** external inflammatory resorption (non-vital, bone radiolucency), subgingival caries (soft, sticky, no bleeding), cervical burnout (band across the neck) (Sikri, 2019).

## 13. Management by type

### Explanation

**Principle.** Treatment removes the stimulus that keeps the clastic cells active: infected pulp (EIR), vital granulation tissue in the canal (internal), invading tissue and its blood supply (ECR), or pressure. Replacement resorption has no stimulus to remove and cannot be stopped. [Certain]

**Internal resorption technique.** Levin calls the treatment "conceptually very easy": instrument the canal apical to the defect to cut the blood supply, dry with paper points, dress with calcium hydroxide to dissolve residual tissue and seal at a later visit (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Cohen's warns of profuse bleeding (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC], makes ultrasonic activation of irrigants "an essential step" (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC] and advocates calcium hydroxide as an antibacterial dressing (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala recommend 5% NaOCl with ultrasonics (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC] and checking for perforation with a curved file on an apex locator (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Obturation: thermoplasticised gutta-percha; Obtura and MicroSeal gave the best fills in ex vivo studies (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Perforation: MTA is the material of choice (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC], often as a hybrid with gutta-percha apically (Patel et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala prefer long-term calcium hydroxide to induce repair of a perforation (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC] and surgery for large perforations (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; Sikri calls surgery the treatment of choice when perforated (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's insists a perforation is "certainly not a contraindication to treatment" (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology adds adhesive composite to resist fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

**The other types** follow sections 5-10 and are collected in the table.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle:** identify the type, then remove its stimulus (infection, granulation tissue, pressure); early treatment gives the best prognosis (Patel et al, 2021).

**Management of internal resorption** (Patel et al, 2021; Levin, 2012; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014)
1. Assess restorability and perforation with CBCT.
2. Access under rubber dam; expect heavy bleeding; extirpate quickly and instrument the canal apical to the defect to cut the blood supply.
3. Irrigate with NaOCl (up to 5%) activated ultrasonically to dissolve tissue in the undercut defect.
4. Calcium hydroxide dressing between visits to remove residual tissue and disinfect.
5. Check perforation (curved file on apex locator, CBCT).
6. Obturate: gutta-percha/lateral compaction apically, thermoplasticised gutta-percha (Obtura) into the defect.
7. Perforated defect: MTA or Biodentine in the defect with gutta-percha apically (hybrid); long-term calcium hydroxide to induce a barrier; surgical repair for large or inaccessible perforations.
8. Bonded composite restoration to reinforce the tooth (Baumann and Beer, 2010); extract if unrestorable.

**Management summary by type**

| Type | Management |
|---|---|
| Surface, transient apical breakdown, internal surface | monitor; no root canal treatment if vital |
| External inflammatory | root canal treatment, calcium hydroxide (long-term if extensive) or Ledermix; 7-10 day elective treatment after replantation |
| Replacement/ankylosis | prevention; no arrest; decoronation in growing patients; implant later |
| External cervical | curettage, trichloroacetic acid, restoration; RCT if pulp involved; class 4 monitor or extract |
| Pressure | remove the cause; monitor |
| Internal | prompt root canal treatment; thermoplastic filling; MTA for perforation |
| Systemic | refer and treat the disease |
| Idiopathic | monitor; prosthetic planning |

## 14. Prognosis, follow-up and conclusion

### Explanation

**Prognosis by type.** Surface resorption repairs almost completely (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. EIR's prognosis is "generally good" (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC] and better the earlier it is diagnosed and treated (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; healing is shown by arrest of resorption, bone healing and a new PDL space (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC], and Levin uses bone healing as the signal to obturate (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Lost dentine is never replaced (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Internal resorption "in general responds favorably to conventional treatment" (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; surgically managed perforated cases are compromised and prone to root fracture (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. ECR depends on extent: Heithersay class I-II 100%, III 77.8%, IV 12.5% (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; extensive mottled lesions with large surface loss have "extremely poor prognosis" and no treatment is recommended (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Replacement resorption ends in loss of the tooth, quickly in children (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

**Follow-up.** Calcium hydroxide cases are reviewed at 3-month intervals (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; bleached teeth need long-term follow-up (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; ECR repairs need monitoring for recurrence (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Persistence of resorption on recall after internal treatment should prompt the thought that an external lesion was misdiagnosed (Levin, 2012) [SRC].

**Conclusion.** Cohen's summary is that "the earlier the detection of root resorption, the better the prognosis" and that CBCT is an excellent tool for confirming ECR and IRR (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prognosis**
- Surface/transient: excellent, spontaneous repair.
- External inflammatory: good if root canal treatment is started early; poor in untreated immature teeth.
- Internal: good if treated before perforation; perforated cases are guarded and prone to fracture.
- Cervical: depends on Heithersay class (1-2 excellent, 3 fair, 4 poor).
- Replacement: tooth eventually lost; slower in adults.
- In every type, dentine already lost is not replaced; healing is by cementum or bone with a new PDL (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019).

**Follow-up:** periodic clinical and radiographic review (3-monthly during calcium hydroxide therapy; Levin, 2012), then at longer intervals; look for healed bone, a continuous lamina dura and PDL space, and no further resorption. Continued resorption after internal treatment suggests a missed external lesion.

**Conclusion.** Root resorption of permanent teeth needs two things: loss of the protective precementum or predentine, and a continuing stimulus. Correct classification and diagnosis (sensibility test, parallax radiographs, CBCT) decide the treatment: root canal treatment arrests infection-related external and internal resorption, cervical lesions need excavation and repair, pressure resorption stops when the cause is removed, and replacement resorption can only be prevented or managed prosthetically. The earlier resorption is detected, the better the prognosis (Patel et al, 2021).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Root resorption is classified as internal and external, then as surface, inflammatory and replacement."**
*Textbooks:* Cohen's starts from location, external vs internal (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's (Andreasen) names types by biology, repair-related, infection-related and ankylosis-related, plus invasive cervical, multiple and the transient events (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala classify by site, aetiology and pathogenesis with the sustaining stimulus as the sub-heading (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; Levin classifies by injury and inflammatory stimulus (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; the AAE Glossary uses surface/inflammatory/replacement and cervical/lateral/apical (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; Sikri reproduces a modified Andreasen table (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
*Where they differ:* (a) the name and nature of cervical resorption: external cervical (Patel et al, 2021), invasive cervical (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019), subepithelial inflammatory (Levin, 2012), progressive external resorption sustained by subgingival plaque (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014), and in the Glossary simply resorption in the coronal third (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; (b) internal replacement resorption exists in Cohen's, Ingle's and Gulabivala's schemes but not in the Glossary's inflammatory-only definition (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; (c) Sikri places external cervical resorption under "replacement" in his management table and calls most cervical defects transient (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], which no other source does.
*Strength:* these are expert classifications, not tested schemes; none of the books reports inter-examiner agreement for the general classifications. [Certain]
*Conclusion:* use location as the first division, Andreasen's biological types as the second, and quote Ng and Gulabivala's sustaining stimulus because it maps directly onto treatment. Do not present any one scheme as the accepted standard. [Likely]

**Claim 2: "A pink spot means internal resorption."**
*Textbooks:* Sikri's table gives the pink spot as "a usual sign" of internal resorption and "no pink spot" for external (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Levin says it "has been thought to be pathognomonic of internal root resorption" but is also a feature of subepithelial (cervical) resorption, and that this belief led to many cervical lesions being treated as internal (Levin, 2012) [SRC]. Endodontology: a feature of both (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala: present in internal resorption of the pulp chamber and in cervical resorption (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Ingle's describes pink discoloration as an early sign of invasive cervical resorption and does not discuss it for internal resorption (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's: rare in internal resorption, relatively more frequent in ECR but "a relatively rare feature" there too (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]. Levin states that apical and lateral EIR does not cause it, because the pulp is non-vital (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; no book says otherwise.
*Evidence vs interpretation:* the books give no prevalence figures, and neither Cohen's nor Sikri states what its position rests on. [Certain]
*Conclusion:* a pink spot shows granulation tissue under thin enamel, from either the pulp or the PDL. It is a sign of resorption, not of its origin; tube-shift radiographs and CBCT decide. Writing "pink spot is pathognomonic of internal resorption" is now a recognised error.

**Claim 3: "Long-term calcium hydroxide is the medicament of choice for external inflammatory resorption."**
*Textbooks:* Levin recommends long-term densely packed calcium hydroxide when treatment starts after 10 days or resorption is active (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; Endodontology reports Trope's same recommendation (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala use long-term calcium hydroxide for extensive resorption (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; the Glossary says it "appears to inhibit inflammatory resorption" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Ingle's shows it raises dentine pH to 9-10 with only "a tendency" toward better arrest in one clinical study (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC].
*Refinement/challenge:* Cohen's advises judicious use because of root fracture (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's gives a 66-72% cervical fracture risk in immature teeth after more than 30 days and none in mature teeth, and suggests MTA barriers to limit exposure (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's calls a tetracycline-cortisone paste (Ledermix) "the most promising medication" topically, and in an experimental dog study its cortisone part had the most effect on osteoclast attack, and that short-term antibiotics alone worked about as well as short-term calcium hydroxide clinically but are now inappropriate because of side-effects (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri says Ledermix has been effective in controlling external inflammatory resorption and lists "Ledermix paste or calcium hydroxide" as alternatives in its management table (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and adds calcitonin and acetazolamide without supporting data (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's, Levin, Ng and Gulabivala and Endodontology do not mention corticosteroid dressings in these chapters; Cohen's says long-term calcium hydroxide "may be beneficial" in established EIR and that the specific canal protocol is irrelevant as long as the biologic objectives are met (Patel et al, 2021) [SRC].
*Strength:* calcium hydroxide rests on clinical series and long use; the Ledermix advantage is from animal experiments; the fracture risk figures come from two clinical studies in immature teeth. Animal data cannot establish clinical superiority. [Certain]
*Conclusion:* disinfection, not the medicament, is what arrests EIR. In mature teeth long-term calcium hydroxide is accepted; in immature teeth keep it short and use an MTA barrier; Ledermix is a reasonable first dressing after trauma (supported by Ingle's and Sikri), but none of the books shows it is better than calcium hydroxide in patients. [Likely]

**Claim 4: "Replacement resorption follows damage to more than 20% of the root surface and cannot be stopped."**
*Textbooks:* Levin gives more than 20% (Levin, 2012) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala "at least 20%" (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; Endodontology says reversal is possible below 20% (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Sikri gives transient below 20% and permanent at 40% or more (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Ingle's uses area, with 1-4 mm² reversible and larger injuries progressive (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. On arrest, Ingle's, Endodontology, Ng and Gulabivala and Sikri all say it cannot be arrested or treated (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; only Ingle's adds that endodontic intervention and calcium hydroxide may slow it but risk accelerating it (Andreasen, Heithersay and Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri nonetheless reports fluoride solutions as effective in decreasing it (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Emdogain is called controversial by Endodontology and successful by Sikri (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
*Strength:* the books give the thresholds without the underlying study designs (Endodontology cites Tronstad, 1988) (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC], and they do not reconcile a percentage with an area. Sikri's claims for fluoride and Emdogain are not supported with data in the chapter. [Likely]
*Conclusion:* write "about 20% of the root surface (Ingle's: more than about 4 mm²)" and that no treatment reliably arrests established replacement resorption; prevention and timing of decoronation are what change the outcome.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Resorption needs injury to precementum/predentine plus a continuing stimulus; without the stimulus it is self-limiting.
- Odontoclast: smaller, fewer nuclei, small clear zone; RANKL drives, OPG blocks.
- Surface resorption and transient apical breakdown repair by themselves: monitor, do not treat a vital tooth.
- EIR needs an infected pulp: root canal treatment 7-10 days after replantation of mature teeth; calcium hydroxide, short in immature teeth.
- Replacement resorption: no cure; prevention; decoronation in children.
- ECR: vital pulp, canal outline intact, moves on tube shift; Heithersay 1-4; TCA, curettage, restoration.
- Internal: canal balloons, stays centred, bone intact; cut the apical blood supply, thermoplastic gutta-percha, MTA for perforation.
- Pink spot can occur in both internal and cervical resorption; Cohen's calls it rare in each, Sikri "a usual sign" of internal resorption.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling the pink spot pathognomonic of internal resorption; it occurs in cervical resorption too, and Cohen's finds it uncommon in both.
- Doing root canal treatment for surface resorption, transient apical breakdown, pressure resorption or early cervical resorption with a vital pulp.
- Saying root canal treatment or calcium hydroxide arrests replacement resorption.
- Forgetting that internal resorption needs a vital apical pulp, so a positive pulp test does not exclude it and a negative one does not exclude active resorption.
- Writing a single classification as "the" classification without naming its author.
