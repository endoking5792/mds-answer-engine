# Principles of cavity preparation: outline, resistance, retention and convenience forms

**Marks:** 20 · Predicted essay

**Answer blueprint:** Introduction and sequence → outline form → resistance form → retention form → convenience form → final procedures → clinical synthesis.

## Introduction

Cavity preparation is the planned shaping of tooth structure to remove disease or defective material while preserving sound tissues and providing a durable restoration. G.V. Black's sequence remains a useful framework, but modern adhesive dentistry emphasises **minimal intervention, selective caries removal and material-specific design** (Summitt, 2013; Boushell and Walter, 2019).

## Sequence of cavity preparation

| Black's classical sequence (Summitt, 2013) | Modern sequence (Boushell and Walter, 2019) |
|---|---|
| 1. Outline form | **Initial:** 1. Initial depth and outline form |
| 2. Resistance form | 2. Primary resistance form |
| 3. Retention form | 3. Primary retention form |
| 4. Convenience form | 4. Convenience form |
| 5. Remove remaining carious dentin | **Final:** 5. Remove defective restoration/soft dentin; 6. Pulp protection; 7. Secondary resistance and retention; 8. Finish external walls; 9. Debride and inspect |
| 6. Finish enamel walls and margins | |
| 7. Toilet of the cavity | |

The sequence is flexible; in deep lesions, selective caries removal may be performed early to reduce pulpal exposure (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

## 1. Outline form

**Definition:** The shape and extent of the external boundaries of the completed preparation.

**Principles**
- Remove caries, defective restorative material and unsupported enamel.
- Preserve sound enamel, dentin, marginal ridges and cusps.
- Place margins on sound tooth structure where they can be finished and maintained.
- Include communicating defects, but avoid unnecessary extension into sound fissures.
- Plan the outline according to lesion extent, tooth morphology, occlusion, esthetics and restorative material.

Initial depth is lesion-guided; Sturdevant's describes about 0.2 mm inside the DEJ for many preparations (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

**Extension for prevention:** Black's traditional concept extended margins to self-cleansing areas. Contemporary minimally invasive practice generally rejects routine extension through non-carious fissures; these may instead be sealed (Overton et al., 2013; Boushell and Walter, 2019).

## 2. Resistance form

**Definition:** The shape and placement of walls and floors that enable the tooth and restoration to withstand masticatory forces without fracture (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

**Principles**
1. **Conserve tooth structure:** minimise preparation width and preserve strong cusps and marginal ridges.
2. **Provide stable floors:** pulpal and gingival floors should support the restoration and resist occlusal loading.
3. **Round internal line angles:** reduce abrupt stress concentration.
4. **Provide adequate restorative thickness:** amalgam generally requires approximately 1.5–2 mm of bulk (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Overton et al., 2013).
5. **Protect weakened cusps:** consider cusp reduction as the preparation approaches half the cusp-to-groove distance; coverage is commonly indicated around two-thirds, depending on tooth and material (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

Avoid unsupported enamel and cup-shaped preparations that promote wedging.

## 3. Retention form

**Definition:** The form that prevents displacement of the restoration by tipping, lifting or dislodging forces (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

| Material | Principal retention features |
|---|---|
| Amalgam | Occlusal convergence of opposing walls; dovetail when required |
| Cast metal | Controlled taper and a single path of withdrawal; grooves or boxes when needed |
| Resin composite | Adhesive bonding to enamel and dentin |
| Glass-ionomer cement | Adhesion to tooth structure; generally conservative preparation |

Excessive taper reduces cast-restoration retention; grooves, slots, boxes, pins and skirts provide auxiliary retention when required (Marzouk et al., n.d.; Fan et al., 2013).

## 4. Convenience form

**Definition:** The form that provides adequate visibility, access and ease of instrumentation and restoration.

Examples include access for caries removal, matrix placement and impression making. Do not sacrifice sound tooth structure for convenience (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Summitt, 2013).

## 5. Final procedures

1. **Remove remaining caries/defective material:** use selective caries removal according to lesion depth and pulpal diagnosis. For deep caries in vital permanent teeth with no symptoms or symptoms of reversible pulpitis, the 2026 EFCD–ESE–ORCA guideline suggests selective removal to soft dentine rather than non-selective removal to hard dentine; stepwise removal is also suggested over non-selective removal to reduce pulp-exposure risk (Schwendicke et al., 2026).
2. **Protect the pulp:** use a liner or base only when clinically indicated.
3. **Add secondary resistance/retention:** grooves, slots, boxes, pins or adhesive treatment only when required.
4. **Finish external walls and margins:** choose the cavosurface design according to the material. A 90-degree butt joint is generally appropriate for amalgam and glass-ceramic; selected cast-metal and composite margins may be bevelled (Boushell and Walter, 2019).
5. **Clean and inspect:** remove debris, rinse, gently dry without desiccating dentin, and inspect before restoration.

## Clinical synthesis

The steps organise preparation, but application depends on **caries activity, remaining tooth structure, pulp status and restorative material**. Preserve sound tissue and provide material-specific resistance and retention.

**Diagram to draw:** Label outline form, pulpal floor, rounded internal angles, converging amalgam walls, restorative bulk and cusp protection in a cavity cross-section.
