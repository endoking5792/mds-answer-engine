## 1. Introduction and classification

### Explanation

Pickard's Table 5.5 classifies "tooth-cutting/caries removal technologies, the substrates acted on, and the mechanism of action"; the mechanical non-rotary group comprises "hand instruments (excavators, chisels), air abrasion (alumina/bioactive glass), air polishing", ultrasonics and sono-abrasion, and separate groups are chemo-mechanical gels (Carisolv, Papacarie) and photo-ablation by lasers (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Mount, Walsh and Brostek note that "recent advances with both air abrasion and laser techniques have added additional instruments to the armamentarium" and that these, with chemo-mechanical removal, let the clinician "work more conservatively" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. The question asks to "discuss", so each method needs its principle, uses, merits and limits, and the points where the books disagree.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Non-rotary methods prepare teeth or remove caries without a rotating bur. They include the oldest method (hand instruments) and the newer alternatives that adhesive, minimally invasive dentistry has made practical. They allow more conservative work with less noise, vibration and anaesthesia (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Classification (after Banerjee and Watson, 2015)**
1. Mechanical, non-rotary: hand instruments; air abrasion (and air polishing); sono-abrasion and ultrasonics (including CVD tips).
2. Photo-ablation: erbium lasers.
3. Chemo-mechanical: Carisolv, Papacarie, enzyme gels.

## 2. Hand instruments

### Explanation

Sturdevant's Box 7.1 ("Classification of hand instruments") divides them into cutting instruments (excavators: ordinary hatchets, hoes, angle formers, spoons; chisels: straight, curved, bin-angle, enamel hatchets, gingival margin trimmers), other cutting instruments (knives, files, scalers, carvers) and non-cutting instruments (condensers, mirrors, explorers, probes) (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: "Modern high-speed equipment has eliminated the need for many hand instruments for tooth preparation", yet "hand instruments remain an essential part of the armamentarium for restorative dentistry" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Black's four-number formula is expressed as blade width, cutting edge angle, blade length and blade angle ("The complete instrument formula (four numbers) is expressed as the blade width" in 0.1 mm, then the angles in centigrades) (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Uses: "Excavators are used for removal of carious tissue, refinement of the internal aspects of the preparation"; "Chisels are used primarily for cutting enamel"; "Spoon excavators (Fig. 7.7) are used for removing soft carious tissue"; and "The gingival margin trimmer is designed to eliminate unsupported enamel on gingival walls of proximal preparations", also used for "bevelling (blunting or rounding) of the axiopulpal line angle" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Mount keeps two hand instruments in contemporary practice: "gingival margin trimmers for planing the enamel margins in small spaces where access for rotary cutting instruments is limited" and "spoon excavators for the final removal of softened, demineralized, infected dentine", with the warning "Take care not to remove all softened dentine because some of it will be affected only and subject to remineralization"; they must "be kept very sharp and are used with a light touch" or there is a "risk of introducing microcracks in the enamel walls or over-extending the cavity outline" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Pickard's explains their advantage over burs: "Hand excavators rely on the tactile feedback offered to the operator" and permit finer discrimination between infected, affected and sound dentine (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Marzouk advises that near the pulp "for removal of carious dentin use of hand instruments is highly advised" (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

ART is the main hand-only technique, and the books differ on its status. Pickard's describes single-step ART as restoring with "chemically adhesive, high-viscosity glass ionomer cement (GIC)" "after the use of simple hand excavators to gain access to and remove the caries-infected dentine", and as following minimally invasive principles (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Sturdevant's restricts it to areas with limited access to care: "This technique and material is not recommended in routine clinical use" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Hand instruments** were once the only means of preparation; high-speed rotary equipment has replaced many, but they remain essential (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Classification:** cutting (excavators: ordinary hatchet, hoe, angle former, spoon; chisels: straight, Wedelstaedt, bin-angle, enamel hatchet, gingival margin trimmer) and non-cutting (condensers, mirrors, explorers, probes). Black's formula: blade width (0.1 mm), cutting edge angle, blade length (mm), blade angle (centigrades) (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Contemporary uses:** spoon excavators for final removal of soft infected dentine, leaving affected dentine to remineralise; gingival margin trimmers for planing enamel margins where burs cannot reach and rounding the axiopulpal line angle; chisels and hatchets for removing unsupported enamel (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Advantages:** tactile feedback allows finer discrimination of infected, affected and sound dentine than burs (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); advised for carious dentine near the pulp (Marzouk et al, undated).
- **Limitations:** slow on sound tissue, must be kept sharp and used with a light touch to avoid enamel microcracks and over-extension (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
- **ART:** hand excavation of infected dentine and restoration with high-viscosity GIC (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Ritter et al (2019) do not recommend it for routine use outside areas with limited access to care.

## 3. Air abrasion

### Explanation

Air abrasion, "also called kinetic cavity preparation, particle beam or kinetic abrasion technology", removes tissue "by brittle micro-fracture" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Mount dates it to Robert Black, 1943 (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Pickard's says "Developed in 1945 by R. B. Black" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Summitt: the Airdent failed because it "did not prepare undercuts", and was accepted after reintroduction "because bonded restorations had become routine" (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Doubling particle size from 27 to 50 µm raises kinetic energy eightfold; "27 microns for removing caries", "34 microns for cutting enamel" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Soft infected dentine "is not hard and brittle" and cuts poorly (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Advantages: "no heat, vibration, pressure, pain, or noise generated" and less cracking, making the surface "more suitable for adhesive bonding" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; SEM shows rounded margins and line angles (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson state "There is no need for local anaesthesia" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Mount lists "Reduced need for anaesthesia" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Limits: no defined walls and margins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; over-cutting without tactile feedback (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; acid etching still bonds better (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; it creates a smear layer (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; unsuitable for "slots, grooves or ditches", large cavities and crowns; alumina dust is a risk to asthmatic staff and eyes (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. It is "an adjunct to, but not a universal replacement for, rotary instruments" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Water-curtain units reduce dust (Summitt, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** A high-velocity stream of alumina particles (27-50 µm) transfers kinetic energy and removes tissue by brittle micro-fracture (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016). Invented by Robert Black in 1943 (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) or 1945 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); returned with bonded restorations because it cannot cut undercuts (Summitt, 2013).

**Variables:** pressure, particle size (27 µm caries, 34 µm enamel), flow rate, nozzle size, distance and angle (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Advantages:** no heat, vibration, noise or pressure; less cracking and rounded internal angles, good for bonding (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016); no anaesthesia (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) or reduced need (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Disadvantages:** no tactile sense and over-cutting; cannot produce defined walls and margins (Ritter et al, 2019); poor on soft carious dentine; creates smear layer, acid etching still needed; dust hazard (asthma, eyes) needing evacuation and protection (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Uses:** minimal occlusal entry, pit and fissure debridement before sealing, small cavities, roughening surfaces to be bonded, stain and composite removal (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Ritter et al, 2019). An adjunct, not a replacement for the handpiece.

## 4. Sono-abrasion and ultrasonic preparation

### Explanation

Pickard's: sonic and ultrasonic instruments "use the principle of probe tip oscillation and micro-cavitation to chip away hard dental tissues" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Modern Operative Dentistry: they "vibrate or oscillate" rather than rotate; sonic units run at about 6000-8000 Hz "depending on the brand", ultrasonic at 25,000-35,000 Hz, mostly piezoelectric (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Marzouk's older view: "ultrasonic instruments are not universally used for cavity preparations" (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Summitt's newer view: a "viable means for conservative cavity preparation" (Summitt, 2013) [SRC], helped by safe-sided tips in which "the diamond abrasive is only present on one side of the tip" (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC] and by CVD diamond, made "through a process called chemical vapor deposition" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC] because nickel-bonded diamond "cannot withstand ultrasonic power" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. More pressure "significantly decreases the grinding effectiveness"; the ideal is "the weight of the handpiece itself"; preparation is "more comfortable for the patient", slower than rotary, harmless to soft tissue, and contraindicated with pacemakers (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Pulpal temperature rise is no greater than with water-cooled rotary cutting, and sonic preparation does not impair adhesion (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** Oscillating diamond tips abrade tissue by vibration and micro-cavitation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sonic (air-driven, about 6000-8000 Hz) and ultrasonic (25,000-35,000 Hz, piezoelectric) units (Gonçalves et al, 2020).

**Advances:** safe-sided tips coated on one side only protect the adjacent tooth in proximal preparations (Hilton and Broome, 2013); CVD diamond tips, since conventional nickel-bonded diamond fails under ultrasound (Garg and Garg, 2011; Gonçalves et al, 2020).

**Advantages:** conservative access, comfort, no soft-tissue damage, pulpal heating and adhesion comparable to rotary preparation (Gonçalves et al, 2020; Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**Disadvantages:** slower; light pressure only; not for bulk reduction; avoid with cardiac pacemakers (Gonçalves et al, 2020).

## 5. Lasers

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: the middle-infrared lasers, "Er:YAG of 2940 nm", Er:YSGG 2790 nm and Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm, are used for cavity preparation, and "the efficiency of ablation is greatest for Er:YAG laser" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mechanism: "an explosive subsurface expansion of interstitially trapped water"; carious tissue is "ablated selectively at lower pulse energies"; clinical histology showed no pulp injury; only 2-5% "of patients will request local anaesthesia"; the ablation sound helps judge when caries removal is complete (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. No smear layer is produced (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]; Poli and co-workers list "no vibration, non-contact" among the advantages (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]. Laser units are "relatively expensive" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Limits: enamel cutting at "<30% bur speed" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]; "generation of a defined margin or tooth preparation surface is not possible"; lasers cannot remove amalgam or ceramic, and "may never replace the high-speed dental handpiece" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Bonding: Mount reports "little difference in terms of bond strengths" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Goldstein's chapter and Poli report lower adhesion unless lased surfaces are acid etched (Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018; Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Lasers used:** Er:YAG (2940 nm), Er:YSGG (2790 nm), Er,Cr:YSGG (2780 nm); Er:YAG ablates most efficiently (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Mechanism:** water absorbs the energy and explodes subsurface, ejecting apatite; carious tissue ablates selectively (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Advantages:** little or no anaesthesia (2-5% request it), no pulp injury in clinical histology, selective caries removal with acoustic feedback (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016); no smear layer (Gonçalves et al, 2020); no vibration (Poli et al, 2023).

**Disadvantages:** slow on enamel (Poli et al, 2023); no defined margins; cannot remove amalgam or ceramic; costly (Ritter et al, 2019). Bond strength: similar (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) or lower without acid etching (Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018; Poli et al, 2023), so phosphoric acid etching after lasing is advised.

## 6. Chemo-mechanical caries removal

### Explanation

Carisolv "was developed by Dan Ericsson in Sweden in the early 1990s"; Mount gives "0.5 % sodium hypochlorite", leucine, lysine and glutamic acid, and sodium hydroxide to pH 11 (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson also give 0.5% (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Pickard's gives "0.1% hypochlorite-based alkaline gel (pH 11)" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Chloramines break down denatured collagen "without accompanying demineralization", and the amino acids make the action "specific for denatured collagen" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Cavitated access is required (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; blunt instruments give "a whisking rather than penetrating action" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC], at "the same force as normal tooth brushing" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; gel is renewed until fresh gel no longer turns opaque (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Long-term clinical outcomes equal conventional preparation; indications include anxiety, needle phobia and anaesthetic allergy, and "It is also well suited to paediatric patients"; it "will not complete the entire cavity design" or remove old restorations (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Papacarie is listed only as a papain-based gel (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Carisolv** (Ericsson, early 1990s): sodium hypochlorite 0.5% (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) or 0.1% (Banerjee and Watson, 2015), with leucine, lysine and glutamic acid at pH 11. Chloramines break down denatured collagen of infected dentine only. **Papacarie** is papain based (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**Technique:** cavitated access, apply gel, whisk softened dentine with blunt instruments at tooth-brushing force, repeat until gel stays clear, then restore.

**Advantages:** self-limiting, less anaesthesia, preserves sound dentine, outcomes equal to conventional methods; useful in anxious, needle-phobic or allergic patients and children (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016). **Disadvantages:** slower; needs access; does not complete the cavity or remove restorations (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

## 7. Comparison and conclusion

### Explanation

Mount's "Relative effectiveness of cavity preparation technologies" table rates hand excavators and Carisolv as acting only on carious dentine, air abrasion as moderate on sound enamel but slow on dentine, ultrasonics slow on sound tissue, and Er:YAG moderate on sound and rapid on carious tissue but ineffective on metal (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Pickard's concludes "at present no perfect instrument exists" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Method | Acts on | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| Hand instruments | Carious dentine, enamel margins | Final excavation, margin planing, ART |
| Air abrasion | Enamel, resin, surfaces to be bonded | Fissures, minimal entry |
| Sono/ultrasonic | Enamel, dentine, carious dentine | Proximal boxes |
| Er:YAG laser | Enamel, dentine, caries (not metal) | Small lesions, anxious patients |
| Carisolv | Infected dentine only | Soft caries without anaesthesia |

**Conclusion.** Non-rotary methods are selective, conservative and comfortable, but each is slower or narrower in scope than the bur; they are adjuncts chosen by substrate and patient, often combined with rotary instruments, because no perfect instrument exists (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: "Non-rotary methods produce cavities as good as burs for bonding."**
*Textbook says:* air abrasion leaves a surface "more suitable for adhesive bonding" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; laser bond strengths show "little difference" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; sonic preparation does not affect adhesion (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].
*Challenge:* acid etching "always produces a better bond than air abrasion alone" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; adhesion to lased dentine was often "much lower" than with acid, largely when single-component adhesives were used without etching (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].
*Strength:* mostly in vitro bond-strength and SEM data. [Certain about the type]
*Conclusion and clinical implication:* the cavity surface is acceptable for bonding only when phosphoric acid etching is still used; write both positions with their authors. [Likely]

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Discussing only lasers and air abrasion and leaving out hand instruments, which are the original non-rotary method.
- Writing that air abrasion removes soft caries efficiently.
- Claiming lasers can remove amalgam or cut defined margins.
- Stating one Carisolv concentration as fact without noting that the books differ.
- Omitting acid etching after air abrasion or laser preparation.
