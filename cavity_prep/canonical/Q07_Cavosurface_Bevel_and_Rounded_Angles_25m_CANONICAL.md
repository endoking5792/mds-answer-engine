## 1. "Integrity of a restoration": where it fails

### Explanation

A restored tooth fails at three places: the margin (marginal fracture of tooth or restoration, gap, leakage, secondary caries), the restoration body (bulk fracture) and the remaining tooth (cusp or wall fracture). Fracture of enamel rods forming the cavity wall or cavosurface margin creates a gap defect (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC], through which leakage and bacteria may cause secondary caries. Occlusal contact directly on the marginal interface causes early fatigue and failure of the margin (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. The cavosurface bevel acts on the first; rounded angles act on all three.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Integrity means the tooth-restoration unit survives without marginal breakdown, bulk fracture of the restoration or fracture of the tooth. Fracture of enamel rods at the cavosurface margin creates a gap defect that leaks and leads to secondary caries (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013); occlusal contact on the margin causes early fatigue failure (Boushell and Walter, 2019). Two design features protect integrity: the **cavosurface bevel** (or its deliberate absence, the butt joint) and **rounded angles**.

## 2. Cavosurface bevel and marginal integrity

### Explanation

**Principle.** Noy (as given by Marzouk) requires the rods forming the cavosurface angle to rest on sound dentin (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; covering their outer ends with restorative material gives the strongest wall but needs a bevel (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and suits only materials stronger and tougher than tooth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; for weaker, brittle materials a wall parallel to the rods is the maximum (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; sharp occlusal margins must be trimmed (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

**Cast gold.** Bevels create obtuse, bulky, strong marginal tooth structure (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] [SRC]; they reduce the marginal error factor (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], threefold or more (Singh, 2020) [SRC]; bevelling gives the strongest type of enamel margin (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC] and a burnishable thin gold edge (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], with a lap, sliding fit gingivally (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]. Metal is 40 degrees except 30 degrees gingivally (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]; enamel above 150 degrees gives an ill-defined margin (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]. Challenge: bevels beyond 45 degrees increase the cement-filled opening (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

**Composite.** Bonds to etched rod ends are twice those to rod sides (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]; a 45 degree bevel exposes rod ends (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]; bevelling adds bonding area (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC] and may prevent enamel microcracks and crazing from polymerisation stress (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. But occlusal bevels put tapering resin into stress areas (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC] and wore more in a clinical study (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and enamel bonds are clinically adequate without bevels (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Amalgam, GIC, ceramic: integrity by not bevelling.** Low edge strength makes a 90 degree angle strongest for amalgam and glass-ceramic (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; a bevel would leave a thin, fracture-prone margin (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. An acute angle fractures enamel; an obtuse one fractures marginal amalgam (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC], causing ditches (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]; survival of amalgam falls with the amount of unsupported enamel (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]. GIC is brittle and needs bulk (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], about 1 mm at a butt joint (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; thin ceramic and composite inlay margins chip (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Functions of the cavosurface bevel (cast gold).**
1. Removes weak, unsupported enamel; covers rod ends with metal, giving the strongest enamel wall (Noy, in Marzouk et al, undated; Boushell and Walter, 2019).
2. Produces obtuse (140-150°) marginal enamel and acute (40°, 30° gingivally) burnishable metal (Sturdevant JR, 2019).
3. Reduces the marginal error factor threefold or more (Singh, 2020); lap, sliding fit gingivally (Sturdevant JR, 2019).
4. Flexible extension: includes defects, places margins in cleansable areas.

**Types (Marzouk et al, undated):** partial, short, long, full, counter, hollow-ground; reverse bevel as an internal gingival-floor feature.

**Bevel in composite.** Acid-etched rod ends bond about twice as strongly as rod sides; a 45° bevel across a 90° margin exposes the ends, adds bonding area and may prevent enamel crazing from polymerisation shrinkage (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013). It also blends the restoration esthetically. On occlusal surfaces the rods are already cut end-on, a bevel only leaves thin resin in a stress area, and bevelled occlusal margins wore more clinically (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**Bevel by material and its effect on integrity.**

| Material | Bevel? | Integrity reason |
|---|---|---|
| Cast gold | Yes | Strong enamel + burnishable metal edge |
| Composite | Accessible enamel, anterior; not occlusal | Rod-end bonding (2×), less crazing; occlusal bevels wear and chip |
| Amalgam | No, 90° butt | Low edge strength: bevel → thin amalgam fractures → ditch |
| GIC/RMGI | No, 90° butt, ~1 mm | Brittle, needs bulk |
| Ceramic | No, butt joint | Thin margins chip |

**Critique.** Fan, Stevenson and Berry (2013) show bevels beyond 45° *widen* the cement line in laboratory models; Vargas et al (2013) report adequate clinical enamel bonding without bevels. A bevel helps integrity only for a material strong at a thin edge or one bonding better to rod ends.

## 3. Rounded angles: tooth and restoration fracture

### Explanation

**Sturdevant.** All transitions between prepared surfaces are designed to be smooth and rounded, not sharp, to limit stress concentration (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Its "third principle": internal line angles are slightly rounded to limit stress concentration, increasing the tooth's resistance to fracture (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; rounding the angles that face the restoration (e.g. axiopulpal) increases resistance to fracture of the restorative material (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. External enamel-wall junctions should be slightly curved (softened) (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. The gingival margin trimmer is used to bevel or round the axiopulpal line angle to limit stress in the restoration (Boushell and Donovan, 2019) [SRC].

**Marzouk.** Enamel-wall junctions should be very rounded even if the dentinal junctions are angular (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]: this decreases stress concentration (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], improves adaptation at corners (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and avoids frail corner rods (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. For amalgam, all line and point angles are rounded but definite (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], keeping the mortise form while avoiding stress concentration in tooth and material (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Circumferential tie junctions are rounded without sharp angles (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], avoiding stress concentration and non-coverage of the margin (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], but only at the tie, not internally (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

**Cast gold: definite or rounded? The books disagree.** Marzouk's inlay pulpal floor meets surrounding walls in definite line angles (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] except at the axial wall, where the joint is very rounded (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], as in amalgam (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], preventing stress concentration in tooth and casting (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Sturdevant's No. 271 bur meets its end slightly rounded so sharp, stress-inducing internal angles are not formed (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC], and the axiopulpal angle is lightly bevelled (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC] for a thicker, stronger wax pattern (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's Fan et al want internal angles well defined (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC] and "precise", with only occlusal line angles rounded (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Singh's comparison table puts rounded line and point angles under amalgam (Singh, 2020) [SRC] and well-defined ones under the inlay (Singh, 2020) [SRC]. The resolution: definite angles help a casting seat in one position and retain by friction; rounding is concentrated where stress concentrates (axiopulpal) [Likely].

**Brittle cast materials and ceramics.** Non-noble alloys and cast ceramics need rounded internal and external anatomy (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; for Class IV (Ni-Cr) internal angles should be more rounded (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], for cast ceramics very rounded (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], and grooves for ceramics have no definite internal line angles (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Ceramic inlay internal angles are rounded (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; ceramic crowns need a rounded axiogingival line angle (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].

**Composite.** Avoid sharp internal angles, which increase stress concentration (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; pear-shaped instruments give 90 degree margins without sharp internal angles (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Mechanism [RECALL].** A sharp re-entrant corner concentrates tensile stress at its apex, the stress-concentration factor rising as the radius falls; cracks start there in brittle materials (enamel, amalgam, ceramic, composite) and propagate along the cusp. Rounding spreads the load over a larger area and also lets the material adapt without voids. Sharp angles are also hard to fill without voids by condensation or injection.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Rounded angles: why (Boushell and Walter, 2019).**
- Internal line angles slightly rounded limit stress concentration and *increase the tooth's resistance to fracture*.
- Rounding angles facing the restoration (axiopulpal) *increases resistance to fracture of the restorative material*; the gingival margin trimmer rounds the axiopulpal angle for this (Boushell and Donovan, 2019).
- External enamel-wall junctions softened.
- Marzouk et al (undated): enamel junctions rounded to cut stress, improve adaptation and avoid frail corner rods.
- Mechanism: a sharp corner concentrates tensile stress where cracks start; rounding spreads it; rounded corners also fill without voids.

**By material.**

| Material | Angles | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Amalgam | Rounded but definite (keeps mortise form) | Marzouk et al, undated |
| Composite | No sharp internal angles | Garg and Garg, 2011; Ahmed et al, 2019 |
| Cast gold | Definite internal angles; axiopulpal rounded/bevelled; tie junctions rounded | Marzouk et al, undated; Sturdevant JR, 2019 |
| Cast gold (alternative) | Internal angles "precise", occlusal angles rounded | Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013 |
| Ni-Cr, cast ceramic | More rounded to very rounded | Marzouk et al, undated |
| Ceramic inlay/crown | Rounded internal and axiogingival angles | Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013; Rouse, 2013 |

**Axiopulpal line angle (Class II).** The most stressed internal angle: it is rounded or lightly bevelled in amalgam (Marzouk et al, undated) and in inlays, where the bevel also gives a thicker, stronger wax pattern (Sturdevant JR, 2019).

**External junctions.** Junctions between bevels, flares and counterbevels of the circumferential tie are rounded to avoid stress concentration, non-coverage of the margin and minor undercuts, but only at the tie, not internally (Marzouk et al, undated).

**Disagreement.** For gold, Singh (2020) and Fan et al (2013) prefer well-defined internal angles (seating, frictional retention) while Sturdevant JR (2019) uses a round-cornered bur; all round the axiopulpal angle.

## 4. Critical analysis

### Explanation

Both features protect integrity by matching geometry to material strength, but the evidence is uneven. The bevel literature quoted in these chapters is largely in vitro (cement-film models, tracer microleakage, bond strengths); the clinical data quoted (occlusal-bevel wear, amalgam survival versus unsupported enamel) favour removing weak enamel and avoiding thin margins rather than routine bevelling [Likely]. For rounded angles, the books give a mechanical rationale but no quantified clinical comparison [Certain: none was found in the chapters read]. The trade-off is retention: sharper, more parallel internal form retains a casting; rounding sacrifices some, which Marzouk compensates with less taper in brittle-material preparations (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Q05 covers retention form.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Critical analysis.**
1. Both features are material-specific: a bevel strengthens a gold margin but weakens amalgam, GIC and ceramic margins; rounding helps every material.
2. Evidence: bevel claims rest mainly on laboratory models and microleakage studies; clinical data favour removing unsupported enamel (amalgam survival: Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) and avoiding occlusal bevels (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
3. Rounding has a sound mechanical basis and agreement across books, but no quantified clinical trial was found; its cost is some loss of frictional retention in cast preparations.
4. Other integrity factors matter as much: planing friable enamel (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013), keeping margins off occlusal contacts, and adequate material bulk.

**Conclusion.** Bevel only where the material is strong at a thin edge or bonds better to rod ends; round every internal and enamel junction, keeping definite (not sharp) angles where a casting needs seating and retention, and keep every margin away from occlusal contacts.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: rounding internal line angles increases fracture resistance of tooth and restoration.** Textbook: Boushell and Walter (2019) state it as a principle for both tooth and restoration; Marzouk et al (undated) apply it to amalgam, enamel junctions and brittle cast materials; Garg and Garg (2011) to composite. Other evidence: no clinical comparison was quoted in any chapter read; Fan, Stevenson and Berry (2013) and Singh (2020) still prefer well-defined internal angles for gold inlays, for seating and retention. Agreement or challenge: agreement on rounding for brittle materials; partial challenge for cast gold internal form. Strength: mechanical reasoning and textbook consensus, not clinical trial. Conclusion: round all line angles slightly; avoid sharp, not definite, angles. [Likely] Clinical implication: use round-cornered burs (No. 271 type, pear-shaped) and round the axiopulpal angle in every Class II preparation.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Gap defect from fractured marginal enamel → leakage → secondary caries.
2. Bevel strengthens the margin only if the material is stronger than enamel (gold) or bonds to rod ends (composite).
3. Gold margin: 40° metal, 140-150° enamel, 30° metal gingivally.
4. No bevel for amalgam, GIC, ceramic: low edge strength.
5. Rounded internal angles: tooth fracture resistance; rounded axiopulpal angle: restoration fracture resistance (Boushell and Walter, 2019).
6. Amalgam angles "rounded but definite"; gold inlay angles definite except axiopulpal.
7. Ni-Cr and ceramics need very rounded internal form.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Saying bevels improve integrity of all restorations.
2. Writing "sharp line angles for retention" for amalgam; Marzouk says rounded but definite.
3. Omitting the restoration side: rounded axiopulpal angles protect the restoration, not only the tooth.
4. Claiming clinical trials prove rounded angles; the support is mechanical and consensus.
5. Ignoring the disagreement over internal angles in cast gold preparations.
