# Critically analyze importance of Cavo surface bevel and rounded angles in the integrity of a restoration.

**Marks:** 25 · Essay

## 1. "Integrity of a restoration": where it fails

Integrity means the tooth-restoration unit survives without marginal breakdown, bulk fracture of the restoration or fracture of the tooth. Fracture of enamel rods at the cavosurface margin creates a gap defect that leaks and leads to secondary caries (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013); occlusal contact on the margin causes early fatigue failure (Boushell and Walter, 2019). Two design features protect integrity: the **cavosurface bevel** (or its deliberate absence, the butt joint) and **rounded angles**.

## 2. Cavosurface bevel and marginal integrity

**Functions of the cavosurface bevel (cast gold).**
1. Removes weak, unsupported enamel; covers rod ends with metal, giving the strongest enamel wall (Noy, in Marzouk et al., n.d.; Boushell and Walter, 2019).
2. Produces obtuse (140-150°) marginal enamel and acute (40°, 30° gingivally) burnishable metal (Sturdevant JR, 2019).
3. Reduces the marginal error factor threefold or more (Singh, 2020); lap, sliding fit gingivally (Sturdevant JR, 2019).
4. Flexible extension: includes defects, places margins in cleansable areas.

**Types (Marzouk et al., n.d.):** partial, short, long, full, counter, hollow-ground; reverse bevel as an internal gingival-floor feature.

**Bevel in composite.** Acid-etched rod ends bond about twice as strongly as rod sides; a 45° bevel across a 90° margin exposes the ends, adds bonding area and may prevent enamel crazing from polymerisation shrinkage (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013). It also blends the restoration esthetically. On occlusal surfaces the rods are already cut end-on, a bevel only leaves thin resin in a stress area, and bevelled occlusal margins wore more clinically (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**Bevel by material and its effect on integrity.**

| Material | Bevel? | Integrity reason |
|---|---|---|
| Cast gold | Yes | Strong enamel + burnishable metal edge |
| Composite | Accessible enamel, anterior; not occlusal | Rod-end bonding (2×), less crazing; occlusal bevels wear and chip |
| Amalgam | No, 90° butt | Low edge strength: bevel → thin amalgam fractures → ditch |
| GIC/RMGI | No, 90° butt, ~1 mm | Brittle, needs bulk |
| Ceramic | No, butt joint | Thin margins chip |

**Critique.** Fan, Stevenson and Berry (2013) show bevels beyond 45° *widen* the cement line in laboratory models; Vargas et al (2013) report adequate clinical enamel bonding without bevels. A bevel helps integrity only for a material strong at a thin edge or one bonding better to rod ends.

## 3. Rounded angles: tooth and restoration fracture

**Rounded angles: why (Boushell and Walter, 2019).**
- Internal line angles slightly rounded limit stress concentration and *increase the tooth's resistance to fracture*.
- Rounding angles facing the restoration (axiopulpal) *increases resistance to fracture of the restorative material*; the gingival margin trimmer rounds the axiopulpal angle for this (Boushell and Donovan, 2019).
- External enamel-wall junctions softened.
- Marzouk et al (undated): enamel junctions rounded to cut stress, improve adaptation and avoid frail corner rods.
- Finite-element analysis found lower stresses along the adhesive interface with smooth, rounded cavity shapes; this supports the biomechanical rationale but does not establish a clinical survival benefit (Hubsch et al., 2002).

**By material.**

| Material | Angles | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Amalgam | Rounded but definite (keeps mortise form) | Marzouk et al., n.d. |
| Composite | No sharp internal angles | Garg and Garg, 2011; Ahmed et al., 2019 |
| Cast gold | Definite internal angles; axiopulpal rounded/bevelled; tie junctions rounded | Marzouk et al., n.d.; Sturdevant JR, 2019 |
| Cast gold (alternative) | Internal angles "precise", occlusal angles rounded | Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013 |
| Ni-Cr, cast ceramic | More rounded to very rounded | Marzouk et al., n.d. |
| Ceramic inlay/crown | Rounded internal and axiogingival angles | Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013; Rouse, 2013 |

**Axiopulpal line angle (Class II).** The most stressed internal angle: it is rounded or lightly bevelled in amalgam (Marzouk et al., n.d.) and in inlays, where the bevel also gives a thicker, stronger wax pattern (Sturdevant JR, 2019).

**External junctions.** Junctions between bevels, flares and counterbevels of the circumferential tie are rounded to avoid stress concentration, non-coverage of the margin and minor undercuts, but only at the tie, not internally (Marzouk et al., n.d.).

**Disagreement.** For gold, Singh (2020) and Fan et al (2013) prefer well-defined internal angles (seating, frictional retention) while Sturdevant JR (2019) uses a round-cornered bur; all round the axiopulpal angle.

## 4. Critical analysis

**Critical analysis.**
1. Both features are material-specific: a bevel strengthens a gold margin but weakens amalgam, GIC and ceramic margins; rounding helps every material.
2. Evidence: bevel claims rest mainly on laboratory models and microleakage studies; clinical data favour removing unsupported enamel (amalgam survival: Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) and avoiding occlusal bevels (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
3. Rounding has a sound mechanical basis and agreement across books, but no quantified clinical trial was found; its cost is some loss of frictional retention in cast preparations.
4. Other integrity factors matter as much: planing friable enamel (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013), keeping margins off occlusal contacts, and adequate material bulk.

**Conclusion.** Bevel only where the material is strong at a thin edge or bonds better to rod ends; round every internal and enamel junction, keeping definite (not sharp) angles where a casting needs seating and retention, and keep every margin away from occlusal contacts.
