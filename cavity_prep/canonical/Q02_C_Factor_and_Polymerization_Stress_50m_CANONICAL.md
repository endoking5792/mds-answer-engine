## 1. Introduction, definition and origin

### Explanation

Resin composite shrinks as it polymerizes. When it is bonded to one surface only, as in a direct facial veneer, the stresses inside it are relieved by flow from the unbonded surface (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]. Inside a three-dimensional bonded preparation that relief is limited by the configuration factor (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]. The concept was proposed by Feilzer et al in 1987 as the ratio of bonded (B) to unbonded (U) surface area, C = B/U (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]; Summitt's records the same 1987 finding that shrinkage stress was related to the C-factor (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC]. Garg and Garg attribute it instead to Davidson and colleagues in the 1980s (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. The two attributions may refer to the same body of work, but the original paper was not checked this session [Guessing].

The physical reasoning is that flow happens over the free surface: where the bonded-to-free ratio is small, flow occurs readily and interfacial stress stays low, so the ratio "can therefore be used to predict interfacial stress" (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Phillips' puts it as residual polymerization stress increasing directly with the ratio (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. The word "predict" is the point the question asks you to analyse critically (section 7).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** All methacrylate resin composites shrink on polymerization. Shrinkage itself cannot be avoided; what matters clinically is the **stress** it generates at the bonded tooth-restoration interface. How much of that stress is relieved depends on how free the composite is to flow, and this is governed by the geometry of the preparation.

**Definition.** The **cavity configuration factor (C-factor)** is the ratio of the bonded surface area of a restoration to its unbonded (free) surface area:

**C-factor = bonded surface area / unbonded surface area (B/U)**

**Origin.** Proposed by Feilzer and co-workers in 1987 (Scotti et al, 2020; Breschi et al, 2013); Garg and Garg (2011) credit Davidson and colleagues in the 1980s.

**Principle.** A composite bonded to only one surface (a direct veneer) relieves its shrinkage stress by flowing from the free surface. In a three-dimensional preparation the free surface is small, so flow cannot compensate and stress builds up at the bonded walls (Perdigão et al, 2019). The ratio of bonded to free area is therefore used to indicate the interfacial stress likely to be caused by shrinkage (McCabe and Walls, 2008); residual stress rises with the ratio (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 2. C-factor values in different preparations

### Explanation

McCabe and Walls explain the scale with a cube of resin: unconstrained, C = 0; bonded on one of six faces, C = 0.2; bonded on two, C = 0.5; bonded on five, C = 5 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. A Class I preparation bonds to five surfaces (mesial, distal, buccal, lingual, pulpal) with only the occlusal surface free, giving C = 5 (Perdigão et al, 2019; Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Scotti and co-workers give 5 for the Class I box, 2 for a Class II simple proximal box (buccal, lingual, axial and gingival bonded; occlusal and proximal free), and 0.2 for a fractured anterior tooth with one bonded and five free surfaces (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. Garg and Garg tabulate Class I and V = 5, Class II = 2, Class III = 1 (three-walled preparation) [SRC], Class IV = 0.5 (two-walled preparation) [SRC] and a smooth-surface restoration = 0.2, and state that Class I preparations have the highest, most unfavourable C-factor (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls agree that a Class IV cavity has a C-factor of 0.5 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Summitt's says the C-factor is favourable for most anterior restorations (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC], and Sturdevant's describes the Class IV as having "a wealth of unbonded surface area" (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC].

Extremes: a thin constrained luting layer can have C-factor values greater than 10 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. For the root canal, Sturdevant's says the C-factor may exceed 200 while coronal restorations range from 1 to 5 (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC], and Summitt's says the same for post spaces (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC]; Phillips' gives "over 1000" for root canals (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. **The books disagree on the root-canal figure (above 200 versus above 1000).** Both are estimates for a geometry that changes with canal length and taper, so write "may exceed 200; some estimate over 1000" and name both books.

Two cautions. First, the C-factor is a ratio of **areas**, not of wall counts: two Class I preparations with the same five walls but different depths differ, and the deeper one has the greater C-factor (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. The textbook values are therefore approximations for average-sized preparations. Second, Garg and Garg give Class V the same value as Class I; this holds only for a box-form Class V, since a saucer-shaped cervical preparation has far less wall area (my reasoning from the area definition [Likely]).

#### ✎ Exam core

| Situation | Bonded : free surfaces | C-factor |
|---|---|---|
| Unconstrained mass of resin | 0 : 6 | 0 |
| Fractured incisal edge / one-surface veneer | 1 : 5 | 0.2 |
| Class IV | 2 : 4 | 0.5 |
| Class III | 3 : 3 | 1 |
| Class II simple proximal box | 4 : 2 | 2 |
| Class I (and box-form Class V) | 5 : 1 | 5 |
| Thin luting cement layer (inlay, veneer, crown) | very large bonded area, tiny free edge | > 10 |
| Root canal / post space | almost entirely bonded | > 200 (Perdigão et al, 2019; Breschi et al, 2013); > 1000 (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) |

(Values: McCabe and Walls, 2008; Scotti et al, 2020; Garg and Garg, 2011.)

**Points to note.**
- Class I is the most unfavourable direct restoration; Class IV and incisal-edge restorations the most favourable (Garg and Garg, 2011; McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Coronal direct restorations range from about 1 to 5 (Perdigão et al, 2019).
- The C-factor is an **area** ratio: for the same five-walled Class I, a deeper preparation has a higher C-factor than a shallow one (Scotti et al, 2020).

## 3. Polymerization shrinkage and the origin of stress

### Explanation

**How much composite shrinks: the books give different ranges.** Sturdevant's Ch.16 gives 2.2-2.4% [SRC] in one paragraph and 2.4-2.8% [SRC] in its summary (Donovan et al, 2019); Summitt's gives volumetric shrinkage of 1.5-5.0% (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]; Garg and Garg give 1.67-5.68% of total volume (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; Scotti and co-workers cite a review with an average of 2-3% and up to 7% (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. The figures differ because they cover different composite types and measurement methods; Sturdevant's two figures in one chapter are probably from different sources. Shrinkage also continues for 24 hours after curing (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC].

**Shrinkage is not stress.** Sturdevant's lists the variables that set shrinkage stress besides the C-factor: rate of polymerization, degree of conversion, composite stiffness and its rate of acquisition of stiffness during polymerization (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC], filler type and volume, monomer type, insertion technique and opacity. Phillips' adds total volume of composite and polymerization speed (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Shrinkage stress is more closely related to stiffness than to the percentage shrinkage (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]; high-stiffness materials generate more stress (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC] and rigid resins produce high stresses (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC]. For this reason low-shrinkage composites do not necessarily have lower shrinkage stress (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Garg and Garg state that "the higher the value of C-factor, the greater is the polymerization shrinkage" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. This is plainly wrong as written:** volumetric shrinkage is a property of the material and is the same whatever the cavity shape. What a high C-factor raises is the **stress** produced by that shrinkage, as every other source states (Perdigão et al, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Write "stress", not "shrinkage".

#### ✎ Exam core

**Magnitude of shrinkage.** Values differ between books: 2.2-2.8% (Donovan et al, 2019), 1.5-5.0% volumetric (Vargas et al, 2013), 1.67-5.68% (Garg and Garg, 2011), average 2-3% and up to 7% (Scotti et al, 2020). Shrinkage continues for about 24 hours after light exposure ("dark" or post-polymerization) (Scotti et al, 2020).

**Shrinkage versus stress.** Volumetric shrinkage is a material property; **stress** is what damages the bond. By Hooke's law, stress depends on the strain (shrinkage) and the stiffness (elastic modulus) of the material, and it is more closely related to stiffness than to percentage shrinkage (Scotti et al, 2020). Therefore a low-shrinkage composite does not necessarily produce less stress (Perdigão et al, 2019).

**Factors determining polymerization stress** (Perdigão et al, 2019; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013):
1. C-factor (configuration of the preparation)
2. Volume of composite cured at one time
3. Rate of polymerization and degree of conversion
4. Stiffness of the composite and how fast it is acquired
5. Filler type and volume; monomer type
6. Insertion technique and curing mode
7. Compliance of the surrounding tooth (section 7)

**A common error.** Garg and Garg (2011) write that a higher C-factor gives greater polymerization **shrinkage**. Cavity shape cannot change the volumetric shrinkage of a material; it changes the **stress** produced, as stated by Perdigão et al (2019) and McCabe and Walls (2008).

## 4. Flow and the gel point

### Explanation

Curing passes through pre-gel, gel point and post-gel phases (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. In the pre-gel phase linear chains can still slide and the material flows, so little stress reaches the interface. When conversion reaches about 10-20% the chains form a gel (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. After the gel point stresses cannot be relieved and concentrate at the bonded interfaces (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; Summitt's calls this rigidity "gelation" and says later stresses are not relieved by material flow (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls stress that the post-gel component is the clinically important part of shrinkage, which is why it must be identified when shrinkage is measured (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC], and that stress measurement may be more pertinent to clinical consequences than shrinkage measurement (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Post-gel stress is transmitted to the interface and tooth and can break the bond, deflect cusps or crack enamel (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC], at levels of 4-8 MPa or more (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC].

**Light-cured versus chemically cured.** Chemically cured composites take more than 3 minutes to set, giving a longer pre-gel phase (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]; their mixing porosity also relaxes stress (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC], so an autocured composite induces less stress than the same bulk of light-cured composite (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], although other problems argue against its posterior use (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. With light curing, polymerization starts at the surface nearest the light, which removes that surface as a stress-relief pathway (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]; in effect the "free" occlusal surface becomes rigid first.

**Direction of shrinkage: an outdated statement.** Garg and Garg say light-cured composite shrinks in the direction of the light (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Summitt's records that this was believed for many years (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], but research shows shrinkage occurs toward the walls to which the composite is most strongly bonded (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; Versluis and co-workers' finite element work showed composites shrink toward the bonded walls, not the light (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. Lösche showed that reflective wedges help only because they reduce transmitted light and delay the gel point (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. Garg and Garg's statement should not be reproduced.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Three phases of polymerization** (Scotti et al, 2020):
1. **Pre-gel phase:** short, poorly linked chains; the material can flow and rearrange, so shrinkage creates little interfacial stress.
2. **Gel point:** at about 10-20% conversion a cross-linked network forms; flow stops.
3. **Post-gel phase:** further shrinkage cannot be compensated by flow; stress is transmitted to the interface and tooth, causing bond rupture, cuspal deflection or enamel microcracks (4-8 MPa or more).

After the gel point stresses cannot be relieved but concentrate near the bonded interfaces (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013). Only the post-gel part of shrinkage is clinically significant (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**Link with C-factor.** Flow in the pre-gel phase can only take place from free surfaces. A high C-factor means little free surface, so less of the shrinkage is compensated before the gel point and more stress remains.

**Light-cured versus chemically cured.** Chemically cured composites set over more than 3 minutes, have a longer pre-gel phase and contain porosity that relaxes stress, so they generate less stress (Scotti et al, 2020; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013). Light curing starts at the surface nearest the light, which then cannot act as a relief pathway (Perdigão et al, 2019).

**Direction of shrinkage.** The old view that light-cured composite shrinks toward the light (still stated by Garg and Garg, 2011) has been replaced: composite shrinks toward the walls to which it is bonded (Hilton and Broome, 2013), as finite element analysis by Versluis and co-workers showed (Scotti et al, 2020).

## 5. Effects of C-factor-related stress on the bond and margins

### Explanation

Unrelieved stresses cause internal bond disruption and marginal gaps, which may increase microleakage and postoperative sensitivity (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]. Gaps form especially at margins with the weakest bonds, usually dentin or cementum (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]. The C-factor may be partly responsible for the lower bond strengths seen when deep dentin is bonded in a three-dimensional preparation (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]. In the Class I preparation the pulpal wall is the interface most affected (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sturdevant's Ch.16 describes the weakest bond, at the pulpal floor, giving way and leaving a fluid-filled gap and sensitivity (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC]. If the bond holds, the tooth itself deforms (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC], and in extreme cases cusps can fracture (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Immediate bond strengths of about 17 MPa are said to be needed to resist contraction stresses and prevent marginal debonding (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]. At the gingival margin of Class II restorations one clinical study rated only 27% as satisfactory (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; on root surfaces Garg and Garg describe gap formation between composite and root surface (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Debonding and marginal gap | Stress exceeds bond strength; gaps form first where the bond is weakest, at dentin or cementum margins (Vargas et al, 2013) |
| Microleakage, staining, recurrent caries | Through the marginal gap (Perdigão et al, 2019) |
| Postoperative sensitivity | Pulpal-floor debonding in Class I leaves a fluid-filled gap (Donovan et al, 2019; Ritter et al, 2019) |
| Reduced bond strength to deep dentin | Partly attributed to high C-factor (Perdigão et al, 2019) |
| Cuspal deflection, enamel cracks, cusp fracture | When the bond holds, the tooth deforms instead (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Internal stresses in the composite | Weaken the restoration |

Immediate bond strength of about 17 MPa is quoted as necessary to resist contraction stress (Perdigão et al, 2019). The gingival margin of Class II restorations is the weakest point; in one clinical study only 27% were satisfactory (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

## 6. Strategies to overcome C-factor and polymerization stress

### Explanation

Shrinkage cannot be avoided, but the stress can be reduced by placement technique (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sturdevant's Ch.16 lists five strategies (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's lists incremental placement, long enamel bevels, low-shrinkage materials, flexible resin liners and slow-setting resin-modified glass-ionomer liners (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC], and modified light-curing protocols. **None has completely eliminated the deleterious effects of shrinkage stress (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC].**

**(a) Preparation design.** A boxlike Class I preparation may increase the negative C-factor effect (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; a narrower, less bulky restoration reduces the adverse effects of shrinkage (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and rounded internal angles reduce stress concentration (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].

**(b) Incremental and oblique layering.** Keep the C-factor close to 0.5 with oblique increments joining at most two walls (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. Thinner layers reduce bonded area and increase free area (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. With incremental placement the C-factor of each increment is lower than in bulk (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and as C-factor falls bond strength rises (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; conversely, as C-factor rises stresses increase dramatically (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Horizontal layers create more stress than oblique ones (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC], and bulk techniques performed worse than incremental ones in vitro and in vivo (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. Sturdevant's Ch.19 says increments may reduce the negative C-factor effects (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] but adds that, regardless of the effect on stress, increments are needed for curing and anatomy (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], a guarded view. **Increment thickness differs between books:** no more than 2 mm (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; first gingival increment no thicker than 1 mm (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; wedge-shaped layers of 1-1.5 mm (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

**(c) Low-modulus intermediate layers.** Sturdevant's Ch.16 recommends a thick, low-modulus primed adhesive layer (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC], a 0.5 mm RMGI liner (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC] and a 0.5 mm flowable liner (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC]. **Other chapters and books disagree.** Sturdevant's Ch.15 says flowable intermediate layers have not been proved effective clinically (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC] and a GIC base did not improve survival at 18 years (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]; Ch.19 says the liner claims are not supported by current clinical evidence (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and calls the sandwich advantages controversial (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's explains the theory (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC] but says its efficacy has not been demonstrated (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]: flowables shrink up to three times more (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], most leakage studies show no benefit (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and clinical trials found no difference in sensitivity or performance (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt's is more positive about glass-ionomer liners, which introduce less polymerization stress than resin composite (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC] and have been shown to improve marginal integrity (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Scotti and co-workers call the liner approach controversial (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC].

**(d) Modulating the cure.** Soft-start aims to prolong the gel phase so the material can redistribute stress (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC], using ramped lights, pulse curing or holding the light at a distance (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's describes about 150 mW/cm2 followed by 650 mW/cm2 or more (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Scotti and co-workers describe pulse-delay, applied only to the last increment (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]. Phillips' says delayed and exponential ramp curing give the greatest reductions in stress (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. On whether it works, the books disagree, side by side: several studies show stress reduction (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]; studies are mixed, some showing limited benefit (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC]; results are equivocal (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC] and a real reduction may need clinically impractical regimens (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; there is little evidence either way (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; little advantage is to be gained by ramped, delayed or soft-start curing (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC], supported by laboratory studies and some clinical evidence (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Light-reflecting wedges improve adaptation on enamel gingival margins (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC] but not leakage on dentin margins (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].

**(e) Materials.** Bulk-fill composites appear to give greater depth of cure and reduced shrinkage stress (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC], with at least 20 s curing (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC]. Water absorption partly relieves residual stress (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]; silorane-based composites have the lowest hygroscopic expansion (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]. A zero-shrinkage material may not be ideal, because water uptake causes slight expansion (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].

**(f) Indirect techniques.** For large, high C-factor cavities an indirect laboratory composite is clinically preferred (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. This moves most of the shrinkage outside the mouth, but the thin luting layer itself has a very high C-factor and can generate a large stress at the tooth and inlay surfaces (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Shrinkage cannot be avoided, but the stress it creates can be reduced (Donovan et al, 2019). No method has completely eliminated the deleterious effects of shrinkage stress (Perdigão et al, 2019).

**A. Preparation design**
- Conservative, adhesive preparations: a narrower, less bulky restoration reduces shrinkage effects; rounded internal angles reduce stress concentration (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- Avoid unnecessary box form in Class I, which increases C-factor effects (Ritter et al, 2019).

**B. Incremental and oblique layering (most widely accepted)**
- Each increment bonds to fewer walls, so its C-factor is lower than that of a bulk fill; aim for about 0.5 with oblique increments touching no more than two walls (Scotti et al, 2020; Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- Oblique layers create less stress than horizontal layers; bulk fill performed worse in vitro and in vivo (Scotti et al, 2020).
- Increment thickness: no more than 2 mm (Ritter et al, 2019); first gingival increment 1 mm or less (Hilton and Broome, 2013); 1-1.5 mm wedge-shaped layers (Garg and Garg, 2011).

**C. Low-modulus intermediate layers ("elastic cavity wall")**
- Thick filled adhesive layer; 0.5 mm RMGI liner; 0.5 mm flowable liner (Donovan et al, 2019).
- Glass-ionomer liners introduce less stress than composite and improve marginal integrity (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- Evidence against: flowable liners shrink up to three times more, show no benefit in most studies and no clinical difference (Hilton and Broome, 2013); liner claims are not supported clinically (Ritter et al, 2019); a GIC base did not improve 18-year survival (Perdigão et al, 2019).

**D. Modulating the cure**
- Soft-start (low then high irradiance, for example about 150 then 650 mW/cm2), ramped, pulse-delay (last increment) and delayed curing prolong the pre-gel phase (Hilton and Broome, 2013; Scotti et al, 2020; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).
- Evidence is conflicting: several studies show stress reduction (Scotti et al, 2020); results equivocal (Hilton and Broome, 2013); little evidence either way (McCabe and Walls, 2008); "little advantage" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).
- Light-reflecting wedges help on enamel gingival margins, not on dentin (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**E. Materials**
- Bulk-fill composites: greater depth of cure and reduced shrinkage stress (Donovan et al, 2019).
- Low-shrinkage monomers such as silorane, and hygroscopic expansion partly relieve stress (Perdigão et al, 2019).
- Chemically cured composite gives less stress (slower cure, porosity) but has other drawbacks (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**F. Indirect restorations**
- For large, high C-factor cavities, indirect laboratory composite or ceramic is preferred (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019); only the thin luting layer shrinks in the mouth, but that layer has C > 10 (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## 7. Critical analysis: does the C-factor actually predict stress?

### Explanation

Craig's states that it seems reasonable that a Class I should show more stress than a Class II or a saucer-shaped Class V, but the clinical condition is complex (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC], and "C-factor alone does not accurately predict stresses or clinical longevity in resin composite or glass ionomer restorations" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. The stresses depend on tooth anatomy, preparation geometry, bond quality and restorative technique (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

The reasons, from the books read:
1. **Area, not wall count.** The deeper of two identical-wall Class I cavities has the higher C-factor (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]; Braga and co-workers found stress was affected more by cavity depth than by diameter (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC]. The class-based numbers in section 2 are therefore rough.
2. **Volume and material.** Total volume, polymerization speed and composite type all set stress (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; stiffness matters more than shrinkage percentage (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC].
3. **Compliance of the substrate.** Stress also depends on how much the surrounding structure can deform (Perdigão et al, 2019) [SRC]. A Class I in a thick-walled molar and the same cavity in a thin-walled premolar have the same C-factor but different stress and cuspal deflection [Likely].
4. **Source of the values.** The C-factor values and stress measurements come from laboratory specimens and calculations (Feilzer's model geometry; finite element analyses), not from clinical outcome studies (my interpretation of the sources [Likely]).

The concept remains useful clinically: it explains why Class I and deep dentin bonds are vulnerable, why layering helps, and why luting cements and root canals are hard to bond. The correct way to write it is as a **qualitative risk index**, not a numerical predictor.

#### ✎ Exam core

**What the C-factor explains well**
- Why Class I restorations debond at the pulpal floor and cause sensitivity, while Class IV and veneer restorations rarely do.
- Why incremental, oblique layering is used.
- Why luting cements (C > 10) and root canals (C > 200) are difficult to bond to.

**Limitations (critical analysis)**
1. **Not a predictor on its own.** Craig's states that C-factor alone does not accurately predict stresses or clinical longevity in resin composite or glass-ionomer restorations; stress depends on tooth anatomy, preparation geometry, bond quality and restorative technique (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
2. **Areas, not walls.** Class-based values (5, 2, 1, 0.5) assume average dimensions. A deeper Class I has a higher C-factor than a shallow one with the same walls (Scotti et al, 2020), and cavity depth affects stress more than diameter (Breschi et al, 2013).
3. **Ignores the material.** Stress depends on volume, curing speed, degree of conversion and elastic modulus (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013; Scotti et al, 2020). A low-shrinkage but stiff composite can still produce high stress (Perdigão et al, 2019).
4. **Ignores the tooth.** Stress depends on the compliance (deformation) of the substrate (Perdigão et al, 2019): thin cusps flex and relieve interfacial stress but suffer deflection and cracking.
5. **Ignores time.** Pre-gel flow, curing rate and post-cure shrinkage over 24 hours change the stress that develops (Scotti et al, 2020).
6. **Laboratory basis.** The values come from model geometries and laboratory tests; clinical trials of the strategies derived from it (liners, soft-start) have mostly not shown a clinical difference (Hilton and Broome, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).

**Conclusion.** The C-factor is a simple and valuable concept for understanding where polymerization stress concentrates, and it justifies conservative preparations and incremental, oblique layering. It should be used as a qualitative risk indicator, read together with material stiffness, volume, curing rate and tooth compliance, not as a number that predicts stress or clinical success.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "The higher the C-factor, the higher the polymerization stress."**
*Textbook says:* residual stress increases directly with the bonded-to-free ratio (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; the ratio can predict interfacial stress (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].
*What other evidence shows:* Craig's says C-factor alone does not accurately predict stress or longevity (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]; depth matters more than diameter (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC]; compliance and stiffness also set stress (Perdigão et al, 2019; Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC].
*Agreement or challenge:* refinement, not contradiction: the trend holds for a given material and specimen, but the number is not a predictor across real teeth. [Certain]
*Strength:* laboratory and calculation data; no clinical study read that links measured C-factor to outcome. [Likely]
*Conclusion and clinical implication:* use C-factor to recognise high-risk situations (Class I, deep boxes, luting, posts) and choose layering or indirect methods; do not quote it as a stress value.

**Claim 2: "Low-modulus liners (flowable, RMGI) absorb shrinkage stress."**
*Textbook says:* Sturdevant's Ch.16 recommends 0.5 mm RMGI and flowable liners (Donovan et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's reports glass-ionomer liners introduce less stress and improve marginal integrity (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].
*What other evidence shows:* Sturdevant's Ch.15 and Ch.19 say the claims are not proved or supported clinically and a GIC base did not improve 18-year survival (Perdigão et al, 2019; Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; flowables shrink up to three times more and clinical trials show no difference (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].
*Agreement or challenge:* direct challenge; the same textbook (Sturdevant's) takes both positions in different chapters. [Certain]
*Strength:* the negative statements rest on clinical trials; the positive ones on theory and in-vitro leakage data. [Likely]
*Conclusion and clinical implication:* a liner may be used for adaptation, pulp protection or fluoride (RMGI near the CEJ), but should not be relied on to reduce shrinkage stress.

**Claim 3: "Soft-start curing reduces polymerization stress."**
*Textbook says:* several studies show reduced stress and better marginal integrity (Scotti et al, 2020) [SRC]; delayed and exponential ramp curing give the largest reductions (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].
*What other evidence shows:* results equivocal and effective regimens may be impractical (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; little evidence (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; little advantage, supported by laboratory studies and some clinical evidence (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].
*Agreement or challenge:* laboratory stress reduction is reported, but clinical benefit is not shown. [Likely]
*Strength:* mostly in-vitro. [Likely]
*Conclusion and clinical implication:* soft-start or pulse-delay is reasonable as an adjunct, never as a substitute for incremental placement and good bonding.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- C-factor = bonded / unbonded surface area; Feilzer et al 1987 (Garg and Garg credit Davidson and colleagues).
- Values: free resin 0, incisal edge 0.2, Class IV 0.5, Class III 1, Class II box 2, Class I 5, luting > 10, root canal > 200 (Phillips' > 1000).
- C-factor raises **stress**, not volumetric shrinkage.
- Pre-gel flow, gel point at about 10-20% conversion, post-gel stress; light-cured composite shrinks toward bonded walls, not toward the light.
- Weakest interface: pulpal floor in Class I; gingival margin on dentin/cementum in Class II.
- Strategies: conservative preparation, oblique increments (two walls, 2 mm or less), liners (disputed), soft-start (disputed), bulk-fill, indirect restorations.
- Critical point: C-factor alone does not predict stress or longevity (Craig's); depth, volume, stiffness and tooth compliance also matter.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing that a high C-factor increases polymerization shrinkage; it increases stress.
- Writing that light-cured composite shrinks toward the light; it shrinks toward the bonded walls.
- Quoting C-factor as a ratio of numbers of walls rather than of surface areas.
- Presenting flowable liners or soft-start curing as proven stress relievers.
- Forgetting the very high C-factor of luting cement layers and root canals.

## MNEMONIC
