# Explain the concept of C- factor in Cavity design. Critically analyze how it influences polymerization stress and outline strategies to overcome it.

**Marks:** 50 · Essay

## 1. Introduction, definition and origin

All methacrylate resin composites shrink on polymerization. Shrinkage itself cannot be avoided; what matters clinically is the **stress** it generates at the bonded tooth-restoration interface. How much of that stress is relieved depends on how free the composite is to flow, and this is governed by the geometry of the preparation. **Definition.** The **cavity configuration factor (C-factor)** is the ratio of the bonded surface area of a restoration to its unbonded (free) surface area: **C-factor = bonded surface area / unbonded surface area (B/U)** **Origin.** Proposed by Feilzer and co-workers in 1987 (Scotti et al., 2020; Breschi et al., 2013); Garg and Garg (2011) credit Davidson and colleagues in the 1980s. **Principle.** A composite bonded to only one surface (a direct veneer) relieves its shrinkage stress by flowing from the free surface. In a three-dimensional preparation the free surface is small, so flow cannot compensate and stress builds up at the bonded walls (Perdigão et al., 2019). The ratio of bonded to free area is therefore used to indicate the interfacial stress likely to be caused by shrinkage (McCabe and Walls, 2008); residual stress rises with the ratio (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 2. C-factor values in different preparations

| Situation | Bonded : free surfaces | C-factor |
|---|---|---|
| Unconstrained mass of resin | 0 : 6 | 0 |
| Fractured incisal edge / one-surface veneer | 1 : 5 | 0.2 |
| Class IV | 2 : 4 | 0.5 |
| Class III | 3 : 3 | 1 |
| Class II simple proximal box | 4 : 2 | 2 |
| Class I (and box-form Class V) | 5 : 1 | 5 |
| Thin luting cement layer (inlay, veneer, crown) | very large bonded area, tiny free edge | > 10 |
| Root canal / post space | almost entirely bonded | > 200 (Perdigão et al., 2019; Breschi et al., 2013); > 1000 (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) | (Values: McCabe and Walls, 2008; Scotti et al., 2020; Garg and Garg, 2011.) **Points to note.**
- Class I is the most unfavourable direct restoration; Class IV and incisal-edge restorations the most favourable (Garg and Garg, 2011; McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Coronal direct restorations range from about 1 to 5 (Perdigão et al., 2019).
- The C-factor is an **area** ratio: for the same five-walled Class I, a deeper preparation has a higher C-factor than a shallow one (Scotti et al., 2020).

## 3. Polymerization shrinkage and the origin of stress

**Magnitude of shrinkage.** Values differ between books: 2.2-2.8% (Donovan et al., 2019), 1.5-5.0% volumetric (Vargas et al., 2013), 1.67-5.68% (Garg and Garg, 2011), average 2-3% and up to 7% (Scotti et al., 2020). Shrinkage continues for about 24 hours after light exposure ("dark" or post-polymerization) (Scotti et al., 2020). **Shrinkage versus stress.** Volumetric shrinkage is a material property; **stress** is what damages the bond. By Hooke's law, stress depends on the strain (shrinkage) and the stiffness (elastic modulus) of the material, and it is more closely related to stiffness than to percentage shrinkage (Scotti et al., 2020). Therefore a low-shrinkage composite does not necessarily produce less stress (Perdigão et al., 2019). **Factors determining polymerization stress** (Perdigão et al., 2019; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013):
1. C-factor (configuration of the preparation)
2. Volume of composite cured at one time
3. Rate of polymerization and degree of conversion
4. Stiffness of the composite and how fast it is acquired
5. Filler type and volume; monomer type
6. Insertion technique and curing mode
7. Compliance of the surrounding tooth (section 7) **A common error.** Garg and Garg (2011) write that a higher C-factor gives greater polymerization **shrinkage**. Cavity shape cannot change the volumetric shrinkage of a material; it changes the **stress** produced, as stated by Perdigão et al (2019) and McCabe and Walls (2008).

## 4. Flow and the gel point

**Three phases of polymerization** (Scotti et al., 2020):
1. **Pre-gel phase:** short, poorly linked chains; the material can flow and rearrange, so shrinkage creates little interfacial stress.
2. **Gel point:** at about 10-20% conversion a cross-linked network forms; flow stops.
3. **Post-gel phase:** further shrinkage cannot be compensated by flow; stress is transmitted to the interface and tooth, causing bond rupture, cuspal deflection or enamel microcracks (4-8 MPa or more). After the gel point stresses cannot be relieved but concentrate near the bonded interfaces (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013). Only the post-gel part of shrinkage is clinically significant (McCabe and Walls, 2008). **Link with C-factor.** Flow in the pre-gel phase can only take place from free surfaces. A high C-factor means little free surface, so less of the shrinkage is compensated before the gel point and more stress remains. **Light-cured versus chemically cured.** Chemically cured composites set over more than 3 minutes, have a longer pre-gel phase and contain porosity that relaxes stress, so they generate less stress (Scotti et al., 2020; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013). Light curing starts at the surface nearest the light, which then cannot act as a relief pathway (Perdigão et al., 2019). **Direction of shrinkage.** The old view that light-cured composite shrinks toward the light (still stated by Garg and Garg, 2011) has been replaced: composite shrinks toward the walls to which it is bonded (Hilton and Broome, 2013), as finite element analysis by Versluis and co-workers showed (Scotti et al., 2020).

## 5. Effects of C-factor-related stress on the bond and margins

| Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Debonding and marginal gap | Stress exceeds bond strength; gaps form first where the bond is weakest, at dentin or cementum margins (Vargas et al., 2013) |
| Microleakage, staining, recurrent caries | Through the marginal gap (Perdigão et al., 2019) |
| Postoperative sensitivity | Pulpal-floor debonding in Class I leaves a fluid-filled gap (Donovan et al., 2019; Ritter et al., 2019) |
| Reduced bond strength to deep dentin | Partly attributed to high C-factor (Perdigão et al., 2019) |
| Cuspal deflection, enamel cracks, cusp fracture | When the bond holds, the tooth deforms instead (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Internal stresses in the composite | Weaken the restoration | Immediate bond strength of about 17 MPa is quoted as necessary to resist contraction stress (Perdigão et al., 2019). The gingival margin of Class II restorations is the weakest point; in one clinical study only 27% were satisfactory (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

## 6. Strategies to overcome C-factor and polymerization stress

Shrinkage cannot be avoided, but the stress it creates can be reduced (Donovan et al., 2019). No method has completely eliminated the deleterious effects of shrinkage stress (Perdigão et al., 2019). **A. Preparation design**
- Conservative, adhesive preparations: a narrower, less bulky restoration reduces shrinkage effects; rounded internal angles reduce stress concentration (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- Avoid unnecessary box form in Class I, which increases C-factor effects (Ritter et al., 2019). **B. Incremental and oblique layering (most widely accepted)**
- Each increment bonds to fewer walls, so its C-factor is lower than that of a bulk fill; aim for about 0.5 with oblique increments touching no more than two walls (Scotti et al., 2020; Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- Oblique layers create less stress than horizontal layers; bulk fill performed worse in vitro and in vivo (Scotti et al., 2020).
- Increment thickness: no more than 2 mm (Ritter et al., 2019); first gingival increment 1 mm or less (Hilton and Broome, 2013); 1-1.5 mm wedge-shaped layers (Garg and Garg, 2011). **C. Low-modulus intermediate layers ("elastic cavity wall")**
- Thick filled adhesive layer; 0.5 mm RMGI liner; 0.5 mm flowable liner (Donovan et al., 2019).
- Glass-ionomer liners introduce less stress than composite and improve marginal integrity (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- Evidence against: flowable liners shrink up to three times more, show no benefit in most studies and no clinical difference (Hilton and Broome, 2013); liner claims are not supported clinically (Ritter et al., 2019); a GIC base did not improve 18-year survival (Perdigão et al., 2019). **D. Modulating the cure**
- Soft-start (low then high irradiance, for example about 150 then 650 mW/cm2), ramped, pulse-delay (last increment) and delayed curing prolong the pre-gel phase (Hilton and Broome, 2013; Scotti et al., 2020; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).
- Evidence is conflicting: several studies show stress reduction (Scotti et al., 2020); results equivocal (Hilton and Broome, 2013); little evidence either way (McCabe and Walls, 2008); "little advantage" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).
- Light-reflecting wedges help on enamel gingival margins, not on dentin (Hilton and Broome, 2013). **E. Materials**
- Bulk-fill composites: greater depth of cure and reduced shrinkage stress (Donovan et al., 2019).
- Low-shrinkage monomers such as silorane, and hygroscopic expansion partly relieve stress (Perdigão et al., 2019).
- Chemically cured composite gives less stress (slower cure, porosity) but has other drawbacks (Hilton and Broome, 2013). **F. Indirect restorations**
- For large, high C-factor cavities, indirect laboratory composite or ceramic is preferred (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019); only the thin luting layer shrinks in the mouth, but that layer has C > 10 (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## 7. Critical analysis: does the C-factor actually predict stress?

**What the C-factor explains well**
- Why Class I restorations debond at the pulpal floor and cause sensitivity, while Class IV and veneer restorations rarely do.
- Why incremental, oblique layering is used.
- Why luting cements (C > 10) and root canals (C > 200) are difficult to bond to. **Limitations (critical analysis)**
1. **Not a predictor on its own.** Craig's states that C-factor alone does not accurately predict stresses or clinical longevity in resin composite or glass-ionomer restorations; stress depends on tooth anatomy, preparation geometry, bond quality and restorative technique (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
2. **Areas, not walls.** Class-based values (5, 2, 1, 0.5) assume average dimensions. A deeper Class I has a higher C-factor than a shallow one with the same walls (Scotti et al., 2020), and cavity depth affects stress more than diameter (Breschi et al., 2013).
3. **Ignores the material.** Stress depends on volume, curing speed, degree of conversion and elastic modulus (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013; Scotti et al., 2020). A low-shrinkage but stiff composite can still produce high stress (Perdigão et al., 2019).
4. **Ignores the tooth.** Stress depends on the compliance (deformation) of the substrate (Perdigão et al., 2019): thin cusps flex and relieve interfacial stress but suffer deflection and cracking.
5. **Ignores time.** Pre-gel flow, curing rate and post-cure shrinkage over 24 hours change the stress that develops (Scotti et al., 2020).
6. **Laboratory basis.** The values come from model geometries and laboratory tests; clinical trials of the strategies derived from it (liners, soft-start) have mostly not shown a clinical difference (Hilton and Broome, 2013; Ritter et al., 2019). **Evidence update.** A 2023 review concluded that C-factor alone cannot accurately predict polymerization shrinkage stress or the mechanical behaviour of the tooth–restoration interface; its rule-of-thumb is applicable only under low, near-rigid compliance, which is not typical clinically (Zhou et al., 2023).

**Conclusion.** The C-factor is a simple and valuable concept for understanding where polymerization stress concentrates, and it justifies conservative preparations and incremental, oblique layering. It should be used as a qualitative risk indicator, read together with material stiffness, volume, curing rate and tooth compliance, not as a number that predicts stress or clinical success.
