## 1. Introduction: why preparations for tooth-coloured materials differ

### Explanation

Tooth-coloured materials (resin composite, glass-ionomer and resin-modified glass-ionomer, ceramic and laboratory composite) are retained mainly by adhesion, so the preparation is governed by removal of the defect, not by mechanical retention. Sturdevant's Table 11.1 contrasts amalgam and composite: amalgam includes adjacent suspicious areas while composite seals them (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; amalgam has a uniform 1.5 mm pulpal depth while composite removes the defect without uniform depth (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; amalgam walls are smoother and composite walls rough (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; and amalgam's primary retention is occlusal convergence while composite has none beyond roughness and bonding (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. For most posterior composites mechanical retention features are not needed (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and sound tooth structure is not removed for "bulk for strength" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's adds that a less bulky restoration reduces shrinkage effects (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], rounded internal angles reduce stress concentration (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and fissures are included only if carious (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk names four ways of retaining direct tooth-coloured materials: acid conditioning of enamel, retentive cavity form, physico-chemical adhesion, and pins or posts (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. His preparations should have far more rounded line angles than amalgam preparations (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], and the intercuspal width may be as small as one-fifth of the intercuspal distance (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Tooth-coloured restorative materials include resin composites, glass-ionomer cements (GIC) and resin-modified glass-ionomers (RMGI), and indirect ceramic or laboratory-composite restorations. Because they bond to tooth structure, their preparations are smaller and simpler than those for amalgam: the defect, not the material, decides the outline.

**Retention modes** (Marzouk et al, undated): acid conditioning of enamel, retentive cavity form, physico-chemical adhesion (GIC), pins and posts, or combinations.

**Composite versus amalgam preparation** (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Singh and Rajkumar, 2020)

| Feature | Amalgam | Composite |
|---|---|---|
| Outline | Includes adjacent suspicious fissures | Defect only; fissures sealed |
| Pulpal/axial depth | Uniform (pulpal 1.5 mm) | Dictated by the lesion |
| Cavosurface angle | 90°, no bevel | 90° or more; bevel where indicated |
| Walls | Smooth | Rough |
| Retention | Occlusal convergence, grooves, pins | Bonding |
| Internal angles | Rounded | More rounded still |

Sound tooth structure is not removed for "bulk for strength" (Ritter et al, 2019); smaller preparations reduce shrinkage effects (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

## 2. Classification of preparation methods

### Explanation

The books classify differently. Singh and Rajkumar list four types (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]: conventional, bevelled conventional, modified and combined. Garg and Garg list three designs: conventional, beveled conventional and modified (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Marzouk classifies by enamel-wall treatment (partial bevel, long bevel, hollow-ground bevel, scalloping, skirting) and by internal form (mortise-shaped versus saucer-shaped designs) (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Sturdevant's current edition no longer names the three classic designs for composite; it describes conservative preparations for small and moderate lesions and more traditional forms for large ones, plus box-only and slot preparations (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo classify by site and size and describe tunnel, slot and proximal approaches (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]. The exam answer can use the Singh/Garg scheme as its backbone and add the others as further methods, as long as it notes that the classic three-type scheme comes from the preclinical textbooks.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification of methods**

**A. Design of preparation for composite** (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020; Garg and Garg, 2011): (1) conventional, (2) bevelled conventional, (3) modified (conservative, "scooped-out"), (4) combined (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020).

**B. Enamel-margin treatment** (Marzouk et al, undated): butt joint, partial bevel, long bevel, hollow-ground bevel, scalloping, skirting.

**C. Other conservative designs:** box-only, facial or lingual slot, tunnel, proximal approach, saucer-shaped preparation, preventive resin restoration (Ritter et al, 2019; Mount and Ngo, 2016; Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**D. Preparations for GIC and RMGI.**

**E. Preparations for indirect tooth-coloured restorations.**

Sturdevant's present edition describes conservative preparations for small lesions and traditional forms for large ones rather than naming the three classic types (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 3. Conventional preparation

### Explanation

Conventional preparations are the same as those for amalgam (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]: external walls extended at uniform, limited depth (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC], producing a butt joint. Garg and Garg give its indication as lesions on root surfaces (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Where a lesion lies partly on crown and partly on root, Singh and Rajkumar's "combined" design uses conventional form on the root and bevelled form on the crown, mostly in Class III preparations extending onto the root (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]. A 90° cavosurface angle gives maximal strength for low edge-strength materials (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], which is why it is kept on cementum and dentin margins. On the root surface both preclinical books use an initial axial depth of 0.75 mm (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020; Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. **They disagree on the retention groove:** Singh and Rajkumar place it 0.25 mm from the root surface and 0.25 mm deep (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]; Garg and Garg place it at least 1 mm from the surface and at least 0.5 mm into dentin (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conventional tooth preparation** (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020; Garg and Garg, 2011)
- Same as an amalgam preparation: external walls at uniform, limited depth; 90° cavosurface angle (butt joint).
- **Indication:** root-surface lesions, and the root portion of lesions extending from crown onto root.
- **Features:** box form; axial depth about 0.75 mm on root surfaces; walls perpendicular to the root surface; retention grooves where needed. Groove position differs: 0.25 mm from the surface and 0.25 mm deep (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) versus at least 1 mm from the surface and 0.5 mm into dentin (Garg and Garg, 2011).
- **Rationale:** cementum and dentin are not etched like enamel; a butt joint gives maximum strength at the margin (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

**Combined preparation:** conventional on the root portion, bevelled on coronal enamel, mostly for Class III lesions extending onto the root (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020).

## 4. Bevelled conventional preparation (absorbed short note)

### Explanation

**Rationale.** A bevel increases the surface area for bonding and thus retention (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], improves esthetics by a gradual increase in composite thickness from the margin (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], and exposes the ends of enamel rods, which etch more effectively and give deeper micro-undercuts (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]; etching rod ends gives a significantly stronger bond than etching rod sides (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's indicates bevels mainly for large restorations with increased retention needs and too little enamel (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Singh and Rajkumar use the design mostly for Class III, IV and V restorations (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC].

**Occlusal surfaces: all sources agree no bevel.** The thin composite over an occlusal bevel would chip under the masticatory forces (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]; bevelling would widen the occlusal isthmus (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; occlusal bevels are not indicated (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC] and gave significantly greater wear than butt margins in a clinical study (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Singh and Rajkumar allow a 0.25-0.5 mm, 45° bevel on a buccal or palatal extension of a Class I (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC].

**Bevel width: the books give different figures** (shown side by side, not ranked):
- Class III: 0.25-0.5 mm (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]; 0.2-0.5 mm at 45° (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; 45° and 0.5-2 mm (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; up to 2 mm or more on the facial surface where esthetics demands (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC].
- Class IV: 0.25-2.0 mm (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]; at least 1 mm all round (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC], since widening beyond 1 mm adds no strength (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]; facial bevel 60°, 2-3 mm, scalloped "infinite" (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]; lingual bevel limited to about 1 mm (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC].
- Class V: bevel occlusal/incisal enamel margins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] at least 0.5 mm wide (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; 0.25-0.5 mm (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; on the incisal/occlusal wall (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC].
The narrower figures are preclinical-textbook rules; the wider ones reflect esthetic layering technique in the clinical textbooks.

**Where not to bevel:** areas of heavy occlusal force and cemental margins (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

**Is the bevel needed at all? A genuine controversy.** Summitt's Ch.10 says enamel bonds are clinically adequate without bevels (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC] and an in-vitro tracer study found no difference in Class 3 microleakage with or without bevels (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]. For Class II, the books disagree: Summitt's calls bevel placement a point of controversy (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC] but recommends 0.5 mm, 45° bevels on facial and lingual proximal margins when the wall meets the surface at 90° (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], citing reduced leakage (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and a gingival bevel only where enamel is well away from the CEJ (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's says bevels are rarely used on proximal box walls (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and not recommended on gingival margins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Singh and Rajkumar bevel facial and lingual box walls only when the box is too wide (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's also reports that an inverse bevel of unsupported gingival enamel reduces microleakage compared with a butt margin (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].

**Marzouk's enamel-wall treatments.** Partial bevel when the cavity itself retains and etching is only to reduce marginal leakage (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; long bevel of the whole enamel wall at 45-70° (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; both lack a definite marginal termination and produce flash (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; hollow-ground bevel keeps a right-angled cavosurface angle (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and is used for inaccessible areas such as gingival walls (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and directly loaded areas (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; scalloping never on gingival walls (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; skirting, removing about 0.1 mm of surface enamel (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], when etched enamel is the main retentive mode (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], involving enamel at least double the defect area (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** A conventional preparation whose accessible enamel cavosurface margins are bevelled; retention comes mainly from etched enamel (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020).

**Advantages**
1. More enamel surface for etching and bonding, so more retention (Boushell and Walter, 2019).
2. Exposes rod **ends**; end-on etching gives deeper micro-undercuts and a stronger bond than etching rod sides (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020; Hilton and Broome, 2013).
3. Better marginal seal.
4. Esthetic blending through a gradual increase in composite thickness (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

**Indications:** Class III, IV and V; large or replacement restorations with high retention needs and little enamel (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020; Boushell and Walter, 2019).

**Contraindications:** occlusal surfaces of posterior teeth; centric contact areas; cementum or dentin margins; thin cervical enamel (Garg and Garg, 2011; Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**Why no occlusal bevel:** rod ends are already exposed; the thin composite over a bevel chips; the isthmus widens (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019). Bevelled occlusal margins wore significantly more clinically (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**Technique:** flame-shaped or round fine diamond at about 45° to the external surface.

| Class | Bevel width according to different books |
|---|---|
| III | 0.25-0.5 mm (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020); 0.2-0.5 mm (Garg and Garg, 2011); 0.5-2 mm (Ritter et al, 2019); 2 mm or more facially for esthetics (Vargas et al, 2013) |
| IV | 0.25-2.0 mm (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020); at least 1 mm, no added strength beyond 1 mm; facial 60°, 2-3 mm "infinite" bevel; lingual about 1 mm (Vargas et al, 2013) |
| V | Occlusal/incisal enamel only; at least 0.5 mm (Ritter et al, 2019); 0.25-0.5 mm (Garg and Garg, 2011) |

**Controversies.** Class II proximal margins: Summitt's recommends 0.5 mm, 45° facial and lingual bevels (less leakage) and a gingival bevel only on enamel well away from the CEJ (Hilton and Broome, 2013); Sturdevant's rarely bevels the proximal box and not gingivally (Ritter et al, 2019). Anterior: enamel bonds are clinically adequate without bevels and Class 3 leakage did not differ in vitro (Vargas et al, 2013).

**Enamel-margin treatments** (Marzouk et al, undated): partial bevel (part of the wall, 45-70°, cavity retains); long bevel (whole wall, poor cavity retention); hollow-ground bevel (keeps a 90° margin; gingival, inaccessible and loaded areas); scalloping (never gingivally); skirt (about 0.1 mm surface enamel around shallow defects, at least double the defect area). Partial and long bevels give no definite finish line and cause flash.

## 5. Modified (conservative, adhesive) preparation

### Explanation

The modified preparation has no specific pulpal or axial depth (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]; only the affected area is removed and there is no specific configuration (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]. Garg and Garg describe infected carious tissue being removed as conservatively as possible by scooping (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Sturdevant's small to moderate Class I composite preparations result in more flared cavosurface forms (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; in small Class III preparations the walls may diverge externally, giving a bevelled marginal design (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; small Class V preparations make no effort to create 90° margins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], as Singh and Rajkumar also say (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's describes the enamel-only Class 3 as a saucer without retentive undercuts (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC] and allows unsupported facial enamel to be left for internal etching (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk's saucer-shaped Class V is used for sensitive erosion and abrasion lesions (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. For a non-carious cervical lesion, Sturdevant's preparation is just debridement and roughening or bevelling of enamel margins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The lingual approach is preferred for Class III (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. For initial Class 1 caries Summitt's makes the preventive resin restoration the usual technique of choice (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Modified (conservative) preparation** (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020; Garg and Garg, 2011; Ritter et al, 2019)
- **Concept:** remove only carious or defective tissue and rely on adhesion; no specific outline, pulpal or axial depth; "scooped-out" or saucer form.
- **Indications:** small to moderate lesions; enamel defects; non-carious cervical lesions.
- **Features:** small round or pear-shaped instruments; walls flare externally, giving a bevel-like margin; no grooves; 90° margins not required; unsupported but non-friable facial enamel may be left and etched internally (Vargas et al, 2013).
- **Class-wise:** Class I: preventive resin restoration with fissure sealing (Hilton and Broome, 2013). Class III: lingual approach to preserve facial enamel (Ritter et al, 2019). Class V and NCCLs: debridement and roughening or bevelling of enamel only (Ritter et al, 2019); saucer design for sensitive erosion and abrasion lesions (Marzouk et al, undated).
- **Advantages:** maximum conservation, esthetics, less shrinkage stress. **Limitation:** depends entirely on bonding and isolation.

## 6. Box-only and slot preparations

### Explanation

**Box-only.** Indicated when only the proximal surface is defective, with no occlusal lesion (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; no bevel or secondary retention is indicated (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Singh and Rajkumar give an initial axial depth of 0.2 mm inside the DEJ (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC] and use a matrix band to protect the adjacent tooth (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC].

**Facial or lingual slot.** Access is from a facial or lingual direction rather than through the marginal ridge (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; if the marginal ridge is undermined, a conventional Class II with occlusal access is used instead (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Singh and Rajkumar indicate it when the marginal ridge is intact with at least 2 mm of enamel (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC] and caries extends up to 0.5 mm into dentin (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC].

**The word "slot" means different things in different books.** Mount and Ngo use "slot cavity" for access **through** the marginal ridge when the lesion is closer than 3.00 mm to its crest (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC] or leaves less than 2.0 mm of ridge (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]; this corresponds to Sturdevant's and Singh's box-only design, whereas their "slot" spares the ridge. Summitt's "proximal slot" is also a ridge-involving proximal preparation, with 70% success after up to 10 years in one study (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Name the access route when writing "slot".

#### ✎ Exam core

**Box-only preparation** (Ritter et al, 2019; Singh and Rajkumar, 2020)
- **Indication:** proximal lesion with no occlusal caries.
- **Technique:** small pear-shaped or round diamond parallel to the long axis, through the marginal ridge toward the lesion; axial depth about 0.2 mm inside the DEJ, otherwise set by the lesion; extensions only as far as the caries; matrix band protects the adjacent tooth. No bevel, no secondary retention.

**Facial or lingual slot preparation** (Ritter et al, 2019; Singh and Rajkumar, 2020)
- **Indication:** proximal lesion reachable from the facial or lingual embrasure, marginal ridge intact (at least 2 mm of enamel), caries up to 0.5 mm into dentin.
- **Technique:** wedge placed; small round bur enters from the embrasure at the level of the lesion; margins 90° or more; ridge not undermined, otherwise convert to a conventional Class II.

**Terminology warning:** Mount and Ngo (2016) and Summitt's use "slot" for a small box through the marginal ridge (lesion within 2-3 mm of the crest), that is, the box-only design; Summitt's reports 70% success after up to 10 years (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

## 7. Tunnel (internal occlusal fossa) preparation

### Explanation

The tunnel was first suggested by Jinks in 1963 (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo use it when the lesion is at least 3.00 mm below the marginal ridge crest (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]. Advantages: ridge preserved; a two-surface Class 2 reduces tooth stiffness by 46% (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC] against only 20% for an occlusal preparation (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; less potential for overhangs (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; a smaller restoration perimeter (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; and easy conversion to a Class 2 (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. A caries-detecting solution should be used (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Disadvantages: it often comes within 1 mm of the pulp (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], leaves a high rate of residual caries (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and tunnel restorations fail at a considerably higher rate (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's questions whether the ridge remains strong (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], reports GIC tunnel restorations inadequate long-term (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], and does not support the technique (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo, by contrast, call the occlusal fossa route the simplest and most conservative approach for suitable lesions (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]. This is a real disagreement between books.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Tunnel preparation** (first suggested by Jinks, 1963; Hilton and Broome, 2013)
- **Concept:** proximal lesion reached through a tunnel from the occlusal fossa beside the marginal ridge, leaving ridge and contact intact.
- **Indication:** small proximal lesion at least 3 mm below the ridge crest (Mount and Ngo, 2016), usually restored with high-strength GIC.
- **Technique:** small tapered diamond angled from the fossa toward the lesion; caries removed with small round burs; caries-detecting dye.
- **Advantages:** ridge preserved (a two-surface Class II reduces stiffness by 46%, an occlusal preparation by 20%); fewer overhangs; smaller perimeter; can be converted to Class II.
- **Disadvantages:** poor visibility, residual caries, preparation often within 1 mm of the pulp, weakened ridge, higher failure rate (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- **Status:** controversial and not supported by Sturdevant's, as GIC tunnel restorations were inadequate long-term (Boushell and Walter, 2019); Mount and Ngo (2016) present it as the most conservative route for suitable lesions.

## 8. Preparations for glass-ionomer and resin-modified glass-ionomer restorations

### Explanation

Root caries in patients with active caries is the primary indication for glass ionomer (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; it is not for typical Class I, II or IV restorations (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Garg and Garg list Class V, Class III and small Class I preparations as indications (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] and stress-bearing Class I, II and IV areas as contraindications (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Preparations are as for composite except bevels are rarely used (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; proximal root lesions use the amalgam slot design (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's says RMGI preparations resemble composite preparations but are not bevelled (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC], and retention grooves are unnecessary (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo require no specific retention design (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]; undermined enamel is acceptable because the cement supports it (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]; only seriously broken-down enamel need be removed (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]; enamel margins are smoothed with a 25 μm diamond (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]; and for lamination the GIC is cut back about 2 mm to expose the enamel wall (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]. For Class II composites with gingival margins near or below the CEJ, Summitt's recommends RMGI as the first increment (open sandwich) (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Dentin is conditioned with a mild acid such as 10% polyacrylic acid (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo also leave affected dentin on the axial wall where removal risks the pulp (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications for GIC/RMGI:** root caries in high-risk patients (primary indication), Class V and erosion or abrasion lesions, Class III and small Class I, tunnel and slot cavities, primary teeth; not stress-bearing Class I, II or IV (Ritter et al, 2019; Garg and Garg, 2011).

**Principles of preparation**
1. Outline set only by the caries; access just sufficient to excavate.
2. **No bevels:** otherwise as for composite (Ritter et al, 2019; Vargas et al, 2013).
3. **No retention grooves:** retention by chemical adhesion (Vargas et al, 2013; Mount and Ngo, 2016).
4. Butt margins; smooth enamel with a fine (25 μm) diamond (Mount and Ngo, 2016).
5. Undermined enamel may stay, supported by the cement; uncavitated demineralised enamel is left to remineralize (Mount and Ngo, 2016).
6. Affected dentin on the axial wall may be left where the pulp is at risk (Mount and Ngo, 2016).
7. Condition with mild acid such as 10% polyacrylic acid (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Designs:** Class V and root lesions as for composite without bevels; proximal root caries after recession by an amalgam-type slot (Ritter et al, 2019); tunnel, slot and proximal approach (Mount and Ngo, 2016); lamination, with GIC cut back about 2 mm to expose enamel before bonding composite (Mount and Ngo, 2016); open sandwich, with RMGI as the gingival increment of a Class II near or below the CEJ (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

## 9. Preparations for indirect tooth-coloured restorations (outline)

### Explanation

Summitt's lists a large interproximal distance making contacts difficult among inlay indications (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], and indicates bonded porcelain restorations when esthetics is paramount and all margins can be placed on enamel (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; the preparation should give 2 mm occlusal clearance (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], have rounded internal angles and occlusally divergent walls (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC] and no grooves or sharp angles (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri gives a 2.0 mm occlusal isthmus (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], at least 2.0 mm depth (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], 10-12° occlusal taper (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], butt gingival margins with no bevel (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], cusps reduced by at least 2.0 mm when covered (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and no marginal bevels because they leave fragile resin margins (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Choice of material: two different rules.** Sikri reports that composite is indicated when the isthmus is less than two-thirds of the intercuspal width and ceramic when it is more (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Summitt's says resin composite should be considered first for two- and three-surface inlays of moderate width (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC] and prefers ceramic as soon as one cusp is covered (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. The two are compatible for narrow inlays but differ for wide inlays without cusp coverage.

The thin luting layer has a large C-factor and can develop a relatively large shrinkage stress at the tooth and inlay surfaces (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC], which is one reason for enamel margins and careful adhesive cementation. Veneers and crowns are covered in Q08.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indirect composite and ceramic inlays and onlays** (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013; Sikri, 2017)
- **Indications:** esthetic demand with large lesions and enamel margins; wide defects; contacts difficult to achieve directly.
- **Design:** depth and occlusal clearance at least 2 mm; isthmus at least 2 mm; walls divergent occlusally (10-12° taper, Sikri) with no undercuts; internal angles rounded; no grooves or bevels; butt gingival margin; weakened cusps reduced by at least 2 mm; enamel margins wherever possible.
- **Material choice:** isthmus under two-thirds of intercuspal width: composite; over two-thirds: ceramic (Sikri, 2017). Summitt's: composite first for moderate-width inlays; ceramic once any cusp is covered.
- The thin resin luting layer has a very high C-factor and develops large interfacial stress (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## 10. Summary and conclusion

### Explanation

The general trend across the books read is from mechanical, amalgam-like boxes toward minimal, defect-driven adhesive preparations, with the bevel now a selective rather than universal feature. Marzouk's general rules (rounded angles, gingival wall as the key wall for resistance and retention (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], no dovetails in anterior teeth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]) remain valid for larger preparations.

#### ✎ Exam core

| Method | Key feature | Main indication |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional | Amalgam-type box, 90° butt joint | Root-surface lesions |
| Bevelled conventional | Box plus 45° enamel bevel | Class III, IV, V; large restorations |
| Modified | Scooped, defect-only | Small lesions, NCCLs |
| Combined | Bevel on crown, conventional on root | Class III/V onto root |
| Box-only | Proximal box, no occlusal step | Proximal lesion, sound occlusal surface |
| Facial/lingual slot | Embrasure access, ridge intact | Proximal lesion, intact ridge |
| Tunnel | Occlusal fossa access | Lesion at least 3 mm below ridge (controversial) |
| GIC/RMGI | No bevel, no grooves | Root caries, cervical lesions |
| Indirect inlay/onlay | 2 mm depth, divergent, rounded, butt margins | Large esthetic posterior restorations |

**General rules:** remove only the defect; rounded internal angles; no dovetails in anterior teeth; the gingival wall is the most critical wall for resistance and retention (Marzouk et al, undated); no occlusal bevels; keep enamel at margins.

**Conclusion.** The method is chosen by lesion size and site, the substrate at the margin, occlusal load and the material. Adhesion has replaced mechanical retention and extension for prevention; bevels and grooves are used selectively, and the books still disagree on bevel width, proximal bevels and tunnel preparations.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Composite margins should be bevelled to improve retention and seal."**
*Textbook says:* bevels increase bonding area and retention and expose rod ends (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]; Class II proximal bevels reduce leakage (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].
*What other evidence shows:* enamel bonds are clinically adequate without bevels and Class 3 leakage did not differ in vitro (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]; proximal box walls are rarely bevelled (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; occlusal bevels increase wear clinically (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].
*Agreement or challenge:* the books agree on no occlusal bevel and on esthetic facial bevels in Class III and IV; they disagree on proximal bevels. [Certain]
*Strength:* the leakage evidence is in vitro; the occlusal-wear finding is clinical. [Likely]
*Conclusion and clinical implication:* bevel accessible anterior enamel margins for esthetics and retention in large restorations; do not bevel occlusal, cemental or thin cervical margins; for Class II proximal walls, state both views.

**Claim 2: "Tunnel preparations conserve tooth structure and should be preferred for small proximal lesions."**
*Textbook says:* Mount and Ngo use it when the lesion is at least 3 mm below the ridge (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]; it avoids the 46% stiffness loss of a Class 2 (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].
*What other evidence shows:* higher clinical failure rates, residual caries and near-pulpal preparation (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; GIC tunnel restorations inadequate long-term and the technique is not supported (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].
*Agreement or challenge:* direct challenge, based on clinical studies cited in Summitt's and Sturdevant's. [Likely]
*Conclusion and clinical implication:* tunnel is a selective option for well-sized lesions with magnification and caries dye; the box-only or slot design is the more predictable default.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Classic types (Singh; Garg): conventional, bevelled conventional, modified, plus combined (Singh).
- Conventional = amalgam-type box, 90° margin, for root surfaces; modified = scooped, defect-only.
- Bevel at 45°; widths differ: Class III 0.25-0.5 (Singh), 0.2-0.5 (Garg), 0.5-2 mm (Sturdevant's); Class IV facial 60°, 2-3 mm infinite bevel (Summitt's).
- No occlusal bevel in any source; proximal Class II bevels disputed (Summitt's yes, Sturdevant's rarely).
- Marzouk: partial, long, hollow-ground bevels, scalloping, skirting.
- Box-only (no occlusal lesion), facial/lingual slot (ridge intact, at least 2 mm), tunnel (lesion at least 3 mm below ridge; controversial).
- GIC: no bevel, no grooves, undermined enamel may stay.
- Indirect: 2 mm depth and isthmus, divergent walls, rounded angles, butt margins, no bevels.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Placing bevels on occlusal margins or on cementum.
- Describing composite preparations with amalgam features (extension for prevention, uniform 1.5 mm depth, dovetails).
- Confusing the facial or lingual slot with Mount's or Summitt's "slot" through the marginal ridge.
- Bevelling GIC margins or adding retention grooves for GIC.
- Quoting one bevel width as the rule without noting that books differ.
