# Bevels, flares and finish lines in the success of restorations

**Marks:** 20 · Essay

**Answer blueprint:** Definitions → types and functions of bevels → marginal geometry → flares → finish lines → material-specific clinical application.

## Introduction and definitions

Marginal design influences restorative strength, adaptation, finishing and periodontal compatibility. In traditional cast-restoration terminology, the **circumferential tie** refers to the peripheral marginal anatomy formed by bevels, flares and finish lines (Marzouk et al., n.d.).

- **Bevel:** an inclined plane at the cavosurface margin, produced by cutting the wall obliquely.
- **Flare:** a flat or concave peripheral extension of a facial or lingual proximal wall.
- **Finish line:** the junction between prepared and unprepared tooth structure in an extracoronal preparation (Fan et al., 2013).

## 1. Classification of bevels

A commonly described tissue-depth classification includes six forms (Marzouk et al., n.d.; Singh, 2020):

| Type | Extent and significance |
|---|---|
| **Partial** | Part of the enamel wall; may remove weak enamel rods. |
| **Short** | Entire enamel wall, without entering dentin. |
| **Long** | Enamel and up to approximately half the dentinal wall; preserves internal wall form in traditional cast-metal designs. |
| **Full** | Entire enamel and dentinal wall; may compromise internal resistance and retention, so generally avoided. |
| **Counterbevel** | Broad bevel on a reduced cusp when the cusp is capped; creates a thin, burnishable metal edge. |
| **Hollow-ground** | Concave form described in some texts to provide additional restorative bulk; use is material- and design-dependent. |

**Terminology:** “Reverse bevel” is inconsistent across texts: Sturdevant uses it for a counterbevel on a reduced cusp, while some authors use it for an internal bevel on the gingival floor. State its location and purpose (Sturdevant, 2019; Marzouk et al., n.d.).

## 2. Functions and marginal geometry

For cast-metal restorations, bevels:
1. Produce a **strong, obtuse enamel margin** and a thin, acute metal edge that can be burnished.
2. Remove unsupported enamel and improve the metal–tooth junction.
3. Permit limited adjustment of marginal metal; a gingival bevel may provide a lap or sliding fit in selected designs.

Their principal purpose is a suitable junction, not compensation for inaccurate or incomplete seating (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

**Traditional cast-metal values (Sturdevant, 2019):**
- **Occlusal:** approximately **40° marginal metal** and **140° enamel**.
- **Gingival:** approximately **30° marginal metal**; bevel width commonly **0.5–1 mm**.
- **Counterbevel:** produces an approximately **30° metal edge**.
- The enamel margin is generally around **140–150°**.

Always specify the reference: marginal metal angle, enamel angle or bevel inclination. They are not interchangeable measurements.

**Do bevels always improve marginal fit?** No. A bevel may allow burnishing of a thin metal margin, but does not guarantee a smaller gap. Fit also depends on casting relief, cement thickness, preparation taper and seating. Fan et al. (2013) describe laboratory and geometric evidence that steep bevels, particularly beyond about 45° to the external tooth surface, may increase the cement-filled opening; this is not proof of a clinical outcome.

## 3. Flares

Flares are principally described for cast-metal proximal preparations.

- **Primary flare:** the basic facial or lingual extension of the proximal wall, traditionally directed at about **45° to the inner dentinal wall**. It brings the preparation out of the contact area and permits finishing (Marzouk et al., n.d.; Sturdevant, 2019).
- **Secondary flare:** a flatter peripheral plane, usually mainly in enamel, superimposed on the primary flare. It may be used for wide lesions, broad or malpositioned contacts and peripheral irregularities. It helps produce accessible, cleansable margins; keep it minimal where esthetics are important, especially mesiofacially on maxillary premolars and first molars (Sturdevant, 2019).

Flaring should remain conservative and should not be transferred automatically to adhesive ceramic or composite preparations.

## 4. Finish lines for extracoronal restorations

A finish line should provide adequate material thickness, a discernible margin and conservative tooth reduction (Fan et al., 2013).

| Finish line | Features and usual application |
|---|---|
| **Knife-edge** | Minimal reduction, but difficult to identify and may produce an overcontoured restoration; selected use only. |
| **Chamfer** | Conservative, rounded concave margin; commonly used for cast-gold crowns. |
| **Shoulder** | Provides restorative bulk; useful for ceramics but requires more tooth reduction and accurate preparation. |
| **Bevelled shoulder** | Shoulder with a marginal bevel; selected for particular metal-restoration designs, not universal. |

For all-ceramic crowns, a rounded internal line angle and butt-joint external margin support ceramic bulk and reduce stress concentration (Rouse, 2013). No single finish line is ideal for every material and clinical situation (Fan et al., 2013).

## 5. Material-specific application

| Restorative material | Marginal approach |
|---|---|
| **Cast gold / cast-metal inlays and onlays** | Selected occlusal, proximal and gingival bevels; thin, burnishable metal margins. Flares may be used in proximal boxes. |
| **Amalgam** | Generally a 90° butt-joint margin for adequate restorative bulk and edge strength; avoid thin bevelled margins. |
| **Glass ionomer** | Butt-joint margin; avoid a thin, brittle bevelled edge (Overton et al., 2013). |
| **Posterior composite** | Occlusal bevels generally not indicated; bevel suitable enamel margins selectively according to access and adhesive design (Hilton and Broome, 2013). |
| **Ceramic inlays/onlays** | Rounded internal angles and butt-joint margins; routine occlusal or gingival bevels offer no established advantage (Fasbinder et al., 2013). |
| **All-ceramic crowns** | Rounded shoulder or other material-appropriate finish line, with a rounded axiogingival transition and butt-joint margin (Rouse, 2013). |

## Conclusion

Bevels and flares are primarily associated with traditional cast-metal preparations, where they help create a strong enamel margin and a thin, burnishable metal edge. Finish lines must provide adequate restorative bulk and a clearly defined margin. **The restorative material determines whether a margin should be bevelled or a butt joint.**
