## 1. Why margins decide success: the circumferential tie

### Explanation

In a cast restoration the weakest link is the tooth/cement/cast joint complex (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Marzouk calls the peripheral marginal anatomy that manages this joint the circumferential tie (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], and argues that cast restorations are the only restorations that can fulfil all of Noy's enamel-wall requirements, because they are stronger than tooth structure (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Bevels, flares and extracoronal finish lines are therefore three forms of one idea: shaping the margin so that tooth and restoration each have a strong edge and the cement line is as thin as possible.

**Definitions.** A bevel is a plane of a cavity wall or floor directed away from the cavity preparation (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Singh repeats this and adds that bevels are flexible extensions allowing inclusion of surface defects (Singh, 2020) [SRC], and also describes a bevel as any abrupt incline between the cavity wall and the cavosurface margin (Singh, 2020) [SRC]. Garg and Garg give the classical definition: the inclination one surface makes with another when not at right angles (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Flares are flat or concave peripheral portions of the facial and lingual walls (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. A finish line is the border where prepared tooth meets unprepared tooth surface (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** The tooth-cement-casting junction is the weakest link of a cast restoration (Marzouk et al, undated). Its marginal design, the *circumferential tie*, comprises bevels, flares and finish lines.

**Definitions.**
- *Bevel:* a plane of a cavity wall or floor directed away from the preparation (Marzouk et al, undated).
- *Flare:* flat or concave peripheral portion of the facial or lingual proximal wall (Marzouk et al, undated).
- *Finish line:* border of the preparation where prepared tooth meets unprepared surface (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).

## 2. Classification of bevels, side by side by author

### Explanation

**Marzouk's six bevels** (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]: *partial* (part of the enamel wall, up to two-thirds) [SRC], not used in cast restorations except to trim weak enamel rods [SRC]; *short* (entire enamel wall, not dentin) [SRC], used mostly with Class I alloys, especially gold types 1 and 2 [SRC]; *long* (all enamel and up to half the dentinal wall) [SRC], the most frequently used bevel for the first three classes of cast materials [SRC], because it preserves the internal boxed-up resistance and retention [SRC]; *full* (all of the dentinal and enamel wall) [SRC], which deprives the preparation of its internal resistance and retention [SRC] and is to be avoided except where no other bevel is possible [SRC]; *counterbevel*, used when capping cusps, opposite an axial wall on the facial or lingual surface [SRC]; *hollow-ground (concave)*, any of the above prepared in concave form for more cast bulk [SRC], which Marzouk calls ideal for Class IV and V cast materials [SRC]. Marzouk's Class IV is nickel-chromium alloys [SRC] and Class V is castable ceramics [SRC]. Marzouk lists the *reverse bevel* separately, as an auxiliary retention means: placed at the expense of the gingival floor [SRC], forming an internal dentinal plane inclining gingivally-axially that locks the restoration [SRC]; it is not used with cast ceramics [SRC].

**Singh** keeps the same six types (Singh, 2020) [SRC] and adds that a counterbevel is usually one-third of the height of the clinical crown (Singh, 2020) [SRC], a dimension Marzouk does not give.

**Garg and Garg** call the first type "ultrashort or partial" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC], describe hollow-ground as not a bevel in the true sense (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] and rarely used (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC], the opposite of Marzouk's "ideal for Class IV and V", and add two reverse (inverted) bevels: one on the gingival seat towards the root in anterior teeth (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] and one in posterior MOD preparations to prevent tipping and increase resistance and retention (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

**Sikri** gives a different four: partial, indicated in direct filling gold (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; short, preferred in cast gold (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; inverted, on the labial shoulder of metal-ceramic crowns for marginal esthetics (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; and reverse, at the cervical wall, which resists rotational displacement during cementation (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. So Marzouk names the long bevel as the commonest for cast gold, Sikri the short bevel; neither cites clinical data for its choice.

**Sturdevant** (J.R. Sturdevant, Ch 23) does not classify bevels by tissue depth; it names a counterbevel (reverse bevel) as a generous-width bevel on the facial or lingual margin of a reduced cusp (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]. Note that Sturdevant uses "reverse bevel" as a synonym of counterbevel, whereas Marzouk, Garg and Sikri use it for an internal gingival-floor bevel: the same term, two different features.

## 3. Functions of bevels and the angles used

### Explanation

**Functions.** Bevels create obtuse-angled marginal tooth structure (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], the bulkiest and strongest configuration (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], with acute-angled marginal alloy that can be burnished. Marginal bevels reduce the error factor (space between cast and tooth) (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], threefold or more (Singh, 2020) [SRC]. They are flexible extensions (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], and gingival bevels can bring margins to cleansable or protected areas (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and give a lap, sliding fit (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]; the gingival bevel also removes weak enamel (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]. A bevel may allow a better seal in slightly undersized castings (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], and a thin gold margin is more readily burnished (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; their primary role is a better junction between metal and tooth (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

On retention the books disagree. Marzouk counts bevels as one of the major retention forms (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], because only at the circumferential tie is there the possibility of direct frictional retention between casting and tooth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Sikri says bevels decrease the direct frictional component and improve retention only indirectly (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

**Angles.** Sturdevant's target is 30-40 degree marginal metal (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC] and a 140-150 degree enamel margin (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]: 40 degree metal and 140 degree enamel occlusally (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]; 40 degrees everywhere except 30 degrees gingivally (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]. Enamel above 150 degrees gives an ill-defined margin (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]; metal above 40 degrees is too bulky to burnish (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]. Occlusal bevel width about one-fourth of the wall depth (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]; gingival bevel 0.5-1 mm (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]; counterbevel giving 30 degree metal (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]; marginal-ridge margins always bevelled (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]. Singh reproduces 140-150 degree tooth (Singh, 2020) [SRC] and 30-40 degree metal (Singh, 2020) [SRC].

Marzouk measures differently: the occlusal bevel at 30-45 degrees to the long axis of the crown or wall proper (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], increased with cavity width (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and for direct wax patterns (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], decreased with steep cusps (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; the gingival long bevel at 30-45 degrees to the wall proper (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] [SRC]; the counterbevel's "embracing angle" to the long axis averaging 30-70 degrees (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

Summitt (Fan, Stevenson and Berry) uses 0.5 mm bevels on occlusal, proximal and gingival margins (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC] at about 45 degrees to the external tooth surface (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Garg and Garg give a 45 degree gingival bevel (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] covering half the gingival wall (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] and a 40 degree occlusal bevel (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; but the same chapter gives a metal wedge of 30-35 degrees and enamel of 140-150 degrees on one page (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] [SRC] and cavosurface 135-140 degrees with metal 35-45 degrees on the next (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] [SRC]. That is an internal inconsistency; quote Sturdevant's values and say so if you use Garg.

Much of the numerical "disagreement" is a difference in reference line: Sturdevant measures the metal angle at the margin; Marzouk measures the bevel against the long axis or wall proper; Summitt measures against the external tooth surface [Likely]. A 45 degree bevel to the external surface and a 40 degree marginal metal angle describe similar geometry [Guessing: depends on wall inclination].

**Functions:** (1) obtuse, strong marginal enamel with an acute, burnishable metal edge (Marzouk et al, undated); (2) remove weak enamel; (3) reduce the marginal error factor threefold or more (Singh, 2020); (4) lap, sliding fit gingivally (Sturdevant JR, 2019); (5) flexible extensions to include defects and reach cleansable areas; (6) retention: major per Marzouk et al (undated), only indirect per Sikri (2020).

## 4. Flares

### Explanation

Marzouk describes the primary flare as the conventional and basic part of the tie (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], always at 45 degrees to the inner dentinal wall proper (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Singh (Singh, 2020) [SRC] and Garg and Garg (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] give the same 45 degree rule. The secondary flare is almost always a flat plane superimposed peripherally on the primary flare (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], usually prepared solely in enamel (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], with variable angulation and extent (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; it is used for very wide lesions, broad or malposed contacts (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], ovoid teeth with peripheral marginal undercuts (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and peripheral surface defects. Sturdevant defines the primary flare as the one that brings the box out of contact (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]; its secondary flare gives 40 degree metal (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC] and makes margins self-cleaning and accessible for finishing (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]; for esthetics the mesiofacial flare of maxillary premolars and first molars is minimal (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC], and Garg says secondary flares are not given where esthetics matter (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

**Sikri dissents.** Sikri calls the 90 degree axial half plus 45 degree primary flare an old concept: flaring from the axiofacial or axiolingual line angle to the margin did not alter retention and resistance (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; the secondary flare is exclusively for gold inlays (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; and the box-only, slice and modified-flare preparations are no longer used (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Singh, in contrast, still teaches box, slice, auxiliary slice and box-cum-slice variants (Singh, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Primary flare:** basic part of the tie on facial and lingual proximal walls, at 45° to the dentinal wall proper (Marzouk et al, undated; Singh, 2020); brings the box out of contact (Sturdevant JR, 2019).
**Secondary flare:** flat plane superimposed on the primary flare, mostly in enamel, variable angle (Marzouk et al, undated); gives 40° metal and self-cleansing margins (Sturdevant JR, 2019). For wide lesions, broad contacts, ovoid teeth with undercuts. Minimal mesiofacially on maxillary premolars and first molars (Sturdevant JR, 2019).
**Dissent:** Sikri (2020) calls the 45° primary flare an old concept and slice designs obsolete; Singh (2020) still teaches them.

## 5. Finish lines for extracoronal preparations

### Explanation

Summitt names knife-edged, chamfer and shoulder as the commonest finish lines (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC] and says there is no agreement on the ideal finish line (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Marzouk: the chamfer is the most universally used design for Class I-III cast materials (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], with little tooth involvement, 0.5 mm maximal depth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; it is contraindicated for Class IV and V cast materials (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; the knife-edge has the least tooth involvement (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], suits only very castable-burnishable gold (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and risks indefinite termination of the casting (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; the bevelled shoulder involves the most tooth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] is ideal for subgingival margins (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and can be used for any class of cast material (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; the hollow-ground bevel is an exaggerated chamfer or concave bevelled shoulder (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

Summitt (Fan et al): knife-edge generally not desirable, hard to see on the die (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC] and tending to overcontoured restorations (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], though used on mesially tipped molars and the lingual of mandibular molars (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; chamfer often preferred for cast gold extracoronal restorations (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC] and more conservative than a shoulder (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; shoulder where bulk is needed (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], with the most critical fit because nothing can be burnished (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; a bevel is recommended for proximal boxes (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC] but on full crowns risks encroaching on the epithelial attachment (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri lists the shoulder for bulk of material (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], and Rouse gives a rounded axial-gingival line angle with a butt-joint margin for all-ceramic crowns (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].

**The slip-joint versus butt-joint argument.** The traditional teaching that bevels reduce marginal gaps rested on mathematical models that ignored cement thickness (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; bevelling beyond 45 degrees significantly increases the cement-filled opening (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], because the steeper the bevel, the larger the vertical discrepancy (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], and one study found better marginal fit with shoulder than bevelled finish lines (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk states the opposite: marginal symptoms of internal spacing are inversely proportional to the gingival bevel's angulation (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. The two are compatible only if the casting is not fully seated; Summitt's argument assumes a fully relieved casting with minimum cement thickness (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], and the chapter adds that taper, die spacer and seating force also govern fit (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. These are laboratory and geometric arguments, not clinical outcome data.

**Continuity.** Junctions between tie elements should have no sharp angles (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], rounded only at the level of the tie, not internally (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; the tie should be the smoothest part of the preparation (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; counterbevel-flare junctions are rounded slightly (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC].

**Direct and ceramic restorations, in brief.** Composite enamel margins may be bevelled or flared beyond 90 degrees for bonding (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], but occlusal bevels are not indicated (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; for ceramic inlays there is no benefit to occlusal or gingival bevels (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Material-by-material detail is in Q06.

| Finish line | Marzouk et al, undated | Fan et al, 2013 (Summitt) |
|---|---|---|
| Knife-edge | Least tooth removal; only burnishable gold | Undesirable: hard to read, overcontours; tipped molars |
| Chamfer | Most universal; ≤0.5 mm; not for Ni-Cr or ceramics | Preferred for gold crowns |
| Shoulder | — | Bulk for ceramics; most critical fit |
| Bevelled shoulder | Most tooth removal; ideal subgingivally | Bevel risks attachment on crowns |
| Hollow-ground | Exaggerated chamfer; ideal for Ni-Cr and castable ceramics | — |

All-ceramic crowns: rounded axiogingival angle with a butt-joint margin (Rouse, 2013). There is no agreement on the ideal finish line (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).

**Do bevels improve fit?** Yes, per Marzouk et al (undated) and Singh (2020). Fan, Stevenson and Berry (2013): that idea came from models ignoring cement; beyond 45° bevels *increase* the cement-filled opening. Both are laboratory arguments.

**Conclusion.** Bevels and flares suit burnishable gold; amalgam, glass ionomer and ceramics need 90° butt joints. Tie junctions must be smooth and rounded.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: a bevelled gingival margin improves the marginal fit of a cast restoration.** Textbook: Marzouk et al (undated) and Singh (2020) say bevels reduce marginal spacing threefold or more, and Boushell and Walter (2019) say they help seal undersized castings. Other evidence: Fan, Stevenson and Berry (2013) report that the classical argument came from geometric models without cement thickness, that bevels steeper than 45° increase the cement-filled opening, and that shoulder crowns fitted better in one study; they also note that taper, die spacer and seating force matter more. Agreement or challenge: a direct challenge to the traditional teaching. Strength: laboratory and modelling data only; no clinical trial comparing bevelled and butt gold margins was cited in the chapters read. Conclusion: a short bevel (about 0.5-1 mm) is still reasonable for burnishable gold on intracoronal boxes, but its value is in burnishing and removing weak enamel, not in automatically closing gaps. [Likely] Clinical implication: do not rely on a long, steep bevel to hide a poorly seated casting; control taper and seating first.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Circumferential tie = bevels (occlusal, gingival) + flares (facial, lingual) + finish lines.
2. Marzouk: six bevels (partial, short, long, full, counter, hollow-ground); reverse bevel is a separate retention feature.
3. Sturdevant: 40° metal / 140° enamel; 30° metal gingivally and at counterbevels; gingival bevel 0.5-1 mm.
4. Primary flare 45° to wall proper; secondary flare for wide lesions, broad contacts, undercuts; minimal mesiofacially.
5. Knife-edge (least tooth), chamfer (gold crowns), shoulder (ceramics), bevelled shoulder (most tooth, subgingival).
6. Bevels beyond 45° may widen the cement line (Fan et al, 2013).
7. No bevels for amalgam, GIC, ceramics; no occlusal bevels for posterior composite.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Using "reverse bevel" without saying whether you mean Sturdevant's counterbevel or Marzouk's internal gingival-floor bevel.
2. Quoting angles without the reference line (metal angle, angle to long axis, or angle to tooth surface).
3. Writing that bevels are used for amalgam or ceramic margins.
4. Treating the primary and secondary flare as the same plane.
5. Stating as fact that bevels always reduce the marginal gap, ignoring the cement-thickness argument.
