## 1. Introduction and the planning question

### Explanation

Sikri frames the choice by remaining tooth structure: direct restorations are usually not advisable in teeth where more than half the tooth structure is lost (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; the factors that decide material and technique are size of the lesion, endodontic/periodontal status, patient compliance, esthetics and operator competence (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. He records that the advent of all-ceramic systems revolutionised the indirect protocol (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and lists, among indications for inlays/onlays, patients who cannot afford long chairside time (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Fasbinder and Neiva stress that CAD/CAM is "an alternative means of restoration fabrication, not the restoration per se" (Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]: planning decides the restoration (inlay, onlay, crown), the material, occlusion and isolation; the fabrication route comes second.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Tooth-coloured indirect restorations are composite or ceramic inlays, onlays, partial crowns (overlays), endocrowns and veneers, made outside the mouth and bonded with resin cement. Their success depends less on mechanical retention than on correct case selection, an adhesive-friendly preparation and a controlled bonding protocol.

**Treatment planning sequence**
1. History: esthetic demand, parafunction (bruxism, clenching), caries risk, oral hygiene, ability to attend two visits or need for a single CAD/CAM visit.
2. Examination of the tooth: size of the lesion, remaining walls and cusps, marginal ridges, cracks, margin position (enamel or dentin, supra- or subgingival), pulp and endodontic status, periodontal health.
3. Occlusion: wear facets, opposing restorations, contact position relative to planned margins.
4. Decision direct versus indirect: direct is usually not advisable when more than half the tooth structure is lost (Sikri, 2017); deciding factors are lesion size, endodontic/periodontal status, compliance, esthetics and operator skill (Sikri, 2017).
5. Choice of restoration and material, then fabrication route (laboratory or chairside CAD/CAM). CAD/CAM is a means of fabrication, not a different restoration (Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019).

## 2. Indications and contraindications

### Explanation

Indirect composite: main indication is esthetic replacement of metallic restorations; also large defects and contact/contour improvement (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Contraindications: bruxism/clenching (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], inability to keep a dry field (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], deep subgingival margins (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and poor hygiene. Ceramic inlays/onlays: contraindicated in poor hygiene, insufficient structure for bonding, poor moisture control and parafunction (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Summitt's: indicated when esthetics is an overriding desire and all margins can be on enamel (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; ideally no parafunction (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; if the tooth cannot be isolated an adhesive restoration should not be placed (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's chapter, quoting Garber's list, adds the endodontically compromised tooth, where an etched ceramic restoration can replace post-core-crown (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC]; Garber's list also includes large carious or traumatic lesions with undermined enamel (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC] and proven or suspected metal allergy (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC]; contraindications include aggressive wear and gold restorations in opposing teeth (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| | Indications | Contraindications |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect composite inlay/onlay | Esthetic replacement of metal restorations (main indication); large defects; improving contacts and contours (Sikri, 2017) | Bruxism/clenching; dry field not possible; deep subgingival margins; poor hygiene (Sikri, 2017) |
| Ceramic inlay/onlay/partial crown | Esthetics with margins on enamel (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013); large lesions with undermined enamel; endodontically compromised teeth as an alternative to post-core-crown; metal allergy (Stappert et al, 2018) | Poor hygiene, too little structure for bonding, no moisture control, parafunction (Sikri, 2017); aggressive wear, opposing gold (Stappert et al, 2018) |

Isolation is decisive: if the tooth cannot be isolated, an adhesive restoration should not be placed (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013).

## 3. Choice of material: composite or ceramic

### Explanation

The books do not agree on a single rule. Sikri reports that "a few authors" use isthmus size: under two-thirds of the intercuspal width composite, over two-thirds ceramic (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Summitt's advises resin composite first for two- and three-surface inlays of moderate width (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], but prefers ceramic as soon as even one cusp is covered (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], and for short molars, where the onlay needs only 2 mm occlusal reduction and no axial reduction for retention (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Ceramic's main advantage is reliability of cementation (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], whereas the composite-cement bond is "a weak link" (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. One in vivo study found higher breakage of Class 2 ceramic inlays than resin inlays (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; in vitro advantages of resin inlays over direct composite are not corroborated in vivo (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Situation | Preferred option |
|---|---|
| Two-/three-surface inlay of moderate width | Resin composite first (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) |
| Isthmus over two-thirds intercuspal width | Ceramic ("a few authors", Sikri, 2017) |
| Any cusp covered | Ceramic onlay (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) |
| Short molar crown | Ceramic onlay: 2 mm occlusal reduction, no axial retention needed (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) |
| Root-treated molar with sound walls | Endocrown (Alani and Patel, 2025) |
| Discoloured, malformed anterior teeth | Porcelain/pressed ceramic veneer |

Ceramic bonds more reliably (etched and silanated), while the composite-cement interface is a weak link (Sikri, 2017).

## 4. Concepts of preparation for posterior inlays and onlays

### Explanation

Summitt's states ceramic and composite preparations are essentially the same and CAD/CAM preparations do not differ from laboratory ones (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], and that there is little research to support any one design (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Shared concepts: divergent, relatively nonretentive design (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013; Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019) [SRC], adhesive-style preparations (Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019) [SRC], rounded boxes but no grooves (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], rounded internal angles (Sikri, 2017; Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC], smooth flowing geometry to avoid overmilling (Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019) [SRC], isthmus 2 mm (Sikri, 2017; Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019) [SRC].

Figures differ by book:
- Taper: Sikri gives 10-12° for composite (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and calls 10-12° mandatory for ceramic (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], against 2-6° for gold (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Summitt's and Sturdevant's say only "divergent", with no number.
- Depth/thickness: Sikri: depth no less than 2.0 mm at the shallowest point (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], axial 1.0-1.5 mm (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], uniform 2.0 mm ideal for ceramic with 1.5 mm axially (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Summitt's: at least 1.5 mm central fossa and 2.0 mm over functional cusps (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Cusp reduction for composite at least 2.0 mm (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Cusp coverage if more than one-third of the cuspal incline is involved (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].
- Proximal flare: 90-100° exit angle (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Basic concept:** retention and resistance come mainly from adhesion, so the preparation is divergent, relatively nonretentive and as conservative as the defect allows (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013; Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019). Ceramic, composite and CAD/CAM preparations are essentially the same (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013).

| Feature | Gold inlay | Composite/ceramic inlay/onlay |
|---|---|---|
| Outline | Narrow | Wider, defect-dictated |
| Wall taper | 2-6° | 10-12° (Sikri, 2017); "divergent" (Summitt's, Sturdevant's) |
| Line angles | Definite | Rounded |
| Bevels | Given | None |
| Retention aids | Grooves, ferrule | Rounded boxes only, no grooves; no ferrule |

**Key dimensions (by source)**
- Isthmus at least 2 mm wide (Sikri, 2017; Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019).
- Depth: no less than 2.0 mm, axial 1.0-1.5 mm; ceramic ideally uniform 2.0 mm, axially 1.5 mm (Sikri, 2017). Summitt's: at least 1.5 mm central fossa, 2.0 mm over functional cusps.
- Cusp coverage when more than one-third of the incline is involved (Sikri, 2017); reduce at least 2.0 mm following cusp inclines.
- Proximal flare 90-100° (Sikri, 2017).
- Rounded internal angles reduce stress and help fabrication and milling (Stappert et al, 2018; Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019).
- Undercuts blocked out with resin composite base; GIC is weak as a base (Sikri, 2017) and is used only for block-out (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013).

## 5. Margin design

### Explanation

Butt joint, no bevel: Sikri requires a flat gingival butt joint with no bevel (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and 90° cavosurface angles for ceramic (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], because bevels leave fragile resin margins (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: bevelled margins must be avoided (Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's: no benefit to bevels (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], butt joint reduces chipping but shows a line (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Both chairside chapters describe a 45° football-diamond modification for esthetic blending (Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's chapter instead describes a hollow-ground chamfer confined to marginal enamel (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC]. Onlays carry no ferrule (Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; margins supragingival where possible (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Source | Recommended margin |
|---|---|
| Sikri, 2017 | Flat butt joint, 90° cavosurface, no bevel |
| Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013; Fasbinder and Neiva, 2019 | 90° butt joint; for esthetics, a 45° modification with a football-shaped diamond keeping ceramic bulk |
| Stappert et al, 2018 | No bevel; hollow-ground chamfer confined to marginal enamel |

All agree on no bevel (thin ceramic or resin edges chip) and on supragingival enamel margins where possible. CAD/CAM onlays use no ferrule.

## 6. Veneers, endocrowns and newer concepts

### Explanation

Veneers: intra-enamel preparation usually indicated (Heymann and Ritter, 2019) [SRC]; window design for most direct composite veneers (Heymann and Ritter, 2019) [SRC], butt-joint incisal or incisal-lapping designs for most indirect porcelain veneers (Heymann and Ritter, 2019) [SRC], with a well-defined chamfer at the gingival crest (Heymann and Ritter, 2019) [SRC]; depth 0.5-0.75 mm midfacially tapering to 0.3-0.5 mm gingivally (Heymann and Ritter, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri: depth cuts 0.5-0.7 mm for maxillary teeth (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], incisal reduction at least 1.0 mm (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], enamel all round the periphery (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Goldstein's extended design: labial 0.3-0.5 mm (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC], incisal shortening 0.5-1.5 mm (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC], minimum thickness 0.4 mm axially and 1.5 mm incisally (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC], bonding mainly to enamel (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC]. Indirect veneers outlast direct (Heymann and Ritter, 2019) [SRC]. Note an internal contradiction between books on bur labels: Goldstein's says LVS-1 gives 0.5 mm (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC], Sikri calls LVS No. 1 0.3 mm and LVS No. 2 0.5 mm (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; the bur numbers should therefore not be memorised, only the depths.

Endocrown: engages the pulp chamber as a single piece (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], occlusal reduction at least 2 mm (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], supragingival margin and adhesive cementation (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Loss of marginal ridges reduces stiffness by up to 45-60% (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], arguing for cusp coverage. IDS: sealing freshly cut dentine immediately after preparation (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], decreasing post-operative sensitivity (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], before impression (Donovan and Oliveira, 2019) [SRC]. Cervical margin relocation lifts a deep proximal margin with composite (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. The cavity-design-optimisation label used in some articles was not found in the books read; IDS and CMR are the book-supported elements.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Veneers:** intra-enamel preparation; window design for direct composite veneers, butt-joint or incisal-lapping designs for porcelain (Heymann and Ritter, 2019).

| Dimension | Heymann and Ritter, 2019 | Sikri, 2017 | Stappert et al, 2018 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labial | 0.5-0.75 mm, 0.3-0.5 mm gingivally | Depth cuts 0.5-0.7 mm (maxillary) | 0.3-0.5 mm; minimum 0.4 mm |
| Incisal | - | At least 1.0 mm | 0.5-1.5 mm; 1.5 mm minimum ceramic |
| Margins | Chamfer at gingival crest | Enamel all round | Mainly enamel, no bevel |

**Endocrown:** single piece engaging the pulp chamber, no post; at least 2 mm occlusal reduction, undercut-free chamber walls, supragingival margin, adhesive cementation (Alani and Patel, 2025).

**Immediate dentine sealing:** freshly cut dentine is bonded immediately after preparation, before impression, reducing sensitivity and contamination (Alani and Patel, 2025; Donovan and Oliveira, 2019).

**Cervical margin relocation (deep margin elevation):** a deep subgingival proximal margin is raised with composite so the indirect margin can be isolated (Alani and Patel, 2025).

## 7. Cementation implications

### Explanation

Resin cement is the only recommended luting material (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; dual-cure cements preferred (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Ceramic: hydrofluoric acid 8-12% for 1-3 min (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], then silane (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; for composite restorations silane is the preferred wetting agent after air abrasion (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; glycerin gel on accessible margins prevents the oxygen-inhibited layer (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Marginal wear is common (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Whether bonded restorations strengthen the tooth is disputed: Sikri says weakened tooth structure can be strengthened (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Summitt's says this is not universally supported (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Outcomes: over 96% success at 10 years for IPS Empress inlays/partial crowns (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; survival 74-100% up to 12 years (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC]; bulk fracture when material is under 2 mm (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- Preparation design anticipates bonding: rubber dam, supragingival enamel margins and IDS on dentine.
- Resin cement only; dual-cure in posterior teeth (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013; Sikri, 2017).
- Ceramic intaglio: 8-12% hydrofluoric acid 1-3 min, then silane (Sikri, 2017). Composite intaglio: air abrasion and silane.
- Tooth: etch, prime, bond; seat, remove excess, cure; glycerin gel on margins.
- Failures are bulk fracture where material is under 2 mm thick and marginal wear (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013).

**Conclusion.** Planning chooses the patient, tooth and material; preparation follows adhesive rules (divergence, rounded angles, uniform thickness, unbevelled enamel margins); exact figures differ between books.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: indirect tooth-coloured restorations are better than direct composite and strengthen the tooth.**
- Textbook says: Sikri lists lower shrinkage, better properties and strengthening of weakened tooth structure (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].
- Other evidence: Summitt's reports that in vitro advantages of resin inlays are not corroborated in vivo, with 5- and 11-year studies showing no difference from direct composite, and that strengthening is not universally supported (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC].
- Agreement/challenge: challenge. Strength: the "advantage" is mostly laboratory (in vitro); the clinical comparisons are few. [Likely]
- Conclusion: indirect composite is justified by access, contour and contact problems or large isthmus, not by an assumed superiority. Clinical implication: try direct first for moderate cavities.

**Claim: taper must be 10-12°.**
- Sikri gives 10-12° (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Summitt's notes little research supports any design (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. The figure is a clinical rule, not a tested optimum. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Adhesion, not mechanics, retains the restoration: divergent, nonretentive, no grooves, no ferrule.
- No bevels; butt joint (Sikri, Summitt's, Sturdevant's) versus enamel chamfer (Goldstein's).
- Isthmus at least 2 mm; ceramic about 1.5-2 mm thick; functional cusp 2 mm.
- Taper 10-12° (Sikri) versus 2-6° for gold.
- Composite first for moderate inlays; ceramic once a cusp is covered (Summitt's).
- Rubber dam is a precondition; no isolation, no adhesive restoration.
- IDS, CMR and endocrown are the newer preparation concepts.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Giving gold-inlay features (bevels, grooves, 2-6° taper) to ceramic preparations.
- Citing one taper or thickness as universal; the books differ.
- Omitting isolation and enamel-margin assessment from treatment planning.
- Treating CAD/CAM as a different preparation; it is a fabrication route.
- Saying bonded inlays always strengthen teeth; the claim is contested.
