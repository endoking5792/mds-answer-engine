## 1. What cavosurface treatment means and what decides it

### Explanation

The cavosurface margin is the surface periphery of the preparation, where the wall meets the tooth surface (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], and the cavosurface angle is the angle between that wall and the adjacent surface (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Sturdevant defines it as the angle of tooth structure between the prepared wall and the external surface (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], varying with location, rod direction and the restorative material (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Garg and Garg say the margin design depends mainly on the compressive, edge and tensile strength of the material (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. External wall finishing is the further development of a specific design at the margin (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], and an occlusal contact on the margin causes early fatigue and failure (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

**Noy's requirements** (as reported by Marzouk): enamel must rest on sound dentin (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; the rods forming the cavosurface angle must have inner ends on sound dentin (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; the strongest wall has those rods covered by restorative material, which only a bevel produces (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], and this applies only when the material is stronger and tougher than tooth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; for weaker, brittler materials the best that can be achieved is a wall parallel to the rods (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; and the angle must be trimmed so margins are not injured during condensation (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Marzouk names amalgam as the main material that cannot take such bevelled walls (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. This one rule organises the whole answer: **bevel when the material is stronger at a thin edge than enamel (gold) or when bonding needs cut rod ends (composite); butt joint when the material is weak in thin section (amalgam, GIC, ceramic).** [Likely: a synthesis, not stated in this form by any one book]

Enamel walls at 90 degrees to the surface can be considered supported and strong (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], and friable enamel should be planed off with hand or slow rotary instruments as a final step (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** The *cavosurface angle* is the angle between a prepared wall and the external tooth surface; the junction is the *cavosurface margin* (Marzouk et al, undated). Its design ("cavosurface treatment") depends on the restorative material, location and enamel-rod direction (Boushell and Walter, 2019), especially the material's edge strength (Garg and Garg, 2011).

**Governing principle (Noy, quoted by Marzouk et al, undated).** Enamel must rest on sound dentin; the rods forming the margin must have inner ends on dentin; covering their outer ends with restorative material (a bevel) gives the strongest wall, *but only if the material is stronger than tooth*. For weaker, brittle materials, the wall should parallel the rods (a butt joint). Hence: gold is bevelled, composite is bevelled for bonding, amalgam, GIC and ceramics are butt-jointed.

## 2. Amalgam: 90 degree butt joint, no bevel

### Explanation

Because of the low edge strength of amalgam and glass-ceramic, a 90 degree cavosurface angle gives maximal strength (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; bevelling would leave a thin, fracture-prone margin (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. On occlusal surfaces the cusp inclines and convergent walls approximate a 90 degree butt joint even though the enamel margin may exceed 90 degrees (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Marzouk: all cavosurface angles should be right-angled to create a butt joint (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], which lets marginal amalgam withstand stress with least failure (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; following rod direction produces this angle (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Summitt: enamel margins at 90 degrees or greater (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC], since very acute amalgam margins fracture more (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]. Garg and Garg explain both failure modes: acute angle, enamel fractures; obtuse angle, marginal amalgam fractures (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. On root surfaces the angle is 90 degrees for amalgam, composite or ceramic (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

Why not 100 degrees? The question's "90-100 degrees" is the occlusal reality Sturdevant describes (enamel slightly over 90 degrees on inclines, amalgam close to 90) rather than a target; no book read gives 100 degrees as a prescribed figure [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Amalgam: 90° butt joint, no bevel.**
- Amalgam has low edge strength; a bevel leaves a thin, fracture-prone amalgam margin (Boushell and Walter, 2019).
- Acute cavosurface angle: enamel fractures. Obtuse angle (bevel): marginal amalgam fractures, creating a ditch (Garg and Garg, 2011; Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- Right-angled cavosurface gives a butt joint with the least failure (Marzouk et al, undated); occlusally, following the rods on cuspal inclines gives enamel slightly over 90° with amalgam near 90° (Boushell and Walter, 2019).
- Finish: plane off friable enamel with a hand instrument; keep margins away from occlusal contacts (Boushell and Walter, 2019).
- Root-surface margins: 90° (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

## 3. Cast gold and other cast metals: bevels and flares

### Explanation

Bevelling gives the strongest type of enamel margin (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], may allow a better seal in slightly undersized castings (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], and leaves a thin gold margin that can be burnished (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Sturdevant's target is 30-40 degree marginal metal (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC] with 140-150 degree enamel (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC], 40 degrees except 30 degrees gingivally (Sturdevant JR, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's Fan et al place 0.5 mm bevels at about 45 degrees to the surface (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC] but warn that bevels beyond 45 degrees increase the cement-filled opening (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk regards cast restorations as the only ones able to satisfy all of Noy's requirements (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. For less castable alloys (nickel-chromium) Marzouk makes every tie element hollow-ground (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], calling hollow-ground bevels ideal for Class IV and V cast materials (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Root-surface margins of intracoronal cast metal are bevelled (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Full classification and angles by author are in Q09.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Cast gold: bevels and flares (the circumferential tie).**
- Gold is stronger than enamel in thin section and burnishable, so a bevel covering the rod ends gives the strongest enamel margin and a burnishable metal edge (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Marzouk et al, undated).
- Occlusal and gingival bevels; facial and lingual primary and secondary flares; marginal metal 40° (30° gingivally), enamel 140-150° (Sturdevant JR, 2019). Summitt: 0.5 mm bevels at about 45° (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).
- Less castable alloys (Ni-Cr): hollow-ground bevels for bulk (Marzouk et al, undated).
- Critique: bevels beyond 45° may widen the cement line (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).

## 4. Composite resin: bevel or not, by location

### Explanation

**Why bevel.** Bonds to acid-etched rod ends are twice as strong as to rod sides (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]; a 45 degree bevel across a 90 degree angle exposes rod ends (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]; Singh says the same (Singh, 2020) [SRC]. Sturdevant: bevelling increases bonding area and retention (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], is indicated mainly for large restorations with high retention needs (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], and gives an esthetic gradual thickness transition (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; enamel margins may be bevelled or flared beyond 90 degrees (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Bevelling may also limit enamel crazing from polymerisation stress (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

**Occlusal: no bevel.** Occlusal bevels would put tapering resin into stress areas (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]; occlusal walls already expose rod ends (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]; occlusal bevels are not indicated (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and a clinical study found more wear with bevelled occlusal margins (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and avoiding them prevents loss of sound tooth structure (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; thin bevelled composite chips (Singh, 2020) [SRC]. Small modern preparations are flared anyway (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Proximal box of Class II: the books disagree.** Sturdevant: bevels are rarely used on proximal box walls (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC] and not recommended gingivally (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt: 0.5 mm, 45 degree bevels on facial and lingual proximal margins (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], which significantly reduce leakage (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; gingival bevel only if the margin is in enamel well away from the CEJ (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; and an inverse bevel of residual enamel leaks less than a butt margin (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Neither margin on cementum should be bevelled (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Anterior (Class III, IV, V).** Sturdevant: 45 degree bevel, 0.5-2 mm wide (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]; facial bevel for colour transition (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]; lingual bevel of a maxillary incisor may be contraindicated at an occlusal contact (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]; Class V cervical margins not bevelled (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt (Vargas et al) for Class IV: facial bevel 60 degrees and 2-3 mm (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC], lingual 45 degrees and about 1 mm (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]; bevel width beyond 1 mm adds no strength (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]. Vargas et al are sceptical for Class III: bevels have been advocated (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC], but enamel bonds are clinically adequate without bevels (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC] and an in vitro tracer study showed no microleakage difference (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Marzouk's older resin designs** (pre-modern adhesives): long bevel of the whole enamel wall at 45-70 degrees (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; both partial and long bevels lack definite termination after etching (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and leave weak acute flash (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], so are avoided in uncleansable, stress or inaccessible areas (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; the hollow-ground bevel gives a right-angled butt joint while still etching rod ends (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], indicated for inaccessible areas such as gingival walls (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Composite: bevel by location.**
- Rationale: bonds to etched rod *ends* are about twice those to rod sides; a 45° bevel exposes the ends (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013); it adds bonding area and esthetic blending (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

| Site | Recommendation | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Occlusal (Class I/II) | No bevel: rods already end-on; thin resin chips; more wear clinically | Ahmed et al, 2019; Hilton and Broome, 2013 |
| Proximal facial/lingual | Sturdevant: rarely. Summitt: 0.5 mm at 45°, less leakage | Ahmed et al, 2019 vs Hilton and Broome, 2013 |
| Gingival | Bevel only on thick enamel away from CEJ; never on cementum | Hilton and Broome, 2013; Overton et al, 2013 |
| Class III/IV facial | 45°, 0.5-2 mm (Sturdevant); 60°, 2-3 mm "infinite" bevel (Summitt) | Ahmed et al, 2019; Vargas et al, 2013 |
| Class IV lingual | 45°, ~1 mm; avoid at occlusal contact | Vargas et al, 2013; Ahmed et al, 2019 |
| Class V cervical | No bevel (no enamel) | Ahmed et al, 2019 |

- Critique: enamel bonds are clinically adequate without bevels and Class III microleakage was the same with or without bevels in vitro (Vargas et al, 2013). Marzouk et al (undated) warn that flat bevels leave weak flash; a hollow-ground bevel gives a butt joint plus etched rod ends at gingival walls.

## 5. Glass ionomer cement: butt joint, no bevel

### Explanation

Cavosurface bevels are contraindicated for amalgam or glass ionomer because of low edge strength (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; glass ionomer is brittle and needs bulk (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], and a 90 degree butt joint about 1 mm deep is a reasonable minimum (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. RMGI Class 3 preparations resemble composite ones but bevels are not prepared (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant: preparations for glass ionomer are as for composite except bevels are rarely used (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]. Because GIC bonds to enamel and dentin, retention grooves are not needed (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC], and the Class 5 preparation is extended only to remove caries and fragile enamel (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Glass ionomer (also answers the 5-mark question).**
- Margin: 90° butt joint; no bevel (Overton et al, 2013; Ahmed et al, 2019).
- Reasons: GIC (and RMGI) is brittle with low edge strength, so a thin edge fractures; it needs bulk, about 1 mm at the margin (Overton et al, 2013).
- It bonds to enamel and dentin, so retention grooves are unnecessary (Vargas et al, 2013); extend only to remove caries and fragile enamel (Overton et al, 2013).

## 6. Ceramics: butt joint, shoulder or rounded margins

### Explanation

For indirect ceramic and composite inlays there is no benefit to occlusal or gingival bevels (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC] because thin margins chip (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; CAD/CAM margins should be well defined 90 degree butt joints (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; internal angles are rounded (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. A newer option bevels the facial margin at 45 degrees with a football diamond (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], supported by one in vitro study (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]. Crowns: a rounded axiogingival angle with a butt-joint margin (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; a shoulder for bulk (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk allows cast-ceramic gingival floors terminating in a 90 degree cavosurface margin (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], with no definite internal line angles in grooves (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Glass-ceramic shares amalgam's low edge strength (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Ceramics.**
- Inlays/onlays: 90° butt joint, no bevels or knife-edges; thin ceramic margins chip (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013); internal angles rounded.
- Crowns: shoulder (or rounded shoulder) with a butt-joint margin for bulk (Rouse, 2013; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).
- Recent advance: a 45° facial margin modification for esthetic blending, so far supported only in vitro (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013).

## 7. Critical analysis

### Explanation

The books agree on the materials at the extremes (gold bevelled; amalgam, GIC and ceramic butt-jointed). They disagree where adhesion changed the rules: the composite proximal box (Sturdevant against Summitt) and the value of bevels at all in anterior composites. Much of the bevel literature quoted is in vitro (microleakage tracers, bond strengths), while the one clinical point quoted, greater wear with occlusal bevels, argues against bevelling [Likely]. For cast gold, the classical fit argument is itself challenged (Q09). "Recent advances" (absorbed 25m) is therefore mainly a move away from routine bevels towards minimal, flared preparations for composite and butt joints for ceramics, with low-maturity evidence for the newer modified ceramic margin.

#### ✎ Exam core

| Material | Margin | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Amalgam | 90° butt | Low edge strength |
| Cast gold | Bevels and flares (40°/30° metal) | Strong, burnishable thin edge |
| Ni-Cr, cast ceramic | Hollow-ground or 90° | Poor castability, brittleness |
| Composite | Bevel anterior and accessible enamel; no occlusal bevel | Rod-end bonding, esthetics; occlusal wear |
| GIC/RMGI | 90° butt, ~1 mm | Brittle, needs bulk |
| Ceramic | 90° butt / shoulder | Chipping |

**Appraisal.** (1) Agreement is strong only at the extremes. (2) The proximal composite box is disputed: Sturdevant rarely bevels, Summitt bevels 0.5 mm at 45°. (3) Most bevel evidence is laboratory; clinically, occlusal bevels increased wear and enamel bonds succeed without bevels. (4) Bevelling sound enamel enlarges the restoration, against minimal intervention (avoiding occlusal bevels prevents loss of sound tooth structure: Hilton and Broome, 2013). (5) Modified ceramic margins are recent and in vitro only. Choose the margin by the material's edge strength, the enamel rod direction and the occlusal contact position.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: enamel margins of composite restorations should be bevelled to improve seal and retention.** Textbook: Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson (2013), Boushell and Walter (2019) and Singh (2020) support bevels because rod-end etching doubles bond strength and adds bonding area; Hilton and Broome (2013) report less leakage with proximal bevels. Other evidence: Vargas et al (2013) report clinically adequate enamel bonding without bevels and no microleakage difference in vitro for Class III; Hilton and Broome (2013) cite a clinical study showing greater wear with bevelled occlusal margins; Ahmed et al (2019) rarely bevel the proximal box. Agreement or challenge: support for bevels is mainly laboratory; clinical data argue against occlusal bevels. Strength: moderate for "no occlusal bevel", weak for routine proximal bevels. Conclusion: bevel where esthetics (anterior facial) or retention (large Class IV) needs it, not routinely. [Likely] Clinical implication: in posterior composites, keep occlusal margins butt and away from contacts; bevel accessible proximal enamel only if it is thick and away from the CEJ.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Rule: bevel if the material is stronger than enamel at a thin edge (gold) or bonds better to rod ends (composite); otherwise butt joint.
2. Amalgam: 90° butt; acute angle breaks enamel, obtuse breaks amalgam.
3. Gold: 40° metal, 140° enamel, 30° metal gingivally.
4. Composite: no occlusal bevel; anterior 45° (Sturdevant) or 60° facial 2-3 mm (Summitt).
5. GIC: 90° butt, about 1 mm bulk, no bevel.
6. Ceramics: 90° butt or shoulder, rounded internal angles, no bevel.
7. Never bevel a margin on cementum.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Bevelling amalgam or GIC margins "to improve seal".
2. Recommending occlusal bevels for posterior composites.
3. Writing one bevel angle for composite without naming the author (45° Sturdevant; 60° facial Summitt).
4. Forgetting that root-surface (cementum) margins are never bevelled for direct materials.
5. Giving the GIC 5-mark answer without the reason (brittleness, need for bulk).

## MNEMONIC

"**Gold Bends, Glass Breaks**": burnishable gold takes a bevel; brittle materials (amalgam, glass ionomer, glass-ceramic) need a butt joint.
