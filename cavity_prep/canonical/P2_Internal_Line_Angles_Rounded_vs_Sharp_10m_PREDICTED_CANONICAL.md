# Internal line angles: rounded versus sharp, and stress concentration

**Marks:** 10 · Short essay · Predicted question

**Answer blueprint:** Definition → biomechanical rationale → material-specific design → clinical application → conclusion.

## Introduction

An **internal line angle** is the junction of two prepared walls whose apex points into the tooth (Boushell and Walter, 2019). In cavity preparation, the angle should generally be smooth and rounded rather than abrupt, while remaining sufficiently definite to preserve the intended preparation form.

## Biomechanical rationale: stress concentration

A sharp re-entrant corner concentrates tensile stress at its apex. The smaller the radius of curvature, the greater the local stress concentration; this can initiate cracks in tooth structure or brittle restorative materials. Rounding the junction distributes stresses over a broader area, reduces sharp stress-raising transitions and improves adaptation of restorative material. Slightly rounded internal angles therefore improve tooth resistance to fracture, while rounding the axiopulpal angle improves the fracture resistance of the restoration (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Boushell and Donovan, 2019).

The **axiopulpal line angle** is particularly important in a two-surface preparation. It is commonly rounded or lightly bevelled using a gingival margin trimmer or an appropriate rotary instrument to reduce stress concentration in the restoration (Boushell and Donovan, 2019; Sturdevant, 2019).

## Design according to restorative material

The required form depends on the material's mechanical behaviour and retention requirements.

| Restorative material | Recommended internal line-angle form |
|---|---|
| Amalgam | Rounded but definite; avoid abrupt sharpness (Marzouk et al., n.d.) |
| Direct composite | Avoid sharp internal angles; maintain suitable cavosurface geometry (Garg and Garg, 2011; Ahmed et al., 2019) |
| Cast ceramic / ceramic inlay | Rounded to reduce stress concentration and improve material adaptation (Fasbinder et al., 2013; Marzouk et al., n.d.) |
| Ni-Cr and other brittle cast materials | More rounded internal angles; reduced taper may be used to compensate for reduced mechanical retention (Marzouk et al., n.d.) |
| Cast gold inlay | Definite internal angles may be required for form and retention, but the axiopulpal junction is rounded or bevelled (Fan et al., 2013; Sturdevant, 2019; Singh, 2020) |
| Direct gold | Selected internal angles may be sharpened with hatchets or angle formers to develop retentive features (Boushell and Donovan, 2019) |

Thus, **rounded does not mean indistinct**. The preparation must retain its intended geometry. A material-specific exception is direct gold, where selected sharp angles may be deliberately created for retention. For gold inlays, authors differ in how sharply the internal angles should be defined: Fan et al. (2013) and Singh (2020) emphasise well-defined angles, whereas Sturdevant (2019) describes a round-cornered bur and a bevelled axiopulpal angle. The axiopulpal junction should not be left as a sharp stress-raising corner.

## Clinical application

During preparation, use an appropriate bur design to create smooth internal transitions; inspect and refine the axiopulpal angle where indicated. At enamel junctions, avoid unsupported enamel rods and abrupt transitions. The final geometry should balance stress reduction, restorative material adaptation, resistance form and retention form.

## Conclusion

Internal line angles should generally be **smooth, rounded and definite**. Rounding reduces stress concentration and fracture risk, particularly for brittle restorative materials; sharper angles are reserved for specific retention requirements, such as selected direct-gold preparations.
