## 1. From Black's design to lesion-dictated design

### Explanation

Black's rule was that the outline for smooth-surface caries be extended to areas that are normally self-cleansing to prevent recurrence around the periphery; the practice of extension for prevention has virtually been eliminated because of the relative caries immunity provided by preventive measures such as fluoride, and its occlusal part has been replaced by enameloplasty, pit-and-fissure sealant and preventive resin or conservative composite restoration (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Marzouk's rationale was that every restoration is surrounded by a leakage space, so the margin should be placed where it is easily cleansable, which he called "extension for prevention, or cutting for immunity" (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Torres and Mehta record the history of isthmus width: a width of one-third of the intercusp distance reduced fracture resistance by about 33%, the standard was reduced to one-quarter, and nowadays extension for prevention is no longer recommended and no stereotyped outlines are followed (Torres and Mehta, 2020) [SRC]. Overton, Summitt and Osborne add that as an amalgam restoration becomes wider faciolingually the tooth is more subject to fracture, and that restorations confined to the marginal ridge (slot restorations) may minimise the severity of fracture compared with those extended through occlusal grooves; existing extended outlines still need replacing, although that outline form "should not be advocated" (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]. Where an occlusal extension is used for resistance, it must be at least one-fourth of the intercuspal distance (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC].

Mount and Ngo note that the Black Class II-III-IV distinction has been made redundant by advances in restorative materials (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]. Prewedging promotes a more conservative preparation and protects adjacent teeth, and a damaged adjacent surface is significantly more likely to need restoration; non-carious fissures may be sealed (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].

The drivers of change are therefore three: caries prevention and sealants (biological), the fracture data on wide isthmuses (mechanical), and adhesive materials (Sections 5 and 6).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** The Class II cavity of G.V. Black was a standard outline: an occlusal dovetail through all fissures plus a proximal box extended to self-cleansing areas ("extension for prevention"). Marzouk described the logic: every restoration has a leakage gap, so margins were placed where they are easily cleansable, "cutting for immunity" (Marzouk et al, undated). Recent advances replace this standard outline with a design dictated by the lesion and the material.

**Why the design changed**
1. **Prevention:** extension for prevention has "virtually been eliminated" because fluoride and oral hygiene give relative caries immunity; fissures are treated by sealants, enameloplasty or preventive resin restorations instead of cutting (Ritter et al, 2019).
2. **Mechanics:** an isthmus of one-third the intercuspal distance cut fracture resistance by about 33%; the width was reduced to one-quarter, and today no stereotyped outline is followed (Torres and Mehta, 2020). Wider amalgam restorations make the tooth more fracture-prone, and slot restorations confined to the marginal ridge may reduce fracture severity (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
3. **Adhesion:** bonded materials need no undercuts, so the outline can follow the lesion only (Section 5).
4. **Classification:** Mount's Site and Size system describes lesions by site (Site 2 = approximal) and size; the Black Class II-III-IV distinction has been made redundant by better restorative materials (Mount and Ngo, 2016).

**Principles of the modern Class II design**
- Outline dictated by the lesion, not by a standard shape; no stereotyped outline (Torres and Mehta, 2020).
- Preserve the marginal ridge and the occlusal enamel wherever possible.
- Seal sound or stained fissures instead of cutting them (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- Protect the adjacent tooth (prewedging, matrix strip, enamel shell); a damaged adjacent surface is more likely to need restoration later (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- Rounded internal angles; depth only as deep as the lesion.
- Retention by grooves for amalgam or by adhesion for composite and glass ionomer.

## 2. Conservative designs for amalgam

### Explanation

Marzouk lists Class II Design 1 (Conventional Design), indicated among others for a patient with high caries and plaque indices, and Class II Design 2 (Modern Design), indicated for a moderate to small proximal lesion not exceeding the area of near approach in patients with good oral hygiene and low plaque and caries indices; in Design 2 the occlusal lesion does not exceed one-fourth the intercuspal distance, there is little if any dovetail, buccal and lingual dentinal retention grooves are present in every case and all line angles are rounded, especially the axiopulpal (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. So Marzouk still ties outline to caries risk, while the modern books tie it to the lesion only.

Sturdevant's: a box-only preparation is indicated if no occlusal caries lesion is present; it is limited to a proximal surface with a narrow proximal contact and a marginal ridge with no occlusal contact; facial and lingual walls converge occlusally; retention grooves are mandatory, 0.5 mm deep at the gingival point angle and extended occlusally to be visible in the occlusal outline (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's calls a restoration with an occlusal outline limited to the marginal ridge a slot restoration; the slot is cut through the marginal ridge until the bur "falls" into soft carious dentin; a shell of enamel is left to protect the adjacent tooth (adjacent surfaces were damaged 69% of the time in one study); mesiodistal dimension about 1.5 mm or more; retentive undercuts at least 0.5 mm deep, opposing each other, preferably long grooves from gingival floor to occlusal surface, because they are the only feature that prevents proximal dislodgement; a well-designed slot can last indefinitely (one in service more than 58 years); fissures that touch the outline should be sealed (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]. Torres and Mehta call it the proximal box or vertical slot preparation; occlusal grooves may be sealed but "should never be grinded"; if 0.5 mm or less separates an occlusal and a proximal cavity they are joined; an in-vitro study found a vertical slot with buccal and lingual locks resisted 18.09 kg proximal displacement against 7.12 kg for an occlusal box with dovetail (Torres and Mehta, 2020) [SRC; laboratory evidence]. Garg and Garg give proximal locks of 0.5 mm gingivally and 0.3 mm occlusally (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Further details: proximal walls meet the surface at 90 degrees; the gingival floor is set by the gingival extent of the lesion; a margin may be left in contact if all undermined enamel is gone; superficial demineralisation (less than half-way through enamel) may be left and treated with fluoride or a resin infiltrant such as Icon (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]; facial and lingual walls should almost oppose each other (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. In-vitro work suggests adhesive systems may enhance resistance and retention of amalgam (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Disagreement:** Sturdevant's limits box-only to a marginal ridge with no occlusal contact (Ritter et al, 2019), whereas Summitt's recommends the slot for any initial proximal lesion without occlusal caries (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013). Groove depth: Sturdevant's 0.5 mm at the gingival point angle; Summitt's at least 0.5 mm along the full length; Garg and Garg 0.5 mm gingivally tapering to 0.3 mm occlusally.

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Conventional versus modern amalgam design (Marzouk et al, undated)**
| | Design 1 (Conventional) | Design 2 (Modern) |
|---|---|---|
| Lesion | Moderate-large, occlusal involved | Small-moderate, within area of near approach |
| Patient | High caries and plaque indices | Good hygiene, low caries |
| Features | Black outline | Occlusal width not over 1/4 intercuspal, little dovetail, rounded line angles, buccal and lingual grooves always |

**B. Proximal slot (box-only) amalgam**
- Indication: proximal lesion with no occlusal caries (Ritter et al, 2019); Sturdevant's adds a narrow contact and a marginal ridge free of occlusal contact.
- Technique: cut through the marginal ridge until the bur "falls" into carious dentin; leave a shell of enamel to protect the adjacent tooth (damaged 69% of the time otherwise) (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- Form: facial and lingual walls converge occlusally; mesiodistal width about 1.5 mm; long, opposing retention grooves at least 0.5 mm deep, the only feature resisting proximal displacement (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013); Garg and Garg give 0.5 mm gingivally, 0.3 mm occlusally.
- Walls meet the proximal surface at 90°; facial and lingual walls should almost oppose each other (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
- Gingival floor placed by the gingival extent of the lesion, not to a fixed level; a margin left in contact with the adjacent tooth may be accepted once all undermined enamel is removed (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- Superficial demineralisation (less than half-way through enamel) in a patient lowering risk may be left and treated with fluoride or a resin infiltrant such as Icon (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- Fissures are sealed, never cut; join the two cavities only if 0.5 mm or less separates them (Torres and Mehta, 2020).
- Evidence: in vitro, slot with locks resisted 18.09 kg versus 7.12 kg for occlusal dovetail (Torres and Mehta, 2020); clinically a slot amalgam has lasted more than 58 years (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).

## 3. Slot preparations from the facial or lingual (horizontal slot, keyhole)

### Explanation

For proximal root caries appreciably gingival to the contact, Sturdevant's approaches from the facial and gives the preparation the form of a slot (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's calls it a keyhole or facial or lingual slot preparation (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk's Class II Design 5 involves part of the proximal surface with a very limited facial or lingual access area, because an occlusal access to such an apically located lesion would be unrealistic (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Torres and Mehta: the horizontal slot needs at least 2 mm of intact enamel below the marginal ridge, was developed by Roggenkamp et al, saves time, preserves tooth structure, gives better aesthetics and suits rotated teeth (Torres and Mehta, 2020) [SRC]; Technique: adjacent tooth protected by a metal matrix band and wedge; occlusal and gingival walls parallel and perpendicular to the axial wall; retention because the cavity is larger buccolingually than occlusogingivally, plus grooves on the gingivoaxial and occlusoaxial line angles (Torres and Mehta, 2020) [SRC]. Sturdevant's gives axial depth 0.75-1 mm gingivally and 1-1.25 mm occlusally, the lingual wall facing facially, and amalgam when isolation is difficult (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Harpreet Singh gives the same 2 mm requirement for the composite slot (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**C. Facial or lingual (horizontal) slot / keyhole**
- Indication: proximal or root-surface lesion below the contact, gingival recession, rotated teeth, with at least 2 mm of intact enamel below the marginal ridge (Torres and Mehta, 2020; Singh and Rajkumar, 2020). Equivalent to Marzouk's Class II Design 5.
- Design: entry from the accessible embrasure; occlusal and gingival walls at 90°, retention grooves in the occlusoaxial and gingivoaxial line angles, none in the axial wall.
- Technique: protect the adjacent tooth with a metal matrix band and wedge; enter with a small round bur below the contact; occlusal and gingival walls parallel to each other and perpendicular to the axial wall (Torres and Mehta, 2020).
- Depth: 0.75-1 mm at the gingival aspect, 1-1.25 mm at the occlusal wall; with a facial approach the lingual wall faces facially to aid condensation (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Retention: facially, the cavity is larger buccolingually than occlusogingivally; proximally, grooves along the occlusoaxial and gingivoaxial line angles (Torres and Mehta, 2020).
- Material: amalgam is particularly indicated when isolation is difficult (Ritter et al, 2019); composite or glass ionomer otherwise.
- Advantages: marginal ridge and contact preserved, less time, better aesthetics, useful in rotated teeth (Torres and Mehta, 2020).

## 4. The tunnel preparation

### Explanation

**Definition and history.** The tunnel preparation joins an occlusal access with the proximal lesion by a tunnel under the marginal ridge, aiming to remove the caries and leave the marginal ridge essentially intact (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Garg and Garg describe it as a tunnel from the occlusal surface ending on the proximal lesion without cutting the marginal ridge (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. It was first suggested by Jinks in 1963 for primary molars, and Hunt and Knight later modified it for small proximal lesions; access is made in the occlusal fossa adjacent to the marginal ridge (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].

**Types.** Mount and Ngo describe the internal occlusal fossa approach; if the proximal enamel is only demineralised it is left intact to remineralise, but if it is cavitated the operator may need "to tunnel through to the exterior" (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]. The names "internal (partial) tunnel" for intact proximal enamel and "external (total) tunnel" for a perforated proximal surface are [RECALL]; the distinction itself is in Mount and Ngo.

**Technique (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].** Indicated when the enamel lesion is at least 3.00 mm apical to the crest of the marginal ridge; small access; bur leaned facially and lingually to create a funnel-shaped access, approximately triangular in outline; removal of all affected dentine on the axial wall is unnecessary; glass-ionomer is the material of choice, with the option of lamination with composite resin.

**Claimed advantages (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].** Less potential for overhang; a two-surface Class 2 preparation reduces tooth stiffness by 46% versus 20% for an occlusal preparation; smaller perimeter, less leakage; can be converted into a conventional Class 2 if the lesion is larger.

**Restoration.** Glass ionomer against a mylar strip; cut back about 2 mm for composite lamination after the cement has matured, which takes up to a week (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].

**Problems.** The bur passes near the pulp, often within 1 mm; visibility is reduced and caries removal uncertain; in-vitro studies show a high rate of residual caries, so a caries-detecting solution should be used; marginal ridge strength falls as the tunnel widens (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's lists the same three drawbacks: walls compromised by lack of access and visibility, unpredictable excavation, questionable marginal ridge strength (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Pickard's: successful only if the lesion is sufficiently small, and there is a good chance of marginal ridge fracture (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC].

**Clinical outcomes.** Tunnel restorations fail at a considerably higher rate; 34-41% show residual caries or enamel caries progression; main failures are marginal ridge fracture and secondary caries; 21% failed in 7 years against no failures for slot amalgams; 46% survival against 76% for saucer-shaped Class 2 composites; Summitt's Table 11-1 gives annual failure rates of 6.7 (Pilebro), 10.0 (Strand), 3.0 (Lumley and Fisher), 7.1 (Hasselrot) and 13.4 (Hörsted-Bindslev) (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]; conclusion: "tunnel restorations are not indicated and should be avoided", slot preparations being more conservative and more predictable (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: trials with glass ionomer found it inadequate for definitive restorations; whether bonded composite does better is unknown; the technique "is not supported in this textbook" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Disagreement.** Mount and Ngo present the tunnel as "the simplest and most conservative approach" for a Site 2 Size 1 lesion 3 mm or more below the ridge, restored with glass ionomer (Mount and Ngo, 2016); Hilton and Broome (2013) and Ritter et al (2019) reject it on clinical failure data. This answer shows both and does not pick one; the clinical data cited are from Summitt's table, which Mount's chapter does not rebut.

#### ✎ Exam core

**D. Tunnel preparation**
- **Definition:** an occlusal access joined to the proximal lesion by a tunnel under the marginal ridge, which is left intact (Ritter et al, 2019; Garg and Garg, 2011). First suggested by Jinks (1963); modified by Hunt and Knight for small proximal lesions (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- **Types:** internal (proximal enamel intact, left to remineralise) and external (cavitated proximal enamel opened to the exterior) (Mount and Ngo, 2016).
- **Indications (Mount and Ngo, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2011):** small Site 2 Size 1 lesion; enamel lesion at least 3 mm below the marginal ridge crest; sound, uncracked marginal ridge; low occlusal load; glass-ionomer restoration (laminated with composite if needed); good access and magnification.
- **Contraindications:** lesion within 3 mm of the ridge (use a slot) (Mount and Ngo, 2016); cracked or weak ridge; large lesion; heavy occlusal load; poor visibility.
- **Technique:** small access just inside the marginal ridge, funnel-shaped, triangular outline; caries-detecting dye; leave affected axial dentine (Mount and Ngo, 2016; Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- **Advantages:** marginal ridge and contact preserved; no overhang; Class 2 cavity reduces stiffness 46% versus 20% for occlusal access; can be converted to a box (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- **Disadvantages:** passes within 1 mm of the pulp; poor visibility; residual caries; marginal ridge weakens as the tunnel widens (Hilton and Broome, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Restoration:** high-strength glass ionomer syringed in two increments against a short mylar strip and wedge; if load or aesthetics demand, cut back about 2 mm after the cement matures (up to a week) and laminate with composite (Mount and Ngo, 2016).
- **Annual failure rates (Hilton and Broome, 2013, Table 11-1):** Pilebro 6.7 (3-year study), Strand 10.0 (3), Lumley and Fisher 3.0 (7), Hasselrot 7.1 (7), Hörsted-Bindslev 13.4 (4); the table gives no unit, read as per cent per year [Likely].
- **Clinical outcome:** higher failure; 34-41% residual or progressing caries; 21% failure in 7 years versus 0% for slots; 46% versus 76% survival against saucer composites; failures by ridge fracture and secondary caries (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- **Verdict:** Mount and Ngo (2016) recommend it for selected lesions; Hilton and Broome (2013) say it "should be avoided"; Ritter et al (2019) do not support it.

## 5. Adhesive designs for composite

### Explanation

Hilton and Broome: posterior composite preparations were once patterned on Black's amalgam design; the current design limits removal to carious tooth structure and fragile enamel; it is shallower (no need to enter dentin if the lesion does not), narrower (less bulk, less polymerisation-shrinkage effect), has rounded internal angles (decreases stress concentration), and has no extension for prevention; extending through fissures does not make the restoration more fracture resistant than a proximal slot; slot composites showed 70% success up to 10 years, attributed to an occlusal stop on enamel; no outline has a "standard" shape; bonded restorations cannot be relied on for long-term reinforcement (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: mechanical retention is not needed; extension is dictated by the defect; three small designs: conventional with occlusal access (often for primary lesions), box-only (only the proximal surface defective; instrument held parallel to the long axis of the crown; "No bevelling or secondary retention is indicated"), and facial or lingual slot (marginal ridge not undermined); larger lesions resemble a traditional amalgam design (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Harpreet Singh gives a box-only axial depth 0.2 mm inside the DEJ, without bevel or secondary retention (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020) [SRC]. Marzouk: isthmus may be one-fifth of the intercuspal distance, line angles extremely rounded, dentinal portions mortise-shaped, saucer-shaped Class II when the lesion is confined to enamel, contacts kept in tooth structure where possible (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

**Bevels.** Hilton and Broome call bevel placement a point of controversy; if the proximal facial and lingual margins meet the surface at a right angle, a 0.5 mm bevel at about 45 degrees is placed, which significantly reduces leakage; gingival bevel only if the margin is in enamel well away from the CEJ; occlusal bevels show no benefit (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. **Disagreement:** Ritter et al (2019) say no bevelling for the box-only composite; Hilton and Broome (2013) bevel right-angled proximal facial and lingual margins. Marzouk et al (undated) keeps mortise dentin and grooves, which Ritter et al (2019) say are unnecessary.

#### ✎ Exam core

**E. Adhesive (composite) Class II designs**
Features (Hilton and Broome, 2013): removal limited to caries and fragile enamel; shallower (no dentin entry if the lesion stays in enamel); narrower (less shrinkage stress, less wear); rounded internal angles; no extension for prevention; no standard shape. Isthmus may be as small as one-fifth of the intercuspal distance (Marzouk et al, undated). Mechanical retention is not needed (Ritter et al, 2019).

Designs for small lesions (Ritter et al, 2019):
1. Conventional with occlusal access: primary lesions reaching the occlusal surface.
2. Box-only: proximal surface only; pear-shaped bur parallel to the long axis through the marginal ridge; no bevel, no secondary retention; axial depth 0.2 mm inside DEJ (Singh and Rajkumar, 2020).
3. Facial or lingual slot: marginal ridge not undermined.
4. Saucer-shaped: lesion confined to enamel with minimal dentin involvement (Marzouk et al, undated).
Large lesions return to a traditional box-and-step form (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Bevels (controversial):** 0.5 mm, 45° on right-angled proximal facial and lingual margins reduces leakage; gingival bevel only on enamel well away from the CEJ; no occlusal bevel (Hilton and Broome, 2013). Ritter et al (2019) place no bevel on the box-only.

**Preparation sequence (Hilton and Broome, 2013):** prewedge (separation gives a tighter contact and protects the adjacent tooth); enter just inside the marginal ridge with a small round bur, extend with a no. 329 bur leaving the proximal enamel intact, then fracture the thinned enamel with a spoon excavator; seal the non-carious fissures. Sonic tips may be used and do not impair adhesion.

**Evidence:** proximal slot composites 70% success at up to 10 years, with an occlusal stop on enamel (Hilton and Broome, 2013). Bonding does not reliably reinforce the tooth long term (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

## 6. Designs for glass ionomer and minimal access designs

### Explanation

Mount and Ngo classify the Site 2 Size 1 lesion by three approaches: internal occlusal fossa (tunnel), slot cavity, and proximal approach; the slot is used if the lesion is closer than 3.00 mm to the marginal ridge crest; elsewhere in the same chapter the slot is used when less than 2.0 mm of ridge remains; the slot is a small box limited to the demineralised enamel, often keeping contact on the facial or lingual margin; no retention design is needed with glass ionomer (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]. **Internal inconsistency:** the same chapter gives 3.00 mm (tunnel threshold) and 2.0 mm (slot threshold); the gap of 2-3 mm is not covered. Sturdevant's uses the amalgam slot design for glass ionomer (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Direct proximal access is possible if the adjacent tooth is missing (Banerjee and Watson, 2011; Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Sonic tips do not adversely affect adhesion; the composite slot is begun just inside the marginal ridge and extended with a no. 329 bur leaving the proximal surface intact until the thinned enamel is removed (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**F. Glass-ionomer and minimal designs (Mount and Ngo, 2016)**
For a Site 2 Size 1 lesion:
1. **Internal occlusal fossa (tunnel):** lesion at least 3 mm below the ridge.
2. **Slot cavity:** lesion closer to the ridge (less than 2-3 mm) or ridge cracked; small box limited to cavitated enamel; contact kept where possible; no retention form.
3. **Proximal approach:** through the open box of an adjacent prepared tooth; also direct access when the adjacent tooth is missing (Banerjee and Watson, 2011; Garg and Garg, 2011).
Glass ionomer releases fluoride so demineralised margins are left to remineralise; it may be laminated with composite. The same slot design as for amalgam is used for glass ionomer (Ritter et al, 2019). Sonic preparation tips do not impair adhesion (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

## 7. Comparison and conclusion

#### ✎ Exam core

| Design | Indication | Retention | Evidence maturity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional (Black) | Large lesions, high risk, replacement | Dovetail, box | Long clinical use; now not advocated as routine |
| Modern / slot amalgam | Proximal lesion, no occlusal caries | Opposing grooves | Clinical: decades of service; in-vitro retention data |
| Facial or lingual slot | Lesion below contact, recession | Line-angle grooves | Technique reports; little comparative data |
| Tunnel | Small lesion 3 mm or more below ridge | Adhesion (GIC) | Clinical: high failure; rejected by Summitt's and Sturdevant's |
| Composite box-only / saucer | Small proximal lesion | Adhesion | Clinical: 70% at 10 years for slot composites |
| GIC slot | Low load, high caries risk | Adhesion | Mostly expert recommendation |

**Conclusion.** The advance is from a standard outline to a lesion-dictated, material-dictated design: preserve the marginal ridge and occlusal enamel, seal fissures instead of cutting them, and rely on adhesion. The evidence base is mature for slot amalgam and slot composite, weak for the horizontal slot and glass-ionomer slot, and unfavourable for the tunnel restoration.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1. Extension through sound fissures is unnecessary for a proximal lesion.**
- Textbook says: no extension for prevention; slot is no less fracture resistant (Hilton and Broome, 2013); occlusal grooves never cut (Torres and Mehta, 2020).
- Other evidence: slot amalgam with long grooves can last decades (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013); in-vitro locks out-performed dovetail (Torres and Mehta, 2020).
- Agreement: all modern books agree; Marzouk et al (undated) still extends for high-risk patients.
- Strength: moderate; clinical series plus laboratory data. [Certain] for the direction.
- Clinical implication: slot plus fissure sealant as the default for an isolated proximal lesion.

**Claim 2. The tunnel preparation is a conservative advance.**
- Textbook says: Mount and Ngo (2016) recommend it for lesions 3 mm or more below the ridge.
- Other evidence: five clinical studies in Summitt's table show high annual failure; 46% versus 76% survival; marginal ridge fracture (Hilton and Broome, 2013).
- Challenge: the clinical data contradict the claimed benefit; Sturdevant's does not support it.
- Strength: clinical cohort data, small numbers. [Likely] that tunnels underperform slots.
- Clinical implication: prefer a slot; if a tunnel is attempted, use caries-detecting dye and magnification, and warn of ridge fracture.

**Claim 3. Bevels improve Class II composite margins.**
- Textbook says: 0.5 mm bevel on right-angled proximal margins reduces leakage (Hilton and Broome, 2013); no bevel on box-only (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Other evidence: leakage data are mostly in vitro (Hilton and Broome, 2013 cite laboratory studies). [Likely]
- Conclusion: bevel only where enamel is thick and the margin is right-angled; never occlusally.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Extension for prevention "virtually eliminated" (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Isthmus: 1/3 then 1/4 intercuspal (amalgam); as small as 1/5 for composite.
- Slot amalgam: opposing grooves at least 0.5 mm deep, the only retention against proximal displacement.
- Horizontal slot: at least 2 mm intact enamel below the ridge (Roggenkamp).
- Tunnel: lesion 3 mm or more below ridge (Mount); passes within 1 mm of pulp; 21% failure versus 0% slot at 7 years.
- Class 2 cavity cuts stiffness 46%; occlusal access 20%.
- Composite box-only: no bevel, no secondary retention (Sturdevant's).
