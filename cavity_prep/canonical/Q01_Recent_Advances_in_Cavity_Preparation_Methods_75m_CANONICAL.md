## 1. Introduction and classification of cavity preparation methods

### Explanation

Pickard's Guide gives the most useful frame for the whole essay: Table 5.5 sorts "tooth-cutting/caries removal technologies, the substrates acted on, and the mechanism of action" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. The mechanisms are mechanical rotary (burs), mechanical non-rotary ("hand instruments (excavators, chisels), air abrasion (alumina/bioactive glass), air polishing", ultrasonics, sono-abrasion), chemo-mechanical (Carisolv gel, amino acid based; Papacarie gel, papain based; experimental pepsin-based gels), photo-ablation (lasers) and "others" acting on bacteria ("photoactive disinfection (PAD), ozone") (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Caridex is listed as "of historical value, not available now for clinical use" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. The value of the table is the substrate column: only rotary, air abrasion, ultrasonics and lasers act on sound enamel and dentine, while chemo-mechanical agents act on carious dentine only and ozone/PAD act on bacteria. This explains why no single method can replace the bur.

Mount, Walsh and Brostek frame the advances the same way: "recent advances with both air abrasion and laser techniques have added additional instruments to the armamentarium", and "chemo-mechanical techniques for the removal of carious dentine are becoming more sophisticated" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Their stated benefit is that air abrasion, pulsed erbium lasers and chemo-mechanical caries removal let the clinician "work more conservatively", in several quadrants in one visit and with less need for local anaesthesia (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Pickard's ends its review with a caution that sets the tone for an appraisal: "at present no perfect instrument exists" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Cavity preparation has moved from hand instruments, through high-speed rotary cutting, to a group of alternative and adjunctive methods driven by adhesive dentistry and minimal intervention: air abrasion, sono-abrasion and ultrasonics, erbium lasers, chemo-mechanical caries removal and antibacterial methods (ozone, photo-activated disinfection). Mount, Walsh and Brostek (2016) state that air abrasion, pulsed erbium lasers and chemo-mechanical caries removal allow the clinician to work more conservatively, in different quadrants at one appointment, with less need for local anaesthesia. Banerjee and Watson (2015) caution that at present no perfect instrument exists.

**Classification by mechanism (Banerjee and Watson, 2015, Table 5.5)**

| Mechanism | Substrate | Technology |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical, rotary | Sound or carious enamel and dentine | Steel, carbide, diamond, ceramic and plastic burs (plastic acts on carious dentine only) |
| Mechanical, non-rotary | Sound or carious enamel and dentine | Hand instruments; air abrasion (alumina/bioactive glass); air polishing; ultrasonics; sono-abrasion |
| Chemo-mechanical | Carious dentine | Caridex (historical); Carisolv (amino acid based); Papacarie (papain based); experimental pepsin gels |
| Photo-ablation | Sound or carious enamel and dentine | Lasers |
| Others | Bacteria | Photo-activated disinfection (PAD), ozone |

## 2. Why the methods changed: from mechanical retention to adhesion

### Explanation

Sturdevant's records the first shift: "Modern high-speed equipment has eliminated the need for many hand instruments for tooth preparation", yet "hand instruments remain an essential part of the armamentarium for restorative dentistry" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The second shift was adhesion. Summitt explains why air abrasion failed in the 1940s and succeeded later: "because the Airdent did not prepare undercuts in preparations, the technology soon lost favor"; "When it was reintroduced in the 1980s, it received a greater degree of acceptance because bonded restorations had become routine" (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt also states that "all restorations placed at that time depended on cavity-preparation shape for retention" (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. This single point is the key to the whole topic: a method that cannot cut boxes, undercuts and sharp line angles became acceptable only when the restoration no longer needed them. It also explains the recurring limitation in every section below: none of the alternative methods can cut a classical G.V. Black preparation, and none is meant to.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Rationale for the advances.** High-speed rotary equipment replaced most hand cutting, though hand instruments remain essential (Ritter et al, 2019). Restorations of that era depended on preparation shape (undercuts) for retention (Summitt, 2013). The first air-abrasion unit (Airdent) failed because it could not prepare undercuts; when reintroduced in the 1980s it was accepted because bonded restorations had become routine (Summitt, 2013). Adhesion, minimal intervention and patient comfort (less noise, vibration and anaesthesia) are therefore the drivers of every newer method.

## 3. Advances in rotary instrumentation

### Explanation

The rotary advances that books describe are modest but examinable. Pickard's stresses that "burs are not self-limiting, and rely on the operator to distinguish" what to remove (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. The response is the plastic bur, which only "removes tooth structure that is softer than itself (anything harder and it will blunt during use)", made as a latch-grip rose-head "PKK (polyketone-ketone) plastic bur" for single use (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Garg and Garg describe the same instrument: "Smart prep instrument is also known as polymer bur or smart bur", which "has property of self-limiting, this means it will not cut the healthy dentin", used "with slow speed handpiece (500-800 rpm)"; they list as disadvantages that it is technique sensitive and costly and that "This instrument leaves large amount of decayed portion unexcavated" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Garg and Garg's list of newer rotary aids (fibre-optic handpiece, smart burs, "Chemical vapors deposition (CVD) diamond burs", fissurotomy burs) includes fibre-optic handpieces ("handpiece with a built in optics have been made available") and fissurotomy burs (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Pickard's mentions "ceramic/plastic burs in a speed-reducing handpiece" for final removal after chemo-mechanical gel (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], and Mount describes dedicated ultra-low-speed burs "for use in an ultra-low speed (450 rpm) handpiece" for use after Carisolv (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. CVD diamond tips belong to the ultrasonic section (section 5).

Handpieces have also changed. Sturdevant's: "Variable control of rpm makes the handpiece more versatile", and "All electric handpieces have an electronic control module" that, with the rheostat, allows precise control of maximum rpm, whereas air-driven speed is harder to control (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount explains why this matters for conservation: with air turbines the load before stalling falls as speed rises, so "It is therefore possible to remove too much tooth structure with ultra-high speed burs if visibility is less than excellent", while "With electric micro-motors, torque can remain reasonably high through an electrical feed-back circuit and tactile sense remains high" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC].

The polymer bur claim shows why the evidence must be weighed: Garg and Garg present it as removing decay "without affecting the healthy dentin" and in the same paragraph admit it leaves decayed tissue behind (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Both are true if the bur stops in caries-affected dentine, which is the intended end point in minimal intervention; whether this is a merit or a defect depends on the excavation philosophy. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advances in rotary cutting**
- **Polymer (smart, Smart Prep) bur:** latch-grip rose-head bur of polymer (PKK, polyketone-ketone) that removes only tissue softer than itself and blunts on sound dentine, hence "self-limiting"; slow speed (500-800 rpm), single use (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Garg and Garg, 2011). Advantages: less risk of pulp exposure, minimal removal. Disadvantages: technique sensitive, costly, leaves much decayed tissue unexcavated (Garg and Garg, 2011).
- **Ceramic burs and ultra-low-speed (450 rpm) burs** for removing softened dentine after chemo-mechanical gels (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
- **Fibre-optic handpieces** that light the bur head; **fissurotomy burs** for minimal fissure preparation (Garg and Garg, 2011).
- **Electric micromotor handpieces:** electronic control of rpm (Ritter et al, 2019); torque held by feedback so tactile sense is kept, whereas air turbines lose torque at high speed and can over-cut when visibility is poor (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
- **CVD diamond tips** for ultrasonic handpieces (section 5).

## 4. Air abrasion (kinetic cavity preparation)

### Explanation

**Principle.** Sturdevant's defines it as "transfer of kinetic energy from a stream of powder particles on the surface of tooth structure" to produce a fractured surface layer (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount gives the synonyms ("Air abrasion, also called kinetic cavity preparation, particle beam or kinetic abrasion technology") and the mechanism, which is "to remove tooth structure by brittle micro-fracture" with alumina in a high-pressure air stream (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Garg and Garg add "advanced particle beam technology or microabrasion" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

**History, where the books disagree.** Mount: "The technique of air abrasion was invented in 1943 by Robert Black" and "re-emerged in the 1990s, largely due to the work of J. Tim Rainey" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Garg and Garg also give 1943 (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Pickard's gives "Developed in 1945 by R. B. Black" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Summitt and Modern Operative Dentistry give only "the 1940s" for the SS White Airdent and place the reintroduction in the 1980s (Summitt, 2013; Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Sturdevant's says air-abrasive cutting was tested "In the mid-1950s" and failed because "no tactile sense was associated with air-abrasive cutting", dust hid the site and etched mirrors, and "Preventing the patient or office personnel from inhaling abrasive dust posed an additional difficulty" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The dates 1943 and 1945 may be invention versus first report; the 1980s and 1990s may be reintroduction of units versus popularisation. The books do not explain the difference, so both should be written with their authors.

**Variables.** Pickard's lists air pressure, powder flow rate, operating distance, particle type, size and shape, and nozzle diameter (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Mount quantifies the trade-off: "Doubling the particle size from 27 to 50 microns multiplies the mass, and thus the kinetic energy, by a factor of eight", so cutting is faster but more uncomfortable; hence "27 microns for removing caries", "34 microns for cutting enamel" and 50 micron for laboratory use (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Modern Operative Dentistry: "Large particles (50 μm) can be used to promote a faster grinding" and 27 μm for slower grinding (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Garg and Garg give 25-30 µm particles at 60-120 psi (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. A selective property matters clinically: "carious enamel cuts preferentially to sound enamel but sound dentine is cut more readily than carious dentine" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC], because "areas of carious infected dentine are not hard and brittle" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Air abrasion is therefore good for entry through enamel but poor for removing soft caries.

**Advantages.** Pickard's: "no heat, vibration, pressure, pain, or noise generated", and surface and subsurface cracking "is far less than when using rotary instruments, so making the air-abraded hard surface more suitable for adhesive bonding" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Mount reports SEM findings of "rounded cavo-surface margins and internal line angles", while bur-cut cavities show chipped margins and "surface microcracking to a depth greater than 50 microns" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. These are laboratory (SEM) observations. Bonsor and Pearson: "The main advantage of air abrasion is that microcavities may be prepared so preserving tooth tissue" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt's pulp chapter: animal studies show "air-abrasion cavity preparation is no more traumatic to the pulp than rotary instrumentation" (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC].

**On anaesthesia and precision the books disagree.** Bonsor and Pearson: "There is no need for local anaesthesia" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Garg and Garg: "Patient requiring air abrasion do not need local anesthesia" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; Mount lists only a "Reduced need for anaesthesia" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Garg and Garg list "Precise and rapid enamel cutting" as an advantage (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC], whereas Sturdevant's states that "air abrasion is not able to produce well-defined preparation wall and margin details" and that the finest stream still cuts wider than tolerable margin errors (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount's position lies between: precise for small entry cavities, but "a distinct learning curve" and "a tendency for over-cutting of sound tooth structure" because there is no tactile feedback (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Sturdevant's adds that the commonest error is "holding the tip at the wrong distance from the surface for the desired action" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Bonding.** Sturdevant's: "Acid etching alone or after roughening, however, always produces a better bond than air abrasion alone" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount: air abrasion "will not effectively remove the smear layer from dentine, but rather creates one", so acid etching is still needed (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC].

**Indications and limits.** Mount: "It should be regarded as an adjunct to, but not a universal replacement for, rotary instruments" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Garg and Garg agree it is "used as an adjunct not as replacement for handpiece" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Sturdevant's restricts contemporary use to stain removal, "debriding pits and fissures before sealing and micromechanical roughening of surfaces to be bonded" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount's primary indications are "preparation of minimal access through enamel for occlusal lesions", small "occlusal, cervical and proximal cavities as well as tunnel preparations", cleaning before adhesive restorations, and "removal of old composite resin restorations and sealants" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Mount lists unsuitable uses: grossly carious dentine, large cavities, subgingival caries, crown and inlay preparations, "preparing precise retention features in cavity preparations such as slots, grooves or ditches", and removal of crowns or porcelain (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson: "these machines are unable to remove existing restorations" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC], which conflicts with Mount's indication for removing old composite and sealants; the difference is the type of restoration (resin versus metal and ceramic).

**Hazards and newer units.** Mount: very high pressures raise discomfort and the "theoretical risk of injury from air emphysema"; alumina "must not be used in a dental practice where operatory staff suffer from asthma"; staff and patient must wear eye protection (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Garg and Garg: "Inhalation of particles can cause systemic problems" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. "When the nozzle is close to the teeth, the particle stream is narrow" and the cut widens as distance increases (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. For dust control, intra-oral high-velocity evacuation plus "additional extra-oral high performance vacuum systems are required" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; with multi-chambered units "rubber dam is advisable for patient comfort" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Water-shrouded units are the main recent change: Summitt's "water curtain reduces the amount of dust" (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]; Pickard's multi-chambered units meter alumina, bioactive glass and bicarbonate in a water shroud (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; Mount notes the water stream improves removal of soft dentine but "the cutting effect appears to be widened" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Bioactive glass powders are used for stain removal, desensitisation and selective removal of demineralised enamel, and "New powders are being investigated for self-limiting carious dentine removal and their remineralization potential" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition and principle.** Air abrasion (kinetic cavity preparation, particle beam technology, microabrasion) transfers kinetic energy from a high-velocity stream of fine alumina particles to the tooth, removing tissue by brittle micro-fracture (Ritter et al, 2019; Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Garg and Garg, 2011).

**History (books differ).** Invented by Robert Black in 1943 (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Garg and Garg, 2011) or 1945 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); SS White Airdent in the 1940s, abandoned because it could not cut undercuts and gave no tactile sense, with dust and mirror-etching problems (Summitt, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019). Reintroduced in the 1980s with bonding (Summitt, 2013; Gonçalves et al, 2020) or re-emerged in the 1990s through Rainey (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Operating variables (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016):** air pressure, powder flow rate, nozzle diameter, tip-to-tooth distance and angle, particle size and type. Doubling particle size from 27 to 50 µm increases kinetic energy eightfold; 27 µm for caries, 34 µm for enamel, 50 µm for laboratory use. Carious enamel is cut faster than sound enamel, but sound dentine faster than soft carious dentine. Garg and Garg (2011) give 25-30 µm particles at 60-120 psi. Approved intra-oral powders are alumina and bioactive glass (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Nozzle close to the tooth gives a narrow cut; farther away a wider, shallower cut.

**Advantages**
- No heat, vibration, pressure or noise; less pain (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Conserves tooth tissue: microcavities (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- Rounded internal line angles and margins, less microcracking than burs, a surface suited to bonding (SEM data; Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Pulp: no more traumatic than rotary instruments in animal studies (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
- Anaesthesia: "not needed" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Garg and Garg, 2011) versus "reduced need" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Disadvantages**
- No tactile sense; learning curve; over-cutting; wrong tip distance is the commonest error (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Ritter et al, 2019).
- Cannot produce well-defined walls and margins (Ritter et al, 2019), though Garg and Garg (2011) list precise enamel cutting as an advantage.
- Poor on soft carious dentine; creates rather than removes smear layer; acid etching still gives a better bond (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Ritter et al, 2019).
- Dust: risk to asthmatic staff, eyes and lungs; theoretical air emphysema at high pressure (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Garg and Garg, 2011). Protection: rubber dam, intra-oral high-volume evacuation plus extra-oral vacuum, masks and eye protection for patient and staff (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**Indications:** pit and fissure debridement before sealing, minimal occlusal and tunnel entry, small cervical and proximal cavities, cleaning before bonding, removing composite and sealants, stain removal, roughening metal and ceramic for bonding (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Ritter et al, 2019). **Not for:** gross caries, large or subgingival cavities, crown/inlay preparations, slots and grooves, removal of metal and ceramic restorations (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

**Recent developments:** water-shrouded and multi-chambered units that reduce dust (Summitt, 2013; Banerjee and Watson, 2015), at the cost of a wider cut (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016); bioactive glass powders for desensitisation and selective enamel removal, with self-limiting powders under research (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Air abrasion is an adjunct, not a replacement, for the handpiece (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Garg and Garg, 2011).

## 5. Sono-abrasion and ultrasonic preparation, including CVD diamond tips

### Explanation

Pickard's states the principle: ultrasonic and sonic instruments "use the principle of probe tip oscillation and micro-cavitation to chip away hard dental tissues" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Modern Operative Dentistry describes oscillatory instruments that, instead of rotating, "vibrate or oscillate in different directions", in three forms: sonic, ultrasonic, and reciprocating "back-and-forth reciprocating linear motion, called the EVA system" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Sonic handpieces are air-driven at about 6000-8000 Hz, "depending on the brand" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]; ultrasonic units work at 25,000-35,000 Hz, and "Most of the ultrasonic devices available in the market use a piezoelectric system" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Marzouk describes the older magnetostrictive design, with the generator "separate from a magnetostrictive transducer located within the handpiece", and slurry delivery against the working point (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

The status of the method has changed. Garg and Garg: "Ultrasound emerged for caries removal in 1950s" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Marzouk, the older text: "ultrasonic instruments are not universally used for cavity preparations" and are mainly for calculus and stain (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Marzouk also links high-amplitude vibration with a pulpal "rebound response" (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Summitt, the newer text: "ultrasonic and sonic tooth preparation is a viable means for conservative cavity preparation" (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. The change is due to new tips. For proximal boxes Summitt describes safe-sided tips in which "the diamond abrasive is only present on one side of the tip" (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC], and Modern Operative Dentistry the same design that "avoids the undesired grinding of the adjacent tooth" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC].

**CVD diamond tips.** These are made "through a process called chemical vapor deposition or CVD" on heads of molybdenum (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Garg and Garg explain why: conventional "diamond powder aggregation with nickel metallic binders cannot withstand ultrasonic power" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. The date differs: Garg and Garg write "In 1996, CVD diamond burs attached to an ultrasonic handpiece were introduced" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; Modern Operative Dentistry says "the points were launched onto the market in 2001" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. The second describes the Brazilian CVDentus product it names, so the two dates may refer to different events; neither book says.

**Technique and evidence.** Modern Operative Dentistry gives the key handling rule: "with the sonic and ultrasonic points, the increase of pressure significantly decreases the grinding effectiveness"; "The ideal pressure ... is the weight of the handpiece itself" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt advises that "It will usually be necessary to initiate the preparation with a rotary high-speed handpiece" (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Modern Operative Dentistry reports that large-volume work such as prosthetic preparation and removal of metal or ceramic prostheses "are not indicated for ultrasonic points", that there is "no piercing of the rubber dam" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC], and that ultrasonic preparation "is more comfortable for the patient", but "should not reflect in complete elimination of the anesthetic procedure"; an in vivo study in human molars found "none of the systems caused significant changes to the odontoblastic layers"; the CVD system is slower than rotary instruments, "an average of 2 minutes longer working time"; it "only works on hard tissues, without harming the soft tissues"; and patients and dentists with a pacemaker should not use ultrasonic devices (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. CVD points "are very durable, resisting to the ultrasound vibrations when grinding hard materials"; because they cut by vibration they need no long-axis symmetry, "improving significantly the access to remote sites"; and less sensitivity is attributed partly to the fact that "the water used in irrigation slightly warms up as it passes through the handpiece" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. The reciprocating handpiece "is known as the EVA system" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt adds that sonic and ultrasonic preparation "can be slower than with normal rotary handpieces", pulpal temperature rise is "not greater than that with water-cooled rotary preparation", and sonic preparation "does not adversely affect adhesion" compared with a carbide bur (Hilton and Broome, 2013) [SRC]. Mount's Table 8.4 ("Relative effectiveness of cavity preparation technologies") rates ultrasonic instruments slow on sound enamel and dentine but rapid on carious dentine (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** Tip oscillation and micro-cavitation chip away hard tissue (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Types (Gonçalves et al, 2020): sonic (air-driven, about 6000-8000 Hz); ultrasonic (25,000-35,000 Hz, mostly piezoelectric; older units magnetostrictive, Marzouk et al, undated); reciprocating (EVA system).

**Evolution.** Ultrasound was used for caries removal from the 1950s (Garg and Garg, 2011) but was not universally used for cavities (Marzouk et al, undated). It is now a viable means of conservative preparation (Summitt, 2013) because of:
- **Safe-sided diamond tips** with abrasive on one side only, protecting the adjacent tooth in proximal boxes (Hilton and Broome, 2013; Gonçalves et al, 2020).
- **CVD diamond tips:** synthetic diamond grown by chemical vapour deposition on molybdenum, because nickel-bonded diamond cannot withstand ultrasonic power (Garg and Garg, 2011; Gonçalves et al, 2020). Introduced in 1996 (Garg and Garg, 2011) or launched in 2001 (Gonçalves et al, 2020).

**CVD tip properties:** very durable under ultrasonic vibration; heads need no rotational symmetry, so flat and angled shapes improve access to remote sites; irrigation water warms slightly in the handpiece, reducing sensitivity (Gonçalves et al, 2020).

**Technique.** Light pressure: the weight of the handpiece; more pressure reduces cutting (Gonçalves et al, 2020). Initial entry is often made with a high-speed bur (Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**Advantages:** conservative access, protection of adjacent tooth, more comfort and less need for anaesthesia, no soft-tissue or rubber-dam damage, pulpal temperature and odontoblast response similar to water-cooled rotary cutting, adhesion not impaired (Gonçalves et al, 2020; Hilton and Broome, 2013).

**Disadvantages:** slower (about 2 minutes longer), not for crown preparation or removal of large volumes, contraindicated with cardiac pacemakers; high-amplitude vibration can provoke a pulpal "rebound response" (Gonçalves et al, 2020; Marzouk et al, undated).

## 6. Lasers

### Explanation

**Which lasers cut teeth.** Sturdevant's: "The lasers in the middle-infrared region have been employed for cavity preparation and caries removal": Er:YAG 2940 nm, Er:YSGG 2790 nm and Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm, all relying on water absorption, so "the efficiency of ablation is greatest for Er:YAG laser" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount: "A wavelength of 2940 nm will match exactly the maximal absorption of water" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Poli and co-workers say that of current dental wavelengths "only three can be used for hard tissue": the two erbium lasers and a newer CO2 variant whose "emission wavelength is 9300 nm" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]. Mount reports that other systems (super-pulsed CO2, Ho:YAG, Nd:YAG, excimer and others) "has not led to clinical application due to concerns about effectiveness, safety, or cost" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson explain why: the conventional CO2 laser melts enamel and raises "serious concerns that the pulp tissue may be damaged"; the Nd:YAG laser is poorly absorbed, so "the tooth has to be painted with a chromophore to allow absorption to occur"; "The Er:YAG has proved to be the most effective of the commercially available lasers for tooth cutting" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

**Mechanism.** Mount: "an explosive subsurface expansion of interstitially trapped water" ejecting apatite crystals (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Modern Operative Dentistry calls it "photomechanical ablation" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Because caries holds more water, carious tissue is "ablated selectively at lower pulse energies" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC], which Poli and co-workers say "forms the basis of selective laser ablation" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]. Enamel "requires more energy for ablation" than dentine (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]; the operator should "use a minimum dose parameter to deliver an optimal outcome" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].

**History and pulp safety.** Mount: Er:YAG cutting with water spray "was first characterized in detail by Raimund Hibst at the University of Ulm in Germany in the mid-1980s"; in teeth later extracted for orthodontic reasons "The studies showed no histological or biochemical injury to the dental pulp arising from the laser cutting procedure" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Summitt: a variety of lasers have "demonstrated minimal pulpal response compared with that of high-speed rotary instrumentation", and water coolant is "The key to minimizing thermal pulpal insult" (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC].

**Advantages claimed.** Mount: erbium lasers produce "a characteristic popping sound" whose pitch changes with caries, so "These sounds assist the user in determining when caries removal is complete"; only 2-5% "of patients will request local anaesthesia" in large trials; and the finding "raises the intriguing possibility that there is an analgesic effect" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Water spray provides energy coupling "as well as cooling between pulses" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Goldstein's chapter: in children "it is better accepted than traditional techniques" (Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018) [SRC]. Mount notes "Healthy tooth structure produces a" high-pitched click, whereas "Carious tooth structure transmits a loud" low-pitched thud; "Most are non-contact handpieces and use a sapphire window" with a visible aiming beam (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Poli and co-workers give a post-laser protocol: "eliminate unsupported enamel prisms by using manual" instruments or fine burs (or bevel with low-energy laser), etch with phosphoric acid, and in deep cavities "it may be advisable to use glass ionomer cement or a flowable composite as liner on the cavity floor" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]. Modern Operative Dentistry: "laser does not produce smear layer, leaving a clean" surface (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC], which is why "Caries-detecting dyes are unsuitable for use with lasers" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Poli and co-workers' Table 8.1 lists "Tissue decontamination due to bacteria removal from dentin tubules" and "no vibration, non-contact" among the advantages, and they add that the same instrument can treat soft tissue (gingivoplasty, crown lengthening) at the same visit (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].

**Limitations.** Poli and co-workers' own comparison table gives enamel cutting speed "<30% bur speed", and warns that lased surfaces can be "up to seven times the roughness index of sound enamel" and, if left uncovered, "can significantly promote plaque retention" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson: cutting a conventional cavity takes longer, and in tunnels "the slower the pulse rate, the faster the tissue is removed"; reflected beams can cause collateral damage (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; they judge that hard-tissue cutting "still remains in its infancy" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's is the most critical: "generation of a defined margin or tooth preparation surface is not possible"; lasers "are not able to remove existing amalgam or ceramic dental restorations"; they are "relatively inefficient and impractical for tooth preparation and have not achieved widespread popularity"; "Lasers may never replace the high-speed dental handpiece"; and units are expensive (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Wavelength-specific safety glasses are needed because they are "absolutely specific for each equipment" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC].

**The two textbook positions.** Mount writes that laser technology for cavity preparation "is currently in a high state of refinement" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC], and supports this with trial-based pulp safety and analgesia; Sturdevant's and Bonsor and Pearson regard it as slow, imprecise at margins and immature. They are describing the same instrument with different weightings: Mount emphasises small carious lesions and anxious patients, Sturdevant's emphasises general preparation of all cavities. [Likely]

**Bonding to lased tooth: a genuine disagreement** (see the Critical Evidence Synthesis).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Hard-tissue lasers.** Middle-infrared erbium lasers: Er:YAG 2940 nm, Er:YSGG 2790 nm, Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm; Er:YAG ablates most efficiently because 2940 nm matches the absorption peak of water (Ritter et al, 2019; Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016). A 9300 nm CO2 laser is the newest hard-tissue option (Poli et al, 2023). Conventional CO2 (enamel melting, pulp heat) and Nd:YAG (needs a chromophore) are unsuitable for cutting (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); other wavelengths have not reached clinical use (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Mechanism.** Laser energy absorbed by water causes explosive subsurface expansion that ejects apatite (photomechanical ablation) (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Gonçalves et al, 2020). Carious tissue, holding more water, ablates at lower energy: selective ablation (Poli et al, 2023). Enamel needs more energy than dentine; water spray cools between pulses.

**History.** Hibst (Ulm) characterised Er:YAG cutting in the mid-1980s; clinical histology in teeth extracted for orthodontics showed no pulp injury (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016). Minimal pulpal response compared with high-speed burs (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).

**Advantages**
- Little or no anaesthesia: only 2-5% of patients request it; analgesic effect proposed (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
- Selective, minimally invasive removal of caries; acoustic feedback ("popping" sound changes when caries is removed) (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
- No smear layer, decontaminated surface, no vibration, better accepted by children (Gonçalves et al, 2020; Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018).
- Simultaneous soft-tissue procedures (Poli et al, 2023).

**Disadvantages**
- Slow on enamel (less than 30% of bur speed); longer for conventional cavities (Poli et al, 2023; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- No defined margin; cannot remove amalgam or ceramic; impractical for routine preparation; may never replace the handpiece; expensive (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Rough lased surface retains plaque if uncovered (Poli et al, 2023); caries dyes give false-positive stain (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
- Eye safety with wavelength-specific glasses; collateral damage from reflection (Gonçalves et al, 2020; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

**Clinical technique.** Non-contact handpiece with sapphire window and aiming beam, water spray, minimum effective energy (higher for enamel than dentine); caries end point by sound (sound tissue: high-pitched click; caries: low-pitched thud) (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Poli et al, 2023). After lasing: remove unsupported enamel with hand instruments or fine burs, etch with phosphoric acid, line deep cavities with GIC or flowable composite, then restore incrementally (Poli et al, 2023).

**Bonding.** Mount, Walsh and Brostek (2016) report little difference in bond strength and identical margins clinically; Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio (2018) and Poli et al (2023) report lower adhesion to lased tooth and advise phosphoric acid etching of lased surfaces. Laser "etching" is better called laser conditioning (Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018).

## 7. Chemo-mechanical caries removal (Carisolv, Papacarie)

### Explanation

**Development and composition.** Mount: the current method "was developed by Dan Ericsson in Sweden in the early 1990s" as Carisolv (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Caridex preceded it and is now of historical value only (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Mount gives the gel as "0.5 % sodium hypochlorite", "amino acids leucine, lysine, and glutamic acid", sodium chloride, "sodium hydroxide to make the solution pH 11" and carboxymethylcellulose as thickener (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson also give "0.5% sodium hypochlorite and three amino acids" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Pickard's states it "Consists of a 0.1% hypochlorite-based alkaline gel (pH 11) with amino acids" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. The books therefore give two different hypochlorite concentrations; the Minimal Intervention topic records the same split. Both figures should be written with authors; the difference may reflect different product generations, which none of these books states.

**Mode of action.** Mount: "proteolytic breakdown of dentine collagen proteins in carious dentine by the chloramines, without accompanying demineralization", and the three charged amino acids make the action "specific for denatured collagen" so sound dentine and mucosa are not affected (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson describe N-chlorinated amino acids that break down quickly: "This ensures that no further denaturing of the collagen occurs after the patient has left the surgery" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Pickard's: "This chemistry confers an element of self-limiting caries removal ability" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

**Technique.** The gel needs a cavitated lesion ("The system requires cavitated lesions for direct access to the carious dentine") (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Special instruments "have a sharp edge but a blunt cutting angle, to provide depth control and give a whisking rather than penetrating action" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC], used with "the same force as normal tooth brushing" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Gel is renewed until fresh gel stays clear: Mount gives the end point "At the point where fresh liquid no longer becomes opaque" and Bonsor and Pearson "until the gel no longer turns cloudy" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

**Evidence and limits.** Mount: "The Powerdrive ultra-low-speed bur is reversible ("The bur rotates in both directions"), and the remaining mildly demineralised surface, "in the presence of a bioactive restorative material, it is capable of remineralization" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. "Long-term clinical studies have not found significant differences in the treatment outcomes" compared with conventional preparation; the gel takes longer, but when anaesthesia time is included "the total time taken for the different techniques is more or less the same" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Mount's summary lists a "low annoyance factor", "an absence of a smear layer", and as disadvantages that it "takes more time" and "removes infected dentine only"; all surfaces must be checked for complete caries removal, "specially the dentino-enamel junction" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson state that the gel is "allowed to soak in for 30 seconds" before scraping (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Indications are adults with "dental fear and anxiety, phobia of needles or an allergy to local anaesthetics" and "It is also well suited to paediatric patients" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Limitations: it "will not complete the entire cavity design", and rotary or other methods "are required to remove existing restorations" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. **Papacarie** is listed only as a "papain based" gel and enzymatic gels "are being investigated" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; no book read gives its composition or clinical data, so it should not be written in more detail from these sources.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** A gel softens only the denatured collagen of caries-infected dentine, which is then removed with non-cutting hand instruments; sound dentine is unaffected, giving self-limiting caries removal (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**Agents.** Caridex (historical); Carisolv (Ericsson, Sweden, early 1990s); Papacarie (papain based); experimental pepsin gels (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Carisolv composition.** Sodium hypochlorite 0.5% (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) or 0.1% (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); amino acids leucine, lysine and glutamic acid; sodium chloride; sodium hydroxide to pH 11; carboxymethylcellulose thickener (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).

**Mechanism.** Chloramines formed from hypochlorite and amino acids break down denatured collagen without demineralisation; the charged amino acids make the action specific for denatured collagen; the N-chloramino acids decay quickly so no action continues after the visit (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

**Technique.** Access to a cavitated lesion; apply gel 30 s; whisk softened dentine with blunt star or mace instruments at tooth-brushing force (or a 450 rpm bur); repeat until fresh gel no longer turns cloudy; inspect, especially the DEJ; restore adhesively, preferably with a bioactive material such as glass ionomer so the remaining mildly demineralised dentine can remineralise (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

**Advantages:** less need for anaesthesia; low annoyance; preserves sound tissue; no smear layer; outcomes equal to conventional methods in long-term clinical studies; total time similar once anaesthesia is counted (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016). Indicated in anxious, needle-phobic or anaesthetic-allergic patients and children.

**Disadvantages:** slower; needs cavitated access; removes infected dentine only and does not complete the cavity; cannot remove enamel or old restorations (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## 8. Antibacterial and other emerging methods: ozone, photo-activated disinfection, enzymes

### Explanation

Pickard's describes PAD as "introducing tolonium chloride into the cavity", taken up by bacteria and activated by light, and ozone as "gaseous ozone infused into early lesions, causing bacterial death"; it concludes "These technologies currently suffer from a lack of clinical research to validate them for routine" use (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

On ozone the books disagree sharply. Goldstein's paediatric chapter credits ozone with "effective antibacterial activity, also on cariogenic bacteria" and states: "because the carious dentin is sterilized, it does not need to be removed", delivered by a silicone cup where "a hermetic seal must be obtained, and application time ranges from 10 to 60 s" (Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018) [SRC]. Modern Operative Dentistry: "the ozone therapy did not meet all expectations and can only be classified as an additional option for controlling dental caries"; "the ozone acts on contact", so proximal and hidden lesions cannot be treated; "There is good in vitro evidence of the prophylactic application of ozone", but "there is only limited information from clinical studies, which are in addition in part contradictory", and "Reinfection of the disinfected surface can rapidly occur" without sealing (Borges, Torres and Schlueter, 2020) [SRC]. The manufacturer's protocol for incipient occlusal lesions is ozone "followed by the application of a remineralizing solution that contains fluoride, calcium, zinc, and phosphate", with home fluoride; "The ozone application should be repeated after 3 and 6 months" (Borges, Torres and Schlueter, 2020) [SRC]. Goldstein's statement is a claim without cited clinical outcome; the Modern Operative Dentistry chapter grades the evidence. [Likely] That chapter says ozone "could potentially be used for disinfection of fissures prior to fissure sealant application" or for non-invasive treatment of early lesions (Borges, Torres and Schlueter, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Ozone.** Gaseous ozone delivered through a sealed silicone cup (10-60 s) kills cariogenic bacteria (Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018). Caprioglio and co-workers claim sterilised carious dentine need not be removed; Borges, Torres and Schlueter (2020) state that ozone did not meet expectations and is only an additional option, acts only where the cup seals (not proximal or hidden lesions), has good in vitro but limited and contradictory clinical evidence, and allows rapid reinfection unless the surface is sealed. Manufacturer's protocol: ozone, then a remineralising solution (fluoride, calcium, zinc, phosphate), home fluoride, and repeat applications at 3 and 6 months (Borges, Torres and Schlueter, 2020). Possible uses: disinfecting fissures before sealing and non-invasive treatment of early occlusal and smooth-surface lesions.

**Photo-activated disinfection (PAD):** tolonium chloride taken up by residual bacteria and activated by light of a specific wavelength (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**Enzymatic gels:** papain and pepsin solutions are being investigated for more self-limiting removal of infected dentine (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

These methods currently lack clinical research to validate routine use (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## 9. Comparison and maturity of evidence

### Explanation

Mount's Table 8.4, "Relative effectiveness of cavity preparation technologies", rates rotary burs moderate on sound tissue and restorations and rapid on carious dentine; air abrasion moderate on sound enamel and resin restorations but slow on dentine, carious dentine and metal; Carisolv rapid on carious dentine and no effect on anything else; Er:YAG moderate on enamel and dentine, rapid on carious dentine and no effect on metallic restorations, while Er:YSGG is slow on carious dentine; ultrasonics slow except on carious dentine; hand excavators moderate on carious dentine only (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. The table rates air abrasion as slowly cutting metallic restorations, while the same chapter's text lists removal of crowns and porcelain as unsuitable; the table describes capability, the text clinical advice. Read with the evidence statements above, the maturity of each method can be graded. The grading below is this answer's synthesis, not a table from any book. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Comparison (after Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016, Table 8.4)**

| Method | Sound enamel | Sound dentine | Carious dentine | Old restorations | Main role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rotary burs | Moderate | Moderate | Rapid | Moderate (metal and resin) | All preparations |
| Air abrasion | Moderate | Slow | Slow | Slow (metal); moderate (resin) | Fissures, minimal entry, surface preparation |
| Ultrasonic | Slow | Slow | Rapid | Slow | Proximal boxes, margins |
| Er:YSGG laser | Moderate | Moderate | Slow | None (metal); moderate (resin) | Small cavities |
| Er:YAG laser | Moderate | Moderate | Rapid | None (metal); moderate (resin) | Small lesions, anxious patients |
| Carisolv | None | None | Rapid | None | Soft caries without anaesthesia |
| Hand excavators | None | None | Moderate | None | Final caries removal, ART |

**Maturity of evidence**
- **Clinically established:** rotary instruments; hand excavation.
- **Clinical data, adjunctive role:** Carisolv (long-term clinical outcomes equal to conventional, Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016); erbium lasers (clinical histology and large trials on anaesthesia, Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; but bonding data conflict, mostly in vitro).
- **Mainly laboratory or animal data:** air abrasion (SEM and animal pulp studies; Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Hilton and Summitt, 2013); sono-abrasion and CVD (limited in vivo studies; Gonçalves et al, 2020).
- **Experimental or unvalidated:** ozone (in vitro positive, clinical contradictory; Borges, Torres and Schlueter, 2020), PAD, enzymatic gels, self-limiting powders, 9300 nm CO2 laser (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Poli et al, 2023).

## 10. Conclusion

### Explanation

The books converge on one conclusion, stated three ways: air abrasion is an adjunct, not a replacement (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; "Lasers may never replace the high-speed dental handpiece" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; and "at present no perfect instrument exists" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. The newer methods are best seen as tools matched to a substrate and a patient.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** Recent cavity preparation methods are products of adhesive, minimally invasive dentistry. Each acts selectively: air abrasion on enamel and surfaces to be bonded, sono-abrasion on proximal and marginal areas, erbium lasers on small carious lesions in anxious patients, chemo-mechanical gels and polymer burs on soft infected dentine, and ozone or PAD on bacteria. None can prepare every cavity or remove every restoration, and the evidence for most is laboratory based or limited. Air abrasion and lasers are adjuncts rather than replacements for the handpiece (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Ritter et al, 2019); the clinician should select the method by substrate, lesion size and patient, combining methods where needed, because no perfect instrument yet exists (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Composite bonds as well to laser-prepared tooth as to bur-cut, acid-etched tooth."**

*What other sources show:* Goldstein's chapter: "Many authors have reported that adhesion to laser-ablated or laser-etched dentin and enamel of permanent teeth is lower", and "acid etching of lased surfaces is still to be preferred" (Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018) [SRC]. *Agreement or challenge:* challenge, partly reconciled. The low values come largely from lased surfaces bonded without phosphoric acid; Poli's clinical protocol itself says "Perform acid etching by using orthophosphoric acid" after lasing (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Pickard's Table 5.5: rotary, non-rotary mechanical, chemo-mechanical, photo-ablation, others (ozone, PAD); state the substrate each acts on.
- Air abrasion: Robert Black, 1943 (Mount; Garg) or 1945 (Pickard's); Airdent failed because no undercuts; returned with bonding; brittle micro-fracture; 27 µm caries, 34 µm enamel, 50 µm lab; adjunct, not replacement.
- Erbium lasers: Er:YAG 2940, Er:YSGG 2790, Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm; water-mediated photomechanical ablation; Hibst, mid-1980s; 2-5% need LA; slow on enamel; no defined margin (Sturdevant's).

- Sono-abrasion: tip oscillation and micro-cavitation; safe-sided tips; CVD diamond on molybdenum (1996 Garg; 2001 Modern Operative); handpiece-weight pressure; avoid with pacemakers.
- Polymer (smart) bur: self-limiting, 500-800 rpm, leaves affected dentine.
- Ozone and PAD: experimental; ozone evidence in vitro, clinical contradictory.
- Bonding to lased tooth: acid etch after lasing.
