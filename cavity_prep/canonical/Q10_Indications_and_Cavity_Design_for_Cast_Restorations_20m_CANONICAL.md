# What are the indications of Cast restorations? Write on basic concept and principles of cavity design for cast restorations.

**Marks:** 20 · Essay

**Answer blueprint:** Introduction → Indications and contraindications → Basic concept → Principles of cavity design → conclusion.

## 1. Indications and contraindications

**Definitions:** inlay = intracoronal casting (occlusal and proximal surfaces); onlay = casting that caps all cusps; partial onlay caps at least one but not all cusps (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019).

**Indications** (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Marzouk et al., n.d.; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013)
1. Large proximal lesions needing the strength of an alloy.
2. Teeth weakened by caries or large failing restorations (onlay instead of crown): casting supports the tooth.
3. Endodontically treated posterior teeth (onlay with cusp coverage).
4. Superior control of contours and contacts; diastema closure; occlusal-plane correction.
5. Abutments for removable partial dentures (rests, guide planes); retainers for fixed prostheses.
6. Adjacent or opposing gold restorations (avoid galvanism).
7. Cracked teeth (rigid splinting); bruxism, since gold wears like enamel.

**Advantages:** strength to restore large missing areas and protect the tooth from fracture; biocompatibility of high-gold alloys (unreactive orally); low wear, similar to enamel, so opposing teeth are not abraded; control of contours and contacts; long survival, 87-97% at 10 years, better than all-ceramic under bruxism (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).

**Disadvantages:** at least two appointments and temporaries; cost; technique sensitivity, since errors in the multi-step indirect process are compounded; metal display; cement line at the margin; loss of retention is the main technical failure (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).

**Alloys used:** four groups: high-gold (ADA No. 5), low-gold, palladium-silver and base-metal alloys (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019); Marzouk adds castable ceramics as a fifth class.

**Contraindications:** high caries rate and poor plaque control; young patients (large pulps, short crowns); esthetic areas; small lesions (amalgam or composite preferred; small inlays wedge cusps apart); high cost and multiple visits.

## 2. Basic concept

**Basic concept.** Unlike amalgam (walls converging occlusally, locked by undercuts), a casting is fabricated indirectly and must be inserted and withdrawn (wax pattern, impression, die, casting) along a **single path of withdrawal**, usually the long axis of the crown. Therefore: no undercuts; walls slightly **diverge occlusally**; retention depends mainly on **preparation geometry** (taper, wall length, opposing walls, surface area), with cement as only an auxiliary aid (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Marzouk et al., n.d.). Retention resists removal along the path; resistance resists oblique displacement and tooth fracture (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013). Intracoronal preparations (inlays) are mortise-shaped, with definite walls and floors meeting at line and point angles; extracoronal preparations (crowns, cusp coverage) are made by occlusal and axial reduction; most onlays combine both (Marzouk et al., n.d.).

## 3. Principles of cavity design

**Principles of cavity design for cast restorations**

1. **Single path of withdrawal**, usually the long axis of the crown; no undercuts; block out undercuts with base.
2. **Taper (occlusal divergence of walls).**

| Source | Recommended taper |
|---|---|
| Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019 | 2-5° per wall from line of draw; least for short walls, up to 5° for the tallest |
| Marzouk et al., n.d. | Average 2-5°; more for long walls, never above 10° |
| Singh, 2020 | 2-5° per wall |
| Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013 | Textbooks 6-12° total; clinically usually >12°; retention falls sharply 5→10° per wall but up to 16° total still adequate |

 Taper must be equal on opposing walls so only one path exists (Marzouk et al., n.d.).
3. **Retention form:** near-parallel opposing walls of adequate length; occlusal dovetail (proximal displacement); 0.3 mm proximal grooves in axial line angles; internal boxes (4-12 times an external box), slots, pins; facial/lingual surface groove extensions; skirts and collars; bevels; reciprocal retention at each end; luting cement is auxiliary only (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Marzouk et al., n.d.).
4. **Resistance form:** flat pulpal and gingival floors; rounded internal angles (No. 271 bur); narrow isthmus; boxes and pins add both retention and resistance, grooves and slots mainly resistance (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).
5. **Protection of the tooth:** cusp capping when extension exceeds half (consider) to two-thirds (usually) of groove-to-cusp distance (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019), or when width exceeds one-third intercuspal distance (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013); 1.5 mm metal over reduced cusps; counterbevel; skirts envelop and brace the tooth.
6. **Margins:** proximal clearance from the adjacent tooth; supragingival where possible; bevels and flares for metal (30-40° marginal metal), detailed under bevels.
7. **Conservation:** minimal extension; small inlays avoided because they wedge cusps.

**Conclusion.** A cast restoration requires a single path of withdrawal, controlled and uniform taper, adequate wall height and surface area, and sufficient resistance features to protect the remaining tooth. The exact taper recommendations vary between sources; avoid excessive taper while ensuring a complete path of insertion.
