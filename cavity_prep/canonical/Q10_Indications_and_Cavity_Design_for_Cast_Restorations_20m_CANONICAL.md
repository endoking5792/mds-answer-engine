## 1. Indications and contraindications

### Explanation

A Class II inlay is intracoronal, involving occlusal and proximal surfaces (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; an onlay caps all cusps (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]. Indications (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]: extensive proximal caries where alloy strength is needed; the onlay as an alternative to a crown for weakened teeth; more control of contours and contacts; endodontically treated posterior teeth; avoiding dissimilar-metal corrosion; diastema closure and occlusal-plane correction; RPD abutments. Marzouk adds that castings impart resistance to the tooth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], are the ultimate restoration for extensive involvement (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and the most rigid way to splint cracked teeth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Summitt's: gold is considered the longest-lived restorative material (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], with 87-97% survival at 10 years (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], better than all-ceramic under bruxism (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], and wear similar to enamel (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; nonworking-side contacts increase vertical fractures (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Advantages: strength to restore large missing areas (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; high-gold alloys unreactive in the mouth (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]. Disadvantages: at least two appointments (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC] and errors compounded through the multi-step process (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]. Four alloy groups are in use (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; Marzouk's classification adds castable ceramics as Class V (read in Ch.17, OCR line not used as a locator).

Contraindications (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]: high caries activity, young patients (short crowns, large pulps), esthetic zones, and small lesions, for which inlays are rarely done; small inlays can wedge and split cusps (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]. Marzouk agrees on young dentition with large pulps (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and uncontrolled plaque and caries (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Summitt's: direct materials are usually indicated over gold for small lesions (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definitions:** inlay = intracoronal casting (occlusal and proximal surfaces); onlay = casting that caps all cusps; partial onlay caps at least one but not all cusps (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019).

**Indications** (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Marzouk et al, undated; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013)
1. Large proximal lesions needing the strength of an alloy.
2. Teeth weakened by caries or large failing restorations (onlay instead of crown): casting supports the tooth.
3. Endodontically treated posterior teeth (onlay with cusp coverage).
4. Superior control of contours and contacts; diastema closure; occlusal-plane correction.
5. Abutments for removable partial dentures (rests, guide planes); retainers for fixed prostheses.
6. Adjacent or opposing gold restorations (avoid galvanism).
7. Cracked teeth (rigid splinting); bruxism, since gold wears like enamel.

**Advantages:** strength to restore large missing areas and protect the tooth from fracture; biocompatibility of high-gold alloys (unreactive orally); low wear, similar to enamel, so opposing teeth are not abraded; control of contours and contacts; long survival, 87-97% at 10 years, better than all-ceramic under bruxism (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).

**Disadvantages:** at least two appointments and temporaries; cost; technique sensitivity, since errors in the multi-step indirect process are compounded; metal display; cement line at the margin; loss of retention is the main technical failure (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).

**Alloys used:** four groups: high-gold (ADA No. 5), low-gold, palladium-silver and base-metal alloys (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019); Marzouk adds castable ceramics as a fifth class.

**Contraindications:** high caries rate and poor plaque control; young patients (large pulps, short crowns); esthetic areas; small lesions (amalgam or composite preferred; small inlays wedge cusps apart); high cost and multiple visits.

## 2. Basic concept

### Explanation

A casting is made outside the mouth and inserted along one path, so the preparation must have no undercuts (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; Marzouk: a single insertion path, opposite to the direction of occlusal loading (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], and castings cannot be prepared with an inverted cone shape or undercuts (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Intracoronal preparations are mortise-shaped with definite walls and floors (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Retention therefore comes mainly from geometry, the most important retentive factor (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; cements cannot substitute for frictional retention (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Summitt's defines retention as resisting removal parallel to the path and resistance as resisting oblique dislodgment (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], and loss of retention is the main technical failure (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Basic concept.** Unlike amalgam (walls converging occlusally, locked by undercuts), a casting is fabricated indirectly and must be inserted and withdrawn (wax pattern, impression, die, casting) along a **single path of withdrawal**, usually the long axis of the crown. Therefore: no undercuts; walls slightly **diverge occlusally**; retention depends mainly on **preparation geometry** (taper, wall length, opposing walls, surface area), with cement as only an auxiliary aid (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Marzouk et al, undated). Retention resists removal along the path; resistance resists oblique displacement and tooth fracture (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013). Intracoronal preparations (inlays) are mortise-shaped, with definite walls and floors meeting at line and point angles; extracoronal preparations (crowns, cusp coverage) are made by occlusal and axial reduction; most onlays combine both (Marzouk et al, undated).

## 3. Principles of cavity design

### Explanation

**Taper: sources side by side.**
- Sturdevant's Ch.23: 2-5 degrees per wall from the line of draw (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; Ch.11 the same and more divergence for taller walls (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Harpreet Singh repeats 2-5 degrees per wall (Singh, 2020) [SRC].
- Marzouk: average 2-5 degrees from the path (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; taper increases with wall length but should not exceed 10 degrees (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; it is also increased to diminish friction (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; one wall must never have more taper than the other in a way that allows more than one path (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].
- Summitt's: textbooks advocate a combined 6-12 degrees, but clinical taper is usually above 12 degrees with success (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; retention drops rapidly from 5 to 10 degrees per wall (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], yet total convergence up to 16 degrees still gives adequate retention (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC].
- **Internal inconsistency in Sturdevant's:** for short walls the inlay section says a *maximum* of 2 degrees (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC] while the onlay section says a *minimum* of 2 degrees (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]. Since the same chapter says divergence rises with height to 5 degrees for the longest walls (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC], the intended meaning appears to be "about 2 degrees, the least taper in the range" for short walls [Likely].

**Retention and resistance features.** Opposing walls: a long wall opposed by a short wall retains only like the short wall (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Boxes and pins improve both retention and resistance; grooves and slots mostly resistance (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; they help little on short, wide, over-tapered preparations (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's features: dovetail resisting proximal displacement (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; 0.3 mm proximal grooves in the axial line angles (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC], especially for short teeth (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; Table 23.3 lists minimal taper, surface groove extensions, proximal grooves, skirts, collars, slots, starting with 2 degrees per wall (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]. Skirts are thin extensions past the transitional line angle (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC] that virtually eliminate postrestorative fracture (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; collars use a 0.8 mm shoulder (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; slots conserve tooth structure compared with boxes (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]. Marzouk: an internal box gives four to twelve times the retention of an external box (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; slots are internal cavities in a floor (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; reciprocal retention at every end (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; posts should not retain a final casting (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; bevels are a major retention form (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Bonded castings: long-term effectiveness unknown (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

**Resistance of the tooth.** Rounded bur ends avoid stress-inducing angles (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; narrow isthmus (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]. Cusp capping: Sturdevant's considers it beyond half the groove-to-cusp distance (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC], with 1.5 mm metal (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's considers an onlay when the inlay exceeds one-third of intercuspal width (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC], since even a conservative MOD decreases stiffness by 50% (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk gives 1.5 mm reduction for cast ceramics (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Counterbevel 30-degree metal (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]; 0.5 mm occlusal step adds retention (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC].

**Not from these locators.** Blocking out undercuts with a base before the impression, and fixed-prosthesis retainers as an indication, are standard teaching not tied to a verified phrase here [RECALL] (Marzouk's list does include fixed-prosthesis retainers, but the OCR line was not used as a locator).

**Margins (outline).** Proximal clearance is mandatory for cast gold (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; bevels beyond 45 degrees increase the cement-filled opening (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. See Q09 for bevels and flares.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principles of cavity design for cast restorations**

1. **Single path of withdrawal**, usually the long axis of the crown; no undercuts; block out undercuts with base.
2. **Taper (occlusal divergence of walls).**

| Source | Recommended taper |
|---|---|
| Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019 | 2-5° per wall from line of draw; least for short walls, up to 5° for the tallest |
| Marzouk et al, undated | Average 2-5°; more for long walls, never above 10° |
| Singh, 2020 | 2-5° per wall |
| Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013 | Textbooks 6-12° total; clinically usually >12°; retention falls sharply 5→10° per wall but up to 16° total still adequate |

   Taper must be equal on opposing walls so only one path exists (Marzouk et al, undated). Sturdevant's says "maximum 2°" (inlay) and "minimum 2°" (onlay) for short walls: an internal inconsistency.
3. **Retention form:** near-parallel opposing walls of adequate length; occlusal dovetail (proximal displacement); 0.3 mm proximal grooves in axial line angles; internal boxes (4-12 times an external box), slots, pins; facial/lingual surface groove extensions; skirts and collars; bevels; reciprocal retention at each end; luting cement is auxiliary only (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Marzouk et al, undated).
4. **Resistance form:** flat pulpal and gingival floors; rounded internal angles (No. 271 bur); narrow isthmus; boxes and pins add both retention and resistance, grooves and slots mainly resistance (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).
5. **Protection of the tooth:** cusp capping when extension exceeds half (consider) to two-thirds (usually) of groove-to-cusp distance (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019), or when width exceeds one-third intercuspal distance (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013); 1.5 mm metal over reduced cusps; counterbevel; skirts envelop and brace the tooth.
6. **Margins:** proximal clearance from the adjacent tooth; supragingival where possible; bevels and flares for metal (30-40° marginal metal), detailed under bevels.
7. **Conservation:** minimal extension; small inlays avoided because they wedge cusps.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: inlay walls must taper only 2-5 degrees per wall.**
- Textbook says: 2-5 degrees per wall (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019; Marzouk et al, undated; Singh, 2020).
- Other evidence: clinically achieved tapers usually exceed 12 degrees total with success, and up to 16 degrees total still retains adequately; retention depends also on wall length and cement (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).
- Agreement/challenge: the 2-5 degree figure is an ideal; Summitt's challenges its clinical necessity.
- Strength: moderate (clinical survey and laboratory retention studies cited by Summitt's, mostly for crowns, not intracoronal inlays).
- Conclusion: aim for minimal taper, especially on short walls, and add boxes or grooves when walls are short. [Likely]
- Clinical implication: on short, wide teeth, reducing convergence is more effective than adding grooves (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Castings: single path, no undercuts, walls diverge occlusally.
2. Taper 2-5° per wall (Sturdevant's, Marzouk); max 10° (Marzouk); 6-12° total textbook ideal, up to 16° adequate (Summitt's).
3. Geometry is the main retention; cement is auxiliary.
4. Boxes and pins: retention plus resistance; grooves and slots: mainly resistance.
5. Cusp capping: half (consider), two-thirds (usually); onlay if inlay width > 1/3 intercuspal (Summitt's).
6. Skirts and collars brace the tooth.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Drawing inlay walls converging occlusally like amalgam.
2. Giving a single taper figure without naming the source.
3. Listing cement as primary retention.
4. Recommending inlays for small lesions.
5. Omitting cusp protection (onlay) for wide preparations.
