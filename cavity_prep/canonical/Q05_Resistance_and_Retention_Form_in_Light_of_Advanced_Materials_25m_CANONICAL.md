# Critically review the concept of resistance and retention form of restoration in light of advanced restorative material.

**Marks:** 25 · Essay

## 1. The classical concept: definitions

Resistance and retention form are steps 2 and 3 of Black's sequence of tooth preparation, originally designed for non-adhesive materials (gold foil, amalgam, cast gold). - **Resistance form:** the shape and placement of preparation walls and floors that enable both the restoration and the remaining tooth to resist fracture under occlusal load (Marzouk et al., n.d.; Boushell and Walter, 2019).
- **Retention form:** the shape of the preparation that prevents displacement of the restoration by tipping or lifting forces (Boushell and Walter, 2019).
- In force terms, retention resists dislodgment along the path of insertion (tensile), resistance resists oblique or compressive forces that displace or fracture (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).
- Both are divided into **primary** (part of the initial preparation) and **secondary** (added after caries removal: grooves, slots, pins, bonding).
- Key principle: retention form required depends on the retention needs of the material (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

## 2. Classical features of resistance form

**Features of primary resistance form** (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Marzouk et al., n.d.) 1. **Minimal extension:** conserve cusps and marginal ridges; Vale's experiment shows resistance to splitting falls progressively as marginal ridges are crossed.
2. **Flat floors (box form):** pulpal and gingival floors perpendicular to the long axis; prevents rocking and wedging.
3. **Rounded internal line angles:** reduce stress concentration in tooth and restoration.
4. **Adequate material thickness:** amalgam 1.5-2 mm, glass-ceramic 2 mm; composite has no minimum thickness.
5. **Cusp capping:** reduce and cover weakened cusps. Sturdevant's: consider at half, strongly recommended at two-thirds of the groove-to-cusp-tip distance; Summitt's: evaluate cusps when width exceeds one-third of the intercuspal distance.
6. **Envelopment:** if the material is stronger than tooth (cast metal), the restoration supports the tooth.

## 3. Classical features of retention form

**Primary retention** (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Marzouk et al., n.d.)
- Amalgam: walls converging occlusally; dovetail; dentin elasticity on condensation.
- Cast metal: near-parallel walls diverging 2-5 degrees per wall from the line of draw; occlusal dovetail; geometry is the main retentive factor; cement is the least effective auxiliary means.
- Composite: micromechanical (and chemical) bonding. **Secondary features (mechanical):** proximal retention grooves, coves (Class III amalgam), slots, amalgapins, pins, steps, internal boxes, facial/lingual groove extensions, skirts and collars (cast), bevels (castings; composite to add enamel bonding area). **Secondary (adhesive):** etching, priming, bonding. Each anatomically distinct part must be independently retentive.

## 4. How each material changes the requirements

| Material | Retention form | Resistance form |
|---|---|---|
| Amalgam (conventional) | Occlusal convergence, dovetail, grooves, coves, slots, amalgapins, pins | Flat floors, box form, 1.5-2 mm bulk, 90° margins, rounded angles, cusp capping |
| Bonded amalgam | Adhesive plus reduced mechanical features | Weak cusps bonded instead of reduced (disputed, see section 5) |
| Cast gold | Near-parallel walls (2-5° per wall), dovetail, boxes, grooves, slots, pins, skirts, collars, bevels | Cusp capping with counterbevel, skirts, collars envelop tooth; boxes and pins add both; grooves and slots mainly resistance |
| Composite | Bonding (none mechanical in small/moderate); grooves, coves, slots only in very large or foundation preparations; enamel bevels | No special form in small/moderate; flat floors and cusp coverage in large; no minimum thickness; box form raises C-factor |
| GIC/RMGI | Chemical bond; mechanical features not usually required | No special form; widely used as base under amalgam, gold, ceramic and composite |
| Ceramic/indirect composite | Divergent, nonretentive; adhesion | Rounded boxes, no grooves; 1.5 mm fossa, 2 mm functional cusps; butt joint, no bevels | (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Ahmed et al., 2019; Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013; Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013)

## 5. Critical review: what adhesion has and has not changed

**Critical appraisal** 1. **Retention has been largely replaced by adhesion; resistance has not.** Composite, GIC and bonded ceramic need little or no mechanical retention, so preparations are now defect-driven, not geometry-driven (Ahmed et al., 2019; Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013). But bonding intracoronal restorations does not increase the long-term resistance of the remaining tooth (Boushell and Walter, 2019); weak cusps still need coverage and large preparations still need flat floors.
2. **Classical form can harm adhesive restorations:** a boxlike form increases C-factor stress in composite; grooves and bevels are contraindicated in ceramics (stress raisers, chipping); extension "to get retention" weakens the tooth (Ahmed et al., 2019; Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013).
3. **Bonded amalgam is disputed.** Sturdevant's: in vitro benefit only, no clinical improvement, not promoted. Summitt's: clinically as successful as pin-retained restorations at 6 years, none debonded; pins plus bonding additive (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013). The claims differ: equivalence to pins versus no superiority to routine technique.
4. **Evidence base is weak:** pins, slots and amalgapins are compared mainly in flattened-molar laboratory tests; in vivo data do not support routine proximal grooves in dovetailed or MOD amalgams (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
5. **Castings remain geometry-dependent:** taper, wall height and boxes are the main retention; adhesive cements help, but long-term data on bonded castings are lacking (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013). **Conclusion.** Black's concept survives as a mechanical principle but is now applied selectively: retention form is material-dependent and often replaced by adhesion, while resistance form of the tooth (cusp protection, conservation of marginal ridges, adequate bulk) remains essential for every material.
