## 1. The classical concept: definitions

### Explanation

Black's steps place resistance and retention form immediately after outline form. **Resistance form** is the architectural form that lets both the restoration and the remaining tooth resist structural failure from occlusal loading (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Sturdevant's restricts *primary* resistance form to walls and floors that withstand forces primarily oriented parallel to the long axis of the tooth (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. **Retention form** is the form that enables the restoration to avoid being dislodged by masticatory loading (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], or that prevents displacement by tipping or lifting forces (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

The two Summitt's chapters sharpen the distinction by force direction: retention resists dislodgment along the path of insertion (tensile forces), resistance resists dislodgment or fracture by oblique or compressive forces (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]; for castings, retention form resists removal parallel to the path of insertion and resistance form resists dislodgment oblique to it (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Note that the Marzouk/Sturdevant definition of resistance is about **fracture** (tooth and restoration), whereas the Summitt cast-gold definition is about **displacement**; a good answer states both meanings.

The pivotal sentence for this question is Sturdevant's: the design of primary retention form is directly related to the retention needs of the anticipated restorative material (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. That is the hinge on which "advanced materials" turn.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Resistance and retention form are steps 2 and 3 of Black's sequence of tooth preparation, originally designed for non-adhesive materials (gold foil, amalgam, cast gold).

- **Resistance form:** the shape and placement of preparation walls and floors that enable both the restoration and the remaining tooth to resist fracture under occlusal load (Marzouk et al, undated; Boushell and Walter, 2019).
- **Retention form:** the shape of the preparation that prevents displacement of the restoration by tipping or lifting forces (Boushell and Walter, 2019).
- In force terms, retention resists dislodgment along the path of insertion (tensile), resistance resists oblique or compressive forces that displace or fracture (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013; Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).
- Both are divided into **primary** (part of the initial preparation) and **secondary** (added after caries removal: grooves, slots, pins, bonding).
- Key principle: retention form required depends on the retention needs of the material (Boushell and Walter, 2019).

## 2. Classical features of resistance form

### Explanation

Sturdevant's lists five principles of primary resistance form: minimal extension (conserving cusps and marginal ridges), relatively horizontal pulpal and gingival floors perpendicular to the long axis, rounded internal line angles to limit stress concentration, adequate restorative-material thickness, and cusp capping when required (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Thickness values are material-specific: amalgam 1.5-2 mm and glass-ceramic 2 mm, whereas polymeric materials such as composite have no minimal thickness (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

Marzouk grounds the rules in mechanics: floors at right angles to the direction of loading (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; among shapes, the inverted truncated cone resists loading better than the box, and the box better than the cone (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Vale's experiment shows progressive loss of resistance to splitting as marginal ridges are crossed, about 10% for one ridge crossed at a quarter of the intercuspal distance (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; and if the restorative material is stronger than tooth, the design lets the restoration support the tooth (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], the logic of cusp capping and onlays.

**Cusp capping: the books disagree on the threshold.**
- Sturdevant's: consider reduction when the outline has extended half the distance from a primary groove to the cusp tip (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; strongly recommended at two-thirds (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].
- Summitt's (amalgam): evaluate the cusps when the faciolingual width exceeds one-third of the intercuspal distance (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC].
The two are measured from different landmarks (groove-to-cusp-tip versus cusp-tip-to-cusp-tip), so they are not numerically interchangeable; both should be quoted with their reference line.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Features of primary resistance form** (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Marzouk et al, undated)

1. **Minimal extension:** conserve cusps and marginal ridges; Vale's experiment shows resistance to splitting falls progressively as marginal ridges are crossed.
2. **Flat floors (box form):** pulpal and gingival floors perpendicular to the long axis; prevents rocking and wedging.
3. **Rounded internal line angles:** reduce stress concentration in tooth and restoration.
4. **Adequate material thickness:** amalgam 1.5-2 mm, glass-ceramic 2 mm; composite has no minimum thickness.
5. **Cusp capping:** reduce and cover weakened cusps. Sturdevant's: consider at half, strongly recommended at two-thirds of the groove-to-cusp-tip distance; Summitt's: evaluate cusps when width exceeds one-third of the intercuspal distance.
6. **Envelopment:** if the material is stronger than tooth (cast metal), the restoration supports the tooth.

## 3. Classical features of retention form

### Explanation

Marzouk divides retention into principal and auxiliary means and insists every preparation have at least one principal means (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Principal means are frictional retention, which depends on four factors (surface area, opposing walls, parallelism, proximity) (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], dentin elasticity, undercuts and dovetails. Auxiliary means include grooves, internal boxes, posts, pins, etching (which in certain situations can be a principal mode) (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and cement, the least effective auxiliary means (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Designing the restoration so occlusal load seats it is an ideal auxiliary form (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

Sturdevant's primary retention per material: amalgam by occlusally converging external walls (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; composite by micromechanical and chemical bonding (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; cast metal by near-parallel walls diverging 2-5 degrees per wall from the line of draw (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], with an occlusal dovetail to prevent tipping (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; preparation geometry remains the most important retentive factor for castings (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

Secondary features: each anatomically distinct area must be independently retentive (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; they are of two types, mechanical features and treatment of the walls with etching, priming and adhesives (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Mechanical ones: retention grooves, coves for incisal retention of Class III amalgam (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], extensions, skirts around line angles in cast gold (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], bevels that add bonding area in composite (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], steps, amalgam pins, slots (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC] and metal pins.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Primary retention** (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Marzouk et al, undated)
- Amalgam: walls converging occlusally; dovetail; dentin elasticity on condensation.
- Cast metal: near-parallel walls diverging 2-5 degrees per wall from the line of draw; occlusal dovetail; geometry is the main retentive factor; cement is the least effective auxiliary means.
- Composite: micromechanical (and chemical) bonding.

**Secondary features (mechanical):** proximal retention grooves, coves (Class III amalgam), slots, amalgapins, pins, steps, internal boxes, facial/lingual groove extensions, skirts and collars (cast), bevels (castings; composite to add enamel bonding area). **Secondary (adhesive):** etching, priming, bonding. Each anatomically distinct part must be independently retentive.

## 4. How each material changes the requirements

### Explanation

Sturdevant's Table 11.1 contrasts amalgam and composite: composite primary retention is "none (roughness/bonding)" (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], and its resistance form is the same as amalgam for large preparations but needs no special form for small to moderate ones (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Most posterior composites need no mechanical retention (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]; preparations must never be extended merely to obtain resistance and retention form (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]; a boxlike form may increase C-factor stresses (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]. In very large Class II composites bonding is the primary retention (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC], with grooves, coves and slots when a crown is to follow (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]; cusp coverage with composite is usually indicated beyond two-thirds of groove-to-cusp distance (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC]. Unsupported but non-friable enamel may be left in low-stress anterior areas under bonded composite (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's agrees that such enamel is reinforced by bonding to its internal surface (Summitt, 2013) [SRC].

**RMGI/GIC:** mechanical retentive features are not typically required because of the chemical bond to the mineral phase (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; RMGI is excellent as a base under amalgam, gold, ceramic and composite (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

**Amalgam (non-bonded):** two resistance considerations, restoration and tooth (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]; in complex amalgams more emphasis on resistance than on retention (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]. Circumferential slots matched four pins in vitro (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]; amalgapins resemble pins in vitro (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]; these flattened-molar tests are not as enlightening as long-term clinical tests (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]. Features should be distributed, not clustered (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]; pins carry crazing, perforation and amalgam-weakening risks (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]. Proximal grooves: in vivo studies do not substantiate their necessity in dovetailed or MOD preparations (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

**Cast gold:** boxes or pins improve both retention and resistance, whereas grooves and slots mostly add resistance (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; grooves and boxes help little on short, wide, over-tapered preparations (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; loss of retention is the main technical failure (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; skirts virtually eliminate postrestorative tooth fracture (Sturdevant and Sulaiman, 2019) [SRC]. Bonded castings: long-term effectiveness not yet known (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

**Ceramic and indirect composite inlays/onlays:** divergent, relatively nonretentive preparations (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; resistance with rounded boxes, but no grooves (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; retention and resistance come primarily from adhesion (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; at least 1.5 mm central fossa and 2.0 mm over functional cusps (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC]; bevels avoided (Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013) [SRC], consistent with Sturdevant's 90-degree margin for low edge strength (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Adhesive bonding of etchable glass-ceramics increases their resistance to fracture (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC].

## 5. Critical review: what adhesion has and has not changed

### Explanation

Three points need a critical view.

**(a) Retention is largely replaced, resistance is not.** Sturdevant's states that bonding of intracoronal restorations enhances retention but does not increase the resistance form of the remaining tooth over the long term (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Hence large composite preparations still use flat floors and cusp coverage (Ahmed et al, 2019) [SRC].

**(b) Bonded amalgam: the books disagree.** Sturdevant's: in vitro research suggests adhesives may enhance resistance and retention (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC], but no clinical improvement has been demonstrated (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC] and the book does not promote bonded amalgams (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]; the perceived benefits have not been substantiated (Ahmed, Boushell and Wilder, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: there is more than adequate evidence that properly bonded amalgams are as successful as pin-retained ones (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]; in a 6-year clinical comparison none of the bonded restorations had debonded (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]; pins and bonding are additive in vitro (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC]. The two are not strictly contradictory: Summitt's claims bonding is *as good as* pins (equivalence), Sturdevant's says it is *not better than* routine amalgam technique (no superiority). Neither shows bonding beats mechanical form.

**(c) Most of the evidence for mechanical features is laboratory.** Slot and amalgapin equivalence to pins is in vitro (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) [SRC], and the one in vivo statement on grooves says they are unnecessary in dovetailed and MOD preparations (Boushell and Walter, 2019) [SRC]. Laboratory equivalence is not clinical equivalence.

Not from these sources: that preparation shape still governs stress distribution in bonded restorations and that bond degradation over time argues for keeping some macro-mechanical form in large restorations [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Critical appraisal**

1. **Retention has been largely replaced by adhesion; resistance has not.** Composite, GIC and bonded ceramic need little or no mechanical retention, so preparations are now defect-driven, not geometry-driven (Ahmed et al, 2019; Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013). But bonding intracoronal restorations does not increase the long-term resistance of the remaining tooth (Boushell and Walter, 2019); weak cusps still need coverage and large preparations still need flat floors.
2. **Classical form can harm adhesive restorations:** a boxlike form increases C-factor stress in composite; grooves and bevels are contraindicated in ceramics (stress raisers, chipping); extension "to get retention" weakens the tooth (Ahmed et al, 2019; Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013).
3. **Bonded amalgam is disputed.** Sturdevant's: in vitro benefit only, no clinical improvement, not promoted. Summitt's: clinically as successful as pin-retained restorations at 6 years, none debonded; pins plus bonding additive (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013). The claims differ: equivalence to pins versus no superiority to routine technique.
4. **Evidence base is weak:** pins, slots and amalgapins are compared mainly in flattened-molar laboratory tests; in vivo data do not support routine proximal grooves in dovetailed or MOD amalgams (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
5. **Castings remain geometry-dependent:** taper, wall height and boxes are the main retention; adhesive cements help, but long-term data on bonded castings are lacking (Fan, Stevenson and Berry, 2013).

**Conclusion.** Black's concept survives as a mechanical principle but is now applied selectively: retention form is material-dependent and often replaced by adhesion, while resistance form of the tooth (cusp protection, conservation of marginal ridges, adequate bulk) remains essential for every material.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: Adhesion allows mechanical retention and resistance form to be abandoned.**
- Textbook says: retention for composite and bonded ceramic comes from bonding (Boushell and Walter, 2019; Fasbinder, Neiva and Robbins, 2013), but bonding does not increase the long-term resistance of the remaining tooth (Boushell and Walter, 2019).
- Other evidence: in amalgam, bonded and pin-retained complex restorations performed similarly over 6 years (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013), whereas Sturdevant's finds no clinical gain over routine technique.
- Agreement/challenge: the books agree on retention, disagree on whether bonding can substitute for amalgam resistance features.
- Strength: moderate for composite retention; low (small clinical series, mainly in vitro) for bonded amalgam resistance.
- Conclusion: retention form, yes, largely; resistance form of the tooth, no. [Likely]
- Clinical implication: in large restorations of any material, still protect weak cusps (cover or envelop) rather than relying on adhesive alone.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Resistance = tooth and restoration withstand load (oblique/compressive); retention = restoration not dislodged (tensile, along path of insertion).
2. Five resistance principles: minimal extension, flat floors, rounded angles, adequate thickness, cusp capping.
3. Retention need depends on the material (Sturdevant's).
4. Cast: 2-5° per wall; geometry is the main retention; cement least effective.
5. Composite: no mechanical retention in small/moderate; box form raises C-factor.
6. Ceramic: divergent, rounded boxes, no grooves, no bevels, 1.5-2 mm thickness.
7. Bonding enhances retention but not long-term tooth resistance.
8. Bonded amalgam: Sturdevant's against, Summitt's for (as good as pins).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Defining resistance form as resistance to displacement only; it is primarily resistance to fracture of tooth and restoration.
2. Saying adhesive materials need no resistance form; large bonded restorations still need cusp protection.
3. Giving amalgam and inlay walls the same orientation (converging versus diverging).
4. Recommending grooves or bevels in ceramic inlays.
5. Quoting one cusp-capping number without saying what distance it is measured on.
