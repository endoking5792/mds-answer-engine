## STUDY MODE

### S1. What "bioactive" means here [basic science]
Glass ionomer is described as a natural bioactive material because it releases fluoride, sodium and phosphate ions, more in acid, and takes up calcium and phosphate from saliva, hardening its surface as it matures [SRC:G28]. Mount says that before GIC all restoratives were biologically inert and the profession's interest in bioactivity was small [SRC:A16]. Ion exchange needs water, and GIC is water-based, which is why it suits remineralisation [SRC:A463]. A DCNA review says most commercial ion-releasing restoratives are glass-ionomer type (with more resin and acid-reactive fillers), on the market from 2013, with behaviour over time unclear and no clinical evidence at the time [SRC:E325].

### S2. Newer ion-releasing materials [classification]
Alkasite (Cention N), bioactive resins (ACTIVA type) and giomers release ions from resin matrices (see Q13). In primary molars a bioactive resin performed comparably to compomer or composite for up to 2 years in three RCTs, but the review cannot show a caries-preventive advantage [LIT:Popa2026]; in vitro it had a lower bond strength to dentine than a resin-modified GIC [LIT:AlHasan2022]; a 24-month RCT of 22 patients found it similar to bulk-fill composite [LIT:Raghip2025].

### S3. Glass hybrid systems [treatment relevance]
A glass hybrid (EQUIA-type) combines a high-viscosity GIC with a nano-filled resin coat. In a 5-year RCT of moderate to large two-surface Class II cavities, survival was 94.5% for the glass hybrid and 94.4% for nano-hybrid composite, while success was lower for glass hybrid (81.9% against 90.7%) [LIT:Miletic2024].

### S4. What the pooled evidence says [prognosis]
A meta-analysis of 40 RCTs (permanent teeth, at least 12 months) found bioactive materials reduced secondary caries risk by 45% (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.46-0.65), but the benefit came from GICs (RR 0.36) and not giomers (RR 1.09), and the pooled comparator mixed amalgam and composite, so the effect may reflect the amalgam comparison [LIT:Zailai2026] [LIT:Ge2023].

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
A bioactive restorative releases ions that interact with tooth and plaque (remineralisation, buffering, antibacterial action); glass ionomer is the original bioactive restorative [SRC:G28] [SRC:A463].

### 2. Glass ionomer as the bioactive standard
- Releases fluoride, sodium and phosphate; takes up calcium and phosphate from saliva [SRC:G28].
- Strontium enters partly demineralised dentine [SRC:A462].
- Ion exchange needs water; GIC is water-based [SRC:A463].

### 3. Ion-releasing alternatives
| Material | Matrix | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Alkasite (Cention N) | resin with alkaline glass | hydroxide, calcium, fluoride; comparable to composite [LIT:Bonchev2025] [LIT:Laporte2026] |
| Bioactive resin (ACTIVA) | resin | comparable to composite or compomer in primary molars to 2 years [LIT:Popa2026] [LIT:Raghip2025] |
| Giomer | resin with pre-reacted glass | no secondary caries benefit in the meta-analysis [LIT:Zailai2026] |
| Compomer | resin | not bioactive in the GIC sense [SRC:A209] |
A DCNA review calls most such products GI-type and says clinical evidence was lacking [SRC:E325].

### 4. Glass hybrid (EQUIA-type) system
High-viscosity GIC restoration plus a resin coat; 5-year survival 94.5% against 94.4% for nano-hybrid composite in moderate two-surface Class II cavities; success 81.9% against 90.7% [LIT:Miletic2024].

### 5. Evidence on secondary caries
- Bioactive materials overall: RR 0.55 against conventional restorations; GIC RR 0.36; giomer RR 1.09 [LIT:Zailai2026].
- GIC against amalgam: lower secondary caries; against composite: no significant difference [LIT:Ge2023].

### 6. Limitations
Short follow-up; remineralisation mainly laboratory evidence; bioactive resins have lower bond strength to dentine than RMGIC in vitro [LIT:AlHasan2022]; term "bioactive" loosely used [SRC:E325].

### 6A. Telling a true glass ionomer from a look-alike
The critical difference is that glass ionomers and resin-modified glass ionomers set in the dark in a clinically acceptable time through the acid-base reaction, which compomers do not; Mount offers a dark-set test on a glass slab under a light-proof cover [SRC:A210] [SRC:A211]. A compomer cannot bond by ion exchange and has no remineralising ion exchange [SRC:A209].

### 6B. When to choose which
GIC or glass hybrid for high caries risk, moisture problems, root and cervical lesions and primary teeth [SRC:C1118] [SRC:G11]; composite where strength and polish matter [SRC:A490]; ion-releasing resins as an option in moderate cavities pending long-term evidence [LIT:Laporte2026].

### 7. Conclusion
GIC remains the benchmark bioactive restorative; glass hybrid systems extend its use to moderate Class II; resin-based ion-releasing materials look promising but are not proven to prevent caries.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- GIC = natural bioactive material (fluoride, sodium, phosphate release; calcium and phosphate uptake) [SRC:G28].
- Glass hybrid: survival 94.5% against 94.4% for composite over 5 years in moderate Class II [LIT:Miletic2024].
- Bioactive materials RR 0.55 for secondary caries, driven by GIC (0.36), not giomer [LIT:Zailai2026].
- Resin-based ion-releasing materials: short-term only [SRC:E325].
