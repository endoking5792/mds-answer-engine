## STUDY MODE

### S1. Where the fluoride comes from [basic science]
Fluoride is used as a flux in making the glass and is held in the glass as extremely fine droplets; some is released from the powder, and considerable amounts after mixing with the polyacid [SRC:A127]. It is not part of the matrix, so release does not damage properties [SRC:A130]. Ions released early lie in the matrix but are free to migrate, depending on concentration gradient and pH, with an initial washout phase [SRC:B322].

### S2. How release and recharge work [mechanism]
Fluoride is about the same size and mobility as hydroxyl, so exchange depends on the fluoride gradient; uptake during high-fluoride exposure turns the cement into a reservoir [SRC:A139]. Exposure to topical fluoride, rinses or toothpaste temporarily reverses the gradient so fluoride diffuses in, then diffuses out again [SRC:C1219]. Recharge depends on the agent, its concentration and frequency; gels recharge more than low-fluoride toothpastes, and the raised release is short-lived, so recharging is daily [SRC:C144]. A two-part recharge material exists in which a charge gel replenishes the restoration by ion exchange [SRC:G26].

### S3. How fluoride acts on tooth and plaque [treatment relevance]
Fluoride replaces hydroxyl in apatite making it acid-resistant, catalyses calcium and phosphate uptake, and enters acidogenic bacteria where it slows enzyme activity and acid production [SRC:B27] [SRC:B170]. Concentrations of 0.03-0.08 ppm shift the balance from net demineralisation to net remineralisation [SRC:C159]. Summitt says enhancing remineralisation is probably the most important anticaries mechanism [SRC:C150]; a direct bactericidal effect is unlikely because the released levels are far lower than bactericidal ones, and the effect is very local, within about 1 mm of the margin [SRC:C146].

### S4. The critical point [prognosis]
Sturdevant notes that discharging occurs as fast as recharging, so recharge is unlikely to improve secondary caries prevention much compared with other materials [SRC:C1219]. McCabe adds that the therapeutic fluoride level required is unknown [SRC:B27]. Laboratory release is highest in GIC [LIT:Justen2024], but the RCT meta-analysis found less secondary caries than amalgam and not than composite [LIT:Ge2023].

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement releases fluoride for years and can take it up again from the environment, acting as a rechargeable reservoir [SRC:G23] [SRC:A142].

### 2. Source of fluoride
Fluoride used as a flux in the glass is incorporated as very fine droplets; considerable release follows mixing with the polyacid [SRC:A127]. It is not part of the matrix [SRC:A130].

### 3. Release pattern
Initial burst, falling over 2-3 months to a low steady level monitored for at least 5 years [SRC:A128] [SRC:A129]. More is released at low pH [SRC:C140].

### 4. Mechanism
- Release is by diffusion and ion exchange down a concentration gradient [SRC:A139] [SRC:B322].
- Fluoride is about the size of hydroxyl, so exchange is easy [SRC:A139].
- Surface washout in the early phase, then slow release from the bulk [SRC:B322] [SRC:C144].

### 5. Recharge
- High external fluoride (toothpaste, gel, rinse, varnish) reverses the gradient; fluoride enters the cement and later leaves it [SRC:C1219] [SRC:A144].
- Gels recharge more than low-fluoride toothpastes; the effect is short-lived, so recharge daily [SRC:C144].
- GIC and resin-modified GIC recharge most; composites least [SRC:C143].
- Fluoride-rich variants (Fuji VII, Fuji Triage) release more [SRC:B197] [SRC:E264].

### 6. Anticaries mechanisms
| Mechanism | Detail |
|---|---|
| Fluorapatite | F replaces OH, acid-resistant crystals [SRC:B27] [SRC:B169] |
| Remineralisation | 0.03-0.08 ppm shifts balance; probably the most important effect [SRC:C159] [SRC:C150] |
| Bacterial effect | F inhibits acid-producing enzymes; almost complete S. mutans inhibition on the cement surface [SRC:B170] [SRC:A132] |
| Local action | within about 1 mm of the margin [SRC:C146] |

### 7. Factors affecting release
Powder:liquid ratio, mixing, temperature, solubility, finishing, storage medium; fluoride release is higher in more open (weaker) materials [SRC:G26].

### 8. Critical appraisal
Laboratory release is highest for GIC [LIT:Justen2024]. Clinically, secondary caries are lower than with amalgam but not than composite [LIT:Ge2023]; discharge is as fast as recharge [SRC:C1219]; the therapeutic level is unknown [SRC:B27].

### 8A. Clinical advice
Advise high-risk patients on daily neutral fluoride toothpaste or gel to recharge GIC restorations; acidulated phosphate fluoride products (pH about 2-3) can erode GIC and are avoided [SRC:A144] [SRC:A145].

### 9. Conclusion
GIC is a fluoride reservoir that releases and recharges by ion exchange; the mechanism is clear in the laboratory, but the clinical benefit over composite is not proven.
