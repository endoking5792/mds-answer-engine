# Enumerate and classify various Pulpal diseases. Write in detail about Ir-Reversible Pulpitis.

## Introduction

Pulp disease arises from physical, chemical and bacterial causes (Gopikrishna, 2021). Clinical symptoms do not correlate with histopathology, so the working classifications are clinical and exist to guide treatment (Gopikrishna, 2021; Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

## Pulpal diseases and classifications

| Classification | Categories |
|---|---|
| Clinical, AAE (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) | Normal pulp; reversible pulpitis; symptomatic and asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis; pulp necrosis; previously treated; previously initiated therapy |
| Grossman (Gopikrishna, 2021) | Inflammatory (reversible, irreversible, chronic hyperplastic pulpitis, internal resorption); degeneration (calcific, fibrous); necrosis |
| WHO (Sikri, 2019) | Pulpitis (initial hyperaemia, acute, chronic ulcerative or hyperplastic); necrosis; degeneration; secondary dentine |
| Histological (AAE, 2012) | Pulpitis described as acute, chronic or hyperplastic |

No classification is definitive; those in use rest on duration and severity, symptoms, or ability to heal (Sikri, 2019).

## Irreversible pulpitis

**Definition:** a persistent inflammatory condition of the pulp, symptomatic or asymptomatic, in which the pulp is incapable of healing (Gopikrishna, 2021); the AAE term is a clinical diagnosis of a vital inflamed pulp incapable of healing (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**Aetiology:** most commonly bacterial invasion through caries; chemical, thermal and mechanical factors may also cause it (Gopikrishna, 2021). It usually follows reversible pulpitis; bacteria enter through dentinal tubules, periodontal disease or anachoresis (Sikri, 2019). In a symptomatic anterior tooth without obvious cause, ask about general anaesthesia, intubation and orthodontic treatment (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**Pathogenesis and histology:** the pulp lacks collateral circulation and lies within rigid dentinal walls (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). A focus of necrosis marks the transition from reversible to irreversible inflammation (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019). Necrotic areas attract neutrophils; their lysosomal enzymes form pus, and microabscesses are walled off by fibrous tissue. If the pulp ulcerates and drains, pressure and pain fall (Gopikrishna, 2021). The remaining pulp has mild or no inflammation and still responds to testing (Sedgley et al., 2021). Prostaglandins, neuropeptides, bradykinin and cytokines are linked to pain (Sedgley et al., 2021), sensitising nociceptors to produce spontaneous or lingering pain (Ruparel and Khan, 2019).

**Types and clinical features:**

| | Symptomatic | Asymptomatic |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Spontaneous or intermittent; lingers minutes to hours after thermal stimulus; later throbbing, nocturnal, postural, referred (Gopikrishna, 2021) | None, even with deep caries (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) |
| Thermal | Cold provokes; heat worsens; cold may relieve (Gopikrishna, 2021) | Little response |
| Course | Untreated, becomes necrotic | May turn symptomatic or necrotic; treat early (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) |

Sikri (2019) equates acute with symptomatic and chronic with asymptomatic; chronic pain is milder and intermittent.

**Diagnosis:** deep caries or restoration, a lingering response to cold or heat, and a poorly localised pain; heat applied to each tooth helps localise it (Gopikrishna, 2021). The electric pulp test needs less current early and more later (Gopikrishna, 2021); sensibility tests are false in 10% to 16% (Bogen, Dammaschke and Chandler, 2021). Percussion is negative (Gopikrishna, 2021), though Sikri (2019) allows tenderness from periapical spread or a crack, and Berman and Rotstein (2021) a thickened ligament in advanced disease.

**Differential diagnosis:** reversible pulpitis, where pain stops when the stimulus is removed (Gopikrishna, 2021); acute apical abscess, with swelling, percussion tenderness, mobility, no vitality response and fever (Gopikrishna, 2021); cracked tooth (Sikri, 2019).

**Management:**

| Author | Approach |
|---|---|
| Gopikrishna (2021) | Pulpectomy; coronal pulpotomy as emergency in posterior teeth; extraction if unrestorable |
| Walton and Ruparel (2021) | Complete cleaning and shaping preferred; if time is short, pulpotomy or partial pulpectomy of the largest canal, over 90% reducing pain; partial pulpectomy of severely inflamed teeth discouraged |
| Bogen, Dammaschke and Chandler (2021) | Normally pulpectomy; calcium silicate pulpotomy a minimally invasive alternative |

Bogen and Chandler (2019) cite a trial of calcium-enriched mixture pulpotomy in molars with similar outcomes to root canal treatment. A dry cotton pellet relieves pain as well as medicaments, and supplemental periodontal, intrapulpal or intraosseous anaesthesia may be needed (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). Antibiotics are not recommended (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021).

**Prognosis:** favourable with proper endodontic treatment and restoration (Gopikrishna, 2021). Untreated, the pulp becomes necrotic (Berman and Rotstein, 2021), silently in about 40% (Sedgley et al., 2021).

## Conclusion

Irreversible pulpitis is a clinical diagnosis of a vital, inflamed pulp that cannot recover; treatment is pulp removal, with pulpotomy an emerging alternative.
