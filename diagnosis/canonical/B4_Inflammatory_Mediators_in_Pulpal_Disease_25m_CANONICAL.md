## 1. Introduction: why mediators matter more in the pulp

### Explanation

The pulp makes most known host mediators. Their purpose is to fight the irritant, but while doing so "the host factors may contribute to the injury of the pulp and escalate the ultimate demise of this tissue"; unlike other connective tissues "the pulp is enclosed in a noncompliant environment and has minimal collateral circulation" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Harty's makes the same point: "the rigid outer covering of hard mineralized tissue makes this a particularly challenging environment", and "the constraints on the pulp tissue as it swells during the inflammatory response causes significant pain to the patient" (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's defines the problem physically: inflammation "takes place in a low-compliance environment composed of rigid dentinal walls", compliance being C = ΔV/ΔP, so a small rise in volume gives a large rise in pressure (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's undergraduate account says the same: the pulp is "encased in the rigid hard tissues with virtually no space for expansion" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Fouad and Khan sum up that pulpitis and apical periodontitis "are mediated by a wide variety of cytokines, chemokines, neuropeptides, proteases" and other molecules (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC].

So the central idea of the essay: the same mediators that protect the pulp also destroy it, and the rigid low-compliance chamber tips the balance toward destruction [Certain].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Pulpitis is an inflammation of a loose connective tissue enclosed in a noncompliant dentine chamber with minimal collateral circulation (Fouad, 2012). Inflammatory mediators are molecules released by resident pulp cells, nerves, immune cells and plasma that start, amplify, modulate and resolve the response. They aim to eliminate bacteria, but in a low-compliance space (C = ΔV/ΔP) the oedema they cause raises tissue pressure and pain (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021; Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017), and the host response itself can "escalate the ultimate demise" of the pulp (Fouad, 2012).

## 2. Sources of mediators: who releases what

### Explanation

**Odontoblasts.** They are the first cells bacteria meet, so "odontoblasts should be considered members of the defense response cells in the dental pulp", sensing pathogens with toll-like receptors, making cytokines and recruiting dendritic cells (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Harty's says TLRs 1 to 6 and 9 "have been demonstrated in odontoblasts and pulp-derived cells"; once infection is detected, odontoblasts first release antimicrobial peptides, then "they will release potent signalling molecules including cytokines and chemokines, which activate the immune response" (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's: "proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and antimicrobial peptides are elaborated by the odontoblast" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. "Activated odontoblasts release molecules such as nitric oxide (NO), beta defensins (BD)", lipopolysaccharide binding protein, IL-6, CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL8, CCL2 and IL-10 (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC].

**Fibroblasts and other resident cells.** Harty's lists cells "including pulp fibroblasts, dental pulp stem cells, neurons", resident immune cells and endothelial cells and concludes "all these cell types should be regarded as being immunocompetent" (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Fibroblasts express TLR-2 strongly, so "fibroblasts also contribute to innate immunologic surveillance of the dental pulp" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]; "once activated they release mediators such as TNFα" and CXCL8 (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]; "dental pulp fibroblasts were also found to express SP" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. IL-1 is "produced mainly by monocytes/macrophages but may also be produced by polymorphonuclear neutrophils, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

**Immune cells.** Macrophages "are capable of producing a large variety of soluble factors, including interleukin (IL) 1, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), growth factors, and other cytokines"; dendritic cells sit at the pulp periphery and "migrate centrally in the pulp after antigenic challenge"; "mast cells are seldom found in the normal pulp tissue, although they are routinely found in chronically inflamed pulps" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. In the rat pulp "dendritic cells were much more important than macrophages in providing the antigen presentation signals" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

**Nerves.** Neuropeptides "reside almost exclusively under normal conditions within the nerve endings of afferent nerves", and pulpal SP levels are "the highest in the body outside the central nervous system" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

**Plasma.** Kinins, complement and clotting factors arrive by exudation (section 4).

**Triggers.** LPS acts through TLR-4 and peptidoglycan and lipoteichoic acid through TLR-2 (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]; "LPS is a potent mediator of bone resorption", but "LPS is by no means the only bacterial irritant involved in the pathogenesis" (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. In carious dentin "the LPS content correlated with the incidence of pain" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. TGF-β1 "inhibits the expression of TLR2 and 4 by odontoblasts" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC], a built-in brake.

**Innate first.** Pulpal degeneration after bacterial exposure "are generally mediated by innate immunity" (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]: cytokine production "could occur without the presence of adaptive immunity" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC], and pulp necrosis "fundamentally rely on the innate rather than the adaptive immune response" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Cellular sources.**
- **Odontoblasts:** first line of defence; sense bacteria by TLRs (TLR-2, TLR-4) and release antimicrobial peptides (β-defensins), NO, IL-6, IL-8 (CXCL8), CCL2 and IL-10 (Fouad, 2012; Fouad and Khan, 2020).
- **Fibroblasts, stem cells, endothelial cells:** immunocompetent; release TNF-α, IL-8, IL-1 and even substance P (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017; Fouad, 2012).
- **Immune cells:** macrophages (IL-1, TNF, growth factors), dendritic cells (antigen presentation), neutrophils (enzymes, free radicals), mast cells (histamine; seen in chronically inflamed pulps), lymphocytes and plasma cells (cytokines, antibodies) (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021).
- **Nerves:** sensory fibres store SP, CGRP and neurokinin A; sympathetic fibres store NPY (Fouad, 2012).
- **Plasma:** kinins, complement, clotting factors.

Bacterial LPS (TLR-4) and lipoteichoic acid (TLR-2) trigger the cascade; pulpal breakdown is driven mainly by innate immunity (Fouad and Khan, 2020).

## 3. The sequence: from irritation to vascular and cellular response

### Explanation

The pulp "responds to advancing irritation in a compartmentalized manner rather than as an entire organ", the zone nearest the irritant being most affected (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Acute inflammation can raise blood flow "up to nearly 200% of control flow", followed by increased permeability (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. As flow slows, "the leukocytes assume a more peripheral position in the vessel (margination)", roll on selectins and adhere via integrins and ICAM-1; after crown preparation or adhesives "a large number of vessels located in the subodontoblastic layer reacted strongly" for E- and P-selectin (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Chemokines and released dentine-matrix molecules create "intense signalling gradients, which lead to immune cell recruitment at sites of infection" (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC].

Which cells arrive first depends on the insult, and the books describe different situations. Fouad and Khan say neutrophils "are among the earliest cells, which are recruited followed by monocytes" (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC], and Sikri's classical account says "the polymorphonuclear neutrophils migrate first, followed by the monocytes and lymphocytes" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's, writing about caries, says "the early inflammatory response to caries is characterized by the focal accumulation of chronic inflammatory cells" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. These are compatible if acute exposure brings neutrophils first while slow caries brings a chronic infiltrate first [Likely]. Harty's says neutrophils and macrophages dominate "especially during the early and acute phase of inflammation", with the adaptive response "becoming increasingly prominent as the caries extends more extensively into the pulp" (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sequence.** Bacterial products → TLR activation → cytokines/chemokines → vasodilatation (flow up to about 200%) and permeability (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) → margination, selectin rolling, integrin/ICAM-1 adhesion → chemotaxis along IL-8, C5a and LTB4 gradients (Fouad, 2012) → phagocytes release enzymes and free radicals. The response is compartmentalized: the zone under the irritant is most inflamed (Fouad, 2012).

## 4. Key mediators: sources and effects

### Explanation

**Vasoactive amines.** Fouad groups "the following vasoactive amines: histamine, serotonin, endothelin, and neuropeptides" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]; strictly, endothelin and neuropeptides are peptides, not amines [Certain]. Histamine "is prevalent in connective tissue around blood vessels and in mast cells, basophils, and platelets" and "is a potent vasodilator and a mediator of vascular permeability"; "thermal injury of the pulp resulted in a fourfold increase"; on its own it hardly stimulates pulpal nerves, so "the pulp tissue must be sensitized by other inflammatory mediators, such as prostaglandins, for histamine to evoke a painful response" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Ikeda and Suda: histamine "increases pulpal blood flow to a moderate extent but produces a profound increase in plasma" extravasation (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]. Sikri adds the classical mechanism, contraction of venular endothelial cells (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Serotonin: the books disagree on its vascular effect. Fouad says it "generally causes vasoconstriction", comes from "serotoninergic nerve terminals, endothelial cells, and platelets", yet also reports that it "caused significant increase in pulpal blood flow in dog canines" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Fristad and Berggreen say it "is released primarily from the platelets, and given intraarterially, it has been shown to reduce PBF" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Both agree on its pain role: it was shown "to sensitize pulpal nerve fibers to various hydrodynamic stimuli" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC] and increases capsaicin-evoked CGRP release in pulps from female patients (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. The direction of the vascular effect probably depends on route and species [Guessing]. Endothelin-1 gave "a dose-dependent reduction of pulpal blood flow" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

**Kinins.** The "nonapeptide bradykinin" is generated by kallikrein from kininogen; "BK has four main proinflammatory actions" (vasodilatation, permeability, pain, leukocyte attraction); "B2 is also the principal BK receptor in the dental pulp"; BK "caused a smaller flow increase but produced more leakage than PGE2"; it interacts with other mediators, "frequently eliciting synergy"; microdialysis showed "a 13-fold increase in BK level in irreversible pulpitis" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Ingle's: "B2 is constitutively present in tissues and is overexpressed in acute pain situations", and "bradykinin can act synergistically with neuropeptides, thrombin, and other factors" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC]. About half its blood-flow "effect is lost in denervated pulp", so it acts partly through nerves (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.13: BK "is a proinflammatory mediator derived from circulating plasma proteins and also causes direct activation of nociceptive neurons, resulting in pain" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.14 calls it "the vasoactive amine bradykinin" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]; this is plainly wrong: bradykinin is a nine-amino-acid peptide (a kinin), as Seltzer's chapter by the same first author states.

**Complement and clotting.** The books give different sizes for the system: "20 different plasma proteins" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC] and "a series of ~45 proteins" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC]; the larger figure presumably counts regulators and receptors [Guessing]. "C3a and C5a, also known as anaphylatoxins, release histamine from mast cells"; but in the pulp "the role of complement activity in pulpal inflammation is not clear at this time" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Thrombin added to pulp fibroblasts "caused a burst in the production of PGE2", and fibrinogen in tubules "may reduce dentin permeability to advancing microbial irritants" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

**Arachidonic acid metabolites.** "It has been known for decades that prostaglandins play a critical role in the pathogenesis of pulpal disease"; LPS-induced pulpitis "resulted in a 9.3-fold increase in PGE2"; "prostaglandins generally cause vasodilation, while thromboxane causes vasoconstriction"; "patients with severe pulpal pain had significantly greater pulpal levels of PGE2" and PGF2α; "COX-2 is an induced proinflammatory enzyme"; "glucocorticoids block the breakdown of membrane phospholipids to arachidonic acid by phospholipases, whereas NSAIDs block the COX pathway" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. PGE2 "increases pulpal blood flow by more than 60% over basal levels" (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]. The older literature agrees: "biosynthetic activity of prostaglandin, especially PGE2, increased in experimentally inflamed dental pulp", and "lipoxygenase products, in addition to cyclooxygenase products of arachidonic acid, are also involved in the development of pulpal inflammation"; this explains the old use of eugenol, since "eugenol, thymol and guaiacol are the potent inhibitors" of cyclooxygenase among phenolic medicaments (Dohi, Okamoto and Tsujimoto, 1990) [SRC]. Lipoxygenase products are "only present in inflammatory cells"; LTB4 "is a potent chemotactic agent and activator of neutrophil functional responses" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. On leukotrienes and pain the books differ: Fouad says "the results of studies on whether leukotrienes reduce pain threshold in the dental pulp have not been conclusive" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC], while Cohen's Table 13.3 lists leukotrienes as sensitizing nociceptors (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC, table read; no locator]. PAF causes "vasodilation that is 100 to 1,000 times more potent than histamine at very low doses" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

**Cytokines.** Inflammation is "a closely regulated balance between the proinflammatory and the anti-inflammatory mediators" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. IL-1 "induces the expression of collagenase from pulp fibroblasts", and "IL-1 was shown to reduce the pain threshold in peripheral tissues, primarily by increasing PGE2 synthesis" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Ingle's names IL-1 and TNF-α as "the main cytokines involved in bone resorption and mediating pulp necrosis and periapical" inflammation (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC]. Paradoxically, mice lacking IL-1 and TNF receptors "had a significantly faster rate of pulpal degeneration", so these cytokines also "have a protective role in the dental pulp against the spread of infection" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

IL-6 is classified differently. Seltzer's says "IL-6 was initially thought to be proinflammatory but is now recognized to be an immunoregulatory and an anti-inflammatory cytokine" and elsewhere that it "has both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory effects" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]; Ingle's lists it among pro-inflammatory cytokines with "IL-6, IL-12, IL-17, and CSFs such as GM-CSF and M-CSF" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC]. Both views are in books with the same first author; IL-6 is best described as dual [Likely]. The brake function belongs to IL-10, IL-4 and IL-13 (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. TGF-β1 "stops spontaneous inflammation mediated by the adaptive immune response" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

**Chemokines.** "Chemokines are a group of ~50 chemoattractant cytokines" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC]. Irreversible pulpitis showed "an almost 23-fold increase in IL-8 levels in the pulp"; odontoblast chemokines "CCL2, CXCL2, and CXCL12 are proangiogenic, whereas CXCL4, CXCL10, and CXCL14 are angiostatic", and under caries "the number of capillaries increases in the pulp under the lesion" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

**Proteases and MMPs.** Neutrophil lysosomal enzymes are antimicrobial, "however, they can also lead to excessive tissue destruction during an inflammatory episode"; "excessive neutrophil accumulation in the pulp may increase the likelihood of tissue necrosis"; α2-macroglobulin rises, "indicating an attempt to control the tissue destruction aspect of these enzymes"; the outcome is "the balance between inflammation and regeneration" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. MMPs "require a zinc ion in their active site"; data conflict: "MMP-2 and MMP-3 were found to be upregulated in one study" but downregulated in another, where "MMP-9 was significantly increased"; MMPs degrading carious dentin are "probably of salivary rather than pulpal origin"; "the balance between activated MMPs and the so-called TIMPs controls the extent of extracellular matrix remodeling" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. "Host MMPs are typically modulated by levels of cytokines and growth factors" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC]. Hyaluronidases, chondroitin sulfatases and MMPs "are examples of the enzymes that can attack components of the interstitium" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's: "the immune cells release degradative enzymes and molecules, such as MMPs and reactive oxygen species (ROS)", which "result in collateral host tissue damage" (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC].

**Nitric oxide.** "iNOS is induced in macrophages and a number of other cells, primarily by cytokines such as IL-1 and TNF"; small endothelial amounts are protective, but "the much larger amounts of NO released by cells in response to cytokines can destroy host tissues"; after cavity preparation NOS activity rose to "a maximum at 4 days postoperatively and declined at 14 days with evidence of necrosis" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. During pulpal inflammation "large amounts of NO are released" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC].

**Reactive oxygen species.** "Oxygen-derived free radicals are potent inflammatory mediators released from neutrophils"; they "combine with NO to form peroxynitrite"; the hydroxyl "radical reduces pulpal blood flow" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]; "in large amounts they cause thrombosis and tissue damage" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC]. On the antioxidant response the books differ: Fouad states CuZnSOD "increased significantly in inflamed pulp" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC], while Ingle's says "several studies have shown an increase of SOD" but "a more recent study showed that there is a reduction of Cu, Zn-SOD in human pulpitis, possibly due to depletion" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC]. Catalase "increases significantly in reversibly inflamed pulp, an increase that is moderated as the inflammation is diagnosed to be irreversible" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC], which fits exhaustion of antioxidant defence as disease becomes irreversible [Likely].

**Antimicrobial peptides and antibodies.** Odontoblasts express human β-defensin 1 and 2 (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]; IL-1, TNF-α and LPS increase HBD2 (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. IgG, IgA or IgM "reduced fluid filtration through dentin in vitro" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC], but in advanced caries "the humoral immune response is accompanied by immunopathologic destruction of pulp tissue" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC].

**Growth factors.** Carious acids "act indirectly by degrading the dentin matrix, thereby liberating bioactive molecules" that drive tertiary dentine (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. TGF-β1 "has been shown to be sufficient and potentially central for the direct signalling of dental hard and soft tissue repair"; VEGF and PDGF, "which are released from the dentine matrix", drive angiogenesis (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. VEGF increases permeability, "estimated to be 50,000 times higher than that of histamine", yet VEGF expression falls and microvessel density drops in irreversible pulpitis (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. VEGF also "promotes chemotaxis and proliferation of human pulp cells" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

## 5. Neurogenic inflammation and pain

### Explanation

Sensory fibres release neuropeptides in response to "numerous stimuli, including tissue injury" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Mechanical stimulation of dentine causes pulpal vasodilatation "presumably by causing the release of neuropeptides from intradental sensory fibers (neurogenic inflammation)" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Under caries or cavity preparation, "sprouting of pulpal nerve fibers was shown to be associated with increased expression of neuropeptides such as SP or CGRP"; severe exposure lowers them, which "may be due to depletion of neuropeptide stores in the nerve endings"; denervation "increased the magnitude of pulpal degeneration following experimental pulpal exposures" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. SP, CGRP and VIP dilate vessels "whereas NPY is a vasoconstrictor"; SP or VIP infusion gave "a brief increase in pulpal blood flow, which was followed by a dramatic and prolonged reduction in pulpal blood flow"; "SP also increased vascular permeability and plasma extravasation"; SP or CGRP made pulp fibroblasts produce "IL-1, IL-6, and TNF" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Cohen's: the net result is "a net increase in tissue pressure that can progress to necrosis in extreme and persistent circumstances" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC].

Neuropeptides are not only harmful: "SP acts as a chemotactic and stimulatory agent for macrophages and T lymphocytes", "CGRP demonstrates immunosuppressive activity", and "SP and CGRP are mitogenic for pulp and odontoblast-like cells" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]; CGRP gave "a twofold increase in the level of expression of bone morphogenetic protein 2" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Ingle's places VIP, NPY and somatostatin in an anti-inflammatory repertoire ("a number of other neuropeptides such as vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)") and says CGRP "may have pro" as well as anti-inflammatory actions (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC].

**Pain.** Painful irreversible pulpitis "contained increased levels of substance P or its receptor, CGRP or its receptor, neurokinin A, and NPY" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]; "the pulpal concentrations of CGRP, SP, and NKA are elevated in painful human teeth" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Mediators cause peripheral sensitization: "firing thresholds may decrease, so that previously non-noxious stimuli may trigger discharges" (allodynia); PGs "sensitize peripheral nociceptors, which increases the algogenic (pain-producing) properties of serotonin and" BK; TNF-α "rapidly sensitizes TRPV1"; TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-8 "are thought to play a role in the neuroplastic changes that occur in nociceptors innervating inflamed tissues, leading to hyperalgesia"; "NGF also seems to play an important role in hyperalgesia"; tetrodotoxin-resistant sodium channels explain why "the tooth may be more difficult to anesthetize" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Bacteria act directly: "LPS sensitizes the ion channel TRPV1 via activation of TLR4 in trigeminal sensory neurons" (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]; nociceptors from carious teeth "express TLR-4 and CD-14" (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri: bradykinin, serotonin and SP sensitize "A-delta fibers and lower their threshold", and mediators are "released in the pulp in direct proportion to the insult" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. "Painful pulpitis is more likely to be associated with nociceptive C fiber activity", and "spontaneous, unprovoked pain generally indicates a pulp that is seriously damaged" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. The lying-down mechanism: "when these patients lie down at the end of the day, their PBF probably increases", raising an already raised tissue pressure (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]; opening the chamber, "drainage of exudate soon produces a reduction in the level of pain" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC].

**Painless pulpitis.** "Not all cases of irreversible pulpitis are associated with pain" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]: "peripheral opioid receptors are present in the dental pulp", and NPY "is thought to act as a modulator of neurogenic inflammation"; painless pulpitis progressing to necrosis "is about 40% to 60% of all pulpitis cases" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Neurogenic inflammation.** Injury makes sensory C and Aδ fibres sprout and release SP, CGRP and neurokinin A (Fouad, 2012). SP and CGRP dilate vessels, SP increases permeability and stimulates IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α from fibroblasts, so tissue pressure rises and can progress to necrosis (Fouad and Levin, 2021). NPY (sympathetic) constricts. CGRP also suppresses immune cells and induces BMP-2, aiding repair.

**Pain.** PGE2, bradykinin, serotonin, TNF-α and NGF sensitize nociceptors (lowered threshold = allodynia, hyperalgesia, spontaneous pain); LPS sensitizes TRPV1 through TLR-4 (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021; Fouad and Khan, 2020). Spontaneous pain signals serious damage; raised pressure on lying down explains nocturnal pain. Opioid receptors and NPY may explain painless pulpitis (40% to 60% of cases) (Fouad and Levin, 2021).

## 6. Progression: reversible pulpitis to irreversible pulpitis to necrosis

### Explanation

The degree of inflammation correlates "with the amount of bacterial irritants and their proximity to the pulp" (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]; immune cell titres "are directly proportional to lesion depth", and cytokines are "upregulated underneath a carious lesion, in a manner that correlates with the depth of caries" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. On when inflammation starts, Harty's says significant inflammatory cells "are not observed in the pulp until the caries has penetrated to within" about 0.5 mm (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC], whereas Cohen's says "a pulp inflammatory response can be seen beneath noncavitated lesions" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's speaks of significant infiltrates, Cohen's of any response [Likely].

**Vascular collapse: books disagree.** Sikri's classical account: "the subsequent increase in tissue pressure causes the venules to collapse" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Seltzer's mediator chapter: "as the inflammatory reaction in the pulp progresses, stasis in pulp vessels eventually ensues" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Ikeda and Suda measured 16.3 mm Hg at the inflamed site against 7.0 mm Hg 1 to 2 mm away, and say such studies "have refuted the concept of pulpal strangulation"; responses are "localized reactions to the release of inflammatory mediators", though "major insults to the pulp ... may lead to widespread increases" (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]. Cohen's agrees, "contradicting the old concept of a wide, generalized collapse of pulpal venules and cessation of blood flow" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Reconciling text: local venule compression explains the biphasic flow seen with BK, because "active arteriolar vasodilation increases interstitial hydrostatic pressure, which in turn could increase flow resistance by compression of venules" (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]. So collapse is local and spreads compartment by compartment, not a sudden whole-pulp strangulation [Likely].

**Endothelial perturbation.** LPS-exposed endothelium "expresses cytokines, chemokines, and thromboxane A2", and reduced perfusion from this "might be the consequence of bacterial infection impairing pulpal defense mechanisms and promoting necrosis" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC].

**Lymphatics: books disagree.** Cohen's: "lack of lymphatic vessel for transport of protein-rich fluid may make the pulp more vulnerable to development of necrosis after severe pulpitis" (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's: "studies have indicated that during inflammation, lymph vessel formation occurs" (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Ikeda and Suda count lymph outflow among the factors limiting pressure (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [read; no locator]. The books are shown side by side, not ranked.

**Tissue destruction to necrosis.** Neutrophil enzymes, MMPs, ROS and large amounts of NO destroy matrix and cells (section 4). Of pulps clinically diagnosed as irreversible pulpitis, 84.4% "displayed areas of coagulation or liquefaction necrosis with bacterial colonization and severe infiltration by leucocytes in parts of the coronal pulp", with milder change elsewhere; Fouad and Khan add "at this point there is inadequate evidence to support that irreversible pulpitis is truly irreversible" (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Progression.**
1. **Reversible pulpitis:** low mediator levels under shallow caries; vasodilatation, mild leakage, focal chronic cells, tertiary dentine. Inflammation increases with bacterial load and proximity (Fouad and Khan, 2020).
2. **Irreversible pulpitis:** bacteria reach the pulp; IL-8, PGE2, bradykinin and neuropeptides rise; local tissue pressure rises (16.3 against 7.0 mm Hg nearby) and compresses venules locally (Ikeda and Suda, 2012). Generalized "strangulation" is refuted by Ikeda and Suda (2012) and Fristad and Berggreen (2021), whereas Sikri (2019) still describes venular collapse; Fouad (2012) says stasis "eventually ensues".
3. **Necrosis:** neutrophil proteases, MMP-9, ROS and excess NO destroy tissue; endothelial perturbation, falling VEGF and limited lymph drainage reduce perfusion (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021). Necrosis spreads compartment by compartment from coronal to apical pulp; 84.4% of "irreversible" pulps show coronal abscess or necrosis (Fouad and Khan, 2020).

## 7. Resolution and repair: the protective side

### Explanation

"The resolution of inflammation is an active instead of a passive process", driven by lipoxins, resolvins, protectins and maresins (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]; "Lipoxins are potent counter-regulatory signals for endogenous" pro-inflammatory mediators (Fouad and Huang, 2019) [SRC], though "although lipoxins have not been reported in the dental" pulp (Fouad, 2012) [SRC]. Harty's: low concentrations of pro-inflammatory mediators "may actually promote regenerative events; however, these molecules may impede repair via inhibitory signaling" at high levels; repair "occurs more successfully in germ-free and inflammation-free animals compared with infected controls"; MTA releases "relatively low levels of cytokines, including IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6 and IL-8"; future therapy may target "inflammation regulation via modulation of the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway" (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Harty's also admits that although studies report "a range of immune cells and inflammatory mediators in carious pulpal tissue", their functional relationships are poorly understood (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Seltzer's concludes that many mediators "may act both in propagation of disease and healing of diseased tissue" (Fouad, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Resolution and clinical relevance.** Resolution is active: lipoxins, resolvins and IL-10 stop neutrophil traffic (Fouad, 2012). Low-grade inflammation promotes repair and dentine bridging, high-grade inflammation inhibits it (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017). Clinically: NSAIDs (COX) and corticosteroids (phospholipase) cut PGs and pain (Fouad, 2012); eugenol inhibits cyclooxygenase (Dohi, Okamoto and Tsujimoto, 1990); calcium silicates release low cytokine levels that favour repair (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017).

**Conclusion.** Pulpal disease is driven less by bacteria directly than by the host mediators they trigger. In the low-compliance pulp, the balance between pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving mediators decides whether the pulp repairs or becomes necrotic (Fouad, 2012).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: mediator levels mark the change from reversible to irreversible pulpitis.** Seltzer's reports 13-fold BK and 23-fold IL-8 rises in irreversible pulpitis and higher PGE2 in painful pulps (Fouad, 2012), and Cohen's links several such mediators to painful pulpitis (Fouad and Levin, 2021). Against this: these are cross-sectional comparisons of extracted pulps, painless pulpitis is 40% to 60% of cases (Fouad and Levin, 2021), MMP findings conflict (Fouad, 2012), and Fouad and Khan (2020) state there is inadequate evidence that irreversible pulpitis is truly irreversible. Conclusion: mediators rise with severity, but no single mediator or threshold yet defines irreversibility in a patient [Likely]; clinically this supports conservative vital pulp therapy where symptoms suggest localized coronal inflammation [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Low-compliance pulp: small volume rise gives large pressure rise (C = ΔV/ΔP).
- Odontoblasts are immune cells: TLRs, cytokines, chemokines, β-defensins, NO.
- Bradykinin is a kinin (nonapeptide), not an amine; B2 receptor; 13-fold up in irreversible pulpitis.
- PGE2 sensitizes nociceptors; NSAIDs block COX, steroids block phospholipase.
- SP/CGRP: neurogenic inflammation, sprouting, pain; CGRP also aids repair (BMP-2).
- IL-1/TNF-α drive necrosis yet protect against spread of infection.
- Pulpal "strangulation" is refuted; pressure rise is local.
- Painless pulpitis: 40% to 60%; opioid receptors, NPY.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling bradykinin a vasoactive amine (Cohen's Ch.14 does; it is a kinin peptide).
- Writing that raised pressure strangles the whole pulp at once; Ikeda and Suda (2012) show the rise is local.
- Treating all mediators as harmful: IL-1/TNF receptor-deficient mice degenerate faster; CGRP and TGF-β1 aid repair.
- Leaving out the pulp's own cells (odontoblasts, fibroblasts) as mediator sources.
- Stating that mediator levels reliably separate reversible from irreversible pulpitis.
