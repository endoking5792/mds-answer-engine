## 1. Introduction: why the pulp is vulnerable

### Explanation

The pulp is the formative organ of the tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Its resistance varies so much that Grossman's quotes the remark that some pulps die if looked at crossly while others "can't be killed with an axe" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Three anatomical features shape its response: it lacks collateral circulation (Sikri, 2019; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC], it is enclosed in unyielding dentine so that there is little or no room for swelling (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and it can wall itself off with reparative dentine (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri adds that a rise of pressure in one area does not affect other areas of the pulp, which explains why inflammation and necrosis can be local (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** The pulp is the formative organ of the tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021). Its diseases follow from three features: enclosure in rigid dentine with no room for swelling, lack of collateral circulation, and the ability to lay down reparative dentine (Sikri, 2019; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). Inflammation therefore tends to stay local, but once the circulation fails the pulp necroses.

## 2. Aetiology: how the books group the causes

### Explanation

Grossman's groups causes as physical (mechanical, thermal, electrical), chemical and bacterial (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri keeps the same three groups but adds lasers and restorations under mechanical causes (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's 6th edition divides irritants into nonliving (mechanical, thermal, chemical) and living (microbial) (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's groups them as microbial, chemical and mechanical and stresses that they often act in synchrony (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. None of these books has a separate "iatrogenic" or "idiopathic" heading: iatrogenic injury sits under mechanical, thermal and chemical causes, and "idiopathic" appears only for internal resorption and unexplained necrosis (see section 3). Grouping them as separate headings is the answer's own arrangement [Certain].

**Classification of causes (side by side)**

| Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021) | Torabinejad's (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) | Harty's (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) |
|---|---|---|
| Physical: mechanical, thermal, electrical | Nonliving: mechanical, thermal, chemical | Mechanical |
| Chemical | | Chemical |
| Bacterial | Living: microbial | Microbial |

Sikri adds lasers and restorations under mechanical causes (Sikri, 2019). For clarity I group them as microbial, traumatic and mechanical, thermal, electrical, chemical, iatrogenic and idiopathic; Harty's notes these often act together.

## 3. Aetiology in detail

### Explanation

**Microbial.** Bacteria are the most common cause of pulp injury (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], the most significant cause of inflammation (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC] and the most common injurious challenge (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Miller suggested bacteria as a cause in 1894 (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Kakehashi's exposed pulps of germ-free rats formed dentine bridges while conventional rats developed necrosis and abscesses (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Routes: caries, accidental exposure, developmental grooves, leakage around restorations, the gingiva and the blood (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; dens invaginatus and evaginatus (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; coronal (most common), lateral or apical routes (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. Anachoresis explains necrosis with no apparent cause (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Bacteria need not reach the pulp: their by-products in dentine are enough (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Named species include Porphyromonas gingivalis, P. endodontalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Once bacteria invade the pulp, damage is almost always irreparable (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The books disagree on how close caries must come: Torabinejad's says even enamel caries attract inflammatory cells (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri says bacteria must be within 1.0 mm (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's says significant inflammation is not seen until caries is within about 0.5 mm (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]; Cohen's says the reaction becomes increasingly severe when RDT falls below 0.5 mm (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. These describe different end points (any inflammatory cells versus significant inflammation) rather than direct contradictions [Likely]. Outcome depends on microbial virulence, intrapulpal pressure, host resistance, circulation and lymphatic drainage (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's notes that falling caries rates have raised the share of other causes (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC].

**Traumatic and mechanical.** Trauma injures the pulp less often in adults than in children (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; intrusion is more likely than lateral luxation or extrusion to cause necrosis (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; damage to the blood supply leads to calcification or necrosis (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Pathological wear (attrition, abrasion, bruxism, abfraction), cracks and barodontalgia complete Grossman's mechanical list; barodontalgia is seen above 5,000 feet and an irreversibly inflamed pulp silent on the ground may hurt at altitude (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's says occlusion has negligible effect while enamel is intact (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC].

**Iatrogenic.** Accidental exposure, too-rapid orthodontic movement, mechanical separators and pins (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; orthodontic force disturbs pulpal blood and nerve supply; deep scaling and curettage injure apical vessels (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Thermal: dry preparation raises temperature by 20°C at 1 mm and 30°C at 0.5 mm from the pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; a 10°C intrapulpal rise caused irreversible pathosis in 15% and a 20°C rise caused abscesses in 60% of primate teeth (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]; water spray is essential (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Remaining dentine thickness (RDT) is "the key factor" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and "the most important factor" (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC], but the thresholds differ: Sikri gives minimal effect at 2.0 mm or more (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's gives damage only below 0.3 mm (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]. Crowned teeth lose vitality in 10% to 15% over 10 years (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]; Cohen's reports necrosis in 15.6% of single crowns and 32.5% of bridge abutments, and states that insults are cumulative (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. Local anaesthetic with adrenaline adds risk by reducing blood flow (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC].

**Electrical.** Dissimilar metals in saliva cause galvanic shock (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Chemical.** Grossman's calls chemical causes probably the least common (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri lists phosphoric acid, 37% etchant, calcium hydroxide and eugenol, and notes arsenic is no longer used (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Torabinejad's adds cavity cleansers such as alcohol, chloroform and hydrogen peroxide (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's takes a different position: the toxicity of materials has been questioned and preventing microleakage matters more than chemical irritancy (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC]; vital bleaching causes reversible sensitivity only (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017) [SRC].

**Idiopathic.** Internal resorption has no known cause, though trauma is common in the history (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's attributes necrosis without caries, restorations or luxation to a longitudinal crack and advises asking about past intubation (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri adds immune responses as initiators (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Microbial (most common).** Bacteria are the commonest cause of pulp disease (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). Kakehashi's germ-free rats formed dentine bridges over exposures; conventional rats developed necrosis. Routes: caries (commonest), exposure, cracks, developmental grooves, dens invaginatus, leaking restorations, periodontal pockets and lateral canals, anachoresis. By-products alone can inflame the pulp. Species: Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, streptococci. Proximity needed is disputed: enamel caries already attract inflammatory cells (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021); Sikri gives 1.0 mm; Harty's about 0.5 mm for significant inflammation.

**2. Traumatic and mechanical.** Luxation (intrusion worst), fractures, cracks, attrition, abrasion, bruxism, barodontalgia above 5,000 feet (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**3. Thermal.** Dry cutting raises temperature 20°C at 1 mm and 30°C at 0.5 mm from the pulp; exothermic cements, conduction through metal fillings, polishing (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**4. Electrical.** Galvanism between dissimilar metals (Sikri, 2019).

**5. Chemical.** Acids, monomer, eugenol, cavity cleansers (Sikri, 2019). Harty's: microleakage matters more than material toxicity.

**6. Iatrogenic.** Accidental exposure, deep preparation, rapid orthodontic movement, pins, deep scaling. RDT is the key factor: minimal effect at 2 mm or more (Sikri, 2019), damage below 0.3 mm (Harty's). Crowns: 10% to 15% lose vitality in 10 years (Duncan, Smith and Cooper, 2017); bridge abutments 32.5% (Fouad and Levin, 2021). Insults are cumulative.

**7. Idiopathic.** Internal resorption (cause unknown, often past trauma); anachoresis; unexplained necrosis from an undetected crack (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

## 4. Pathogenesis in brief

### Explanation

Sikri states that the initial response may be chronic inflammation, because most irritants progress slowly (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. As inflammation increases, tissue pressure rises, stasis occurs and the pulp necroses (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's adds that the only clinical distinction possible is between symptomatic and asymptomatic stages (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pathogenesis.** Irritant → chronic inflammation beneath the tubules → reparative dentine → if irritation continues, microabscesses and raised local pressure → stasis → partial then total necrosis → apical periodontitis (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 5. Classification of pulp diseases (books side by side)

### Explanation

Sikri says there is no definite, accepted classification and lists schemes based on duration (acute, subacute, chronic), symptoms and ability to heal (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's agrees there is no universally agreed terminology (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri gives Gofung (1928) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; the WHO scheme with initial pulpitis (hyperaemia), acute and suppurative pulpitis, chronic ulcerative and hyperplastic pulpitis, necrosis, degeneration and secondary or irregular dentine (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; an older "Grossman's classification" with hyperaemia, calcific, atrophic and fibrous degeneration and three necrosis types (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Stock's (which includes a "concussed pulp"); Walton and Torabinejad's (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; and Abbott's, which adds "pulpless, infected root canal system" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's 14th edition itself (Box 4.3) has inflammatory diseases (reversible acute and chronic; irreversible symptomatic, asymptomatic, hyperplastic, internal resorption), degeneration (calcific, fibrous) and necrosis (necrobiosis, necrosis without infection, necrotic and infected) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's "Grossman's classification" therefore does not match the 14th edition; it appears to reproduce an older edition [Likely]. Abbott's scheme in Ingle's (Table 8-1) lists hyperplasia under degenerative changes but notes it is "a form of irreversible pulpitis" and excludes internal resorption from the table (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Abbott and Yu summarised 13 different classifications (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. The AAE terms (normal, reversible pulpitis, symptomatic and asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis, necrosis, previously treated, previously initiated) come from the 2009 consensus conference (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]; Cohen's dates its version to the AAE in 2016 (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

There is no universally accepted classification (Sikri, 2019; Chandler and Chong, 2017).

| WHO (in Sikri, 2019) | Grossman's 14th (Gopikrishna, 2021) | Abbott (Abbott, 2019) | AAE 2009 (Okazaki et al, 2017) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pulpitis: initial (hyperaemia), acute (suppurative), chronic (ulcerative, hyperplastic) | Inflammatory: reversible (acute, chronic); irreversible (symptomatic, asymptomatic, hyperplastic, internal resorption) | Clinically normal; reversible and irreversible pulpitis, each acute or chronic | Normal pulp; reversible pulpitis; symptomatic and asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis |
| Necrosis | Necrosis: necrobiosis, without infection, infected | Necrobiosis; necrosis without infection; necrotic and infected; pulpless infected canal | Pulp necrosis |
| Degeneration: denticles, calcification | Degeneration: calcific, fibrous | Pulp canal calcification; hyperplasia; atrophy (in text) | Previously treated; previously initiated |

Sikri's older "Grossman's" scheme adds hyperaemia, atrophic degeneration and coagulative, liquefactive and caseous necrosis.

## 6. Inflammatory diseases of the pulp

### Explanation

**Hyperaemia.** Sikri treats it as a symptom, not a disease, active (more arterial inflow) or passive (less venous outflow) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's mentions transient hyperaemia with menstruation, pregnancy, colds and sinusitis (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Reversible pulpitis.** The pulp can return to normal once the stimulus is removed (Gopikrishna, 2021; Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Subtypes differ: Grossman's uses acute (hours or days) and chronic (months) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], following Abbott's definitions of acute and chronic (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri uses symptomatic and asymptomatic, the latter from incipient caries (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Pain is sharp, brief, provoked (cold more than heat) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], lasting up to 5 to 10 seconds (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Torabinejad's says the patient often has no symptoms (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Dentine hypersensitivity must be separated (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. The difference from irreversible pulpitis is quantitative (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Histology: reparative dentine, disrupted odontoblasts, dilated vessels, oedema (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Treatment: prevention first, then removal of the irritant (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri adds follow-up and RCT if not healed (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Ingle's lists conservative pulp therapy such as pulp capping, partial pulpotomy, pulpotomy or partial pulpectomy (Abbott, 2019) [SRC] and calls the diagnosis provisional until healing is confirmed (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

**Irreversible pulpitis.** Persistent inflammation with a pulp incapable of healing (Gopikrishna, 2021; Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; usually caries (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Symptomatic: spontaneous, lingering, postural pain from raised intrapulpal pressure, nocturnal, referred to the ear from lower teeth, worse with heat and eased by cold late (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri extends referral to the pre- and postauricular region and lower border of the mandible (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; a brief Aδ pain then a lingering C-fibre ache (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Ingle's: minor temperature change (tap water) provokes pain; spontaneous, night and lying-down pain are positive signs of acute irreversible pulpitis (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Asymptomatic: deep caries without symptoms; treat early (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]. EPT: more current needed in the asymptomatic stage (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Histology: microabscesses walled off by fibrous tissue, then chronic ulcerative pulpitis draining through the exposure (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; severe inflammation and partial necrosis near the exposure (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Treatment: pulpectomy, or pulpotomy as an emergency (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Essential Endodontology and Cohen's now accept pulpotomy as definitive in selected cases (Fouad and Khan, 2020; Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC].

**Chronic hyperplastic pulpitis (pulp polyp).** Productive inflammation of a young pulp with a large carious exposure (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; needs a large open cavity, a young resistant pulp and chronic low-grade irritation (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; primary molars and first permanent molars (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; reaches full size in a couple of months (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; trace the stalk to separate it from gingival hyperplasia (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; epithelium more common on primary teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's classes it, with internal resorption, as irreversible pulpitis (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; Abbott also calls hyperplasia a form of irreversible pulpitis (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Treatment: remove the polyp then pulpectomy (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri also allows formocresol pulpotomy in primary teeth (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Internal resorption.** An idiopathic slow or fast resorptive process (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; clastic cells are not normal pulp residents (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Pink spot in the crown; round or ovoid radiolucency continuous with the canal; the defect is larger on the pulpal wall than on the root surface (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri divides it into surface, inflammatory and replacement (bone-like tissue) types (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Pulp extirpation stops it; MTA for perforations (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Hyperaemia.** Sikri: a symptom, not a disease; active or passive.

**B. Reversible pulpitis.** Mild inflammation; the pulp returns to normal once the stimulus goes (Gopikrishna, 2021). Sharp, brief (5 to 10 seconds), provoked pain, cold more than heat; normal percussion and radiograph. Grossman's subtypes are acute and chronic; Sikri's are symptomatic and asymptomatic. Exclude dentine hypersensitivity. Treatment: remove irritant, seal, review (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019); Ingle's adds pulp capping or partial pulpotomy where needed and calls the diagnosis provisional (Abbott, 2019).

**C. Irreversible pulpitis.** Pulp incapable of healing (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- *Symptomatic:* spontaneous, lingering, nocturnal, postural pain; referred to ear or temple; late stage worse with heat, eased by cold (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- *Asymptomatic:* deep caries or exposure without pain; treat before necrosis.
- Histology: microabscesses, then chronic ulcerative pulpitis.
- Treatment: pulpectomy; pulpotomy as emergency (Gopikrishna, 2021), now also definitive in selected cases (Fouad and Khan, 2020).

**D. Chronic hyperplastic pulpitis.** Young pulp, large open cavity, low-grade irritation; fleshy red polyp, bleeds, painless to cut; trace the stalk. Grossman's, Torabinejad's and Abbott treat it as irreversible pulpitis. Treatment: excise, pulpectomy; formocresol pulpotomy in primary teeth (Sikri, 2019).

**E. Internal resorption.** Idiopathic, often after trauma; pink spot; ovoid radiolucency continuous with the canal. Types: surface, inflammatory, replacement (Sikri, 2019). Extirpation stops it; MTA for perforation.

## 7. Pulp necrosis

### Explanation

Necrosis is death of the pulp, partial or total (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and may not involve all canals (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Trauma may cause ischaemic infarction and dry gangrenous necrosis before inflammation develops (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's adopted the "necrobiosis" term (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], which Sikri prefers for partial necrosis (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; in necrobiosis, sensibility tests do not correlate with symptoms (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Without infection there is no periapical response (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. For a necrotic and infected pulp the books disagree on the key radiographic sign: Abbott gives no periapical changes, because a radiolucency appears only once the canal becomes pulpless and infected within one to two months (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's, which reproduces Abbott's three necrosis subtypes but omits the "pulpless and infected" category, states that a periapical radiolucency will be present (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's appears to have merged two of Abbott's stages [Likely]. Harty's agrees that uninfected necrosis leaves the periapex normal (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Signs: discoloration often first (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; a history of severe pain that stopped suddenly (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; in about 40% irreversible pulpitis progresses silently to necrosis (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC], 40% to 60% in Cohen's (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]; necrosis is asymptomatic until the periapex is involved (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Heat sensitivity relieved by cold is attributed to expanding gases (Sikri, 2019; Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Histology: coagulation, liquefaction and caseation (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; odour from indole, skatole, putrescine and cadaverine (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Treatment: pulpectomy and obturation (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's advises monitoring alone for necrosis with no signs of infection (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pulp necrosis.** Death of the pulp, partial or total (Gopikrishna, 2021). Causes: untreated pulpitis, or trauma causing ischaemic (dry gangrenous) necrosis. Types (Abbott, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021): necrobiosis (part inflamed, part necrotic, mixed symptoms); necrosis without infection (normal periapex); necrotic and infected. Grossman's says the infected stage shows a periapical radiolucency; Abbott says not yet, the radiolucency marks the later pulpless infected canal. Features: discoloration, no response to cold or EPT, pain that stopped suddenly; about 40% of pulpitis dies silently (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). Histology: coagulation, liquefaction, caseation. Treatment: RCT; uninfected traumatic necrosis may be monitored (Abbott, 2019).

## 8. Degenerative changes: calcification, fibrosis, atrophy

### Explanation

Degeneration is usually seen in older people (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; ageing brings fibrosis and atrophy (Goodis, Kahn and Simon, 2012) [SRC]. Pulp stones are laminated "like the skin of an onion" and occur in more than 60% of adult teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; prevalence rises with age (Goodis, Kahn and Simon, 2012) [SRC]. On systemic links the books disagree: Torabinejad's links excessive pulp stones to cardiovascular disease and statins (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; Seltzer and Bender found no relationship with renal disease or carotid calcification (Goodis, Kahn and Simon, 2012) [SRC]. Calcific metamorphosis is complete calcification after trauma (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's advises no RCT unless symptomatic (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri and Torabinejad's do not regard calcification as pathology (Sikri, 2019; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC], and Sikri notes deep scaling may cause localized calcification (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Abbott rejects "pulp canal obliteration" because a canal always remains, prefers "pulp canal calcification", and requires the pulp diagnosis to be stated with it (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; sensibility tests are unreliable (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Fibrous degeneration gives a leathery pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Atrophy: fewer, smaller cells, more collagen, delayed response, no treatment (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Abbott calls it a normal physiological ageing process (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Degenerative changes** (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019):
- *Calcific:* pulp stones (free, attached or embedded; over 60% of adult teeth) and diffuse calcification. After trauma, calcific metamorphosis: yellow crown, unreliable tests; treat only if symptomatic or periapical disease appears. Abbott prefers "pulp canal calcification", not "obliteration", since a canal persists. Torabinejad's links stones to cardiovascular disease; Seltzer and Bender find no systemic link.
- *Fibrous:* leathery pulp.
- *Atrophy:* age change, fewer cells, more collagen, delayed test response; no treatment.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: "There is no correlation between clinical symptoms and the histological state of the pulp."** Grossman's states this flatly (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], citing the older position that Cohen's Ch.14 also summarises (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's cites Ricucci et al: 96.6% agreement for normal pulp or reversible pulpitis and 84.4% for irreversible pulpitis (Abbott, 2019) [SRC], and Torabinejad's speaks of "reasonably good agreement" (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's notes that the study was retrospective and used one dentist's subjective definitions (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC], and Essential Endodontology adds that inflammation was often limited to one pulp horn (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. The study itself was not read. Conclusion: write that correlation is better than once taught for the reversible versus irreversible decision, but the evidence is a single retrospective series, so "irreversible" is a clinical label for a pulp whose coronal part may still be salvageable by pulpotomy [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Causes: microbial (commonest), traumatic and mechanical, thermal, electrical, chemical, iatrogenic, idiopathic.
- Kakehashi: no bacteria, no pulpal or periapical disease.
- RDT is the key iatrogenic factor (2 mm safe, Sikri; below 0.5 mm severe, Cohen's).
- Reversible versus irreversible: duration of pain after the stimulus, and spontaneity.
- Hyperplastic pulpitis and internal resorption count as irreversible pulpitis.
- Necrosis types: necrobiosis, uninfected, infected; histology: coagulation, liquefaction, caseation.
- Pulp stones in over 60% of adult teeth; calcific metamorphosis after trauma.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Listing pulp diseases without aetiology, when the question asks for both.
- Calling hyperaemia a disease without noting Sikri's view that it is a symptom.
- Writing "pulp canal obliteration" as a disease that always needs RCT.
- Presenting one book's classification as "the" classification; show at least two side by side.
- Writing that hyperplastic pulpitis is painful or seen mainly in the elderly.
