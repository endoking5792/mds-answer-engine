## 1. Introduction: why so many classifications

### Explanation

Harty's states that there is no universally agreed terminology or classification for pulpal and periapical diseases (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Sikri says the same for pulpal disease (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Abbott and Yu summarised 13 different classifications of pulp and root canal conditions (Abbott, 2019) [SRC] and argue a classification must be possible to use clinically, meaningful about the tissues and useful for choosing management (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Because clinical signs correlate poorly with histopathology, clinical classifications were built to guide treatment (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** There is no universally agreed classification of pulpal and periapical disease (Chandler and Chong, 2017; Sikri, 2019). Abbott counted 13 schemes and asks that a classification be usable clinically, meaningful histologically and useful for management (Abbott, 2019). Clinical schemes exist because symptoms do not reliably reveal histology (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

## 2. Classification of pulpal disease

### Explanation

**AAE 2009 consensus** (reproduced in Endodontic Prognosis): normal pulp, reversible pulpitis, symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (lingering thermal, spontaneous or referred pain), asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis (no symptoms but inflammation from caries, excavation or trauma), pulp necrosis, previously treated, previously initiated therapy (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's uses the same categories and dates them to the AAE in 2016 (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; its reference is the AAE Glossary of Endodontic Terms, 9th edition, 2016 (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], so the two dates name different documents carrying the same terms rather than a disagreement [Likely]. **Grossman's 14th** (Box 4.3): inflammatory (reversible acute and chronic; irreversible symptomatic, asymptomatic, chronic hyperplastic, internal resorption), degeneration (calcific, fibrous), necrosis (necrobiosis, without infection, necrotic and infected) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. **Abbott (Ingle's Table 8-1)**: clinically normal pulp; reversible and irreversible pulpitis, each acute or chronic; necrobiosis, necrosis without infection, necrotic and infected pulp; pulpless and infected canal; previous root canal treatment (with or without infection, technical standard); degenerative changes (pulp canal calcification, hyperplasia as a form of irreversible pulpitis) (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Abbott defends "acute" (rapid onset, short severe course) and "chronic" (long-standing, mild occasional pain) and quotes Gutmann that "symptomatic/asymptomatic" has little literature support (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's also keeps the older acute and chronic terms (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC] in a short scheme: normal pulp, reversible pulpitis (often from caries or coronal leakage), irreversible pulpitis and pulp necrosis (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; normal periapical tissues, acute apical periodontitis, acute periapical abscess, chronic apical periodontitis with the phoenix abscess as its acute exacerbation, and chronic periapical abscess (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. **WHO** (in Sikri): pulpitis (initial or hyperaemia; acute or suppurative; chronic ulcerative, hyperplastic, unspecified), necrosis, degeneration, secondary or irregular dentine (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri also lists Gofung, an older Grossman's, Stock's, Walton and Torabinejad's and Abbott's schemes (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

## 3. Classification of periapical disease

### Explanation

**AAE 2009**: normal apical tissues, symptomatic apical periodontitis, asymptomatic apical periodontitis, acute apical abscess, chronic apical abscess, condensing osteitis (a diffuse radiopaque reaction to low-grade inflammation); "posttreatment" qualifiers for treated teeth (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]. **Grossman's 14th** classifies on aetiology, symptoms and histopathology (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]: symptomatic (primary and secondary symptomatic apical periodontitis, the secondary form having a radiolucency and being Grossman's name for the phoenix abscess, and symptomatic (acute) alveolar abscess), asymptomatic (apical periodontitis, asymptomatic (chronic) alveolar abscess, condensing osteitis), persistent apical periodontitis, external root resorption, nonendodontic disease; histopathological: granuloma, abscess, cyst (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. **Abbott**: apical periodontitis acute primary (no radiolucency) or secondary (radiolucency), chronic, condensing osteitis; foreign body reaction; abscess acute primary or secondary, chronic; facial cellulitis; extra-radicular infection; pocket and true cysts; periapical scar (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; condensing osteitis is a form of chronic apical periodontitis (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. **WHO ICD-10**: K04.4 acute apical periodontitis of pulpal origin, K04.5 chronic apical periodontitis, K04.6 abscess with sinus, K04.7 abscess without sinus, K04.8 radicular cyst (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]; Sikri prints these as codes 4.4 to 4.8, with 4.5 labelled granuloma (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's heading for 4.6 reads "with sinus to maxillary antrum" and then lists nasal, oral and skin sinuses beneath it (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Ørstavik notes that K04.6 is subdivided by the site of the sinus tract (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC], so Sikri's heading appears to have absorbed one subdivision [Likely]. Ørstavik also treats condensing osteitis as a possible variant of asymptomatic or chronic apical periodontitis (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC], as Abbott does. Ørstavik notes AAE "symptomatic" covers more teeth than ICD "acute" (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]. **Ingle's older scheme** (in Sikri): painful and non-painful (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri also gives Prinz's and an older Grossman's with subacute abscess and phoenix abscess (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Histological: granuloma, abscess, cyst (Gopikrishna, 2021). Older Ingle's: painful and non-painful (Sikri, 2019).

## 4. Etio-pathology

### Explanation

Causes are physical, chemical and bacterial (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], or nonliving and living, with microbes the most significant (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Germ-free rats showed minimal inflammation after exposure (Kakehashi, cited by Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; Sundqvist found canals of traumatised teeth without lesions aseptic (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Routes: coronal (most common), lateral, apical (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. Periapical causes add over-instrumentation and overfilling (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; a high restoration makes a vital tooth tender, a reversible condition (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Inflammation raises tissue pressure, stasis follows and the pulp necroses (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; irreversible pulpitis shows severe inflammation and partial necrosis near the exposure (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; about 40% becomes necrotic silently (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Cysts make up 7% to 54% of lesions in different series and heal as well as granulomas once infection is controlled (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; Ørstavik agrees the cyst versus granuloma distinction is abandoned but notes true cysts may heal poorly and need surgery (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]; Abbott: pocket cysts can only be diagnosed histologically (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Causes:** microbial (most significant), traumatic, iatrogenic (deep preparation, heat, over-instrumentation, overfilling, irritant irrigants), chemical (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). Kakehashi: no bacteria, no pulpal or periapical disease.

**Pathogenesis:** caries or leakage → bacterial products through tubules → reversible pulpitis → microabscesses, partial necrosis (irreversible pulpitis) → raised pressure, stasis, necrosis (about 40% silently) → infected canal → apical periodontitis with bone resorption → granuloma, abscess or cyst (7% to 54%) depending on virulence and host response (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). Cysts and granulomas heal alike once infection is removed; true cysts may need surgery (Ørstavik, 2020).

## 5. Differential diagnosis

### Explanation

Reversible and irreversible pulpitis differ quantitatively (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]: reversible pain lasts up to 5 to 10 seconds, irreversible gives an Aδ pain then a lingering C-fibre ache (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; spontaneous, night and lying-down pain mark acute irreversible pulpitis (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Late irreversible pulpitis is separated from an acute abscess by swelling, tenderness, mobility, no response to tests and fever (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Percussion pain is the key to symptomatic apical periodontitis (Gopikrishna, 2021; Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; a radiolucency without symptoms means asymptomatic apical periodontitis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; an acute abscess adds swelling and often fever (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; a chronic abscess has a sinus tract (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], traced with gutta-percha (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Necrotic and infected pulp: Abbott says no periapical change yet (Abbott, 2019) [SRC], whereas Grossman's says a radiolucency is present (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's says uninfected necrosis leaves the periapex normal (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Mimics: dentine hypersensitivity (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; pulp polyp versus gingiva, traced by the stalk (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; internal versus external resorption (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; condensing osteitis versus enostosis (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; nonendodontic lesions with intact lamina dura and responsive teeth (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC].

| Chronic apical abscess | Little; sinus tract | No response | Not tender | Radiolucency; GP tracing |

(Gopikrishna, 2021; Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Chandler and Chong, 2017). Exclude dentine hypersensitivity, cracked tooth, enostosis and nonendodontic lesions (intact lamina dura, responsive teeth).

## 6. Management

### Explanation

Reversible pulpitis: remove the noxious stimulus (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's likewise expects removal of the cause, usually caries or coronal leakage, to relieve it (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Abbott differs: reversible pulpitis will normally require conservative pulp therapy such as a direct pulp cap, partial pulpotomy, pulpotomy or partial pulpectomy, and the diagnosis is confirmed only after healing (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Irreversible pulpitis: pulpectomy (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], or RCT as first choice (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; pulpotomy is now viable (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC] and symptomatic cases with a normal periapex respond well to emergency pulpotomy (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's holds that once irreversible pulpitis is confirmed the pulp must be removed (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC], that emergency pulpotomy is a temporary measure for pain relief (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC], and that antibiotics have no beneficial role (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Extraction is always an option (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Necrosis without infection: monitor (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's instead indicates root canal treatment once necrosis is confirmed (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC] and, after trauma to a mature tooth with little chance of pulp survival, within 7 to 10 days to prevent infection (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Symptomatic apical periodontitis from a high point: adjust the occlusion; otherwise remove irritants (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Acute abscess: drain through the canal and incise fluctuant swelling (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; cellulitis needs immediate systemic antibiotics plus RCT (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; the acute abscess is the most serious endodontic diagnosis (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC], and prescribing antibiotics without drainage is incorrect (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Sinus tracts heal once the canal is treated (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Condensing osteitis needs no treatment unless the pulp is irreversibly diseased (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Periapical scar: review only (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Prognosis is best when RCT is done for irreversible pulpitis, before apical periodontitis (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Diagnosis | Management |
|---|---|
| Reversible pulpitis | Remove cause, seal, review (Gopikrishna, 2021; Chandler and Chong, 2017); Abbott normally adds pulp cap or partial pulpotomy |
| Irreversible pulpitis | Pulpectomy and RCT; emergency pulpotomy temporary (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) or definitive in selected cases (Fouad and Khan, 2020); no antibiotics |
| Hyperplastic pulpitis | Excise polyp, RCT |
| Internal resorption | Extirpate, RCT with thermoplastic GP; MTA for perforation |
| Necrosis | RCT (Chandler and Chong, 2017); Abbott monitors necrosis without infection |
| Symptomatic apical periodontitis | Adjust occlusion if vital and high; else RCT |
| Asymptomatic apical periodontitis, chronic abscess | RCT with intracanal medicament; review; surgery if persistent |
| Acute apical abscess | Drain via canal, incise fluctuant swelling; antibiotics only for spreading infection or cellulitis |
| Condensing osteitis | Treat the pulp; no separate treatment |
| Cyst | RCT first; surgery if non-healing |

Extraction is always an alternative (Abbott, 2019). Prognosis is best when irreversible pulpitis is treated before apical periodontitis develops (Okazaki et al, 2017).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: "Clinical classification cannot tell us the histological state of the pulp, so reversible and irreversible pulpitis are guesses."**
*Textbook:* Grossman's: no correlation exists between histopathology and symptoms (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's calls correlation challenging (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].
*Refinement:* Ingle's cites Ricucci et al with 96.6% agreement for normal or reversible and 84.4% for irreversible pulpitis (Abbott, 2019) [SRC], confirmed by the study's PubMed abstract (57 of 59 and 27 of 32 teeth; Ricucci et al, 2014) [LIT]; Torabinejad's reports "reasonably good agreement" in carious teeth (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's disagrees: correlation is poor, "especially true with irreversible pulpitis" (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].
*Strength:* one retrospective series with one dentist's subjective definitions (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]; inflammation and infection were often localized to the coronal pulp (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC], and in some teeth only one pulp horn was inflamed (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. The study itself was not read.
*Conclusion:* the old "no correlation" statement overstates the problem, but "irreversible" may not be truly irreversible (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]; the label decides between pulp preservation and removal rather than describing histology [Likely].

**Claim: "Symptomatic/asymptomatic should replace acute/chronic."** AAE adopted symptomatic/asymptomatic (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]; Abbott and Harty's keep acute/chronic, Abbott quoting Gutmann that the newer terms lack literature support (Abbott, 2019; Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Ørstavik notes AAE "symptomatic" covers more teeth than ICD "acute" (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]. Both systems are in use; name the one you follow [Certain].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- No universally agreed classification; know AAE 2009, Grossman's, Abbott and WHO ICD-10 (K04.4 to K04.8).
- AAE pulp: normal, reversible, symptomatic/asymptomatic irreversible, necrosis, previously treated, previously initiated.
- AAE periapex: normal, SAP, AAP, acute abscess, chronic abscess, condensing osteitis.
- Primary versus secondary symptomatic apical periodontitis: absence or presence of a radiolucency.
- Kakehashi: bacteria are essential.
- Lingering and spontaneous pain separates irreversible from reversible pulpitis; percussion tenderness marks apical involvement.
- Antibiotics only for spreading infection, not for pulpitis.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Giving only the pulpal classification when the question asks for pulpal and periapical.
- Mixing AAE and older terms in one table without saying which scheme is which.
- Prescribing antibiotics for irreversible pulpitis.
- Calling every well-defined radiolucency a cyst; cysts are diagnosed only histologically.
- Leaving out the differential diagnosis table, which is a named part of the question.
