## 1. Definition and importance

### Explanation

Harty's opens by noting there is no universally agreed diagnostic terminology for pulpal and periapical disease (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri defines periradicular diseases as diseases around the apex of a tooth, which "may be of endodontic" or non-endodontic origin (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. In the narrower sense the subject is apical periodontitis, "an inflammatory disease caused by bacterial infection of the root canal system" (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017) [SRC]. Lin and Huang compare it with marginal periodontitis: crest bone is lost in one, apical bone in the other (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

It is common: prevalence rises with age to 62% in people older than 60 (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], and a 2012 systematic review cited by Landwehr found periapical radiolucencies in 5% of all teeth (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]. Most of it is silent: about 40% of necrotic teeth with chronic apical periodontitis develop with minimal pain (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC], and Lin and Huang add that pulp necrosis and chronic apical periodontitis are usually asymptomatic (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Periapical (periradicular) pathosis is disease of the tissues around the root apex: cementum, periodontal ligament (PDL) and alveolar bone. It is usually of endodontic origin but may be non-endodontic (Sikri, 2019). The principal endodontic form is **apical periodontitis**, an inflammatory disease caused by bacterial infection of the root canal system (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017). It is the apical counterpart of marginal periodontitis: apical bone is resorbed instead of crestal bone (Lin and Huang, 2021).

**Importance.** Prevalence rises with age to 62% over 60 years (Lin and Huang, 2021); most lesions are asymptomatic and found on radiographs. There is no universally agreed terminology (Chandler and Chong, 2017), so the classification must be stated.

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

Lin and Huang list the sources of classification: the WHO, several textbooks, the American Board of Endodontists, and the AAE consensus conference of autumn 2008 (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.1 now uses the AAE terminology as restated in 2016 (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], and Ingle's follows the AAE consensus, dividing lesions into three clinical groups: apical periodontitis (symptomatic, asymptomatic), apical abscesses (acute, chronic), and condensing osteitis (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. The AAE definitions given in Cohen's are: symptomatic apical periodontitis, inflammation of the apical periodontium producing a painful response to biting, percussion or palpation; asymptomatic apical periodontitis, inflammation and destruction of apical periodontium of pulpal origin appearing as a radiolucency without symptoms; acute apical abscess, rapid onset, spontaneous pain, pus and swelling; chronic apical abscess, gradual onset, little or no discomfort and intermittent discharge through a sinus tract (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

Grossman's classifies on etiology, symptoms and histopathology (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and keeps two layers: a clinical one that splits symptomatic apical periodontitis into **primary** (no radiolucency) and **secondary** (the phoenix abscess, with a radiolucency) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and a histopathological one (granuloma, abscess, cyst).

Sikri gives the WHO codes (the ICD dental application): 4.4 acute apical periodontitis of pulpal origin, 4.5 chronic apical periodontitis (granuloma), 4.6 periapical abscess with sinus, 4.7 periapical abscess without sinus, 4.8 radicular cyst (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's text writes code 4.6 as "with sinus to maxillary antrum" and lists nasal, oral and skin sinuses beneath it (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Ørstavik's ICD-10 table gives K04.6 as periapical abscess with sinus (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC], "further subdivided in relation to sinus tract location" (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]; the antral sinus is one of those subdivisions, so Sikri has merged the heading with its first subtype. [Likely] The same table pairs the AAE chronic apical abscess with K04.6 and the acute apical abscess with K04.7 (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC].

**Older classifications.** The early writers used "pericementitis": nonseptic and septic pericementitis, pericemental abscess and alveolar abscess (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; the term was later changed to periodontitis, with traumatic, chemical/iatrogenic and bacterial categories (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Prinz divided acute pericementitis (apical, marginal, diffuse suppurative, intraradicular) from chronic pericementitis (apical suppurative, proliferative) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Coolidge listed acute inflammation (traumatic and chemical periodontitis, apical periodontitis from infection, acute alveolar abscess), chronic inflammation (suppurative and proliferative periodontitis), granuloma, radicular cyst and condensing osteitis (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's older list was acute apical periodontitis, acute, chronic and "subacute" alveolar abscess, granuloma and cyst (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; it was later modified into acute periradicular disease (acute apical periodontitis, vital or non-vital; acute alveolar abscess; phoenix abscess), chronic periradicular disease (chronic apical periodontitis, chronic alveolar abscess, cystic apical periodontitis; persistent apical periodontitis), condensing osteitis, external root resorption and non-endodontic disease (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. That modified list is the frame of the current Grossman's scheme with symptomatic/asymptomatic names. [Likely] Ingle's earlier scheme divided painful from non-painful disease (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]: painful = acute apical periodontitis and "advanced apical periodontitis" (acute apical abscess, phoenix abscess, chronic apical abscess) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; non-painful = condensing osteitis and chronic apical periodontitis, incipient or advanced (granuloma, suppurative apical periodontitis, apical cyst) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. As Sikri records it, suppurative apical periodontitis appears in both groups. [Certain]

Harty's notes that symptomatic/asymptomatic have been suggested to replace acute/chronic, but keeps "the more conservative terms" (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

The schemes disagree on two points: whether the phoenix abscess is a type of apical periodontitis (Grossman's "secondary symptomatic apical periodontitis"; Harty's Ch.3 describes it under chronic apical periodontitis) or of abscess (Ingle's; Harty's Ch.14 counts the acute phase of chronic apical periodontitis as an acute periapical abscess, Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC], and whether condensing osteitis is listed (Ingle's, Grossman's) or omitted (Cohen's Ch.1). These are naming differences, not differences in the disease. [Certain]

**AAE definitions (Berman and Rotstein, 2021):**
- *Symptomatic apical periodontitis:* inflammation of the apical periodontium with pain on biting, percussion or palpation; may or may not show a radiolucency.
- *Asymptomatic apical periodontitis:* inflammation and destruction of apical periodontium of pulpal origin, seen as a radiolucency, without symptoms.
- *Acute apical abscess:* rapid onset, spontaneous pain, tenderness, pus and swelling.
- *Chronic apical abscess:* gradual onset, little or no discomfort, intermittent discharge through a sinus tract.

**Histopathological classification:** apical granuloma, apical abscess, apical (radicular) cyst, true or pocket (Gopikrishna, 2021).

*WHO note:* Sikri writes 4.6 as abscess "with sinus to maxillary antrum"; in ICD-10, K04.6 is periapical abscess with sinus, subdivided by where the sinus opens (Ørstavik, 2020).

**Older classifications (Sikri, 2019):**
- *Early writers:* nonseptic pericementitis, septic pericementitis, pericemental abscess, alveolar abscess; "pericementitis" later renamed periodontitis (traumatic, chemical, bacterial).
- *Prinz:* acute pericementitis (apical, marginal, diffuse suppurative, intraradicular); chronic pericementitis (apical suppurative, proliferative).
- *Coolidge:* acute (traumatic and chemical periodontitis, infective apical periodontitis, acute alveolar abscess); chronic (suppurative, proliferative periodontitis); granuloma; radicular cyst; condensing osteitis.
- *Grossman (older):* acute apical periodontitis; acute, chronic and subacute alveolar abscess; granuloma; cyst. *Modified:* acute (apical periodontitis, alveolar abscess, phoenix abscess); chronic (chronic apical periodontitis, chronic alveolar abscess, cystic apical periodontitis, persistent apical periodontitis); condensing osteitis; external root resorption; non-endodontic disease.
- *Ingle (earlier):* painful (acute apical periodontitis; advanced: acute apical abscess, phoenix abscess, chronic apical abscess) versus non-painful (condensing osteitis; chronic apical periodontitis, incipient or advanced: granuloma, suppurative apical periodontitis, cyst).

Clinical and histological labels do not map one to one.

## 3. Aetiology

### Explanation

Lin and Huang separate exogenous causes (microbes and their toxins, chemicals, mechanical irritation, foreign bodies, trauma) from endogenous ones (cholesterol crystals, urate, cytokines) (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

**Microbial.** In germ-free rats exposed pulps produced no necrosis or periradicular inflammation (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's text adds that only minimal inflammation occurred over the 72-day period (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Traumatised intact teeth with necrotic but uninfected pulps showed no periapical bone destruction (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Sundqvist found these canals aseptic (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Primary infection is a polymicrobial mix dominated by obligate anaerobes (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], 10 to 30 species per canal (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017) [SRC], organised as biofilm in 80% of untreated canals (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017) [SRC]. Treponema species are linked with pain and acute infection (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. In root-filled teeth E. faecalis is frequent (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC] and Grossman's calls it therapy-resistant (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Non-microbial (brief).** Physical (overinstrumentation, overfilling) and chemical insults (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; a restoration left high makes a vital tooth tender to percussion (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; foreign materials (sealer, paper points, cotton) cause foreign body granulomas (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; cholesterol crystals are found in 18% to 44% of lesions (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], though Ricucci and co-workers call them "the product and not the cause" (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's lists Nair's extraradicular factors in persistent disease (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Microbial (main cause)**
- Kakehashi: no periapical lesion in germ-free rats (Lin and Huang, 2021; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021).
- Sundqvist: canals of traumatised teeth without lesions were aseptic.
- Primary infection: polymicrobial, obligate anaerobes dominate (Lin and Huang, 2021); 10-30 species per canal, biofilm in 80% (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017). Treponema linked with acute symptoms (Fouad and Khan, 2020).
- Secondary/persistent: E. faecalis, a therapy-resistant microbe (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Routes: caries (commonest), trauma, cracks, periodontal pocket, restorative procedures.
- Virulence factors: LPS, LTA, peptidoglycan, enzymes, metabolites.

**B. Non-microbial**
- Physical: trauma, high restoration, overinstrumentation, overfilling (Lin and Huang, 2021; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021).
- Chemical: irrigants, medicaments, filling materials.
- Foreign bodies: sealer, paper points, cotton fibres.
- Endogenous: cholesterol crystals (18-44% of lesions) (Lin and Huang, 2021); called a product, not a cause, by Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira (2019).

**C. Types of endodontic infection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017)**
- Primary intraradicular: organisms colonising the necrotic pulp; cause of primary apical periodontitis.
- Secondary: organisms entering during or after treatment.
- Persistent: survivors of the primary infection; main cause of post-treatment disease.
- Extraradicular: bacteria invading the periapical tissues; seen in abscesses and actinomycosis.

## 4. Pathogenesis: host response and bone resorption

### Explanation

Lin and Huang stress the difference between apical inflammation and apical infection (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; bacteria are usually in the canal, not in the lesion (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], and Nair could not see bacteria in most asymptomatic lesions (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Apical periodontitis can begin as an extension of apical pulpitis before total pulp necrosis (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri states the same (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Ricucci found vital apical tissue in about one third of teeth with lesions (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; Fouad and Khan conclude the pulp need not be totally necrotic (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC].

**Why a lesion forms.** The PDL is too thin to mount an immune response that can stop advancing bacteria (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC], so bone is resorbed to make room for a soft tissue lesion: bone resorption is the hallmark (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. The lesion is a "second line of defense" (Sasaki and Stashenko, 2012) [SRC] or "defense barrier" (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC] that protects against acute spreading infection and osteomyelitis (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. Ingle's calls the immune response a double-edged sword (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC].

**Innate response.** TLR4/CD14 recognises LPS; TLR2 detects Gram-positive bacteria (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Acute apical periodontitis reflects the innate response (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]: mast cells trigger it (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; PMNs peak at 24 to 48 hours and macrophages arrive at 48 to 96 hours (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; PMNs also form NETs (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. Complement fragments C3a and C5a may attract PMNs, but complement probably does not play a major role, since C5-deficient animals form lesions of normal size (Sasaki and Stashenko, 2012) [SRC]. Much of the tissue damage is from leukocyte lysosomal enzymes and MMPs (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Neurogenic inflammation: CGRP for vasodilation and substance P for extravasation (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; VIP levels are inversely related to lesion size (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

**Adaptive response.** Asymptomatic apical periodontitis is an immune response to chronic antigenic stimulation (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]; antigens are taken to regional lymph nodes (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Rôças and co-workers name lymphocytes and plasma cells as predominant (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC], Lin and Huang name lymphocytes and macrophages (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], Grossman's macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and Sasaki and Stashenko record "some disagreement" on this point (Sasaki and Stashenko, 2012) [SRC]. IgG cells are about 70% of plasma cells (Sasaki and Stashenko, 2012) [SRC]. TH1 drives all stages, TH17 cells are present (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. B cells, not T cells, prevent dissemination (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; immunodeficient animals develop spreading infection (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC].

**Bone resorption.** Bacteria act indirectly through host mediators (Sasaki and Stashenko, 2012) [SRC]. IL-1, IL-6, IL-11, IL-17 and TNF-α induce osteoclast differentiation (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. OPG is a decoy receptor for RANKL (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; a raised RANKL/OPG ratio drives resorption (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC] and tracks lesion expansion (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. RANKL and the ratio peak at 2 to 3 weeks, then OPG rises, a negative feedback loop (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; IL-4 and IL-10 limit resorption (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. M1 macrophages prevail in active, M2 in stable lesions (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. Granulomas express more RANKL than cysts (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Osteoclasts acidify the resorption zone with H+ pumps (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. In animals rapid destruction occurs between 10 and 20 days (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], and lesions take 5 to 8 weeks to show radiographically in large animals (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. An equilibrium between aggression and defence is usually achieved (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sequence:** caries → pulpitis → necrosis and canal infection (biofilm) → egress of bacterial products at the foramen → periapical inflammation. It may start before total pulp necrosis (Lin and Huang, 2021; Sikri, 2019): vital apical tissue in about one third of teeth with lesions (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).

**Inflammation versus infection.** Bacteria are usually in the canal, not in the lesion, except in abscesses, sinus tracts and extraradicular infection (Lin and Huang, 2021).

**Why bone is lost.** The thin PDL cannot hold enough defence cells, so bone is resorbed to make room for a granulomatous lesion: bone resorption is the hallmark (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). The lesion is a "second line of defence" (Sasaki and Stashenko, 2012) that prevents spreading infection and osteomyelitis (Fouad and Khan, 2020).

**1. Innate (acute) response**
- PAMPs (LPS via TLR4/CD14; LTA via TLR2) activate macrophages (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Mast cells (histamine), complement (C3a, C5a), vasodilation, exudate.
- PMNs peak 24-48 h; macrophages second wave 48-96 h; NETs.
- Tissue damage mainly from leukocyte lysosomal enzymes and MMPs.
- Neurogenic: CGRP (vasodilation), substance P (extravasation); VIP inversely related to lesion size.

**2. Adaptive (chronic) response**
- Antigens to regional lymph nodes; effector cells return (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).
- Lymphocytes, plasma cells (IgG about 70%) and macrophages; sources differ on which predominates.
- TH1, TH17: progression; TH2, Tregs, IL-10: restraint.
- B cells and antibody prevent spread (Lin and Huang, 2021).

**3. Bone resorption (RANKL/OPG)**
- Cytokines IL-1, IL-6, IL-11, IL-17, TNF-α and PGE2 make osteoblasts and T cells express RANKL; RANKL binds RANK on osteoclast precursors; OPG is the decoy (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- High RANKL/OPG ratio = expansion (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022); peaks 2-3 weeks, then OPG rises, limiting loss (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021).
- M1 macrophages in active, M2 in stable lesions.
- Equilibrium between aggression and defence gives a stable lesion (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).

**Time course (animal models):** PMNs and monocytes reach the periapex 1-3 days after pulp exposure (Sasaki and Stashenko, 2012); rapid bone destruction between days 10 and 20, then a stationary phase (Lin and Huang, 2021); RANKL peaks at 2-3 weeks; lesions become radiographically visible at 5-8 weeks in large animals (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021).

**Double-edged sword:** the same response that confines infection causes pain, pus and bone loss (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).

## 5. Symptomatic apical periodontitis

### Explanation

Grossman's defines primary symptomatic apical periodontitis as painful inflammation of the periodontium from trauma, irritation or infection, whether the pulp is vital or not (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Pain comes from increased vascular permeability (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; the raised hydrostatic pressure is "arguably the main cause" (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]; Sikri adds that pain lasts until bone resorbs and relieves pressure (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Lin and Huang call the tenderness mechanical allodynia and hyperalgesia (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Pain on percussion is the classical feature (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's advises percussing gently with the tip of the index finger (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Percussion pain does not tell vital from non-vital (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Bone destruction is usually not visible in primary acute apical periodontitis (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; a frank radiolucency means exacerbation of an asymptomatic lesion (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's: exquisitely tender to touch, biting or percussion (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** painful inflammation of the apical periodontium from trauma, irritation or infection, pulp vital or non-vital (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Causes:** vital tooth: high restoration, wedged object, blow; non-vital: spread from necrotic pulp, overinstrumentation, irritant medicaments, overfilling.

**Mechanism:** exudate in a confined PDL raises hydrostatic pressure on nerve endings (main cause of pain), plus bradykinin, PGs and LPS (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022); the tooth is slightly extruded.

**Clinical:** localised pain on biting, "high" tooth, exquisite tenderness to percussion, the classical sign (Gopikrishna, 2021); percuss with a fingertip (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019). Pulp tests: normal in occlusal trauma, negative if necrotic.

**Radiograph:** normal or widened PDL space; a radiolucency means an exacerbated chronic lesion (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019). Grossman's uses this to separate primary (no radiolucency) from secondary symptomatic AP (radiolucency present) (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Histology:** dilated vessels, PMNs, serous exudate.

**Treatment:** remove the cause: occlusal adjustment, or canal debridement and RCT.

## 6. Asymptomatic apical periodontitis and periapical granuloma

### Explanation

Grossman's calls it the symptomless sequel of symptomatic apical periodontitis (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and a cell-mediated response to bacterial products (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Bone destruction is its hallmark (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

**The word "granuloma".** Lin and Huang argue that granuloma and chronic apical periodontitis should not be used interchangeably (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], because a true granuloma is relatively avascular whereas chronic apical periodontitis is very vascular (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's notes the apical granuloma, unlike granulomas elsewhere, contains extravascular PMNs (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's keeps "granuloma" as the commonest histological form (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], with a fibrous capsule continuous with the PDL (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and rests of Malassez (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Both usages are found; the exam answer should give both.

**Frequency.** Bhaskar's 2,308 specimens: 48% granulomas (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; Nair's 256: 50% granulomas, 35% abscesses, 15% cysts (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; Ricucci's serial-section study: 40%, 32% cysts, 28% abscesses (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. Correct diagnosis needs serial sectioning (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC].

Chronic lesions are innervated (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Apical root resorption was seen radiographically in only 19% but histologically in 81% (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; cementum resists resorption more than bone (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. Root blunting means working length should be adjusted (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** inflammation and destruction of apical periodontium of pulpal origin, seen as a radiolucency without symptoms (Berman and Rotstein, 2021); a cell-mediated response to canal bacteria (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Clinical:** no symptoms or a "different" feeling; history of past pain; discoloured crown; negative pulp tests; not tender (differs from SAP).

**Radiograph:** widened PDL early, then a well-defined periapical radiolucency with loss of lamina dura; occasionally lateral or furcal; root blunting from resorption.

**Histopathology (periapical granuloma):** commonest form (48% Bhaskar; 50% Nair; Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019). Lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages, some PMNs, foam cells, giant cells, cholesterol clefts, fibroblasts and new vessels; rests of Malassez; peripheral fibrous capsule continuous with the PDL (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Terminology note:** Lin and Huang (2021) say "granuloma" and chronic apical periodontitis should not be used interchangeably because a true granuloma is avascular and the apical lesion is very vascular; Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021) retains the term.

**Other features:** bacteria are rarely seen inside the lesion (Nair, cited by Lin and Huang, 2021); the lesion is innervated, so an instrument passed beyond the apex without anaesthesia hurts (Lin and Huang, 2021). Apical root resorption was seen on radiographs in only 19% of teeth but histologically in 81% (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019); a blunted apex means working length must be adjusted.

**Treatment:** RCT; healing with trabecular bone.

## 7. Apical abscess: acute, chronic and phoenix

### Explanation

An abscess is a localised collection of purulent exudate (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; the pus is poorly oxygenated and of low pH (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; it is the extreme form of PMN tissue injury (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. In an acute abscess an extraradicular infection is established (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC].

**Acute.** Grossman's definition matches the AAE (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; temperature may rise several degrees (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; the patient is frequently febrile (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's calls it the most serious clinical diagnosis in endodontics (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Pain drops dramatically when pus breaks through the periosteum (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's: it may follow acute apical periodontitis or be an acute phase of chronic apical periodontitis (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC], and prescribing antibiotics without draining is incorrect (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. It may spread along fascial planes to cellulitis (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. No specific organism was related to the abscess in 100 cases (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. A periodontal abscess usually involves a vital tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Chronic.** A long-standing low-grade infection draining through a sinus tract (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; synonym chronic suppurative apical periodontitis (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; gradual onset, little or no discomfort (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; borders less well defined (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; pus follows the path of least resistance (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; sulcular drainage creates a pseudopocket that mimics a periodontal pocket or root fracture (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's Ch.13 agrees that such coronal drainage mimics a periodontal abscess but is in reality a sinus tract of pulpal origin (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]. Harty's: the sinus tract distinguishes it from chronic apical periodontitis (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. The sinus heals once the canal is treated (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Phoenix abscess.** An acute reaction superimposed on asymptomatic apical periodontitis (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], most often after starting RCT on an asymptomatic tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; the name is from the mythological bird (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's describes it as an abscess arising from a long-standing asymptomatic lesion (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's Ch.3 describes it, under chronic apical periodontitis, as an acute exacerbation usually in a tooth with a pre-existing radiolucency (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Torabinejad's suggests a shift in canal microbiota as a cause (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC].

**Disagreement on management.** Grossman's: treat as an acute apical abscess (drainage) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri: "Only anti-inflammatory drugs including steroids are helpful" and "Surgical incision etc. is not advised" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's does not use the phoenix label in its treatment chapter, but for a flare-up with pain and swelling it advises drainage plus analgesics and antibiotics (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC], and notes flare-ups are more likely in teeth with a periapical lesion (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; reading the flare-up as the same clinical event is an inference. [Likely] Shown side by side. Sikri's position is hard to reconcile with the principle that pain in acute inflammation is relieved by draining exudate (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]; where pus is present, drainage applies. [Likely]

**Disagreement on radiograph.** Grossman's phoenix clinical note says PDL widening is the only change in an acute apical abscess (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and its acute abscess note that slight PDL widening and loss of apical lamina dura "may be the only" changes (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.1 and Ingle's say anything from widened PDL to a radiolucency, depending on whether it arose de novo or from a chronic lesion (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's Ch.3 says normal periapical tissues or a radiolucency (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC], and Harty's Ch.14 that radiographs may not help because acute lesions are not visible until bone, including the cortical plates, has been resorbed (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Grossman's notes describe only the de novo case. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Acute apical abscess**
- *Definition:* inflammatory reaction to pulpal infection and necrosis with rapid onset, spontaneous pain, tenderness, pus and swelling (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- *Features:* severe throbbing pain, extruded mobile tooth, swelling, fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy; "most serious clinical diagnosis in endodontics" (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). Pain eases dramatically once pus breaks through the periosteum (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).
- *Radiograph:* widened PDL (de novo) to radiolucency (from a chronic lesion); may look normal early (Chandler and Chong, 2017; Hülsmann and Chong, 2017).
- *Histology:* liquefaction, dense PMNs, bacteria in the tissue (extraradicular infection).
- *Spread:* cellulitis, fascial spaces (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- *Differential:* periodontal abscess (vital tooth, pocket, swelling nearer the gingival margin) (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Chronic apical abscess (chronic suppurative apical periodontitis)**
- Gradual onset, little or no discomfort, intermittent pus through a sinus tract (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Radiolucency, often less well defined (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).
- Sinus traced with a gutta-percha cone; pus follows the path of least resistance; sulcular drainage mimics a periodontal pocket or root fracture (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Sinus heals after canal disinfection (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021).

**Phoenix abscess (secondary symptomatic AP)**
- Acute exacerbation of an existing asymptomatic lesion, often after starting RCT (Gopikrishna, 2021); radiolucency present.
- Cause: shift in canal microbiota or host balance (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021).
- *Management, books differ:* Grossman's: as acute abscess, with drainage (Gopikrishna, 2021); Harty's (flare-up): drainage, analgesics, antibiotics (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017); Sikri: anti-inflammatories and antihistamines only, no incision (Sikri, 2019).

## 8. Radicular (apical) cyst: true versus pocket

### Explanation

Lin and Huang call the radicular cyst unique in pathogenesis (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], a cavity lined by non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], not a disease separate from chronic apical periodontitis (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's defines it as a closed epithelium-lined cavity of endodontic origin (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Formation.** Rests of Malassez proliferate in about 52% of lesions (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Nair found epithelium in 52% but cysts in only 15% (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. EGF and KGF stimulate the rests (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Theories: nutritional deficiency (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], abscess (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], merging of epithelial strands (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], and an immunologic theory (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; three stages (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Expansion: books disagree.** Fouad and Khan: pressure from the cyst fluid (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC], helped by bone-resorptive cytokines secreted by the epithelium (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. Ingle's: marsupialisation shrinking cysts supports osmotic pressure (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC], but pocket cysts also grow, and pro-resorptive mediators are the main cause of lesion growth (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Lin and Huang: the osmotic hypothesis overlooks cellular aspects (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC] and expansion is by MMP degradation of the capsule (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

**Types.** True: completely enclosed in epithelium (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], no communication with the canal (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Pocket: lumen in contact with the canal through the foramen (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC], originally "bay cyst" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], probably begun by neutrophils gathering at the foramen around biofilm (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. A cyst that looks true may show communication hundreds of sections away (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC].

**Prevalence.** Bhaskar found 42% cysts among 2,308 specimens and Nair 15% among 256 (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; Ricucci's serial-section study found 32% (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. Ranges: 15% to 20% in lesions from extracted teeth (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; 6% to 55% (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; 7% to 54% (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. The range reflects that some pathologists call any epithelium a cyst (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. About 75% occur in the maxilla (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; about 8% contain ciliated epithelium (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC].

**Error flagged.** Sikri writes that the periapical cyst "is an acute inflammatory cyst with a distinct pathologic cavity completely enclosed" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The context (contrasted with the pocket cyst, which Sikri calls "an apical inflammatory cyst") shows this describes the apical true cyst; Sikri's own preceding paragraph calls the periapical cyst a chronic inflammatory response (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and radicular cysts are chronic lesions (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; "acute" appears to be a slip for "apical". [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** an inflammatory lesion of endodontic origin with a closed cavity lined by epithelium (Gopikrishna, 2021): non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium with a fibrous capsule (Lin and Huang, 2021). It arises within chronic apical periodontitis and is not a separate disease (Lin and Huang, 2021).

**Origin:** epithelial rests of Malassez (remnants of Hertwig's sheath) stimulated by cytokines and growth factors (EGF, KGF) (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019). Epithelium in 52% of lesions, cysts in only 15% (Nair, cited by Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).

**Theories:** (1) nutritional deficiency (breakdown); (2) abscess; (3) merging/fusion of epithelial strands (Lin and Huang, 2021); (4) immunologic (Gopikrishna, 2021). Stages: proliferation of rests, cavity formation, expansion.

**Expansion (disputed):** fluid/osmotic pressure (Fouad and Khan, 2020; marsupialisation shrinks cysts) versus MMPs, pro-resorptive mediators and bone resorption (Lin and Huang, 2021; Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).

| | True cyst | Pocket (bay) cyst |
|---|---|---|
| Lumen | Completely enclosed in epithelium | Open to the root canal through the foramen |
| Origin | Within epithelialised lesion | Neutrophil microabscess at foramen walled by epithelium (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Diagnosis | Serial sections of lesion attached to apex | Same |
| Healing after RCT | Disputed (see synthesis) | Usually expected; Ingle's thinks pocket cysts may be more recalcitrant |

**Prevalence:** 15-20% (Lin and Huang, 2021); 6-55% (Gopikrishna, 2021); 7-54% (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021); variation from differing criteria (Fouad and Khan, 2020). 75% in maxilla.

**Clinical:** asymptomatic; large lesions displace roots; lumen may hold cholesterol crystals, fluid, bacteria. About 8% contain ciliated (respiratory-type) epithelium, mostly near the maxillary sinus (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).

**Diagnosis:** only by biopsy or extraction (Lin and Huang, 2021); true and pocket cysts can be told apart only on serial sections of the lesion attached to the apex, since communication may appear hundreds of sections away (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).

**Error in one book:** Sikri (2019) calls the true cyst "an acute inflammatory cyst"; radicular cysts are chronic (Lin and Huang, 2021).

## 9. Condensing osteitis and other periapical entities

### Explanation

Lin and Huang liken condensing osteitis to chronic osteomyelitis with proliferative periostitis (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's defines it as a diffuse radiopacity from a low-grade stimulus (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], attributed to low-grade infection or high tissue resistance (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. The radiopacity comes from narrowing of medullary spaces, not more calcification (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Site: mandibular first molar (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; mandibular molars with necrosis or asymptomatic pulpitis (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Age: children and young adults (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri says children and teenagers (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Lamina dura: Lin and Huang say usually intact (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Landwehr says a widened PDL space (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]: a small disagreement. Outcome: most remodel (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; 85% resolve to some extent (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]; may persist (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's: no treatment if the pulp is not irreversibly diseased (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; often confused with enostosis (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC].

Persistent apical periodontitis is post-treatment disease in a root-filled tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Independent extraradicular infection is uncommon, usually actinomycosis (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's calls it rare (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; it is almost invariably a sequel to intraradicular infection (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. A periapical scar is diagnosed by history (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and forms most often when both cortical plates are lost (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Condensing osteitis (focal sclerosing osteitis)**
- Diffuse periapical radiopacity from a low-grade inflammatory stimulus (Gopikrishna, 2021); analogous to chronic osteomyelitis with proliferative periostitis (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Radiopacity from narrowed marrow spaces (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019).
- Young patients; mandibular first molar; pulp vital (pulpitis) or necrotic (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Lamina dura usually intact (Lin and Huang, 2021) versus widened PDL (Landwehr, 2021).
- Treat the pulp; most remodel (85% resolve to some extent; Landwehr, 2021); no treatment of the lesion itself without irreversible pulp disease (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). Differential: idiopathic osteosclerosis (enostosis).

**Persistent (post-treatment) apical periodontitis:** intraradicular biofilm, E. faecalis, extraradicular infection (actinomycosis), cholesterol, foreign body, true cyst (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Periapical scar:** fibrous healing, often after loss of both cortical plates; no treatment (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 10. Diagnosis: clinical, radiographic and differential

### Explanation

There is no good correlation between symptoms and histology (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Lesions in cancellous bone may not be visible until the cortex is involved (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Histology is the gold standard (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; CBCT is more sensitive than periapical radiography (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's: all attempts to correlate radiographic appearance with histology failed (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; neither size nor shape indicates a cyst (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Landwehr: no way to separate cyst from granuloma on conventional imaging (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]; the old 1.5 cm rule is obsolete (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]; CBCT agreed with histology in only 76% in one study (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]. Fouad and Khan: radiography and CBCT attempts have not been successful (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. Sikri, against this, writes that a hazy lesion "is a granuloma" and a corticated one a cyst (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], before conceding on the next page that a small cyst cannot be told from a granuloma and that "positive differentiation between cyst and granuloma cannot be made from radiographs" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri therefore contradicts itself. [Certain] Ørstavik records that the historical emphasis on telling a cyst from a granuloma has been abandoned (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]. Lin and Huang note cysts may show a radiopaque rim (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], which is suggestive, not diagnostic. Cysts are diagnosed only by biopsy or extraction (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

**Non-endodontic lesions.** Many periapical lesions are non-endodontic (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri gives 10-12% (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Gutmann: the vast majority of biopsied lesions are granulomas and 0.7-5.0% prove to be other lesions (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. The figures differ (10-12% versus 0.7-5.0%) and are shown side by side. Clues: vital pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], intact lamina dura and normal pulp tests (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontic lesions should be unilocular with non-responsive pulps (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]; multilocular: OKC, ameloblastoma, central giant cell lesion (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]; radiopacity raises suspicion of non-inflammatory origin (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]; cortical perforation, mobility and irregular root resorption are ominous (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]; malignancy may cause numbness (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Anatomical radiolucencies move with angulation, cysts do not (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; a globulomaxillary cyst is unrelated to pulp death (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Clinical diagnosis** uses history, pulp sensibility tests, percussion, palpation, mobility, probing, sinus tracing and radiographs. Symptoms correlate poorly with histology; histology is the gold standard (Lin and Huang, 2021).

**Radiographic signs:** widened PDL, loss of lamina dura, periapical radiolucency, radiopacity (condensing osteitis), root blunting. Cancellous lesions may be invisible until the cortex is involved; CBCT is more sensitive (Lin and Huang, 2021).

**Cyst versus granuloma:** cannot be separated on radiographs (Gopikrishna, 2021; Landwehr, 2021) or reliably on CBCT (76% agreement; Landwehr, 2021; Fouad and Khan, 2020). Suggestive only: large size, corticated rim (Lin and Huang, 2021), root displacement. Sikri (2019) states a hazy lesion is a granuloma and a corticated one a cyst: contradicted by the other books and by Sikri's own next page.

**Differential diagnosis (non-endodontic lesions: 10-12%, Sikri, 2019; 0.7-5% of biopsies, Gutmann, 2017):**

| Feature | Endodontic lesion | Non-endodontic lesion |
|---|---|---|
| Pulp | Necrotic (non-responsive) | Usually vital (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Lamina dura | Lost | Often intact (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) |
| Locularity | Unilocular | Multilocular: OKC, ameloblastoma, CGCL (Landwehr, 2021) |
| Radiopacity | Absent (except condensing osteitis) | Raises suspicion |
| Angulation | Stays at apex | Anatomical shadows shift (Gopikrishna, 2021) |

Others: periapical cemento-osseous dysplasia, cementoblastoma, nasopalatine, globulomaxillary, lateral periodontal and traumatic bone cysts, metastases. Warning signs: cortical perforation, mobility, irregular root resorption, numbness (Landwehr, 2021; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021). Periodontal abscess: vital tooth, pocket. Biopsy when in doubt.

## 11. Outcomes and healing

### Explanation

Outcomes of acute apical periodontitis: restitution, abscess, or chronicity (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; an abscess heals by reabsorption of pus once the canal is disinfected (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Chronic apical periodontitis may regenerate or repair after RCT (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Healing is mostly regeneration (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's defines regeneration versus repair (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Macrophages secrete lipoxins, resolvins, protectins (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; resorbed root is repaired by cementoid tissue (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; the PDL is first affected and last restored (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]; healing proceeds by vascularity, collagen, osseous islands and cellular cementum (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. Sikri expects healing in 6 months to 1 year (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Scar forms when the periosteum is extensively destroyed (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Surgical healing is faster (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; non-surgical healing relies on biologic debridement (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

Diabetes reduces healing (Lin and Huang, 2021; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021; Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]; HIV patients heal as well as others (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. IL-1β genotype was found in 70.6% of persistent versus 24.6% of healed cases (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; polymorphisms in IL1B, MMP2, MMP3 are linked (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Type 2 diabetes raises prevalence (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Evidence that apical periodontitis is a focus of systemic infection is insufficient (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC].

Teeth with apical periodontitis heal in 78% of cases (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; outcome is 10% to 25% lower than without disease (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Outcomes of acute lesions:** restitution if irritants are removed; abscess if pyogenic bacteria invade; chronicity if irritants persist (Lin and Huang, 2021). **Chronic lesions:** repair after RCT, acute exacerbation, sinus tract, cellulitis.

**Healing after removal of canal infection**
- Inflammation resolves: PMN apoptosis; macrophages secrete lipoxins, resolvins, protectins (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Granulation tissue, collagen, osseous islands, cellular cementum (Fouad and Khan, 2020).
- Mostly regeneration; resorbed root repaired by cementoid tissue (Lin and Huang, 2021); PDL first affected, last restored (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021).
- Scar if both cortical plates and periosteum are destroyed.
- Time: about 6 months to 1 year (Sikri, 2019); surgical healing faster.

**Prognosis:** 78% of teeth with lesions heal after RCT (Lin and Huang, 2021); 10-25% lower than teeth without lesions (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017). Reduced by diabetes and some gene polymorphisms (IL-1β genotype in 70.6% of persistent versus 24.6% of healed cases); HIV patients heal normally (Lin and Huang, 2021).

**Systemic links:** type 2 diabetes is associated with higher prevalence of apical periodontitis, but evidence that it acts as a focus of infection for systemic disease is insufficient (Lin and Huang, 2021).

## 12. Management principles

### Explanation

Lin and Huang: treatment of the various types is basically the same, non-surgical RCT (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's: all periradicular diseases are treated alike (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Drainage relieves pain in acute cases (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]; Grossman's: establish drainage and control the systemic reaction (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's: incise any fluctuant swelling (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; leave a tooth open to drain for no longer than 24 hours (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; where the canal cannot be drained, incise or aspirate a fluctuant swelling through a large-bore needle (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

**Cysts: the main disagreement.** Grossman's: periapical rarefactions resolve after RCT in 80-98% of cases, and a percentage of these healed areas may have been cysts (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], so the figure is not a cyst healing rate; non-surgical RCT is the treatment of choice (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; the immune system removes the epithelium (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's reports 75% healing of large "cystic" lesions (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri: surgery is the treatment of choice (Sikri, 2019) [SRC] because cysts have least potential to heal without it (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Gutmann: advances have eliminated most of the old "cookbook" indications for surgery, presence of a cyst among them (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. See the synthesis below for true cysts.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle:** remove the cause, the infected canal; most periapical lesions are treated alike by non-surgical RCT (Lin and Huang, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).

| Condition | Management |
|---|---|
| SAP, vital tooth (occlusal trauma) | Relieve occlusion; review |
| SAP, necrotic pulp | Cleaning and shaping, Ca(OH)2, RCT; analgesics |
| Acute apical abscess | Drainage through canal (open for 24 h at most); incision and drainage of fluctuant swelling (Chandler and Chong, 2017); antibiotics only as an adjunct; prescribing them without drainage is incorrect (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017); then RCT |
| Phoenix abscess | As acute abscess (Gopikrishna, 2021); flare-up: drainage, analgesics, antibiotics (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017); Sikri (2019): anti-inflammatories, no incision |
| AAP / granuloma | RCT; recall |
| Chronic apical abscess | RCT; sinus heals without separate treatment |
| Cyst-like lesion | RCT first (80-98% of rarefactions resolve, some probably cysts; Gopikrishna, 2021); surgery if non-healing; Sikri (2019) advises surgery |
| Condensing osteitis | Treat the pulp if irreversibly diseased; otherwise review |
| Persistent AP | Retreatment, then surgery |
| Suspected non-endodontic lesion | Biopsy, referral |

**Follow-up:** clinical and radiographic review at 6 months, 1 year and later; surgery or extraction if the lesion persists.

**Conclusion.** Periapical pathosis is mostly a host defence against intracanal infection. Clinical labels guide treatment, histology gives the definitive diagnosis, and eliminating the canal infection lets most lesions, cysts included, heal.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "True apical cysts do not heal after non-surgical root canal treatment."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's states true cysts are less likely to heal because they are self-sustaining and surgery is needed (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], citing Nair. Sikri advises surgery for cysts (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Fouad and Khan record the argument that true cysts are few and resistant (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC].
*Challenge:* on the same page and later in the chapter Lin and Huang argue true cysts, like pocket cysts, may regress by apoptosis once infection is controlled (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC], and that a true cyst is unlike the self-sustaining odontogenic keratocyst (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's records the counter-argument (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]: a lesion that persists after its cause is removed is a neoplasm by definition (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC], and the pocket cyst is the more likely to be recalcitrant (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's says there has never been direct evidence for the "true cysts need surgery" hypothesis (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC] and that both lesion types heal equally (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC].
Ørstavik also writes that true cysts separated from the infection "may show impaired healing" and need surgery (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]. Against this, Ricucci and co-workers found bacteria in all true and bay cysts (48% and 52% of the cysts) and conclude there is no reason to think true cysts self-sustaining (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC].
*Strength:* Lin and Huang state there is no direct evidence either way (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; both positions rest on histology and reasoning, not on trials, because true cysts cannot be diagnosed before treatment (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; Fouad and Khan: "These hypotheses remain to be proven" (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC]. [Certain]
*Conclusion and implication:* the sources disagree and neither side is proven. Clinically the answer is the same: RCT first, surgery for lesions that fail to heal. [Likely]

**Claim 2: "Cysts and granulomas can be told apart radiographically."**
*Textbook:* Sikri says a hazy lesion is a granuloma and a corticated one a cyst (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], but on the next page says positive differentiation cannot be made from radiographs (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021), Landwehr (2021), Ingle's and Fouad and Khan (2020) say they cannot be separated on radiographs, and CBCT studies are inconsistent (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]. Cellular and molecular studies have also failed to show notable differences (Fouad and Khan, 2020) [SRC].
*Conclusion:* the large majority of sources reject radiographic diagnosis; biopsy with serial sections is required. Sikri's sentence should not be reproduced as fact. [Certain]

**Claim 3: "Apical periodontitis follows total pulp necrosis."**
*Textbook:* Grossman's says asymptomatic apical periodontitis develops only some time after the pulp has died (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].
*Refinement:* Lin and Huang, Ricucci and co-workers, Sikri, Fouad and Khan, and Sasaki and Stashenko all show periapical inflammation and bone loss starting before total necrosis (sections 4 and B5). The two views fit if Grossman's is read as referring to the radiographically visible chronic lesion. [Likely]
*Implication:* a tooth with a periapical radiolucency may still have vital apical tissue and may need anaesthesia.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- AAE terms: SAP, AAP, AAA, CAA, condensing osteitis; WHO codes 4.4-4.8.
- Kakehashi (germ-free rats) and Sundqvist (aseptic canals without lesions) prove bacterial aetiology.
- Lesion = second line of defence; bone resorption via RANKL/OPG; starts before total necrosis.
- Granuloma most common (40-50%); cysts 15% (Nair) to 32% (Ricucci, serial sections) to 42% (Bhaskar); quoted ranges 6-55%.
- True cyst enclosed; pocket (bay) cyst opens to canal; serial sections needed.
- No radiographic way to tell cyst from granuloma.
- Treatment of all forms is basically RCT; 78% of teeth with lesions heal.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Giving only one classification; the question expects AAE plus histopathological (and WHO) schemes.
- Stating that a radiograph or lesion size can diagnose a cyst.
- Writing that bacteria are usually present in a granuloma.
- Saying all cysts need surgery without stating the disagreement and the RCT-first rule.
- Treating condensing osteitis as always needing RCT even when the pulp is healthy.
