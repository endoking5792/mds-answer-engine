## 1. Sensibility versus vitality: definitions, classification and comparison -> answers "Compare and contrast between pulp sensibility and pulp vitality tests" 5m Mar 2018; "Describe the various diagnostic aids used to assess the status of the pulp" Mar 1992; "Vitality tests as diagnostic aids" 10m Apr 1998

### Explanation

**Terms.** Abbott, in Ingle's, is the strongest voice on wording: the tests have "traditionally" been called vitality tests "but this is an incorrect term to use"; "sensibility" is "the ability to respond to a stimulus" and "vitality" is "the capacity to live, grow, or develop", which in dentistry implies a viable blood supply (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. He also rejects "sensitivity tests" as an erroneous term (sensitivity means being easily irritated), and argues that "vital" and "nonvital" should no longer be used because about 12 pulp conditions could be called vital and 10 nonvital, each treated differently (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. The other books are not uniform. Torabinejad's book heads the section "Pulp Sensitivity Tests" and Harty's "Pulp sensitivity tests" (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Grossman's heads it "Assessment of pulp vitality" and calls thermal and electric tests "neural sensibility tests", noting that this is done "indirectly" through neural sensitivity and that "true vitality status" needs assessment of the blood supply (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri uses "pulp vitality tests" as the umbrella and says thermal and electric tests are "also called 'Pulp Sensibility Tests'", whereas laser Doppler flowmetry and pulse oximetry indicate the blood supply (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's defines the "pulp test (pulp sensibility test)" as a procedure to determine pulp status by electrical, mechanical or thermal stimuli or by assessing the blood supply (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. So in an exam, use "sensibility" for nerve-based tests and "vitality" for blood-flow tests, and say that Torabinejad's and Harty's books use "sensitivity" in the same sense [Certain].

**A flawed sentence in Cohen's.** Cohen's states that "the sensibility of the pulp is determined by the intactness and health of the vascular supply, not by the status of the pulpal nerve fibers" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. By Cohen's own definition (sensibility is the neural response) and by Weine's ("the vascularity rather than the innervation determines the vitality of pulp") and Okazaki et al's ("the vitality of the pulp is determined by the physiological function of blood supply") wording, the word should be "vitality", not "sensibility" (Weine, 2004; Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]. Read it as a slip [Likely].

**Why the distinction matters.** Sensibility tests assume that neural status reflects blood supply (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. The assumption fails both ways. After trauma, neural regeneration is slower than vascular regeneration, so a revascularising pulp may not respond (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]; conversely, nerve tissue can remain after the pulp tissue and vessels have necrosed, giving a misleading positive (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC]. Neither group of tests stages pulpitis: their "inability to stage the severity of pulpal disease" is the central limitation (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC], and the true status can only be determined histologically (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Abbott adds that a sensibility test answers two questions: does the pulp respond, and how (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

**The ideal test** is accurate, reproducible and noninjurious (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. **Uses** (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]: localising pain and excluding non-odontogenic pain, differentiating radiolucencies, preoperative and postoperative assessment (a pulp-capped tooth that stops responding is a failure), and follow-up of trauma.

**Classification.** Grossman's Box 6.10 lists neural sensibility tests (thermal, electric, anaesthetic test, test cavity) and pulp vascularity tests (pulse oximetry, laser Doppler flowmetry, and others: dual-wavelength spectrophotometry, thermography, crown surface temperature) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri adds tests of tooth temperature (cholesteric liquid crystals, electronic thermography, infrared thermography with the Hughes Probeye camera), the optical reflection vitalometer and photoplethysmography (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sigurdsson lists Xenon-133 radioisotope injection among experimental methods (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** The pulp cannot be inspected directly, so its status is inferred from indirect tests. Only histology gives the true status, and no clinical test stages pulpitis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Galicia and Khan, 2024). The ideal test is accurate, reproducible and noninjurious (Sikri, 2019).

**Definitions (Abbott, 2019)**
- **Sensibility:** "the ability to respond to a stimulus". Thermal and electric tests are sensibility tests.
- **Vitality:** "the capacity to live, grow, or develop"; in dentistry, a viable blood supply. Laser Doppler flowmetry and pulse oximetry are true vitality tests.
- Abbott calls "vitality test" for thermal or electric tests, and "sensitivity test", incorrect terms. Torabinejad's and Harty's books still use "pulp sensitivity tests" (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Chandler and Chong, 2017).

**Comparison**

| Feature | Sensibility tests | Vitality tests |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Response of A-delta nerve fibres | Pulpal blood flow or oxygen saturation |
| Examples | Cold, heat, EPT, test cavity | LDF, pulse oximetry, dual wavelength spectrophotometry, photoplethysmography |
| Nature | Subjective (patient reports) | Objective (instrument reading) |
| Reproduces symptoms | Yes (cold, heat) | No |
| After trauma | Often false negative | Detects revascularisation earlier |
| Immature teeth | Unreliable | Reliable |
| Cost, time | Cheap, quick, chairside | Expensive, slow, mostly research |

Key point: neural regeneration after trauma is slower than vascular regeneration, so blood-flow tests are, in principle, more sensitive (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007); nerve fibres may also survive in a necrotic pulp (Galicia and Khan, 2024). Weine: "the vascularity rather than the innervation determines the vitality of pulp" (Weine, 2004).

**Two questions a sensibility test answers (Abbott, 2019):** first, does the pulp respond (no response suggests necrosis or a pulpless canal); second, how does it respond (normal, exaggerated, lingering). Abbott also advises against "vital" and "nonvital" as diagnoses, because each covers many conditions with different treatments.

**Classification of pulp tests (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019)**
1. Tests of neural status (sensibility): thermal (cold, heat); electric pulp test; anaesthetic test; test cavity.
2. Tests of blood supply (vitality): laser Doppler flowmetry; pulse oximetry; dual wavelength spectrophotometry; optical reflection vitalometer; photoplethysmography.
3. Tests of tooth temperature: cholesteric liquid crystals; electronic thermography; infrared thermography (Hughes Probeye camera).
4. Experimental and newer: Xenon-133 (Sigurdsson, 2020); ultrasound Doppler; pulpal inflammatory biomarkers.

**Uses:** locate the painful tooth and exclude non-odontogenic pain; differentiate periapical radiolucencies; assess the pulp before restorative, orthodontic or periodontal treatment; review after pulp capping and after trauma (Sikri, 2019).

## 2. Thermal tests: cold and heat -> answers "Pulp sensibility test" 10m Jun 2016; "Write about Pulp vitality Tests" 10m Sep 2007 (Cons); "Pulp vitality testing" 10m Dec 2021 (with section 3)

### Explanation

**Mechanism.** Cold contracts dentinal fluid and the outward hydrodynamic flow stimulates A-delta fibres (Sikri, 2019; Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Grossman's names this Brännström's theory (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cold also relieves the pain of acute pulpitis by vasoconstriction, which lowers intrapulpal pressure; heat causes vasodilation and raises intrapulpal pressure (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], Van Hassel's theory in Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. A normal response is felt "but disappears immediately" on removal; abnormal responses are no response, a lingering or intensifying pain, or an immediate excruciating pain (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. A heightened and lingering response to cold suggests symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Cold agents and temperatures.** The books give different temperatures for the same agents, shown side by side:

| Agent | Temperature, by author |
|---|---|
| Ice | About 0°C; melts and drips onto gingiva and adjacent teeth (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Abbott, 2019) [SRC] |
| Ethyl chloride | "4°C" (Sikri, 2019); "only about -4°C" (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC] |
| Dichlorodifluoromethane | -21°C (Sikri, 2019) [SRC] |
| Tetrafluoroethane spray | -26.2°C (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Sigurdsson, 2020); -15 to -26°C (Sikri, 2019); -50°C (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC] |
| CO2 snow (dry ice) | -78°C (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021); -78.5°C (Chandler and Chong, 2017); -56 to -98°C (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC] |

Harty's -50°C for tetrafluoroethane disagrees with the -26.2°C of Cohen's and Sigurdsson; Abbott gives about -50°C for the propane/butane/isobutane mixture and about -26°C for tetrafluoroethane, so Harty's figure probably belongs to the gas mixture [Likely]. Sikri's "4°C" for ethyl chloride is probably a lost minus sign [Likely].

**Which cold agent: a real disagreement.** Abbott (citing Chen and Abbott) finds dry ice sticks (Odontotest) "the most reliable cold test", cheapest and quickest, and sprays "generally less effective than dry ice, especially when testing teeth with porcelain crowns" (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's book says the opposite: refrigerant sprays are "the most convenient and reliable means of cold testing" and give the most accurate results through porcelain-fused-to-metal crowns (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's calls the spray "the most popular method", reproducible and equivalent to CO2 (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], and Sigurdsson reports "no difference" between tetrafluoroethane and CO2 ice (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC]. Miller et al, cited by Abbott, showed that PFM and ceramic crowns do not insulate the pulp (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Technique detail: a large #2 cotton pellet cools more than a small #4 pellet (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Dachi recommended a quarter-inch cone of ice for 5 seconds (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; ice for 5 to 8 seconds increases sensitivity (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]; "the colder the applied stimulus the more reliable the test" (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. False negatives are common with calcific metamorphosis; gingival recession and attachment loss decrease the response to cold (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Heat.** Indicated when the chief complaint is pain with hot food or drink; wait 10 seconds between teeth because responses may be delayed (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; delayed responses are commoner with heat than cold (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Temperature: gutta-percha above 65.5°C (Gopikrishna, 2021; Weine, 2004) [SRC]; 65°C preferred (Sikri, 2019) and recommended for the System B (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; heat for no more than 5 seconds because longer application stimulates C fibres (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. **The methods are disputed.** The dry rubber prophy cup is "a safe alternative" in Torabinejad's book, which also says hot water and heated metal instruments "can be damaging to the dental pulp and their use should be avoided" (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's says the rubber-wheel method "is not recommended" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Abbott and Harty reject it because the vibration may be felt as pain (Abbott, 2019; Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Weine uses a rubber wheel on a gold crown as "an excellent hot test" (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Hot water under rubber dam is used by Cohen's, Grossman's ("coffee-hot") and Abbott (heavy-body dam) (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021; Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. A heated burnisher is held 1 to 2 mm from the tooth (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

**Value of heat.** Abbott: normal pulps do not respond to heat, so heat cannot separate normal from necrotic (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Sigurdsson calls it "probably a more clinically informative" test (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC]. Heat responses can occur in root-treated teeth with missed anatomy (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC], and in gangrenous pulps through gas expansion (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC].

**Heat pain relieved by cold.** Cohen's: "a tooth that responds to heat and then is relieved by cold is found to be necrotic" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's places the same picture under irreversible pulpitis, where cold "would cause temporary relief of pain" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Both are reported; clinically the tooth is heading for or already in necrosis and needs root canal treatment either way [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** Thermal tests stimulate A-delta fibres by hydrodynamic fluid movement: cold contracts dentinal fluid (Brännström); heat causes vasodilation and raised intrapulpal pressure (Van Hassel) (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019). Their merit is that they reproduce the patient's symptoms.

**Cold test**
- Agents: ice sticks (0°C), ethyl chloride (about -4°C), tetrafluoroethane spray (-26.2°C), CO2 snow (-78°C) (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Sigurdsson, 2020; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Technique: control tooth first; isolate and dry; large #2 cotton pellet sprayed and placed on the middle third of the facial surface (Berman and Rotstein, 2021); ice for 5 to 8 seconds (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007).
- Merits: cheap, quick, most reliable single test (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021); usable on crowns (CO2, spray); differentiates reversible from irreversible pulpitis.
- Demerits: ice drips (false positive); false negatives in calcified teeth; reduced response with recession (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Best agent is disputed: dry ice is most reliable (Abbott, 2019) versus spray is most convenient and reliable, even through PFM crowns (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021); no difference between them (Sigurdsson, 2020).

**Heat test**
- Indication: pain with hot food or drink (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Agents: heated gutta-percha (above 65.5°C), hot water under rubber dam, System B tip at 65°C, hot burnisher held 1 to 2 mm away, rubber wheel (Gopikrishna, 2021; Abbott, 2019; Sikri, 2019).
- Technique: lubricate the tooth; apply for no more than 5 seconds (Chandler and Chong, 2017); start distally; wait 10 seconds between teeth (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Merits: reproduces heat pain; hot water bathes crowned teeth.
- Demerits: least accurate test; risk of burns; normal pulps may not respond (Abbott, 2019); false responses in gangrenous or root-filled teeth (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Rubber cup: "safe alternative" (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) versus not recommended because of vibration (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Chandler and Chong, 2017).
- Hot water: used under rubber dam by Berman and Rotstein (2021), Gopikrishna (2021) and Abbott (2019), but "should be avoided" as damaging in Torabinejad's book (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Heat value: Abbott (2019) says normal pulps do not respond to heat; Sigurdsson (2020) calls heat "probably a more clinically informative" test.

**Interpretation (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019)**

| Response | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Mild, subsides in 1 to 2 s, like control | Normal pulp |
| Sharp, short, subsides quickly | Reversible pulpitis |
| Intense, lingering | Irreversible pulpitis |
| Heat pain relieved by cold | Irreversible pulpitis (Gopikrishna, 2021) or necrosis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) |
| No response | Necrosis or false negative; confirm with EPT |

## 3. Electric pulp testing -> answers "Pulp sensibility test" 10m Jun 2016; "Pulp vitality tests" 7m May 2013; "Pulp vitality testing" 10m Dec 2021 (with section 2)

### Explanation

**History and principle.** Gutmann and Lovdahl date the electric pulp tester to 1867; Abbott dates its first reported use to Raper in 1921 (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. The two dates need not conflict (invention versus first clinical report) [Likely]. EPT delivers a high-frequency current that causes ionic changes in dentinal fluid and stimulates A-delta fibres (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; a current-measuring instrument is preferable to voltage measurement because enamel resistance varies (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC].

**Technique.** Isolate and dry; test the control tooth first; coat the probe with toothpaste; place it on the incisal third of anteriors (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], the mid-third of the mesiobuccal cusp of molars and buccal cusp of premolars (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], near the pulp horn where nerve density is highest (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]. Thresholds rise from incisor to premolar to molar with enamel and dentine thickness (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Gloves break the circuit, so use a lip clip, the patient's finger on the handle, or the operator's ungloved hand (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Increase current slowly (up to 4.0 mA, Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Repeat counts differ: test at least twice (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; not more than twice because nerve tissue "may react at a lower threshold" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; not more than four times (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. For crowns, bridge with an explorer touching exposed tooth structure (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

**Do the numbers mean anything? The books disagree.** Grossman's interprets an "early response" (lower than control) and "delayed response" (higher) as diseased pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Weine calls a much lower reading "hyperactive" and a much higher one "degenerating" (Weine, 2004) [SRC]; Okazaki et al say an inflamed tooth can "respond at lower values" (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]; Cohen's allows significance only if the reading differs significantly from a control (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Against this, Abbott: a pulpitic tooth "does not necessarily respond at a lower level of current" (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Torabinejad's book: "The exact number of the reading is of no significance" (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's: the readout is "relative" (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; the trauma atlas: the belief that low readings mean acute pulpitis is not supported by recent investigations (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Scales differ between devices (0 to 80, 0 to 4, 0 to 5) and do not indicate current (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; EPTs apply power on a logarithmic scale, so a response only at the highest settings cannot be accepted as normal (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].

**Where EPT is strong.** Results are "most accurate when no response is obtained" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Seltzer et al, cited by Sigurdsson, found complete necrosis in 72% and localised necrosis in 25.7% of non-responding teeth (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC]. EPT may be more accurate than cold in calcific metamorphosis because it does not rely on fluid flow (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Gutmann: if a tooth responds to hot or cold, EPT "may be redundant" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. EPT does not induce pain and cannot reproduce symptoms (Abbott, 2019) [SRC], though Harty's states a lingering dull ache after EPT reflects C-fibre stimulation and irreversible pulpitis (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

**False responses.** Positive: partial necrosis, anxiety, poor isolation, metal restorations (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; saliva, splints and bridges, current passing to the attachment apparatus through liquefaction necrosis (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; gangrenous pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; acute alveolar abscess, which Weine considers rare (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Negative: calcification, recent trauma, immature apex, analgesic or sedative drugs, poor contact (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; sclerotic dentine, flat batteries (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; fibrotic pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. EPTs give false results up to 20% of the time (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Contraindicated with cardiac pacemakers (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** A graded electric current causes ionic changes in dentinal fluid and stimulates A-delta fibres; a "tingling" or "warm" sensation shows that viable nerve fibres exist (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**Parts:** main unit with rheostat and display, probe electrode, lip clip (Sikri, 2019).

**Technique**
1. Explain the test; test the contralateral or adjacent control tooth first.
2. Isolate with cotton rolls and dry; remove calculus.
3. Coat the probe with toothpaste; place on the incisal third (anteriors) or mid-third of the buccal cusp (posteriors), near the pulp horn and away from gingiva and restorations (Gopikrishna, 2021).
4. Complete the circuit: lip clip or patient's finger on the handle, since gloves break it (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
5. Increase current slowly; record the reading; repeat (at least twice, Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
6. Crowned tooth: bridge with an explorer to exposed tooth structure.

**Interpretation.** Response = viable nerve fibres; no response = probable necrosis, the most accurate result (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Whether the number matters is disputed: early response means diseased pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021; Weine, 2004) versus the number has no significance (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Abbott, 2019).

**Points of disagreement**
- Repeats: at least twice (Berman and Rotstein, 2021); not more than twice (Sikri, 2019); not more than four times (Weine, 2004), because stimulation becomes additive.
- Cold or EPT first: if a tooth responds to hot or cold, EPT "may be redundant" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- Power rises on a logarithmic scale, so a response only at the highest setting is not normal (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- No response is strong evidence: Seltzer et al, cited by Sigurdsson, found complete or localised necrosis in 97.7% of non-responding teeth (Sigurdsson, 2020).

**Merits:** quick; quantitative display; not painful; no reliance on fluid flow, so useful in calcified teeth (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021); reliable when there is no response.

**Demerits:** does not show inflammation or blood supply; needs exposed tooth structure; false readings in multirooted teeth; unreliable in immature and recently traumatised teeth; contraindicated with pacemakers (Sikri, 2019); false results up to 20% (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).

| False positive | False negative |
|---|---|
| Partial necrosis (multirooted) | Calcified canals, sclerotic dentine |
| Anxiety | Recent trauma |
| Saliva, poor isolation | Immature apex |
| Contact with metal, gingiva | Analgesics, sedatives |
| Splints, bridges | Poor contact, large restorations, flat battery |
| Liquefaction necrosis, gangrene | Fibrotic pulp |

## 4. Merits and demerits compared: false responses, sensitivity, specificity, predictive values and accuracy -> answers "Describe the merits and demerits of evaluation technique[s] which diagnose the pulp status" Apr 2003 (core); "Pulp vitality testing" 10m Dec 2021

### Explanation

**Definitions.** Sensitivity is the capacity to diagnose pathology (pulp necrosis) and specificity is "the capacity to diagnose correctly a healthy status"; 100% of both is "a utopian situation which presently does not exist" (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. A test favouring sensitivity leads to root canal treatment of vital or healing pulps; where pulp-related root resorption threatens, "a high specificity" should be used (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. PPV is the probability that a tooth without a response has a necrotic pulp; NPV is the probability that a responding tooth is vital (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC].

**A slip in Cohen's.** Cohen's writes "The sensibility, which is the ability of a test to identify teeth that are diseased, was 0.83 for the cold test..." (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. The statistic described is sensitivity, not sensibility; read it so [Certain].

Combined cold plus EPT: NPV 97% and PPV 90% (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC] and "a high DOR" (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC].

**Clinical studies quoted by Abbott (2019) [SRC].** Chen and Abbott: CO2 most likely to identify pulp disease, especially in restored teeth; EPT accurate when there is no disease but unreliable when disease is present; ice of little value. Fuss et al: CO2 and EPT most reliable, ice and ethyl chloride unreliable. Peters et al (1488 pulps in 60 adults): no response to both CO2 and EPT almost certainly indicates disease; response to CO2 but not EPT is probably a false EPT result. The heat test was least accurate and least specific. Weislader et al: 97% of teeth responding to all three tests bled on access; combinations of cold and electric tests were more accurate.

**General demerits.** No test reflects true histology (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; correlation with histology is poor (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]; results are "more likely to be correct when teeth are healthy than diseased" (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; responses differ on different days and lack reproducibility (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; methods are non-standardised (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC]; 800 mg ibuprofen one hour before testing alters results (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC]. A multirooted tooth with one vital root may respond although other roots are necrotic (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. No response to both cold and EPT in a mature, non-traumatised tooth indicates necrosis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], hence Abbott's advice to own both systems (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definitions (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007; Okazaki et al, 2017)**
- **Sensitivity:** ability to identify disease (necrotic pulp gives no response).
- **Specificity:** ability to identify health (vital pulp responds).
- **PPV:** probability that a non-responding tooth is necrotic. **NPV:** probability that a responding tooth is vital.
- **Accuracy:** proportion of all teeth correctly classified.
- 100% of both is "utopian"; where root resorption threatens after trauma, choose high specificity.

**Merits and demerits**

Cohen's labels sensitivity as "sensibility"; this is a slip. The books report different values for apparently the same study (Petersson et al, 1999, named by Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Combination.** Cold plus EPT gives NPV 97% and PPV 90% (Okazaki et al, 2017); if a mature, non-traumatised tooth fails both, the pulp is necrotic (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Peters et al, cited by Abbott: response to CO2 but not EPT means the EPT is probably false (Abbott, 2019).

**Clinical studies (cited by Abbott, 2019):** CO2 was most likely to identify pulp disease (Chen and Abbott); 97% of teeth responding to cold, CO2 and EPT bled on access (Weislader et al); heat was least accurate.

**Common demerits:** subjective; poor correlation with histology; cannot stage pulpitis; non-standardised; ibuprofen alters results (Sigurdsson, 2020); multirooted teeth with partial necrosis; results more reliable in healthy than diseased teeth (Chandler and Chong, 2017).

## 5. Laser Doppler flowmetry -> answers "Laser doppler flowmetry" 10m Apr 2001; "Discuss the various diagnostic tools for assessing the pulpal blood flow" 10m Oct 2012; "Pulp vascularity tests" 10m Oct 2024 (with section 6)

### Explanation

**Principle.** Light directed through the crown is scattered; it shifts in frequency when it hits moving red blood cells but stays unshifted in static tissue (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri describes a He-Ne laser (632.8 nm) through a fibreoptic probe with one fibre emitting and another receiving, measuring a small volume of about 10 mm3; the output is proportional to red cell flux, "volume of cells illuminated × mean cell velocity" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's says a diode projects an infrared beam (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; the two describe different laser sources, both used [Likely]. Harty's: the beam travels along enamel prisms and dentinal tubules; Fourier analysis can improve accuracy (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

**Technique points.** Rubber dam to avoid gingival interference (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; a stent of rubber-based impression material or acrylic to block periodontal blood flow and stabilise the probe (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Probe 2 to 3 mm from the facial gingiva (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]; Ikeda and Suda also recommend a 633-nm source 2 to 3 mm above the gingival margin and a dual-probe system (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]. Perfusion is higher supine than sitting (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC].

**Evidence for.** Several studies found LDF accurate, reliable and reproducible; its data are objective, and it is "a great indicator" in luxation injuries (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. LDF was "far better than EPT and ethyl chloride" (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. In traumatised incisors without EPT response, LDF sensitivity was 97% and specificity 100% (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]; it detects revascularisation 3 to 4 months before EPT, moving detection from 6 to 3 months after injury; in 80 patients with luxated incisors, a cut-off of 2.9 at splint removal gave sensitivity 70% and specificity 93% (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Reliability above 80 to 90% (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]; accuracy 97% (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC]. Harty's calls it "the most promising technique" (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

**Evidence against.** Up to 80% of the signal may come from periodontal tissue (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]; gingival vessels give false readings, systemic drugs alter flow, it is motion-sensitive, molars give poor reflection, and equipment is expensive (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; blood pigment in a discoloured crown interferes, and each tooth takes 10 to 15 min (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]; Chen and Abbott found it less reliable than CO2 and EPT (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; "technique sensitive" (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC]. **Restored teeth: the books disagree.** Harty's: "it may be used on extensively restored or carious teeth" (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Galicia and Khan: "does not work well if the tooth is heavily restored" (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC]; Ikeda and Suda: contraindicated in heavily restored teeth (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]; Chen and Abbott: suitable for unrestored teeth (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Modification: colour power Doppler shows the direction of flow (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Laser Doppler flowmetry (LDF) is a noninvasive, objective method of measuring pulpal blood flow, a true vitality test (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Abbott, 2019).

**Principle (Doppler shift).** A low-power laser beam (He-Ne, 632.8 nm, Sikri, 2019; or infrared diode, Berman and Rotstein, 2021) is carried by a fibreoptic probe through enamel and dentine into the pulp. Light scattered by moving red blood cells is frequency-shifted; light from static tissue is not. A second fibre returns the light to a photodetector; the signal is proportional to red cell flux (number × velocity of cells in about 10 mm3) (Sikri, 2019).

**Technique**
1. Rubber dam or opaque stent (impression material or acrylic) to exclude gingival blood flow and hold the probe (Abbott, 2019).
2. Probe on the labial surface 2 to 3 mm from the gingival margin (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007).
3. Patient still; compare with a control tooth; record the trace.

**Merits**
- Objective; no patient response needed, so useful in children and anxious patients (Sikri, 2019).
- Measures blood flow, the real marker of vitality.
- In trauma: sensitivity 97%, specificity 100% (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007); detects revascularisation about 3 months earlier than EPT.
- Monitors replanted teeth and immature teeth.
- Reliability above 80 to 90% (Ikeda and Suda, 2012); accuracy 97% (Galicia and Khan, 2024).

**Demerits**
- Up to 80% of the signal may be periodontal (Ikeda and Suda, 2012); gingival vessels give false readings.
- Expensive; 10 to 15 minutes per tooth; needs a custom jig (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007; Sikri, 2019).
- Motion artefact; systemic drugs alter flow; poor signal in molars; crown discoloration interferes.
- Restored teeth disputed: usable (Chandler and Chong, 2017) versus not (Galicia and Khan, 2024; Ikeda and Suda, 2012).
- Measures coronal flow only; not routine in practice (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**Evidence in trauma (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007):** in 80 patients with luxated incisors, a blood-flow value of 2.9 at splint removal separated pulps that revascularised from those that became necrotic (sensitivity 70%, specificity 93%). Chen and Abbott, cited by Abbott (2019), found LDF suitable for unrestored teeth but less reliable than CO2 and EPT.

**Variables:** perfusion is higher supine than sitting; only coronal flow is measured (Ikeda and Suda, 2012).

**Advances:** dual-probe systems, Fourier analysis (Chandler and Chong, 2017), colour power Doppler (Sikri, 2019).

## 6. Pulse oximetry and other blood-flow and newer methods -> answers "Pulp vascularity tests" 10m Oct 2024; "Discuss the various diagnostic tools for assessing the pulpal blood flow" 10m Oct 2012; "Recent advances in pulp sensibility testing" 10m Jul 2023; "Recent developments in vitality testing of tooth" 10m Apr 2015; "Discuss the new diagnostic methods of testing pulp vitality" 15m Mar 2007; "Recent advances in the assessment of pulp vitality" 7m Jun 2017; "Recent advances in diagnostic aids in pulp vitality tests" 7m May 2019

### Explanation

**Pulse oximetry.** It transmits red and infrared light through tissue; absorption depends on the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated haemoglobin, and a photodetector on the opposite side allows calculation of pulse rate and saturation (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. The infrared wavelength differs between books: 800 nm (Sikri, 2019) and 940 nm (Gopikrishna, 2021); both give red at 660 nm [SRC]. Merits: detects pulpal circulation independent of gingival circulation, useful in trauma (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; custom-made sensors were more accurate than electric and thermal tests (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; accuracy 97% (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC]. Demerits: pulpal flow is not pulsatile because of arterioles, enamel and dentine insulate, and only bound haemoglobin is detected (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; restorations obstruct light (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; no specifically designed probes (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; "still in developmental stage and has not been commercialized yet" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; "no pulse oximeter device licensed for dental" use (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC]; more research needed (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]; too cumbersome for routine use (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

**Other blood-flow methods.** Dual wavelength spectrophotometry measures oxygenation in the capillary bed and does not depend on pulsatile flow; small, portable, inexpensive, uses filtered visible light (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Optical reflection vitalometer: same principle as pulse oximetry with reflected light (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Photoplethysmography detects optical density changes with red and infrared light (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; transmitted-light photoplethysmography may suit young permanent teeth, with peak signal at 565 nm (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]. Xenon-133 radioisotope injection is experimental (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC]. Ultrasound Doppler: Sikri's reference list includes a 2010 study of pulpal blood flow measurement with ultrasound Doppler imaging (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; no book read describes its clinical use for pulp testing, so details are [RECALL].

**Surface temperature.** Cholesteric liquid crystals (Howell et al, 1970) change colour with temperature but are "no longer advocated"; electronic thermography measures surface temperature change; infrared thermography (Hughes Probeye camera) detects changes of 0.1°C; the patient must be at rest and acclimatised (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's adds thermocouples and thermistors and calls most of these experimental (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

**State of the field.** Torabinejad's book: blood-flow methods are "on the horizon" but "more experimental than clinically practical" and expensive (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Abbott: unsuitable for routine use, and they do not reproduce symptoms (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Sigurdsson: never made available commercially (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC]; Ikeda and Suda: concerns about sensitivity, specificity and ease of use (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC]; Gutmann and Lovdahl: no new method has been refined for clinical use (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. The newest direction is biological: IL-8 and IL-6 discriminate healthy pulps from irreversible pulpitis, but no marker yet separates reversible from irreversible pulpitis (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Rationale.** Sensibility tests show nerve function only. Tests of blood flow or oxygenation are true vitality tests and are the recent advances (Gopikrishna, 2021; Abbott, 2019).

**1. Pulse oximetry**
- Principle: two LEDs, red (660 nm) and infrared (800 nm, Sikri, 2019; 940 nm, Gopikrishna, 2021), and a photodetector on the other side of the tooth. Oxygenated and reduced haemoglobin absorb these differently; the pulsatile change gives saturation and pulse rate (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Merits: objective; independent of gingival circulation; useful in trauma; custom sensors more accurate than thermal and electric tests (Berman and Rotstein, 2021); accuracy 97% (Galicia and Khan, 2024).
- Demerits: pulpal flow is weakly pulsatile; enamel and dentine scatter light; restorations block it; no licensed dental device (Galicia and Khan, 2024); "not commercialized yet" (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**2. Dual wavelength spectrophotometry:** measures capillary oxygenation, not pulse; small, portable, cheap; visible light (Sikri, 2019).

**3. Optical reflection vitalometer:** pulse-oximetry principle using reflected light (Sikri, 2019).

**4. Photoplethysmography:** light source and photodetector record optical density changes from pulpal blood volume; transmitted-light type suits young permanent teeth, peak 565 nm (Ikeda and Suda, 2012).

**5. Tooth surface temperature (Sikri, 2019)**
- Cholesteric liquid crystals (Howell et al, 1970): colour change; no longer advocated.
- Electronic thermography.
- Infrared thermography (Hughes Probeye camera): detects 0.1°C changes.
- Demerit: patient must rest and acclimatise.

**6. Others:** Xenon-133 radioisotope (Sigurdsson, 2020); ultrasound Doppler imaging of pulpal blood flow (listed by Sikri, 2019) [RECALL for details]; laser Doppler flowmetry (section 5).

**7. Biomarkers:** IL-8 and IL-6 distinguish healthy pulp from irreversible pulpitis but not reversible from irreversible (Galicia and Khan, 2024).

**Thermocouples and thermistors** also measure crown temperature; most of these methods are experimental (Chandler and Chong, 2017).

**Merits of the group:** objective, independent of patient reporting, not painful, and they measure the real marker of vitality. **Demerits:** concerns about sensitivity, specificity and ease of use (Ikeda and Suda, 2012); no new method has been refined for clinical application (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).

**Appraisal.** These methods are objective and suit trauma and immature teeth, but remain experimental, costly and slow (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Sigurdsson, 2020); thermal and electric tests remain the primary clinical methods (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

## 7. Special situations: trauma, immature teeth, crowned and calcified teeth, test cavity -> answers "Pulp vitality testing" 10m Dec 2021 (clinical application); see sibling answer Q8 for "Test cavity" 10m

### Explanation

**Trauma.** Pulp testing after injury is "a controversial issue"; testing at the time of injury establishes a point of reference (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Responses may be lost after luxation and return after weeks or months; loosened teeth may hurt from probe pressure, so reposition and splint first, and test before giving local anaesthetic (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. The time to recovery differs between books: within 2 months (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]; negative for up to 3 months after root fracture (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; 3 to 6 months (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. EPT is "not recommended" for concussed teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], but Weine calls EPT "excellent" for traumatised anteriors (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Splints and steel crowns divert current: keep the electrode at least 1 mm from metal or use CO2 snow (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC].

**Immature teeth.** Immature teeth lack fully developed A-delta fibres (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Weine says these develop only in late adolescence and EPT has "dubious value on youngsters" (Weine, 2004) [SRC]; Sigurdsson attributes it to the incomplete plexus of Raschkow (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC], while the trauma atlas cites a study finding myelinated fibres in the odontoblast layer regardless of stage (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Cold is more reliable than EPT in immature teeth (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; CO2 gives consistent results even in immature teeth (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Children: Sikri calls testing inconclusive in children (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], but Hori et al, cited by Abbott, showed electric, cold and heat tests reliable in primary teeth (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

**Older, orthodontic and calcified teeth.** Weaker response in aged patients (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; higher thresholds during orthodontic movement (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC], where CO2 is more reliable than EPT (Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC]. A calcified (PCC) tooth may not respond to cold; this is only recognised radiographically, and a calcified tooth without periapical change does not need root canal treatment (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Thermal and electric tests cannot assess the apical pulp after pulpotomy (Ikeda and Suda, 2012) [SRC].

**Crowned teeth.** CO2 snow is the test of choice (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; cold works through all crown types (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; EPT needs a bridging technique (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

**CO2 and enamel: a disagreement.** Sikri: CO2 "may cause infarction lines on enamel" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; the trauma atlas reports new infraction lines but adds that this "has not been substantiated in later studies" (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]; Baumann and Beer: cracks may occur at the earliest after 2 minutes (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Cohen's and Sigurdsson: no damage to enamel or pulp (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Sigurdsson, 2020) [SRC].

**Test cavity: the widest disagreement.** Weine: "unquestionably the most accurate of the pulp vitality tests", a last resort, cut "without water spray" for maximum heat (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Cohen's: invasive and irreversible, used only when other tests are impossible or inconclusive, cut with coolant; pain "signifies only that there is some viable nerve tissue" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's: pain indicates vitality and "no endodontic treatment is indicated"; the anaesthetic test is preferable (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's book lists it as an adjunct (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Baumann and Beer as "the last diagnostic method of choice" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Against it: in replanted teeth pain appeared only at the dentine-pulp border (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]; necrotic pulps may still have viable A-delta fibres (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; in calcified teeth it may expose the pulp and commit the tooth to treatment (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; Abbott calls it unspecific, likely to give false responses, "a barbaric procedure" with "no place in modern dentistry" (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Traumatised teeth**
- Test at the first visit as a baseline; reposition and splint before testing; test before local anaesthetic (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007).
- Loss of response is common after luxation and may return: within 2 months (Okazaki et al, 2017), up to 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021), 3 to 6 months (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). Do not start root canal treatment on a negative test alone.
- EPT not recommended for concussed teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021); Weine (2004) disagrees.
- Splints and steel crowns divert current: keep 1 mm from metal or use CO2 snow.
- LDF detects revascularisation about 3 months earlier than EPT.

**2. Immature teeth:** A-delta fibres not fully developed (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021); EPT unreliable; cold, especially CO2, more reliable (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007); monitor root development. Children can be tested (Hori et al, cited by Abbott, 2019), although Sikri (2019) finds tests inconclusive in children.

**3. Older, calcified and orthodontic teeth:** weaker or absent response because of reduced neural tissue and narrowed tubules (Sikri, 2019); higher thresholds during orthodontic movement (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007); after pulpotomy, neither thermal nor electric tests show the state of the apical pulp (Ikeda and Suda, 2012); a calcified tooth without periapical change needs no treatment (Abbott, 2019); CO2 more reliable than EPT during orthodontic movement (Sigurdsson, 2020).

**4. Crowned teeth:** CO2 snow is the test of choice (Gopikrishna, 2021); cold penetrates PFM and ceramic crowns (Abbott, 2019); EPT by bridging with an explorer (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**5. CO2 and enamel:** infraction lines possible (Sikri, 2019); cracks only after at least 2 minutes of application (Baumann and Beer, 2010); not substantiated in later studies (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007); no damage (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Sigurdsson, 2020).

**6. Test cavity (last resort)**
- Technique: no anaesthetic; small class I cavity with a high-speed round bur; stop and restore when the patient feels pain (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- For: "most accurate" (Weine, 2004); useful for crowned teeth when other tests fail.
- Against: invasive and irreversible; pain shows only viable nerve fibres; necrotic pulps may respond; calcified teeth may not respond until exposure (Chandler and Chong, 2017; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); "barbaric", "no place in modern dentistry" (Abbott, 2019).
- Alternative: selective anaesthetic test (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 8. Note on non-odontogenic pain -> answers the second part of "Discuss the recent advances in pulp vitality testing. Add a note on non-odontogenic pain" 20m May 2009 (Q7 answers the differential diagnosis of pain separately)

### Explanation

**Why it belongs here.** A normal response to pulp testing eliminates pulp pathology as the cause of pain (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Pain felt away from its true source is heterotopic: referred, central or projected; referred pain is not stopped unless the primary source is anaesthetised (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC].

**Classification.** Cohen's groups non-odontogenic toothache into five: musculoskeletal and somatic, neurovascular (headache), neuropathic, pain from a pathologic process, and psychogenic (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter lists musculoskeletal, joint, neurovascular, neuropathic (including atypical odontalgia and neuroma), referred (cervical spine, vascular, cardiac, sinus), neoplasia and salivary gland causes (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Key entities (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC].** Myofascial pain: muscular pains "appear to be the most common"; trigger points in the masseter, temporalis and lateral pterygoid refer dull aching pain; local anaesthetic into the trigger point resolves it. Sinus or nasal pain: pressure below the eyes, tenderness of several maxillary teeth. Migraine lasts 4 to 72 hours; cluster headache is relieved by 100% oxygen in 10 minutes; paroxysmal hemicrania responds 100% to indomethacin. Trigeminal neuralgia: slight pressure on a trigger zone gives severe pain; carbamazepine is first-line; a patient under 40 should be screened for multiple sclerosis. Herpes zoster pain can precede the vesicles by days or weeks. Persistent dentoalveolar pain disorder (PDAP) is the new term for atypical odontalgia; when local anaesthetic no longer helps, the neuropathy has a central component. Cardiac pain may present solely in the lower left jaw. Tumours often show sensory loss along the nerve. Psychogenic toothache is a somatoform disorder. In diagnostic anaesthesia, pain that persists after the usual signs of anaesthesia suggests a central component; MRI of the brain in patients without local pathosis has revealed intracranial findings; and re-entering a site of traumatic neuritis further traumatises the nerve. Torabinejad's chapter adds: cardiac toothache increases with exercise and is relieved by nitroglycerin; sinus toothache increases on lowering the head; dental pain often wakes the patient; a history of failed treatment by many providers is "a red flag" (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Non-odontogenic toothache is pain felt in a tooth whose source lies elsewhere. It is a heterotopic pain (referred, central or projected); referred pain is relieved only by anaesthetising the true source, not the tooth (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021). A tooth that responds normally to pulp tests is not the source (Sikri, 2019).

**Classification (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021)**
1. **Musculoskeletal:** myofascial pain, the commonest. Trigger points in the masseter, temporalis and lateral pterygoid refer constant dull aching pain to teeth; palpation reproduces it; trigger-point anaesthesia abolishes it. Also sinus or nasal mucosal pain (several maxillary teeth tender, worse on bending the head, Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) and salivary gland pain.
2. **Neurovascular (headaches):** migraine (4 to 72 hours, pulsatile, nausea, photophobia); cluster headache (unilateral, autonomic signs, relieved by 100% oxygen); paroxysmal hemicrania (responds to indomethacin). Episodic, with pain-free intervals.
3. **Neuropathic:** trigeminal neuralgia (sharp, shooting, trigger zone, light touch triggers severe pain; carbamazepine; screen for multiple sclerosis under 40); neuroma; neuritis (herpes zoster, paraformaldehyde paste, overfilling); neuropathy, now called persistent dentoalveolar pain disorder (PDAP), formerly atypical odontalgia or phantom tooth pain.
4. **Pathologic process:** cardiac angina (lower left jaw, worse on exercise, relieved by nitroglycerin, Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021); intracranial lesions; tumours of the sinus or jaw, often with sensory loss.
5. **Psychogenic:** somatoform disorders.

**Clues that the pain is not dental (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021; Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021)**
- No dental cause; normal pulp tests and radiographs.
- Pain not provoked or relieved by tooth stimulation or local anaesthesia of the tooth.
- Burning, shooting or constant pain; several teeth; bilateral or spreading.
- Paraesthesia or numbness.
- Repeated failed treatment ("a red flag").

**Investigations:** pulp tests, percussion and radiographs to exclude dental disease; palpation of the masticatory muscles and sinuses; diagnostic anaesthesia (trigger-point injection, nerve blocks); pain persisting after effective anaesthesia suggests a central component (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021); regional imaging (panoramic, CT, MRI) when a tumour or intracranial cause is suspected. Ask whether pain wakes the patient, which is typical of dental pain (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Management:** do not treat the tooth on symptoms alone; refer to orofacial pain, medical or neurological specialists; avoid repeated dental procedures, which may worsen neuropathic pain.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1. "Cold test is the most accurate pulp test" (the accuracy figures).**
- Textbooks: cold accuracy 86%, EPT 81%, heat 71% (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021); cold "precision" 90% (Baumann and Beer, 2010); cold accuracy 0.85 to 0.95 in a systematic review (Galicia and Khan, 2024).
- Refinement: the books disagree on predictive values for apparently the same study: heat PPV 48% (Chandler and Chong, 2017) versus 100% (Okazaki et al, 2017); and PPV and NPV labels swap between books (Sigurdsson, 2020, versus Okazaki et al, 2017). Cohen's mislabels sensitivity as "sensibility".
- Strength: clinical diagnostic-accuracy studies with access or histology as reference; adequate for ranking, weak for absolute values [Likely].
- Conclusion: cold ranks first and heat last in every source read; exact figures should be quoted with their author. Combining cold and EPT improves accuracy (Okazaki et al, 2017; Galicia and Khan, 2024) [Certain].
- Clinical implication: own both a cold system and an EPT, and never decide on a single test.

**Claim 2. "Refrigerant spray is as good as CO2 snow."**
- For: spray is the most convenient and reliable, even through PFM crowns (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021); equivalent to CO2 (Berman and Rotstein, 2021); no difference (Sigurdsson, 2020).
- Against: dry ice is the most reliable cold test, sprays less effective with porcelain crowns (Abbott, 2019, citing Chen and Abbott).
- Interpretation: the difference is mostly technique (pellet size, re-spraying, liquid run-off) [Guessing]. Both are acceptable; CO2 is preferred for crowns and immature teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021; Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [Likely].

**Claim 3. "Blood-flow tests (LDF, pulse oximetry) are ready to replace sensibility tests."**
- For: LDF sensitivity 97% and specificity 100% in traumatised incisors and earlier detection of revascularisation (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007); accuracy 97% for both (Galicia and Khan, 2024).
- Against: up to 80% periodontal signal (Ikeda and Suda, 2012); 10 to 15 minutes per tooth and high cost (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007); no licensed dental oximeter (Galicia and Khan, 2024); not commercialised (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sigurdsson, 2020).
- Limitation: neither group of tests stages pulpitis (Galicia and Khan, 2024).
- Conclusion: valuable in trauma and immature teeth, not yet routine [Certain].

**Claim 4. "The test cavity is the most accurate test."** Weine (2004) says so; Abbott (2019) calls it barbaric; Cohen's, Grossman's and Gutmann and Lovdahl keep it as a last resort. The argument against it rests on reasoning and small studies (replanted teeth, necrotic pulps with viable fibres), not on comparative trials [Likely]. It shows only nerve response, like EPT (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Sensibility = nerve response (cold, heat, EPT); vitality = blood supply (LDF, pulse oximetry) (Abbott, 2019).
- Accuracy: cold 86%, EPT 81%, heat 71%; heat specificity only 0.41 (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- No response to both cold and EPT in a mature, non-traumatised tooth = necrosis.
- EPT number has no diagnostic meaning (most books); Grossman's and Weine disagree.
- CO2 snow (-78°C) for crowned and immature teeth.
- LDF in trauma: sensitivity 97%, specificity 100%; detects revascularisation about 3 months earlier.
- Pulse oximetry: red 660 nm plus infrared 800 or 940 nm; not commercialised.
- Test cavity: last resort or "barbaric" depending on the author.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling thermal and electric tests "vitality tests" without noting that they test sensibility.
- Interpreting a low EPT reading as pulpitis as if it were settled; the books disagree.
- Quoting one set of accuracy figures as fact, or copying Cohen's "sensibility" for sensitivity.
- Declaring necrosis on one negative test in a recently traumatised or immature tooth.
- Writing that LDF and pulse oximetry are routine chairside tests.

## MNEMONIC
**"Nerves Answer, Blood Proves"**: nerve tests (cold, heat, EPT) give the patient's answer; blood-flow tests (LDF, oximetry) prove vitality.
