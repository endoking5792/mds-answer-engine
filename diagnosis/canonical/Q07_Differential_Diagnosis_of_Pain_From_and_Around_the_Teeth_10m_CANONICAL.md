## 1. Introduction and the two groups

### Explanation

Only two structures are primary sources of odontogenic pain: the pulp-dentin complex and the periradicular tissues (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]. Pulpal pain is carried by C fibres and is dull, aching or throbbing, whereas dentinal pain is the quick, short, sharp A-delta sensation (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]. Pulpal neurons of mandibular and maxillary teeth converge centrally, so pulpal pain is poorly localised and referred across arches (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; periradicular pain is easier to localise because the PDL is rich in mechanoreceptors (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad add that pain is never referred across the midline but readily from arch to arch (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Nonodontogenic toothache falls into five groups: musculoskeletal, neurovascular, neuropathic, pain from a pathologic (distant organic) process, and psychogenic (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]. Eli and colleagues stress that pulp vitality tests are the most important aid in separating these conditions (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Toothache arises from only two primary sources, the pulp-dentin complex and the periradicular tissues (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021). Pain felt in teeth may also be heterotopic (referred) from muscles, sinus, the heart or nerves. Pulpal pain is poorly localised and refers across arches but never across the midline (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

## 2. Pain originating from and around the teeth (odontogenic)

### Explanation

**Dentine hypersensitivity:** sharp, quickly reversible pain from exposed (mostly cervical) dentin, without pulp pathosis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. **Reversible pulpitis:** uncomfortable stimulation that reverses quickly (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. **Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis:** heightened and prolonged pain after a thermal stimulus, with spontaneous pain (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Gutmann and Lovdahl take a response longer than about 10 seconds as irreversible pulpitis (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. **Necrosis with apical extension:** heat sensitivity relieved by cold (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. **Symptomatic apical periodontitis:** pain to biting, percussion or palpation (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. **Acute apical abscess:** rapid onset, spontaneous pain, pus and swelling (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. **Periodontal:** periodontal disease is largely painless, except an acute lateral periodontal abscess (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], which can be confused with an acute periapical abscess; probing separates them (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. **Occlusal trauma and PDL strain:** percussion tenderness from bruxism, with normal pulp tests (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; PDL pain is dull, aching and can affect several teeth (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. **Cracked tooth:** pain only when one cusp is loaded (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], often on release (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. **Barodontalgia:** pain with pressure change, in degenerating or necrotic pulps (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Odontogenic pain**

| Condition | Key features |
|---|---|
| Dentine hypersensitivity | Sharp, short, to stimulus; exposed cervical dentin (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) |
| Reversible pulpitis | Provoked pain, subsides quickly |
| Irreversible pulpitis | Spontaneous, lingering (over about 10 s) thermal pain, poorly localised, may wake patient (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) |
| Necrosis/apical periodontitis | Heat pain relieved by cold; tender to bite and percussion, well localised |
| Acute apical abscess | Swelling, pus, no pulp response |
| Periodontal abscess | Vital pulp, deep pocket on probing (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) |
| Occlusal trauma | Several teeth tender, pulps normal |
| Cracked tooth | Pain on loading one cusp, on release |

## 3. Pain from outside the teeth (nonodontogenic)

### Explanation

**Myofascial:** muscle pain is the most common nonodontogenic source (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; Eli and colleagues call musculoskeletal conditions the major cause (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. The pain is diffuse, constant, dull and aching (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; trigger points are taut bands (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC], usually in masseter, temporalis and lateral pterygoid (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; palpation of the muscle reproduces the toothache but tooth percussion does not (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC], and trigger-point anaesthesia resolves it (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]. In endodontic patients with persistent nonodontogenic pain, about three quarters had myofascial and one quarter neuropathic pain (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC].

**Sinus/nasal:** the cardinal symptoms are purulent discharge, obstruction and facial pressure (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; pain rises on lowering the head, coughing or sneezing (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; several maxillary posterior teeth are tender to percussion yet respond to vitality tests (Okeson, 2012) [SRC]; Gutmann and Lovdahl note percussion tenderness on all teeth in the quadrant (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. **Books differ on the anaesthetic test here.** Okeson: an intraoral block only partially relieves the pain (Okeson, 2012) [SRC], and relief after intranasal 4% lidocaine spray is diagnostic (Okeson, 2012) [SRC]. Eli and colleagues: topical anaesthesia to the offending area should eliminate the pain (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. [Likely] both mean anaesthesia of the nasal or sinus mucosa (the source), not of the tooth; the 6th-edition wording does not say which area.

**Neurovascular:** intense and often pulsatile (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC], episodic with complete remission between episodes (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; local anaesthetic is unpredictable (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]. Migraine lasts 4 to 72 hours (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; cluster headache wakes the patient, is not relieved by intraoral block (Okeson, 2012) [SRC], and is relieved by 100% oxygen within 10 minutes (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC].

**Neuropathic:** trigeminal neuralgia is intense, sharp, shooting and unilateral (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC], like electric shocks (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; episodes do not occur in sleep and last seconds to a minute (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC], are not set off by hot or cold on the teeth (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC], and do not often wake the patient (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Anaesthetising the trigger zone may reduce pain and mislead (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; carbamazepine is first line (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]. Persistent pain after pulpectomy or extraction (atypical odontalgia) is now termed persistent dentoalveolar pain disorder (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; Eli and colleagues require duration over 4 months with normal radiographs and no cause (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Cardiac:** not aggravated by provoking teeth (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; increases with exertion (Okeson, 2012) [SRC]; relieved by rest or sublingual nitroglycerin (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; about 1 in 15 ischaemic events presents with jaw pain only (Okeson, 2012) [SRC]; it refers most commonly to the left mandible (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Psychogenic: the books disagree.** Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck: psychogenic pain is rare (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC], felt in many teeth and jumping from tooth to tooth (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]; Okeson gives the same features (Okeson, 2012) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad (2002): psychogenic pain is common and should be in the differential (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and a psychiatric basis is strongly suspected for atypical facial pain (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Eli and colleagues (2021): psychological factors have not been established as a cause of nondental toothache (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. [Likely] the shift reflects reclassification of atypical odontalgia as neuropathic pain after 2002.

#### ✎ Exam core

**B. Nonodontogenic pain (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021)**

| Source | Distinguishing features |
|---|---|
| Myofascial (commonest) | Dull, constant; trigger points in masseter/temporalis; palpation reproduces pain, percussion does not; trigger-point injection relieves |
| Sinus/nasal | Several maxillary teeth tender but vital; worse on bending; nasal discharge; intranasal lidocaine relieves (Okeson, 2012) |
| Neurovascular (migraine, cluster) | Episodic, pulsatile, remission between attacks; cluster relieved by 100% oxygen |
| Trigeminal neuralgia | Electric, seconds; trigger zone; not thermal; no sleep pain; carbamazepine |
| Persistent dentoalveolar pain | Continuous after pulpectomy/extraction; no cause, normal radiograph |
| Cardiac | Increases with exertion; relieved by nitroglycerin; left mandible |
| Psychogenic | Many teeth, jumps around; rare (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) but "common" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |

## 4. Clinical rules that separate the two

### Explanation

Okeson's rules: provoking the site of referred pain does not increase it, and anaesthetising the site does not decrease it (Okeson, 2012) [SRC]; the right question after anaesthesia is "I know you are numb, but does it still hurt?" (Okeson, 2012) [SRC]. Thermal allodynia marks pulpitis (Okeson, 2012) [SRC]. Bilateral pain or multiple painful teeth points to a nonodontogenic cause (Okeson, 2012) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad say anaesthetising the site may reduce but not eliminate referred pain (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], a softer statement than Okeson's. Pain is commonly referred to the most recently treated tooth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Dental pain often wakes the patient (Eli, Said-Al-Naief and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Periradicular pain unrelieved by local anaesthetic strongly suggests a nonodontogenic cause (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021) [SRC]. The dentist's task is to rule out a dental cause and refer (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**C. Distinguishing rules (Okeson, 2012)**
1. Odontogenic pain has a local cause and is reproduced by stimulating the tooth; referred pain is not.
2. Anaesthetising the tooth abolishes odontogenic pain; referred pain persists (ask "does it still hurt?").
3. Anaesthetising the true source (trigger point, nasal mucosa) abolishes referred pain.
4. Bilateral, multiple-tooth, burning or electric pain, or pain unchanged by dental treatment, suggests nonodontogenic origin.

**Conclusion.** Never treat a tooth until pain is reproduced in it; otherwise refer (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Two primary odontogenic sources only: pulp-dentin complex and periradicular tissues.
- Myofascial pain is the commonest nonodontogenic toothache.
- Diagnostic local anaesthetic: relieves odontogenic pain, not referred pain.
- Sinus: multiple vital maxillary teeth tender to percussion, worse on bending.
- Cardiac: worse on exertion, relieved by nitroglycerin.
- Trigeminal neuralgia: electric, trigger zone, no sleep pain, carbamazepine.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Listing nonodontogenic causes without the feature that separates each from a toothache.
- Forgetting periodontal, occlusal and cracked-tooth pain in the odontogenic group.
- Calling atypical odontalgia "psychogenic" without noting that current books class it as neuropathic.
- Assuming trigger-zone anaesthesia excludes trigeminal neuralgia.
