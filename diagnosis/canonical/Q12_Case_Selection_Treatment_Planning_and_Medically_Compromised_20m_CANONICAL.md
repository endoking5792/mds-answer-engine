## 1. Case selection

### Explanation

Case selection starts after diagnosis and needs assessment of medical, psychosocial and dental factors and case complexity (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's lists eleven factors ending with the operator's skill (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Jain groups them as strategic value, periodontal factors, patient factors and restorability (Jain, 2025) [SRC]. Treating an unrestorable tooth is as unethical as extracting a sound one (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; Jain contraindicates RCT if the crown cannot engage 1.5 to 2.0 mm of ferrule (Jain, 2025) [SRC]. Class III mobility favours extraction (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], though attachment loss alone does not contraindicate RCT (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Without a dental dam there is no RCT (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's contraindications include vertical root fracture and grade III mobility (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. On strategic value, Grossman's calls a terminal second molar hard to replace (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] while Jain gives second and third molars little strategic value unless they support a prosthesis (Jain, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Case selection and treatment planning begin after diagnosis and weigh medical, psychosocial and dental factors against case complexity (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021).

**Factors** (Gopikrishna, 2021; Jain, 2025):
- **Patient:** systemic health, anxiety, mouth opening, gag reflex, motivation, cost.
- **Strategic value:** role in the arch or prosthesis. Grossman's treats a terminal second molar as strategic; Jain does not unless it supports a prosthesis.
- **Restorability:** sound structure after caries removal; 1.5 to 2 mm ferrule.
- **Periodontium:** mobility, bone support, crown-root ratio.
- **Tooth:** curvature, calcification, resorption, open apex, previous treatment, fracture.
- **Operator skill.**

**Contraindications:** nonrestorable tooth, grade III mobility, vertical root fracture, no strategic value, inability to place a dental dam (Sikri, 2019; Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021). Treating an unrestorable tooth is as unethical as extracting a sound one. Loss of attachment alone is not a contraindication if the remaining periodontium is healthy (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014); class III mobility favours extraction (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 2. Case difficulty and treatment planning

### Explanation

The AAE form grades patient, diagnostic and treatment, and additional considerations as minimal, moderate or high difficulty; one high factor makes the case high (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; skill and complexity decide referral (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Planning runs in three stages, initial, definitive and review, with pain relief first and canals dressed "on hold" if needed (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Consent alternatives always include no treatment and extraction (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**AAE Case Difficulty Assessment Form:** three categories (patient; diagnostic and treatment; additional: trauma, previous treatment, perio) graded minimal, moderate or high; one high-difficulty factor makes the case high (Gopikrishna, 2021). Refer when complexity exceeds skill (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021); medically complex patients who cannot sit long, need sedation or close monitoring are often better treated by a specialist (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021).

**Plan in phases** (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014): (1) emergency relief of pain and swelling; (2) stabilisation: caries control, hygiene, dressed canals; (3) definitive RCT, retreatment or surgery, then restoration; (4) review. Consent covers diagnosis, risks, prognosis and alternatives including no treatment and extraction (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 3. Note on managing medically compromised patients

### Explanation

**General principles.** Most medical conditions do not contraindicate treatment; the most important step is to communicate with the physician and document advice (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. A specialist may be better placed to treat such patients (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's opens with "Never Treat a Stranger"; 25% to 30% of dental patients report a relevant condition; the written questionnaire must be supplemented by interview; herbal products (ginkgo, garlic, vitamin E) inhibit platelets (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC]. Vital signs are recorded (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. The ASA class does not advise treatment modifications (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]; ASA III or above almost always needs medical consultation, a letter from the physician is preferred, and Ingle's proposes a Multi-Dimension Risk Assessment Model (health status, procedural stress, anxiety) (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC]. Salivary cortisol rose with extraction but not with root canal treatment (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC].

**Cardiovascular.** Postpone elective care after MI within 30 days or with unstable angina; up to 0.036 mg epinephrine (two cartridges 1:100,000) is safe for intermediate risk (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. BP below 160/100 tolerates routine care; above 180/110 refer and treat emergencies only; above 210/120 refer urgently; ischaemic heart disease: morning, short appointments, anxiolysis; orofacial pain is the only symptom in about 6% of MIs (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC]. Infective endocarditis prophylaxis for prosthetic valves, previous IE and select congenital disease (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's gives the AHA regimen 1 hour before (amoxicillin 2 g) and lists mitral valve prolapse among conditions no longer needing it (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; a missed dose may be given up to 2 hours after (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri still prints 2 g penicillin V before and 1 g every 6 hours after (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], an older regimen superseded by the single-dose AHA regimen in Grossman's [Likely].

**Anticoagulants.** Do not routinely stop warfarin; INR 3.5 or less for dental or surgical endodontics; dabigatran needs no INR (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's: therapeutic INR 2.0 to 3.5, nonsurgical RCT needs no change, periapical surgery may be harder to keep haemostatic even within range, and stopping warfarin 2 days before surgery caused no bleeding in one study (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's Ch.2: anticoagulants "should not be altered", keep INR 2 to 3 (Aminoshariae and Fouad, 2021) [SRC].

**Diabetes.** Well-controlled diabetics without complications can be treated; bring a glucometer; antibiotics generally not required (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]; HbA1c goal under 7%; treat hypoglycaemia with 15 g oral carbohydrate (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri prescribes prophylactic antibiotics as standard protocol (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], in contrast to Cohen's. Outcome: diabetes lowers success when a lesion is present (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021; Aminoshariae and Fouad, 2021) [SRC], although one study cited in Cohen's found no influence (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC].

**Pregnancy.** Avoid elective care in the first trimester; second trimester safest; lidocaine safe; lead apron with thyroid collar; avoid ibuprofen from 30 weeks (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]; raise the right hip 10 to 12 cm to prevent supine hypotension (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Malignancy, radiation, MRONJ.** Metastases to the jaw (breast, lung, thyroid, prostate) mimic endodontic lesions; a vital tooth means a nonodontogenic lesion; treat symptomatic nonvital teeth at least 1 week before radiation; routine care if granulocytes over 2000/mm3 and platelets over 50,000/mm3 (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. After radiation, teeth are kept by RCT to avoid osteoradionecrosis, with 91% success in one series (Aminoshariae and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. High-risk MRONJ patients: RCT instead of extraction, avoid surgery, chlorhexidine rinse, no vasoconstrictor, no patency filing (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]; oral bisphosphonates do not change outcome, and with IV bisphosphonates clamps are placed carefully to avoid soft tissue and bone injury (Aminoshariae and Fouad, 2021) [SRC].

**HIV.** CD4 over 350 cells/mm3: all treatment (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri gives 400 (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; healing is not altered (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021; Aminoshariae and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. **Prosthetic joints:** prophylaxis not recommended routinely (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. **Renal dialysis:** treat the day after dialysis, treat infections aggressively (Johnson and Epstein, 2019; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. **Steroids:** nonsurgical RCT needs no supplementation (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC]. **Seizures:** most controlled patients receive routine care (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC]. **Haemophilia:** Sikri warns of bleeding with inferior alveolar blocks, keeps clamps off the gingiva and suggests gradual fixation and removal of pulp over several visits (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. **Psychiatric drugs** interact with sedatives and vasoconstrictors (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. **NSAIDs** are avoided in antithrombotic therapy, past MI, renal disease and severe asthma (Aminoshariae and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. True allergy to endodontic materials warrants referral (Aminoshariae and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. Medical conditions may also favour RCT over extraction (bisphosphonates, radiation) (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principles:** most medical conditions do not contraindicate RCT (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021); "never treat a stranger" (Johnson and Epstein, 2019).
1. Written history plus interview; medications, herbal products, allergies.
2. Vital signs; ASA class (ASA III and above need medical consultation), or the Multi-Dimension Risk Assessment Model: health status, procedural stress, anxiety (Johnson and Epstein, 2019).
3. Written physician consultation, recorded.
4. Stress reduction: short morning appointments, anxiolysis, profound anaesthesia.
5. RCT is often preferred to extraction in these patients (bleeding disorders, radiation, antiresorptives) because it avoids a surgical wound (Gopikrishna, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).
6. Drugs: check interactions; avoid NSAIDs with antithrombotics, past MI, renal disease and severe asthma (Aminoshariae and Fouad, 2021); true allergy to endodontic materials needs referral.

**Key controversies to state:** INR limit (Cohen's 3.5 or less; Torabinejad's 2 to 3); diabetic antibiotic prophylaxis (Sikri: routine; Cohen's and Ingle's: not required for controlled diabetics); HIV CD4 cut-off (350 versus 400).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Warfarin need not be stopped, but the INR must be within a safe range."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's: INR 3.5 or less allows dental or surgical endodontics (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's: therapeutic range 2.0 to 3.5; nonsurgical RCT needs no change, but periapical surgery may be hard even within range, and stopping warfarin 2 days before surgery caused no bleeding or thromboembolism in one cohort (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's: anticoagulants should not be altered; keep INR at 2 to 3 (Aminoshariae and Fouad, 2021) [SRC].
*Assessment:* all three agree on not stopping routinely for nonsurgical work; the upper limit (3 or 3.5) and the option of stopping before surgery differ. The supporting studies were not read this session. [Likely]
*Conclusion:* write "INR on the day, within therapeutic range (Cohen's limit 3.5), physician decides any change; local haemostatic measures for surgery". [Likely]

**Claim 2: "Diabetic patients need prophylactic antibiotics for endodontic treatment."**
*Textbook:* Sikri: the standard protocol prescribes prophylactic antibiotics (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's: generally not required, considered only for brittle diabetes with poor oral health and fasting glucose over 200 mg/dL (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's: well-controlled diabetics need none even for surgery (Johnson and Epstein, 2019) [SRC].
*Assessment:* Sikri stands against two texts that state indications; routine prophylaxis also conflicts with current antibiotic stewardship [Likely]. Sikri's antibiotic table elsewhere in the chapter also follows an older endocarditis regimen [Likely].
*Conclusion:* write that prophylaxis is not routine for diabetics; name Sikri's view as differing. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Most medical conditions do not contraindicate RCT; consult the physician and record it.
- ASA does not say how to modify treatment; MD-RAM adds procedural stress and anxiety.
- MI under 30 days: postpone; epinephrine up to 0.036 mg in cardiac patients.
- BP over 180/110: emergency care only.
- IE prophylaxis: prosthetic valve, previous IE, select congenital heart disease; amoxicillin 2 g 1 h before.
- Warfarin not stopped; INR checked.
- Pregnancy: second trimester; MRONJ and radiation: RCT rather than extraction.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Giving the old multi-dose penicillin regimen for endocarditis prophylaxis or listing mitral valve prolapse as needing cover.
- Advising warfarin withdrawal before routine RCT.
- Writing case selection only and leaving the medically compromised note as a list of diseases without management.
- Prescribing routine antibiotic prophylaxis for all diabetics or prosthetic joints.
