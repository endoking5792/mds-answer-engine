## 1. Introduction: what diagnosis and treatment planning are

### Explanation

Cohen's defines diagnosis as "the art and science of detecting and distinguishing deviations from health and the cause and nature thereof" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's gives the working definition, recognising and identifying disease "by means of signs, symptoms, and tests", and names the elements: data gathering and analysis leading to a differential diagnosis, a definitive diagnosis and a treatment plan (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's closes its chapter with five questions to answer before any treatment: is the problem dental, is the pulp involved, why, what is the prognosis, and what is the appropriate treatment (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Jain calls an accurate pulpal and periapical diagnosis "the most important step" in deciding treatment and notes that 90% of orofacial pain patients need an endodontic assessment (Jain, 2025) [SRC]. Johnson and colleagues add the ethical edge: "no treatment is better than rendering the wrong treatment" (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Treatment planning follows diagnosis: Cohen's says case selection and planning "begins after a clinician has diagnosed an endodontic problem" (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Diagnosis is the art and science of detecting and distinguishing deviations from health and their cause and nature (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). It proceeds by data gathering (history, examination, tests, imaging) and analysis to a differential diagnosis, a definitive diagnosis and then a treatment plan (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021). Treatment planning begins only after the diagnosis is made (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021). The best treatment on a wrong diagnosis fails: no treatment is better than the wrong treatment (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021).

Before any endodontic treatment five questions must be answered (Berman and Rotstein, 2021):
1. Is the problem of dental origin?
2. Is the pulp pathologically involved?
3. Why is the pulpal pathosis present?
4. What is the prognosis?
5. What is the appropriate treatment?

## 2. Examination and diagnostic tests (compact)

### Explanation

History: directive questions should be open-ended; nonlocalised pain is typical of early pulpitis; "true swelling must be associated with pulp necrosis" although patients may perceive swelling in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Vital signs are taken; a limited periodontal examination is part of every endodontic workup; sinus tracts are traced with gutta-percha (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Tests are applied to suspect and control teeth, and "these are not tests of teeth; they are tests of a patient's response"; immature teeth give unreliable sensitivity responses (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Percussion tenderness shows periodontal ligament inflammation, not pulp vitality (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Radiographs: periapical lesions of endodontic origin stay at the apex at different cone angles; "a sizable radiolucency with a vital pulp is not endodontic"; a cyst cannot be identified radiographically; CBCT is an aid in difficult diagnoses (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. The detail of each test is in D1.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Steps in examination** (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021):
- **Chief complaint and history of present illness:** location, onset, duration, quality, intensity, aggravating and relieving factors, asked with open-ended questions. True swelling means pulp necrosis.
- **Medical history:** conditions, drugs, allergies (section 5 and Q12).
- **Dental history:** previous treatment, trauma, pain.
- **Vital signs;** temperature if swelling or malaise.
- **Extraoral and intraoral examination:** swelling, lymph nodes, sinus tract (traced with gutta-percha), caries, restorations, cracks; periodontal probing and mobility on every tooth examined.
- **Clinical tests on suspect and control teeth:** percussion, palpation, bite test; cold, heat and electric pulp tests; test cavity and selective anaesthesia as adjuncts. Percussion shows periodontal inflammation, not pulp vitality (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- **Radiographs:** two angulations and a bitewing; CBCT for difficult diagnoses, complex anatomy, resorption and trauma. An endodontic lesion stays at the apex at different angles; a large radiolucency on a vital tooth is not endodontic; a cyst cannot be diagnosed on a radiograph.

## 3. Diagnostic terminology

### Explanation

Cohen's uses the AAE terminology of 2016 (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Jain dates the standardised terms to the AAE proposal of 2008 (Jain, 2025) [SRC]. The two refer to the same system (the consensus terms with later glossary revisions) [Likely]. These are clinical categories because correlating signs and symptoms with histopathology "is challenging" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's stresses that only a limited number of diagnoses exist (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Key definitions: symptomatic irreversible pulpitis: the vital inflamed pulp "is incapable of healing", with lingering pain to cold (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC], often 30 s or longer (Jain, 2025) [SRC]; asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis: no symptoms, treat soon (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; necrosis: the only category that describes histology, may be partial in multirooted teeth (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; previously treated and previously initiated therapy (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Apical: acute apical abscess is diagnosed whenever swelling is present (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; chronic apical abscess drains through a sinus tract (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], which needs no separate treatment (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; condensing osteitis resolves in about half of cases after treatment (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

The AAE clinical terminology (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Jain, 2025) is used because clinical signs correlate poorly with histology. Every tooth gets **one pulpal and one apical diagnosis**.

| Pulpal diagnosis | Key findings | Usual treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Normal pulp | Symptom-free, transient response to tests | None |
| Reversible pulpitis | Sharp pain to stimulus, subsides in seconds | Remove the cause |
| Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis | Spontaneous pain, lingering pain to cold (often 30 s or more), referred pain | Pulpectomy/RCT (or vital pulp therapy in selected cases) |
| Asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis | No symptoms; deep caries likely to expose | RCT soon |
| Pulp necrosis | No response to tests; may be partial in multirooted teeth | RCT |
| Previously treated | Canals obturated | Retreatment (nonsurgical or surgical) |
| Previously initiated therapy | Pulpotomy or pulpectomy done | Complete RCT |

| Apical diagnosis | Key findings |
|---|---|
| Normal apical tissues | Not tender; lamina dura intact |
| Symptomatic apical periodontitis | Pain on biting or percussion; PDL widening or radiolucency |
| Asymptomatic apical periodontitis | Radiolucency, no symptoms |
| Acute apical abscess | Rapid onset, pain, swelling, pus; may have fever |
| Chronic apical abscess | Little discomfort, intermittent pus through a sinus tract |
| Condensing osteitis | Diffuse radiopacity at apex; resolves in about 50% after RCT |

(Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021)

## 4. Arriving at the diagnosis

### Explanation

A diagnosis "cannot be made from a single complaint" (Jain, 2025) [SRC]; pathosis "is never without a cause" (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. When subjective and objective findings disagree, add tests (selective anaesthesia), retest on another day because ibuprofen alters cold, percussion and palpation results, or refer; the diagnosis may change when the pulp is seen (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Adjunct diagnoses that change the plan: longitudinal fractures, trauma, resorption and endo-perio lesions; a failed tooth with vertical root fracture has a hopeless prognosis; true combined lesions have a guarded prognosis (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Patients localise a stimulated tooth correctly only about a third of the time, so referred pain misleads; non-odontogenic pain goes to an orofacial pain clinic (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Jain adds that waiting until symptoms localise is sometimes the best option (Jain, 2025) [SRC]. A patient must be told when a suspected fracture makes the prognosis questionable (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Reasoning to the diagnosis:**
1. Reproduce the chief complaint with a test; findings must agree (Jain, 2025).
2. Identify the cause (caries, restoration, trauma, crack, periodontal disease): pathosis is never without a cause (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).
3. Name the pulpal and apical diagnoses.
4. Add **adjunct diagnoses**: crack or vertical root fracture, resorption, trauma, endo-perio lesion (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).
5. If findings conflict: selective anaesthesia, CBCT, retest on another day (analgesics alter test results), or refer. If pain does not localise, it is acceptable to wait (Jain, 2025).
6. Exclude non-odontogenic pain (sinusitis, myofascial, neuropathic, cardiac); refer to an orofacial pain clinic if not dental (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

## 5. Case selection: factors that decide whether to treat

### Explanation

Cohen's insists on a complete evaluation of medical, psychosocial and dental factors and case complexity, and notes the options now include vital pulp therapy, regeneration and replantation (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's opens with case selection errors forming 22% of failures in Ingle and Beveridge's study, and lists eleven factors ending with the operator's skill; case selection is "dictated by what we see in the radiograph" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Jain and Sikri reduce the decision to four factors: strategic value, periodontal factors, patient factors and restorability (Jain, 2025; Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

The books differ on **strategic value**. Grossman's treats a terminal second molar as strategic because it is "difficult to replace by itself" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Jain says a second or third molar is "of little strategic value, unless it is required to support a prosthesis" (Jain, 2025) [SRC]; Gulabivala and Ng rank unopposed, functionless teeth below single standing teeth (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. They are judging different situations (Grossman's a missing first molar, Jain a full arch) [Likely]. Grossman's adds that treatment must never be "dictated only by the strategic need for the tooth" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Restorability: completing RCT on an unrestorable tooth is "as unethical as extraction of a sound tooth"; existing restorations and caries are removed to judge it (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; Jain contraindicates treatment when the crown cannot engage 1.5 to 2.0 mm of ferrule (Jain, 2025) [SRC]. Periodontium: class III mobility favours extraction, but a firm tooth with bone loss can be treated (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; loss of attachment alone is not a contraindication (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Patient: a complex plan is wrong for a poorly motivated patient (Rosenberg, Malek and Okazaki, 2019) [SRC], and the patient must not dictate a poor-prognosis plan (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Inability to place a dental dam excludes RCT (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri lists contraindications: nonrestorable tooth, grade III mobility, vertical root fracture, no strategic value (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Most medical conditions do not contraindicate treatment (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Factors in case selection** (Gopikrishna, 2021; Jain, 2025; Sikri, 2019):

| Factor | Points to assess |
|---|---|
| Patient | Medical status (ASA), anxiety, mouth opening, gag reflex, motivation, cost, wishes |
| Strategic value | Role in the arch and in planned prosthesis, abutment value, aesthetics |
| Restorability | Sound tooth structure after removal of caries and restorations; ferrule 1.5 to 2 mm; margins supragingival |
| Periodontium | Probing, mobility, bone support, crown-root ratio, furcation |
| Endodontic factors | Anatomy, curvature, calcification, resorption, open apex, previous treatment, fractures |
| Operator | Skill, equipment (microscope, CBCT) |

**Strategic value: a disagreement.** Grossman's: a terminal second molar is strategic because it is hard to replace (Gopikrishna, 2021). Jain: second and third molars have little strategic value unless they support a prosthesis (Jain, 2025). Both agree that strategic need alone must not dictate treatment.

**Contraindications** (Sikri, 2019; Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021): nonrestorable tooth; grade III mobility or inadequate periodontal support; vertical root fracture; tooth of no present or future strategic value; inability to isolate with a dam. Treating an unrestorable tooth is as unethical as extracting a sound one. Loss of attachment alone is not a contraindication if the periodontium is healthy (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014). Medical conditions rarely contraindicate RCT (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021).

## 6. Case difficulty assessment: the AAE form

### Explanation

Grossman's reproduces the AAE Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form and its guidelines; Torabinejad's names the same three categories (Gopikrishna, 2021; Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Levels: minimal (outcome attainable by a competent practitioner with limited experience), moderate (challenging for an experienced practitioner), high (one high factor or several moderate ones; challenging even for the most experienced) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Examples from the form: ASA 3 to 5 is high; second or third molar high; curvature over 30 degrees or S-shaped high; open apex over 1.5 mm high; tooth over 25 mm high; extensive coronal destruction moderate (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The point score (1, 2, 5) and the 20 and 40 cut-offs are "for educational purposes only" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri uses a modified form that places premolars with the first molar in the moderate column (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], whereas the AAE form puts anterior and premolar teeth in minimal (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; quote the AAE version. Torabinejad's cautions that position in the arch alone is a poor guide (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

The AAE Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form grades a case as **minimal, moderate or high** difficulty under three categories (Gopikrishna, 2021; Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021):

| Category | Minimal | Moderate | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Patient: medical history | ASA 1 | ASA 2 | ASA 3 to 5 |
| Anaesthesia, disposition, opening, gag | No problems | Anxious but cooperative; slight limitation; gags occasionally | Vasoconstrictor intolerance, uncooperative, significant limitation, extreme gag |
| Emergency | Minimal pain or swelling | Moderate | Severe |
| B. Diagnosis and radiographs | Consistent signs; easy films | Extensive differential diagnosis | Confusing signs; chronic facial pain; very difficult films |
| Position, isolation | Anterior or premolar; routine dam | First molar; simple dam modification | Second or third molar; extensive modification |
| Crown and root morphology | Normal; curvature under 10 degrees | Full crown, bridge abutment, extensive coronal destruction; curvature 10 to 30 degrees | Curvature over 30 degrees or S-shaped; tooth over 25 mm; open apex over 1.5 mm; fusion, dens in dente |
| Canals, resorption | Visible; none | Reduced, pulp stones; minimal apical resorption | Not visible; internal, external or extensive apical resorption |
| C. Additional: trauma, previous treatment, perio | None; mild perio | Subluxation; previous access without complications; moderate perio | Luxation, avulsion, root fracture; previous treatment or complications; severe perio, combined lesion |

**Levels:** minimal, predictable for a competent practitioner with limited experience; moderate, challenging for an experienced practitioner; high, at least one high factor or several moderate ones, challenging even for the most experienced. The point score (1, 2, 5; refer above 40) is for teaching only (Gopikrishna, 2021). Sikri's modified form moves premolars to moderate (Sikri, 2019).

## 7. Treatment options and sequencing

### Explanation

Torabinejad's links diagnosis to treatment: reversible pulpitis by removing the cause; irreversible pulpitis and necrosis by endodontic care; previously treated teeth with pathosis by retreatment; urgency follows symptoms; definitive treatment must follow any emergency care (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng describe three stages, planned initial treatment, planned definitive treatment and planned review; immediate relief of pain precedes all else; antibiotics alone are inappropriate for acute symptoms; stabilisation puts teeth "on hold" with dressed canals while other care proceeds; a "provisional treatment plan" precedes the definitive one; planning for failure is part of the plan (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Sikri and Jain give similar staged lists (Sikri, 2019; Jain, 2025) [SRC].

Visits: a patient in severe pain is not a candidate for single visit treatment; canals should not be partly instrumented; what can be done and what should be done differ (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Scheduling: 5 to 7 days between instrumentation and obturation in vital multivisit cases, about 1 week for nonvital cases with calcium hydroxide, every 24 to 48 hours for acute nonvital cases (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Surgery without prior nonsurgical treatment is a last resort; the bacterial path of ingress must be found before surgery; intentional replantation still leaves the implant option; regenerative treatment depends on remaining structure, tooth location, maturity, visits and compliance (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontics versus implant: both are highly predictable; extracting a tooth that can be saved with good prognosis is not reasonable, and the AAE regards treating hopeless teeth or implanting savable ones as unethical (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. A Markov model cited by Gulabivala and Ng found RCT highly cost-effective as the first line for a maxillary central incisor (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Treatment options:** no treatment or review; remove the irritant (reversible pulpitis); vital pulp therapy; regenerative treatment or apexification in immature teeth; nonsurgical RCT; nonsurgical retreatment; endodontic surgery; intentional replantation; root resection or hemisection; extraction with replacement (implant, FPD, RPD) (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Rosenberg and Malek, 2021).

**Sequencing** (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014; Sikri, 2019; Jain, 2025):
1. **Emergency phase:** relieve pain and swelling (pulpotomy or pulpectomy, drainage, extraction of hopeless teeth); antibiotics alone are not treatment.
2. **Stabilisation phase:** caries control, oral hygiene, dressed canals ("on hold"), provisional restorations.
3. **Definitive phase:** RCT or retreatment of restorable teeth, surgery if needed, periodontal therapy, then cores, crowns and prostheses.
4. **Review and maintenance:** recall, prevention, a preplanned response to failure.

**Visit planning** (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021): single visit for uncomplicated, asymptomatic cases; multivisit for severe pain, swelling, exudate or complex anatomy; never leave canals partly instrumented; obturate vital cases 5 to 7 days and nonvital cases about 1 week after instrumentation; review acute cases every 24 to 48 hours.

**Rules:** surgery without prior nonsurgical treatment is a last resort; find the path of bacterial ingress before retreatment; do not extract a tooth that can be saved with good prognosis, and do not treat a hopeless one (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021).

## 8. Prognosis

### Explanation

Cohen's and Ingle's (same authors) report from a systematic review that absence of periapical radiolucency significantly improves outcome and vitality does not matter when the periapex is healthy; sinus tract, narrow deep probing and pain affect outcome; preoperative pain affects survival; retreatment outcomes match primary treatment when the apical infection can be reached (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Restoration: four factors favour survival (crown, two proximal contacts, not an abutment, nonmolar) and coronal restoration quality is "as important as" root filling quality (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's reports better prognosis when canals are instrumented to within 0 to 2 mm of working length (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], quotes 93 to 98% freedom from apical periodontitis and uses Friedman's healed, healing and disease categories (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; first review at 1 year, follow doubtful lesions up to 4 years (Rosenberg, Malek and Okazaki, 2019) [SRC]. Disagreements on lesion size, symptoms and restoration quality are set out in the synthesis below.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prognostic factors** (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021):
- **Preoperative:** periapical radiolucency (strongest negative factor); pulp vitality irrelevant if the periapex is healthy; sinus tract, deep narrow pocket and preoperative pain lower outcome; systemic disease; periodontal status.
- **Intraoperative:** apical extent of preparation and filling (within 0 to 2 mm of length), procedural errors.
- **Postoperative:** coronal seal and crown, proximal contacts, abutment use.

**Books disagree on two factors:** lesion size (Cohen's: may affect outcome; Grossman's and Torabinejad's: does not) and symptoms (Cohen's: pain and sinus tract lower outcome; Grossman's: symptoms do not influence healing). Write both.

Retreatment can match primary treatment if the apical infection can be reached (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021). Outcome is classed as healed, healing or disease (Friedman, cited by Gopikrishna, 2021); review at 1 year and up to 4 years.

## 9. Referral

### Explanation

Johnson and colleagues quote the ADA code on knowing one's limits, state that the standard of care is the same for general dentists and specialists, list complex diagnoses (conflicting tests, trauma, symptomatic treated teeth, non-endodontic lesions), and set out referral before, during (flare-ups, accidents, failed anaesthesia) and after treatment (persistent pain, lesion, sinus tract); initial referral is better than midtreatment referral; deciding "when not to treat" may be the most important decision; Burry's data, cited by them, showed better 10-year molar outcomes with endodontists (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's makes operator skill and case complexity the key variables (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]; Jain agrees that referral before treatment is better (Jain, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**When to refer** (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021; Rosenberg and Malek, 2021): the key variables are the clinician's skill and the case's complexity. The standard of care is the same for general dentists and endodontists, so a case beyond one's skill must be referred.
- **Before treatment:** high difficulty on the AAE form; conflicting tests; trauma; non-endodontic lesions; medically complex patients; retreatment and surgery.
- **During treatment:** flare-ups not controlled, procedural accidents, failed anaesthesia.
- **After treatment:** persistent pain, persistent or new lesion, sinus tract that does not heal.
Refer at the start rather than midtreatment: it prevents accidents and keeps the patient's trust (Jain, 2025). Send findings, radiographs and the restorative plan; the endodontist reports back in writing.

## 10. Informed consent and records

### Explanation

Grossman's lists what must be disclosed in lay language: diagnosis, nature of treatment, risks, prognosis, alternatives with their risks and prognosis, and a chance to ask questions; a perfect result is not guaranteed; failure to warn of a risk invites a claim even when the standard of care was met (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's requires alternatives to include no treatment and extraction, allows local anaesthesia so a patient in pain can discuss calmly, and warns of adjunctive procedures such as crown lengthening or orthodontic extrusion; all responses are recorded (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's suggests a record line that all reasonable options including extraction were explained, and notes that anxiety impairs understanding, so written and verbal explanations help (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng advise records of findings, option appraisal, rationale and consent (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Informed consent** (Gopikrishna, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) in lay language: diagnosis; proposed treatment; its risks; prognosis; alternatives (always including no treatment and extraction) with their risks and prognosis; cost, number of visits and further procedures (post, crown, crown lengthening, orthodontic extrusion); opportunity to ask questions. A perfect result cannot be guaranteed. Anxiety impairs understanding, so give written as well as verbal information (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021).

**Records:** findings, test results, radiographs, diagnosis, options discussed, the patient's choice and consent, for example "all reasonable options including extraction were presented and explained" (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021; Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

**Conclusion.** Correct diagnosis, honest assessment of restorability, periodontal support, strategic value and case difficulty, a staged plan, timely referral and documented consent together decide the outcome of endodontic treatment.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "The size of the periapical lesion affects prognosis."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's and Ingle's: the size of the radiolucency "may also affect" outcome (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's: earlier studies favoured lesions under 5 mm but "recent studies have shown that the size of radiolucency does not influence the healing potential" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's: size "is of little concern", although the presence of a lesion reduces prognosis (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri: lesions under 10 mm repair more often than larger ones (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
*Assessment:* all four agree that a lesion's presence matters; they split on size. Sikri's 10 mm and Grossman's 5 mm thresholds come from older studies; Cohen's cites a systematic review. The cited studies were not read this session. [Likely]
*Conclusion:* write that presence of a lesion is the strongest preoperative factor and that the effect of size is disputed between texts. Large lesions still heal after correct nonsurgical treatment, so size alone is not a reason for surgery or extraction. [Likely]

**Claim 2: "Preoperative symptoms influence healing."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's: sinus tract, pain and discharging sinus have a significant effect on outcome, and preoperative pain affects tooth survival (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's: "Presence or absence of clinical symptoms does not influence the healing potential" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].
*Assessment:* a direct contradiction. Cohen's cites the factors from a named outcome review; Grossman's gives no source in the note. [Guessing] that Grossman's refers to older data on pain alone, while Cohen's includes sinus tracts that signal established infection.
*Conclusion:* in the exam, give both, and use symptoms for planning visits (Cohen's advises multivisit treatment for severe pain) rather than for quoting a prognosis. [Likely]

**Claim 3: "The root filling matters more than the coronal restoration."**
*Textbook:* Sikri: technical quality of preparation and obturation has "definite impact" on outcome "as compared to" the coronal restoration (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's: a meta-analysis cited there found that poor quality in either lowers healing equally, so the coronal restoration "is as important as" the root filling (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC].
*Assessment:* the sources disagree on emphasis, not on the need for both. Cohen's statement is tied to a named meta-analysis; Sikri gives no source. [Likely]
*Conclusion:* plan the coronal restoration before starting RCT and place it promptly; Torabinejad's advises replacing a provisional within about 30 days (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Five questions before treatment: dental origin, pulp involved, why, prognosis, appropriate treatment (Cohen's).
- One pulpal plus one apical diagnosis, AAE terms; swelling means acute apical abscess.
- Four case-selection factors: strategic value, periodontium, patient, restorability.
- AAE form: three categories, three levels; one high factor makes the case high difficulty.
- Phases: emergency, stabilisation, definitive, review.
- No dam, no RCT; unrestorable tooth, no RCT.
- Same standard of care for generalist and specialist; refer before, not during, treatment.
- Consent always includes no treatment and extraction as alternatives.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing histological terms (acute or chronic pulpitis) instead of the AAE clinical terms, or giving only a pulpal diagnosis.
- Listing tests at length and leaving no time for case selection, the AAE form, sequencing, referral and consent.
- Treating a tooth before checking restorability after removing caries and old restorations.
- Quoting the AAE point score as a clinical rule; it is for teaching only.
- Stating lesion size or symptoms as settled prognostic factors when the textbooks disagree.
