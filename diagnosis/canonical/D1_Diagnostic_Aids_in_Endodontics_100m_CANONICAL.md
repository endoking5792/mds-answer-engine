## 1. Definition, the diagnostic process and classification of diagnostic aids

### Explanation

The books define diagnosis in three overlapping ways. Cohen's: diagnosis is the art and science of detecting and distinguishing deviations from health and the cause and nature thereof (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; the Molar Guidebook uses almost the same words, "divergence from health and its cause" (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. Torabinejad's: the science of recognising and identifying disease by means of signs, symptoms and tests (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's adds the classical "art of distinguishing one disease from another" and separates differential diagnosis from diagnosis by exclusion, which eliminates all possible diseases until one remains (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. All three stress that the cause must be identified, which is why "cause" appears as a separate element in the diagnosis (section 10).

Cohen's divides the process into five stages: the patient's reasons, questioning about symptoms and history, objective tests, correlation into a tentative differential list, and a definitive diagnosis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Abbott in Ingle's gives a longer "11 stages of the diagnostic process" (history, examination, tests, imaging, diagnosis, cause, options, discussion, consent, tooth investigation, records) and compares a missed step with a jigsaw where a missing piece may mean the puzzle cannot be solved (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri describes the qualities of the diagnostician: sympathetic ears to hear, eyes to see, hands to feel (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Jain gives the scale of the problem: 90% of all patients with orofacial pain need an endodontic assessment (Jain, 2025) [SRC].

Classification of aids. No textbook gives one master classification. Sikri tabulates clinical inspection methods, radiographic techniques, pulp vitality tests (neural, blood supply, tooth temperature) and "newer methods" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's Box 6.6 lists visual and tactile inspection, percussion, palpation, mobility and depressibility, bite test, radiography and pulp vitality tests (neural sensibility and vascularity) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The exam box below merges the two in the order the examination is actually done [Certain that this is a synthesis, not one book's list].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Diagnosis is "the art and science of detecting and distinguishing deviations from health and the cause and nature thereof" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021); it is the science of recognising disease by signs, symptoms and tests (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021). Correct treatment depends on correct diagnosis; about 90% of patients with orofacial pain need an endodontic assessment (Jain, 2025).

**Diagnostic process (five stages, Berman and Rotstein, 2021):** patient's complaint → history → objective tests → correlation into a differential list → definitive diagnosis. Abbott (2019) expands this to 11 stages ending with tooth investigation and records: a missing step is like a missing jigsaw piece. Grossman's workflow ends with finalising the management option with the patient, informed consent and treatment (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Approaches:** differential diagnosis distinguishes one disease from similar disorders by their differences; diagnosis by exclusion eliminates possibilities until one remains (Gopikrishna, 2021). The diagnostician needs "ears to hear, eyes to see, hands to feel" plus a systematic routine (Sikri, 2019).

**Classification of diagnostic aids** (merged from Sikri, 2019 and Gopikrishna, 2021)
1. History: chief complaint, history of present illness, medical, dental history.
2. Clinical examination: extraoral; intraoral soft and hard tissue (visual, tactile, magnification).
3. Periradicular tests: palpation, percussion, mobility and depressibility, periodontal probing.
4. Pulp tests: (a) sensibility (neural): cold, heat, electric; (b) vitality (vascular): laser Doppler flowmetry, pulse oximetry, dual wavelength spectrophotometry, photoplethysmography; (c) tooth temperature: thermography, cholesteric liquid crystals.
5. Special tests: bite test (Tooth Slooth, FracFinder), transillumination, staining, test cavity, selective anaesthesia, caries removal.
6. Imaging: periapical, bitewing, digital, CBCT; MRI and ultrasound (experimental).
7. Newer aids: OCT, dental MRI, ultrasound imaging, computer expert systems.

## 2. History: chief complaint, history of present illness, medical and dental history

### Explanation

Chief complaint. Cohen's and Torabinejad's both say it must be documented in the patient's own words (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's adds that questions should be open-ended where possible (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Abbott in Ingle's says leading questions should be avoided wherever possible, because patients answer the way they think the dentist wants, and that the patient should be asked what they mean by "sensitive", since it can mean cold, sweet or biting pain (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

History of present illness. Cohen's frames five directions of questioning: localization, commencement, intensity, provocation or attenuation, and duration; it asks which drugs the patient took in the previous 4 to 6 hours, because analgesics alter test responses (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's characterises the pain: sharp, piercing, lancinating pain fits Aδ fibres; dull, boring, gnawing pain fits C fibres; diffuse pain is explained by the absence of proprioceptive fibres in the pulp; pain on changing head position that wakes the patient usually signals irreversible pulpitis (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's notes that true swelling must be associated with pulp necrosis, and that swelling with trismus, dysphagia or dyspnoea is a progressive cellulitis needing urgent referral (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's general chapter calls the history the single most important factor in arriving at a diagnosis and gives a core pain-history checklist (Table 2-1): duration, character, periodicity, severity, site, radiation, provoking, relieving and associated factors; thermal and electric tests, differential local anaesthesia, removal of restorations and radiographs must supplement, never replace, the history and examination (Escudier, 2017) [SRC]. This is a third scheme alongside Cohen's five directions; the content overlaps [Certain].

Medical history. Cohen's records baseline blood pressure and pulse at each visit and reads the questionnaire from two perspectives: conditions that change how care is delivered, and conditions that have oral manifestations or mimic dental pathosis; acute maxillary sinusitis is a common cause of diagnostic confusion (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's adds that tenderness to pressure of a whole quadrant of teeth is characteristic of sinusitis (Escudier, 2017) [SRC]. Torabinejad's adds that bisphosphonates, biologics and chemotherapy agents carry osteonecrosis risk and so push the choice towards root canal treatment rather than extraction (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's quantifies the risk as up to 20% with intravenous bisphosphonates against 0 to 0.04% with oral therapy, and notes that diabetic patients have a higher prevalence of periradicular lesions (Escudier, 2017) [SRC]. Harty's asks specifically about allergy to latex and to household bleach, because sodium hypochlorite is the irrigant (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

Dental history and records. Cohen's recommends the SOAP format: Subjective, Objective, Appraisal, and Plan (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri adds occupational and personal history, for example abrasion in tailors and cobblers who hold pins between the front teeth (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Chief complaint:** recorded in the patient's own words; open-ended, non-leading questions (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021). Usually pain, swelling, loss of function or aesthetics.

**2. History of present illness (pain analysis).** Five directions: localization, commencement, intensity, provocation or attenuation, duration (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
| Feature | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Sharp, lancinating, localised | Aδ fibres; dentine or reversible pulpitis |
| Dull, boring, diffuse, radiating | C fibres; irreversible pulpitis (no proprioceptors in pulp) |
| Spontaneous, nocturnal, postural | Irreversible pulpitis (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Pain on biting, tooth "high" | Periradicular inflammation or crack |
| Relieved by cold | Advanced irreversible pulpitis or necrosis |
| Swelling | Pulp necrosis; with trismus, dysphagia or dyspnoea: cellulitis, refer (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) |
Ask about analgesics taken in the previous 4 to 6 hours (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Pain is now a standard vital sign: rate it 0 to 10 as a baseline for comparison after treatment (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Duration is judged against control teeth: lingering after the stimulus is the key distinction between reversible and irreversible pulpitis. Traumatic injuries need their own workup (concussion, jaw fracture, lacerations, tetanus status) (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021). Clarify what the patient means by "sensitive" (cold, sweet or biting) and avoid leading questions (Abbott, 2019).

Harty's core checklist (Escudier, 2017): duration, character, periodicity, severity, site, radiation, provoking, relieving and associated factors; the history is the single most important factor in diagnosis.

**3. Medical history:** vital signs each visit; conditions needing modification of care (cardiac, bleeding, diabetes, pregnancy, bisphosphonates, radiotherapy) and conditions that mimic toothache (sinusitis, angina, trigeminal neuralgia); allergies, including latex and hypochlorite (Chandler and Chong, 2017).

**4. Dental history:** recent restorations, trauma, previous endodontic treatment, orthodontics, general anaesthesia or intubation, frequency of dental visits and attitude to treatment. Record in SOAP format: Subjective, Objective, Appraisal, Plan (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**5. Personal, occupational and family history:** habits (bruxism, smoking), occupation (abrasion in tailors who hold pins between the teeth), inherited defects such as dentinogenesis imperfecta (Sikri, 2019).

## 3. Extraoral and intraoral examination

### Explanation

Extraoral. Cohen's: observe the patient entering, look for asymmetry, palpate swellings (localised or diffuse, firm or fluctuant) and the cervical and submandibular lymph nodes; loss of the nasolabial fold may be the earliest sign of a canine space infection (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Swelling sites point to the source tooth: canine space for the maxillary canine, posterior cheek for the buccal space, submental for mandibular incisors, anterior palate for the maxillary lateral incisor (more than 50% of whose apices deviate distally or palatally), and the parapharyngeal space, which can be life threatening to the airway (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Extraoral sinus tracts are often mistreated by physicians and dermatologists; Torabinejad's shows such a case resolved by root canal treatment alone (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Abbott adds that jaw opening should be assessed for trismus (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

Intraoral soft tissue. A stoma or parulis usually indicates a necrotic pulp and chronic apical abscess (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's notes that "fistula" is often inappropriately used for a sinus tract, and describes tracing with a size 25 or 30 gutta-percha cone threaded into the stoma until resistance, then a radiograph (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

Hard tissue. Grossman's uses the "three Cs": color, contour, and consistency; it warns that not all discoloured teeth need endodontic treatment (amalgam, tetracycline) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri adds magnification (loupes, microscope) and the intraoral camera, using miniature CCD video devices with fibre-optic probes (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Abbott makes a point no other book makes as strongly: the so-called "special tests" should all be considered routine and mandatory tests for every suspected endodontic case, and he cites a Michigan study in which about 40% of teeth with infected canals and radiolucencies had never had symptoms of pulpitis (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. This is a stance, not a contradiction of other books, which still label some tests "special" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Extraoral examination:** facial asymmetry, swelling (localised or diffuse, firm or fluctuant), skin sinus tract or scar, lymph nodes (tender, firm nodes plus fever = spreading infection), mouth opening (trismus), TMJ and muscles (Abbott, 2019). Site of swelling points to the tooth (Berman and Rotstein, 2021):
| Swelling | Usual source |
|---|---|
| Loss of nasolabial fold, canine space | Maxillary canine |
| Upper lip, base of nose | Maxillary central incisors |
| Posterior cheek (buccal space) | Maxillary premolars and molars, mandibular premolars and first molar |
| Submental | Mandibular incisors |
| Submandibular | Mandibular molars draining below the mylohyoid |
| Anterior palate | Maxillary lateral incisor, palatal root of first premolar |
| Tonsillar or pharyngeal (parapharyngeal) | Molars; can be life threatening to the airway |
Extraoral sinus tracts are often mistreated by dermatologists; they heal after root canal treatment of the causative tooth (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Intraoral examination**
- Soft tissues: dried and inspected; swelling, sinus stoma or parulis (usually necrotic pulp with chronic apical abscess; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).
- **Sinus tract tracing:** a size 25-30 gutta-percha cone is threaded into the stoma and a radiograph taken to locate the source tooth and root (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). A tooth with a draining sinus is usually painless. The correct term is sinus tract, not fistula. A stoma in the gingival crevice must be differentiated from a vertical root fracture or a developmental groove (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Hard tissues: the "three Cs", colour, contour, consistency (Gopikrishna, 2021): caries, defective restorations, discolouration (loss of translucency suggests a pulpless tooth, but not every discoloured tooth needs root canal treatment), wear facets, cracks.
- Aids to vision: loupes, operating microscope, intraoral camera (Sikri, 2019).
Abbott (2019) argues that all tests below are routine, not "special", for any suspected endodontic case.

## 4. Periradicular tests: palpation, percussion, mobility and periodontal probing

### Explanation

Principle. Grossman's, Sikri and Cohen's agree that palpation, percussion, mobility and depressibility test the attachment apparatus, not the pulp, and are not diagnostic while disease is confined to the pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019; Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

Palpation. Firm digital pressure over the apices; a positive response indicates active periradicular inflammation but does not show whether its origin is endodontic or periodontal (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's lists what palpation shows: fluctuance for incision, pain, adenopathy, and the presence of bone crepitus (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Percussion. Pain to percussion does not indicate whether the tooth is vital; it reflects periodontal ligament inflammation, which the patient can localise because there are relatively few proprioceptors in the pulp but many in the periodontal ligament (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Technique: gentle finger tapping first, then the back end of a mirror handle, vertically and horizontally, control teeth first (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. To remove bias Grossman's changes the sequence of teeth between tests (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and the Molar Guidebook labels teeth "A, B and C" so the patient compares them blind (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. Torabinejad's explains tender neighbours: cytokines and neuropeptides spread locally (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. The books differ on recording: Sikri records percussion notes, with a metallic note for ankylosis (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Abbott says it is impossible to have a negative response to percussion and grades it normal, tender, very tender, different sensation or ankylosis sound (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's trauma chapter uses the same sign: a laterally luxated tooth whose apex is locked in bone emits a high pitched or metallic tone on percussion (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Jain calls percussion a very reliable means of diagnosing irreversible pulpitis or apical periodontitis (Jain, 2025) [SRC], which overstates it for pulpitis, since Cohen's and Grossman's both say percussion does not test the pulp [Likely: Jain's sentence means late pulpitis that has reached the ligament].

Mobility. Three grading schemes appear, side by side:
- Cohen's Box 1.2: +1 first distinguishable movement; +2 horizontal movement no greater than 1 mm; +3 greater than 1 mm, with or without rotation or vertical depressibility; anything over +1 is abnormal (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's Miller index, Sikri and Harty's give the same three grades (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019; Chandler and Chong, 2017).
- Abbott's version of the Miller index: Grade 0 physiological, Grade I up to 1 mm, Grade II more than 1 mm, Grade III severe horizontal and vertical movement (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].
The numbers therefore shift by one grade between books; write which scheme you use [Certain]. Harty's lists periodontal disease, root fracture and acute apical periodontitis as common causes of increased mobility (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Grossman's adds that depressibility means a poor to hopeless prognosis and that teeth with third-degree mobility should not be treated unless mobility falls once periodontal pressure is relieved (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Periodontal probing. Isolated vertical bone loss may be endodontic; a vertical root fracture gives a narrow pocket extending deep down the root with otherwise normal periodontium (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's describes the Williams, UNC-15 and WHO probes, warns that sinus tracts opening into the crevice are missed without a probe, and gives Glickman's furcation grades I to IV, whereas Cohen's uses three classes (Gopikrishna, 2021; Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Abbott prefers a narrow WHO ball-end probe walked in a continuous, stepwise manner around the whole circumference (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's recommends a nonmetallic probe for narrow defects (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Gutmann and Lovdahl note that probings 1 mm to either side of a fracture pocket may be normal (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Harty's endo-perio chapter gives the probing pattern that separates the lesions: a sinus tract of endodontic origin draining through the sulcus is usually narrow and lacks width; a primary periodontal lesion gives a wide pocket that becomes shallower as the probe moves laterally, with plaque or calculus and normal pulp responses; a palatogingival groove gives a tubular, isolated pocket; when a lesion treated as endodontic heals on probing and radiograph, a retrospective diagnosis is confirmed (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]. Abbott also includes occlusal analysis (fremitus, articulating paper) because traumatic occlusion can cause pulpitis (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

These tests assess the **attachment apparatus, not the pulp** (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019).

**Palpation:** firm fingertip pressure over the root apices, buccal and lingual, compared with the other side. Detects tenderness, swelling (hard, soft, fluctuant), bony expansion, crepitus, lymphadenopathy. Positive = periradicular inflammation, but cannot separate endodontic from periodontal origin (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**Percussion:** control teeth first; light finger tap, then mirror handle; vertical (apical periodontitis) and horizontal (lateral periodontium). Change the order of teeth to avoid bias (Gopikrishna, 2021). Tenderness = periodontal ligament inflammation from pulpal disease, occlusal trauma, periodontal abscess, high restoration or sinusitis; it does not show whether the pulp is vital (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Record as normal, tender, very tender, "feels different" (chronic apical periodontitis) or dull ankylosis sound (Abbott, 2019); a metallic note suggests ankylosis (Sikri, 2019). Patients localise percussion pain well because proprioceptors are plentiful in the periodontal ligament but few in the pulp (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Blind comparison ("tooth A, B or C") reduces bias (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025). Adjacent teeth may be tender from local spread of inflammatory mediators (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Mobility and depressibility:** two instrument handles, buccolingual and vertical.
| Grade | Cohen / Miller (Grossman's, Harty's) | Miller as given by Abbott (2019) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First sign of movement | Up to 1 mm |
| 2 | ≤1 mm horizontal | >1 mm horizontal |
| 3 | >1 mm, or depressible | Horizontal and vertical |
Depressibility = poor to hopeless prognosis (Gopikrishna, 2021). Mobility from an apical lesion should decrease after successful root canal treatment (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Periodontal probing:** walk a narrow (WHO or nonmetallic) probe around the whole tooth (Abbott, 2019). Wide, generalised pockets = periodontal; isolated narrow deep pocket = sinus tract through the sulcus, vertical root fracture or endo-perio lesion (Berman and Rotstein, 2021); probings 1 mm either side of a fracture pocket may be normal (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). Endodontic sinus tract: narrow probing; primary periodontal pocket: wide, shallowing laterally, plaque and calculus, normal pulp tests; palatogingival groove: tubular isolated pocket (Rotstein, 2017). Probes: Williams, UNC-15, WHO (Gopikrishna, 2021). Record furcation involvement (Glickman grades I-IV in Grossman's; Classes I-III in Cohen's). A pocket of endodontic origin may resolve after root canal treatment; a pocket on a vital tooth will not (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**Occlusal analysis:** wear facets, fremitus, high spots with articulating paper (Abbott, 2019).

## 5. Pulp sensibility and vitality: terminology and principles

### Explanation

Terminology. Abbott insists that thermal and electric tests are "sensibility" tests, defined as the ability to respond to a stimulus, while "vitality" in dentistry implies a viable blood supply; he calls "sensitivity tests" an error (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's uses "pulp test (pulp sensibility test)" but then states that the sensibility of the pulp is determined by the intactness and health of the vascular supply, not by the status of the pulpal nerve fibres (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. This sentence contradicts the chapter's own definition (sensibility = neural response) and is better read as being about "vitality" [Likely: a wording error in Cohen's]. Torabinejad's and Harty's call them "pulp sensitivity tests" (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC], the very term Abbott rejects.

Principle and limits. A response confirms only functioning nerve fibres; the neural component is the last part of the pulp to undergo necrosis, so false results occur (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. Torabinejad's stresses that these are not tests of teeth but tests of a patient's response (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's says there is not necessarily a good correlation between clinical signs and pulp histology (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; this is debated (critical synthesis, claim 1). False responses: Cohen's Box 1.4 lists anxiety, poor isolation, metal contact and partial necrosis as false positives and calcification, trauma, immature apex, drugs and poor contact as false negatives; Sikri adds current transferred through liquefaction necrosis and sclerotic dentine in older teeth (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Technique rules common to all books: explain the test, test control teeth first (contralateral, then adjacent, suspect tooth last), use hand signals, and wait about 15 seconds between teeth (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025; Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sensibility vs vitality.** Thermal and electric tests are **sensibility** tests ("the ability to respond to a stimulus"); they show functioning Aδ fibres, not blood supply. **Vitality** means a viable blood supply and is measured by laser Doppler flowmetry or pulse oximetry (Abbott, 2019). The ideal test is accurate, reproducible and noninjurious (Sikri, 2019). These are tests of the patient's response, not of the tooth (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Rules:** explain the test; isolate and dry; test the contralateral and adjacent control teeth first and the suspect tooth last; hand signal on feeling and lingering; about 15 s between teeth (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025).

## 6. Pulp tests: cold, heat, electric (compact section)

### Explanation

Mechanism. Cold causes outward hydrodynamic fluid flow stimulating Aδ fibres (Brännström's theory in Grossman's Box 6.12); heat causes vasodilatation and raised intrapulpal pressure (Van Hassel's theory, Box 6.11) (Gopikrishna, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

Cold agents: the books disagree on the best one.
- Refrigerant spray: Cohen's calls it the most popular, reproducible and equivalent to CO2, with tetrafluoroethane at −26.2°C on a large #2 cotton pellet (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's says cold testing is the most reliable measure and sprays the most convenient and reliable (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; the Molar Guidebook calls spray on a small pellet "the best and simplest" (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC].
- Dry ice (CO2): Abbott, citing Chen and Abbott, says dry ice sticks are the most reliable cold test, made in the Odontotest device; sprays turn liquid quickly in the warm mouth and can stimulate the gingiva; ice is of little value (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's makes CO2 snow (−78°C) the test of choice for crowned teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and Abbott shows that porcelain crowns do not insulate the pulp against cold (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri warns that CO2 may cause enamel infarction lines (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's says CO2 does not cause any significant enamel crazing (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].
- Temperature figures differ: Harty's gives tetrafluoroethane spray as about −50°C (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC], whereas Cohen's gives −26.2°C (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], the Molar Guidebook about −26°C (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC] and Sikri −15°C to −26°C (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Abbott's prose (minus signs OCR-damaged in the file) separates the two products now used, tetrafluoroethane or a propane/butane/isobutane mixture, and gives about −26°C for tetrafluoroethane and about −50°C for the mixture (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's −50°C therefore matches the mixture, not tetrafluoroethane, and is very probably a misattribution [Likely]. Pellet size also differs: Cohen's found a large #2 pellet gave a lower intrapulpal temperature (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], whereas Harty's and the Molar Guidebook use a small pledget or pellet so that only one tooth is stimulated (Chandler and Chong, 2017; Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. Harty's also states that the colder the stimulus the more reliable the test, which matters most in posterior teeth with thicker enamel and dentine, and that CO2 snow (−78.5°C), being the coldest, is particularly useful for crowned teeth; a cold-water bath under dam suits poorly localised cold pain (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

Heat. The books disagree on hot water and on what a normal response is.
- Cohen's, Grossman's, Harty's, the Molar Guidebook and Abbott all use hot water under rubber dam, tooth by tooth (Abbott recommends heavy dam) (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021; Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Torabinejad's says heated metal instruments or hot water can damage the pulp and should be avoided, and calls a dry rubber prophylaxis cup a safe alternative (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's says the prophylaxis cup is seldom used because its vibration may give a false positive (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].
- Cohen's normal response to heat is a sensation that disappears on removal (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Abbott says teeth with clinically normal pulps do not respond to heat, so heat cannot separate normal from necrotic and is useful only when heat is the sole complaint (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].
- Safety limits: about 65°C with an electronic heat carrier (Abbott, 2019; Sikri, 2019); no more than 5 seconds (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025; Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; wait 10 seconds between teeth because of delayed responses (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. A tooth that hurts with heat and is relieved by cold is typically necrotic (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

Electric pulp test (EPT). Toothpaste on the probe, placed on the incisal third of the facial surface (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], or the mid-third of the mesiobuccal cusp of molars (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; thresholds rise from incisor to molar as enamel and dentine thicken (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Readings matter only relative to a control tooth; results are most accurate when there is no response at all (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. EPT cannot show inflammation (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) and does not reproduce pain (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's still reads early and delayed responses as diseased (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's goes further, saying a lingering dull ache after the probe is removed reflects C-fibre stimulation and irreversible pulpal inflammation (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Both conflict with Torabinejad's and Abbott's view above that EPT cannot show inflammation. Harty's adds saliva conduction through the periodontal ligament as a false positive, failure to complete the circuit as a false negative, and unreliability in immature and concussed teeth (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Crowns: a bridging technique through an explorer (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Calcified canals: Cohen's lists calcific obliterations in the root canals as a cause of false negatives (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], while Torabinejad's, Abbott and the Molar Guidebook say EPT is more accurate than cold in pulp canal calcification (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Abbott, 2019; Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. The two views fit if EPT is the better of two imperfect tests in such teeth [Likely]. Pacemakers: Sikri says EPT is contraindicated (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; the Molar Guidebook cites a 2006 study showing no interference with implanted pacemakers or defibrillators (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. Orthodontic arch wires conduct current, so use cold (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

Accuracy. Cohen's cites sensitivities of 0.83 (cold), 0.86 (heat), 0.72 (EPT), heat specificity only 41%, and overall accuracy cold 86%, EPT 81%, heat 71% (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's and the Molar Guidebook quote the same three accuracies (Gopikrishna, 2021; Witherspoon and Regan, 2025). The PubMed abstract of Petersson et al confirms these sensitivities and specificities (Petersson et al, 1999) [LIT]. Harty's cites a nonsensitive reaction meaning necrosis 89% (cold), 88% (EPT), 48% (heat), and a sensitive reaction meaning a vital pulp 90%, 84% and 83%, concluding that accuracy runs cold, then electric, then heat (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC], the same order as Cohen's. Note that Cohen's calls the true-positive rate "sensibility"; the statistical term is sensitivity, as the Molar Guidebook defines it (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. Combination: no response to both cold and EPT in a mature, non-traumatised tooth = necrosis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; in Weisleder's study, cited by Abbott, 97% of teeth responding to all three tests bled on access (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Gutmann and Lovdahl go further: if a tooth responds to heat or cold, EPT may be redundant (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].

Lingering, the books do not agree on the threshold: Harty's allows reversible pulpitis pain up to 5 to 10 seconds; Gutmann and Lovdahl call any response of 10 seconds or more abnormal; the Molar Guidebook uses more than 15 to 20 seconds; Jain writes "often 30 s or longer" (Chandler and Chong, 2017; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Witherspoon and Regan, 2025; Jain, 2025) [SRC]. Write "lingering compared with the control tooth" [Certain that no single figure is agreed].

False responses and special cases: immature teeth lack fully developed Aδ fibres (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC], so cold is most dependable in children (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]; traumatised teeth may not respond for up to 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC], so lack of response after injury is not an indication for immediate root canal treatment (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; ibuprofen alters cold, percussion and palpation results (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; recession lowers cold sensitivity (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; a gangrenous necrotic pulp gives a false positive EPT response (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Test | Agent / method | Normal | Abnormal | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold** (hydrodynamic, Aδ) | Tetrafluoroethane spray (−26°C) on a pellet (large in Cohen's, small in Harty's); CO2 snow (−78°C) for crowns; ice | Short sharp response, gone in seconds | Lingering = irreversible pulpitis; none = necrosis | Calcified canals, recession, ice drips (false +) |
| **Heat** (vasodilatation) | Heated GP with Vaseline, electronic carrier (65°C), hot water under dam; max 5 s | Mild, transient (Cohen's) or no response (Abbott) | Immediate intense or lingering pain; relief by cold | Least specific; delayed responses; pulp damage |
| **EPT** (ionic change, Aδ) | Toothpaste on probe, incisal third or mesiobuccal cusp, lip clip | Response near control value | No response at maximum = necrosis | Cannot show inflammation; immature, traumatised, crowns, arch wires; liquefied pulp (false +) |

**EPT technique** (Gopikrishna, 2021): explain; isolate and dry; test the control tooth first; toothpaste on the electrode on dry enamel (incisal third of anteriors, mid-third of the mesiobuccal cusp of molars), away from restorations and gingiva; complete the circuit with lip clip or patient's finger; turn the rheostat slowly; patient signals "tingling" or "warmth"; record and repeat. Interdental dam strips limit conduction to adjacent teeth (Chandler and Chong, 2017).

**False responses**
| False positive | False negative |
|---|---|
| Anxiety, poor isolation, contact with metal or gingiva (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) | Calcified canals, recently traumatised teeth, immature apex |
| Partial necrosis in a multirooted tooth | Analgesics or sedatives, poor contact, dead battery |
| Liquefaction necrosis conducting current (Sikri, 2019) | Older teeth with sclerotic dentine (Sikri, 2019) |

**Interpreting thermal response** (Sikri, 2019): mild response subsiding in 1-2 s = normal; strong momentary pain subsiding quickly = reversible pulpitis; pain lingering several seconds = irreversible pulpitis; no response = necrosis or false negative.

**Accuracy:** cold 86%, EPT 81%, heat 71%; heat specificity only 41% (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). EPT correctly identified 71% of necrotic and 92% of vital pulps; most tests have high negative predictive values of 0.80-0.90 (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025). A multirooted tooth may respond if only one root is vital (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). **Best practice:** combine cold with EPT; no response to both in a mature, non-traumatised tooth = necrosis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Abbott, 2019).

**Disagreements:** best cold agent: spray (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) versus CO2 (Abbott, 2019). Hot water: used by most books, avoided by Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad (2021). In calcified canals EPT is preferred (Abbott, 2019), though Cohen's lists calcification as an EPT false negative. EPT in pacemaker patients: contraindicated (Sikri, 2019) versus no interference in a 2006 study (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025). Lingering threshold varies from 10 s (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) to 15-20 s (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) or 30 s (Jain, 2025). A lingering dull ache after EPT signals irreversible pulpitis (Chandler and Chong, 2017), whereas EPT cannot show inflammation (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Abbott, 2019).

**Special cases:** immature teeth lack mature Aδ fibres, so prefer cold (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021); traumatised teeth may not respond for 3 months, so no response is not an indication for immediate root canal treatment (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017); analgesics alter responses (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021); ageing slows and weakens the cold response (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025); crowns: CO2 cold (cold is readily conducted through crowns) or EPT bridging through an explorer (Abbott, 2019; Berman and Rotstein, 2021); orthodontic wires: cold only (Abbott, 2019). Vitality tests (LDF, pulse oximetry) are in section 9.

## 7. Special tests: bite test, transillumination, staining, test cavity, selective anaesthesia

### Explanation

Bite test. Pain on biting comes from symptomatic apical periodontitis (pain wherever pressure is applied) or a crack (pain only when one cusp is loaded in a certain direction) (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Devices: cotton applicators, orangewood sticks, rubber wheels, and the Tooth Slooth and FracFinder (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Gutmann and Lovdahl prefer semi-hard gutta-percha stopping or an orangewood stick (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Mechanism: biting pressure temporarily separates the fractured segments (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Pain on release: Cohen's calls it a common finding with a cracked tooth; Grossman's makes "pain on release = cracked tooth" a rule; Sikri and the Molar Guidebook agree (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021; Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. Abbott disagrees, citing Abbott and Leow: 68% of patients with cracked teeth and reversible pulpitis had pain on application, only 8% on release (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; the study's PubMed abstract confirms 100 cracked teeth with reversible pulpitis, 80 of them managed without endodontic treatment, but does not give these percentages (Abbott and Leow, 2009) [LIT]. Release pain therefore supports, but does not prove, a crack [Likely].

Transillumination. Fractures block transilluminated light: the side nearer the light glows, the side beyond the crack is dark (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; a craze line confined to enamel lets light pass (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Abbott traces it to Cameron's "Dentalamp" (1916), cites Jang's study in which transillumination had the highest sensitivity, positive predictive value and reliability compared with operating light and microscope, and reports no added advantage from a dye; he prefers a small 3.5-4 mm tip (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri extends it to caries, calculus and pulp status (necrotic pulp chambers look darker) and asks for the room lights to be switched off (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's cautions that dyes and transillumination cannot always show the full extent of a fracture (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

Staining. Methylene blue painted on, excess removed with 70% isopropyl alcohol (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's adds iodine and caries-detection dyes and sealing the dye in for a week (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri adds India ink, crystal violet and erythrosine (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Wedging after removing the restoration shows whether the segments separate: little or no separation is an incomplete fracture (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

Test cavity. Here the books disagree sharply.
- Last resort, still acceptable: Cohen's calls it an invasive irreversible test used only when other tests are impossible or inconclusive, done without anaesthesia with a #1 or #2 round bur with air-water coolant; pain on reaching dentine shows only that some viable nerve tissue remains, and the cavity is restored (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's: if no pain, cavity preparation may continue into the chamber (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Gutmann and Lovdahl: in calcified teeth the patient may feel nothing until the pulp is exposed (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Harty's frames it as historical ("historically ... suggested" when all other tests are inconclusive), done without anaesthetic and with copious irrigation; sensitivity may come from Aδ fibres surviving in a necrotic pulp, and a receded pulp with heavy tertiary dentine can give a false negative (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC], the same point Gutmann and Lovdahl make for calcified teeth. Torabinejad's includes it as "dentin stimulation" (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].
- Rejected: Abbott calls it barbaric, with no place in modern dentistry, giving no information beyond cold and EPT and many false responses (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; the Molar Guidebook authors see no place for test cavities (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC].
- Technique conflict: Sikri prepares the cavity "without anesthesia and without coolant" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], whereas Cohen's, Harty's and Grossman's use water coolant. Cutting dentine without coolant risks heat injury to a pulp that may be healthy, so coolant should be written [Likely].

Selective anaesthesia (anaesthetic test). Agreed purpose: localise poorly localised or referred pain when pulp tests are inconclusive; it identifies the arch better than the tooth (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Technique differs:
- Cohen's and Harty's: intraligamentary injection from the distal sulcus of the most posterior maxillary tooth, moving forward one tooth at a time, then the mandible (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].
- Grossman's: infiltration or intraligamentary, posterior tooth first; inferior alveolar block if the arch is unknown; a last resort, safer than a test cavity (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri: 0.2 ml in the distal sulcus (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
- Torabinejad's: maxillary infiltration first, starting at the most mesial suspected site and working distally, block last; PDL injections anaesthetise several teeth and are not useful (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Abbott also starts mesially and says mandibular infiltration is unreliable, so the test is unreliable there (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].
The mesial-to-distal order avoids anaesthetising a tooth that was not meant to be included through anterior spread of the solution [Guessing: the books do not explain their order]. Torabinejad's also lists caries removal: a soft carious exposure in an otherwise asymptomatic, normally responding tooth is asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Bite test:** indicated when the patient reports pain on biting. Devices: cotton roll, orangewood stick, rubber wheel, gutta-percha stopping, Tooth Slooth or FracFinder (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). The cup is placed on one cusp at a time, control teeth first; the patient bites slowly to full closure, holds firm pressure for a few seconds, then releases quickly (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Biting separates the fractured segments temporarily (Chandler and Chong, 2017). Pain from every cusp = apical periodontitis; pain from one cusp in one direction = crack. Pain on release is classically a crack (Gopikrishna, 2021), but Abbott (2019) found 68% of cracked teeth hurt on application and only 8% on release, so release pain is not specific.

**2. Transillumination:** fibre-optic light at the CEJ in a dark room; a crack blocks light (dark beyond the line), a craze line transmits it (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021). Abbott (2019) found it more sensitive than the microscope, with no gain from adding dye.

**3. Staining:** methylene blue, iodine or caries-detector dye; excess removed with 70% isopropyl alcohol; viewed under magnification. Wedging shows whether segments separate (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021). Neither shows the full depth of a crack. Longitudinal fractures: craze line, fractured cusp, cracked tooth, split tooth, vertical root fracture (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021); craze lines do not extend into dentine and need no treatment (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**4. Test cavity:** small Class I cavity with #1-2 round bur, water coolant, no anaesthesia, used when other tests are impossible (e.g. full crown with no tooth structure for bridging). Pain at dentine = some viable nerve (restore); no pain = probable necrosis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Invasive and irreversible; Abbott (2019) and Witherspoon and Regan (2025) consider it obsolete; calcified canals may give no response until exposure (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).

**5. Selective anaesthesia:** for diffuse or referred pain when tests are inconclusive; it localises the arch better than the tooth.

| Gopikrishna (2021) | Infiltration or PDL posteriorly; IANB if the arch is unknown |
| Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad (2021); Abbott (2019) | Maxillary infiltration, mesial to distal; PDL injection not useful; mandible unreliable |

**6. Caries removal:** a carious exposure in an asymptomatic tooth = asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 8. Radiographic examination and CBCT (brief)

### Explanation

Cohen's: few tests give as much information as radiography, but the image should be used only as one sign; two pretreatment images at different angulations are often enough (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's adds a second film at a 20° horizontal shift and lists the applications given by Walton and Gomez; Sikri explains the buccal object (SLOB, Clark's) rule (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Inter-observer agreement in Goldman's study is reported as 50% by Cohen's and 67% by Grossman's (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; the books may be citing different parts of the study (Cohen's cites two Goldman papers) [Guessing].

Limits. Widening or a break in the lamina dura is the most consistent finding in a nonvital tooth; loss limited to cancellous bone is not detected (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's quotes Bender that a lesion is usually larger than its image; Sikri gives "only after 33%" of bone destruction (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. A radiograph cannot differentiate abscess, granuloma or cyst; histology is needed (Gopikrishna, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's gives four signs of an endodontic lesion: absent apical lamina dura, radiolucency that stays at the apex on angled films and CBCT, a "hanging drop" shape, and an identifiable cause; a sizable radiolucency with a vital pulp is not endodontic (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's adds Ørstavik's periapical index (PAI) for research (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Abbott calls the panoramic film a screening radiograph only (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

Harty's chapters add three limits: radiographs used in isolation will not indicate the pulp condition or treatment need (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; acute lesions are not radiologically visible until bone, including the cortical plates, is resorbed, so adjacent teeth must be sensitivity-tested to find the culprit (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. For trauma, Harty's follows the IADT set of a 90-degree periapical, an occlusal and laterally angled periapicals (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC].

Digital radiography uses 50% to 90% less radiation (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; the Molar Guidebook says wet film can no longer be recommended (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC].

CBCT: positions differ.
- Adjunct only: Cohen's says CBCT is a diagnostic adjunct, not a replacement (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's says it must be justified case by case, warns of false positive periapical diagnoses, and names the greater radiation dose as its key limitation (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Harty's trauma chapter values it for root fractures and lateral luxations but does not consider it routine (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Abbott notes that teeth with normal pulps may show widened PDL spaces or even radiolucencies on CBCT (Abbott, 2019) [SRC].
- Increasingly routine: Torabinejad's says CBCT, though not yet the standard of care, has become increasingly routine, and lists its indications (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; the Molar Guidebook authors consider routine narrow field CBCT in molar endodontics a judicious use of radiation (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC].
Limitations: artefacts, image noise and poor soft-tissue contrast (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Full detail belongs to the Imaging topic.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Intraoral periapical radiograph** (paralleling technique) is essential but is only "one sign" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Take two angulations (20° horizontal shift; SLOB rule) to separate roots and canals (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019). Bitewings show caries depth, restorations and crestal bone; the panoramic film is only a screening view (Abbott, 2019).

**Radiograph shows:** caries, restorations, pulp chamber size, calcification, resorption, root number and curvature, PDL widening, lamina dura, periapical radiolucency or condensing osteitis, fractures, previous treatment, sinus tract tracing; it also detects procedural errors (perforation, ledge, separated instrument) and is used for follow-up (Gopikrishna, 2021). Image requirements: tooth centred, at least 5 mm of bone beyond the apex, no elongation (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019). Normal structures (mental foramen, incisive canal, sinus) and non-endodontic lesions can mimic periapical pathosis, so radiographs are always read with pulp tests (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Signs of an endodontic lesion** (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021): apical lamina dura lost; lesion stays at the apex on angled views; "hanging drop"; identifiable cause. A large lesion with a vital pulp is not endodontic.

**Limitations:** 2D image; lesions confined to cancellous bone are invisible (Berman and Rotstein, 2021); the lesion is larger than its image (Gopikrishna, 2021); an acute abscess may look normal until cortical bone is resorbed (Hülsmann and Chong, 2017); cannot tell cyst from granuloma; interpreters agreed in only 50-67% of cases (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Trauma:** 90-degree periapical, occlusal and laterally angled views (IADT, as given by Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).

**Digital radiography (RVG):** 50-90% less dose, instant, enhancement, storage (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

**CBCT:** voxels, axial, sagittal and coronal slices, no superimposition. Indications: contradictory signs, hidden lesions (maxillary posterior), missed canals, resorption, root fracture, trauma, surgical planning (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021). It is an adjunct, justified case by case, with more dose, artefacts and false positives (Chandler and Chong, 2017; Abbott, 2019).

## 9. Recent and newer diagnostic aids

### Explanation

Vascular (true vitality) tests.
- Laser Doppler flowmetry: the light's frequency shifts when it hits moving red blood cells; Sikri gives a He-Ne laser at 632.8 nm (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Its advantage is objective data rather than subjective patient responses, and it is useful after luxation injuries (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's calls it the most promising technique (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC], and in severely luxated teeth blood flow can be detected well before the pulp responds to EPT (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's describes the beam as infrared (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], Sikri as red He-Ne light. The books differ on restored teeth: Harty's says LDF may be used on extensively restored or carious teeth, though it is time consuming, technique-sensitive and costly (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Abbott, citing Chen and Abbott, found it suitable for unrestored teeth and less reliable than CO2 and EPT (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; the PubMed abstract of that study instead gives LDF accuracy 96.3%, not significantly different from EPT (97.7%) or CO2 (97.0%), and calls all tests except ice reliable (Chen and Abbott, 2011) [LIT]. Drawbacks: it needs a stent of impression material or acrylic, gingival blood flow gives false readings, and it is not used routinely (Abbott, 2019; Sikri, 2019; Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].
- Pulse oximetry: two wavelengths, red and infrared (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's gives 660 nm and 940 nm, Sikri 660 nm and 800 nm (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The 940 nm figure matches standard oximeters [RECALL] [Likely]. Views on readiness differ: Cohen's says custom-made sensors were more accurate than electric and thermal tests, though devices are cumbersome (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's says the technology has not been commercialised (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri notes that pulpal flow is hard to read as a pulse because the pulp is encased in enamel and dentine (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
- Dual wavelength spectrophotometry measures oxygenation in the capillary bed and does not need a pulse; the optical reflection vitalometer uses reflected rather than transmitted light; photoplethysmography detects optical density changes (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's lists transmitted light photoplethysmography among "recent technologies" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's sums them up as more experimental than clinically practical (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's says most "physiometric" methods are experimental (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

Tooth temperature. Vital teeth are warmer than nonvital teeth; cholesteric liquid crystals (Howell, 1970) are no longer advocated; infrared thermography detects changes as small as 0.1°C (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Imaging and other newer aids.
- Optical coherence tomography has been suggested for enamel cracks (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].
- MRI: Cohen's says its endodontic use is still limited (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri describes SWIFT (sweep imaging with Fourier transformation) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The Molar Guidebook describes dedicated dental MRI (ddMRI) with a low-field 0.55 T scanner, compressed sensing and artificial intelligence in the protocols, bright apical marrow (oedema) in apical periodontitis, radiation-free follow-up, and metal artefacts as its limit (Spin-Neto, Liedke and Kirkevang, 2025) [SRC]. This is the most recent source read and the only one treating MRI as near-clinical [Likely].
- Ultrasound imaging: Sikri says it differentiates between cyst and granuloma (hypoechoic fluid cavity without Doppler flow versus vascular granuloma), but it fails in the posterior region because of the thick cortical plate (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. This contrasts with Grossman's and Torabinejad's statement that only histology separates cyst from granuloma, although they speak of radiographs, not ultrasound (Gopikrishna, 2021; Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's statement that ultrasound is above the range of human hearing "(1–20 kHz)" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC] misstates human hearing (about 20 Hz to 20 kHz); diagnostic ultrasound uses megahertz frequencies [RECALL] [Likely].
- Digital subtraction radiography for monitoring lesion healing, tuned-aperture CT (rarely used), C-arm real-time imaging, the COMENDEX computer expert system and laser optical disc storage (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
- Molecular aids (host biomarkers, microbial detection) belong to answer D3 [Certain, by agreed structure].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Vascular (true vitality) tests**
| Aid | Principle | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Laser Doppler flowmetry | Light (He-Ne 632.8 nm) is frequency-shifted by moving RBCs (Doppler) | Objective; good after trauma; needs stent; gingival flow interferes; expensive (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Sikri, 2019) |
| Pulse oximetry | Red 660 nm and infrared light; oxygenated vs reduced haemoglobin | Custom probes promising (Berman and Rotstein, 2021); not commercialised (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Dual wavelength spectrophotometry | Oxygenation in the capillary bed; no pulse needed | Experimental |
| Optical reflection vitalometer / photoplethysmography | Reflected or transmitted light changes with blood volume | Experimental |
Torabinejad's group calls these more experimental than practical (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021). LDF can be used on extensively restored teeth (Chandler and Chong, 2017), but Abbott (2019) found it suited to unrestored teeth; after luxation, flow returns before the EPT response (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).

**LDF in more detail** (Sikri, 2019): samples about 10 mm3 of tissue; rubber dam and a custom stent hold the probe; advantages: reflects vascularity, noninvasive, useful in children, immature and traumatised teeth, monitors revascularisation of replanted teeth; disadvantages: motion sensitivity, gingival flow, systemic drugs alter flow, thick enamel in molars, cost. **Pulse oximetry:** detects pulpal circulation independent of gingival flow and helps in trauma; but pulp is encased in hard tissue and readings are mixed (Sikri, 2019).

**B. Tooth temperature:** infrared thermography (detects 0.1°C), electronic thermography; cholesteric liquid crystals now abandoned (Sikri, 2019).

**C. Newer imaging**
- OCT: enamel cracks (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Dental MRI (ddMRI): low-field 0.55 T, AI-assisted protocols; shows apical oedema, radiation-free follow-up; limited by metal artefacts (Spin-Neto, Liedke and Kirkevang, 2025). SWIFT MRI images hard and soft tissue (Sikri, 2019).
- Ultrasound with colour Doppler: no ionising radiation; hard tissue hyperechoic, fluid anechoic; cyst hypoechoic and avascular, granuloma vascular; works only where the cortical plate is thin or perforated, so not feasible posteriorly (Sikri, 2019).
- Digital subtraction radiography (healing), TACT, C-arm real-time imaging (Sikri, 2019).

**D. Computer aids:** COMENDEX expert system; laser optical disc storage (Sikri, 2019).

## 10. Diagnostic terminology (AAE) and arriving at the diagnosis

### Explanation

Cohen's uses the AAE 2016 terms (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]: pulpal (normal pulp; reversible pulpitis; symptomatic and asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis; pulp necrosis; previously treated; previously initiated therapy) and apical (normal apical tissues; symptomatic and asymptomatic apical periodontitis; acute and chronic apical abscess); Torabinejad's adds condensing osteitis, which resolves in about 50% of teeth after root canal treatment (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Not all books accept it. Harty's says there is no universally agreed terminology and keeps acute/chronic terms (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]; Abbott uses his own scheme (acute and chronic reversible pulpitis, pulp necrobiosis, pulpless and infected canal, primary and secondary acute apical periodontitis) and argues that a necrotic pulp in itself does not cause apical periodontitis (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]; Jain criticises the AAE scheme because it does not separate teeth painful enough for emergency care from those that merely react more to tests (Jain, 2025) [SRC]. Cohen's advises avoiding the term "cracked tooth syndrome" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

Arriving at the diagnosis. Abbott: there should always be at least two diagnoses (pulp or root canal, and periapical), and four elements: the tooth, the pulp, the periapical tissues and the cause (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. The examination should reproduce the chief complaint; if it cannot, irreversible treatment should be withheld (Abbott, 2019) [SRC], matching Harty's rule that invasive treatment waits until a clear diagnosis is possible (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Grossman's: one should not rely on any single test (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's: endodontic pathosis is never without a cause, and analgesics may justify retesting another day (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's makes restorability part of the diagnosis: it must be assessed before treatment, if necessary by removing the whole restoration, and aids such as the Tooth Restorability Index help; it also notes that in acute apical periodontitis a negative sensitivity response indicates an endodontic cause (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Patients localise a stimulated tooth only 37.2% of the time (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], so referred pain must be considered. Okazaki and co-authors stress that a clinical diagnosis is provisional, histology being the gold standard (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's closes with five questions, beginning "Is the existing problem of dental origin?" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**AAE diagnostic terminology (Berman and Rotstein, 2021)**
| Pulpal | Apical |
|---|---|
| Normal pulp | Normal apical tissues |
| Reversible pulpitis | Symptomatic apical periodontitis |
| Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis | Asymptomatic apical periodontitis |
| Asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis | Acute apical abscess |
| Pulp necrosis | Chronic apical abscess |
| Previously treated; previously initiated therapy | Condensing osteitis (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021) |
Key definitions (Berman and Rotstein, 2021): reversible pulpitis, inflammation that should resolve and the pulp return to normal; symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, a vital inflamed pulp incapable of healing, with lingering thermal pain; necrosis, usually nonresponsive to pulp testing; symptomatic apical periodontitis, pain to biting, percussion or palpation; acute apical abscess, rapid onset, spontaneous pain, pus and swelling; chronic apical abscess, gradual onset, little discomfort, intermittent pus through a sinus tract. Dentine hypersensitivity must be separated from reversible pulpitis.

No terminology is universally agreed (Chandler and Chong, 2017); Abbott (2019) uses acute/chronic and "pulpless infected" categories; Jain (2025) notes that the AAE terms do not grade pain severity.

**Arriving at the diagnosis**
1. Always give two diagnoses, pulpal and apical, plus the tooth and the cause (Abbott, 2019).
2. Reproduce the chief complaint; if it cannot be reproduced, do not start irreversible treatment (Abbott, 2019; Chandler and Chong, 2017).
3. Never rely on a single test (Gopikrishna, 2021); several corroborating findings make a stronger diagnosis.
4. Consider referred and non-odontogenic pain: patients localise the stimulated tooth only 37% of the time (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
5. Retest another day if analgesics have been taken (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021); refer if still uncertain.
6. Assess restorability before treatment, removing the restoration if in doubt (Chandler and Chong, 2017).
7. Record the diagnosis, prognosis and consent.

**Conclusion.** No single aid is diagnostic. History gives the provisional diagnosis; periradicular tests, combined cold and electric tests, special tests and radiographs confirm it. Vascular tests, CBCT, dental MRI and OCT are improving objectivity, but most remain adjuncts or research tools.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Clinical pulp tests do not correlate with pulp histology."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's states there is not necessarily a good correlation between clinical signs and pulp histology (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's states a poor correlation between signs and symptoms and histopathology, "especially true with irreversible pulpitis", and that tests are more likely correct when teeth are healthy than diseased (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].
*Refinement:* the Molar Guidebook says this view has recently been challenged (Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC], and Okazaki and co-authors cite Ricucci and colleagues (2014) reporting clinical-histological agreement for irreversible pulpitis as high as 84.4% (Okazaki et al, 2017) [SRC]; the older pessimism comes from earlier work such as Seltzer and Bender's, also cited there.
*Conflict:* Harty's singles out irreversible pulpitis as the condition worst predicted clinically (Chandler and Chong, 2017), while the 84.4% figure is for exactly that condition (Okazaki et al, 2017); both are stated side by side [Certain].
*Strength:* the newer figure is one histological series cited by a textbook; it was not read this session. The disagreement partly reflects different histological criteria for "irreversible", which Okazaki and co-authors themselves note [Likely].
*Conclusion:* write that correlation was historically thought poor but recent histological work suggests that a careful clinical diagnosis of irreversible pulpitis is usually right. The tests remain sensibility tests and say nothing about blood supply [Certain].

**Claim 2: "The test cavity is a valid last-resort pulp test."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's, Grossman's, Sikri and Gutmann and Lovdahl include it as a last resort (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; Harty's describes it but as a historical technique (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Abbott calls it barbaric, with no place in modern dentistry, and the Molar Guidebook agrees (Abbott, 2019; Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. Their argument is logical rather than experimental: the test measures the same neural response as cold or EPT, is invasive, and fails in calcified teeth (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].
*Strength:* no book cites an outcome study for either position [Certain].
*Conclusion:* in the exam, describe it with its indication (crowned tooth, no tooth structure for bridging, inconclusive cold test), then state that recent textbooks consider it obsolete because CO2 testing works through crowns (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Use water coolant whatever Sikri says [Likely].

**Claim 3: "Refrigerant spray is as reliable as CO2 snow."**
*Textbook:* Cohen's says spray results are equivalent to CO2 (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's and the Molar Guidebook prefer spray (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Abbott, citing Chen and Abbott, reports that CO2 was the most likely test to identify pulp disease, especially in restored teeth, and that sprays become liquid quickly in the mouth and can give false positives (Abbott, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's supports the principle: the colder the stimulus the more reliable the test, and CO2, being the coldest, is particularly useful for crowned teeth (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].
*Strength:* both sides rely on clinical comparative studies cited second-hand; the ranking may depend on whether normal or diseased pulps are tested (Abbott reports that CO2, electric and spray tests are all reliable for clinically normal pulps; Abbott, 2019) [SRC] [Likely].
*Conclusion:* spray on a large pellet is adequate for routine use; CO2 is the better choice for crowned or diseased teeth. Either way, combine it with EPT (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Abbott, 2019) [SRC].

**Claim 4: "CBCT should be routine in endodontic diagnosis."**
*Textbook:* Torabinejad's sees CBCT becoming increasingly routine, and the Molar Guidebook supports routine narrow field CBCT for molars (Blicher, Walton and Torabinejad, 2021; Witherspoon and Regan, 2025) [SRC]. Harty's requires case-by-case justification, warns of false positives and names dose as the key limitation, and its trauma chapter does not consider CBCT routine (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017); Abbott notes radiolucencies around normal pulps; Cohen's calls CBCT an adjunct (Chandler and Chong, 2017; Abbott, 2019; Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].
*Interpretation:* CBCT's higher sensitivity is agreed; the dispute is about specificity and dose. A lesion seen only on CBCT in a tooth that responds normally should not be treated on the image alone [Likely].
*Conclusion:* CBCT is an indicated adjunct in difficult cases, not a replacement for clinical tests.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Five stages of diagnosis (Cohen's); 11 stages and "two diagnoses plus cause" (Abbott).
- Pain history: localization, commencement, intensity, provocation, duration; ask about analgesics in the last 4-6 h.
- Palpation, percussion, mobility, probing test the attachment apparatus, not the pulp; an isolated narrow deep pocket suggests a vertical root fracture.
- Sensibility (nerve) vs vitality (blood supply); cold 86%, EPT 81%, heat 71% accurate; cold + EPT together.
- Bite test: pain on release supports a crack, but 68% of cracks hurt on application (Abbott).
- Transillumination: a crack blocks light, a craze line transmits it.
- Selective anaesthesia localises the arch better than the tooth; technique differs between books.
- Newer aids: LDF, pulse oximetry, DWLS, thermography, OCT, dental MRI (0.55 T), ultrasound; mostly experimental.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling thermal and electric tests "vitality tests" without noting that they test nerves, not blood supply.
- Writing that percussion tenderness means the pulp is vital or nonvital; it shows only periodontal ligament inflammation.
- Presenting "pain on release = cracked tooth" as definitive without the contrary evidence.
- Quoting a mobility grade without saying which scheme (Cohen's/Miller vs Abbott's Miller), since the 1 mm cut-off shifts by one grade.
- Listing CBCT, LDF or MRI as routine first-line tests; they are adjuncts or experimental.

## MNEMONIC
**"Hear, See, Feel, Test, Image"**: History, inspection (extraoral and intraoral), palpation/percussion/mobility/probing, pulp and special tests, radiographs/CBCT. For the special tests: **"BiTe STAin"**: Bite test, Transillumination, Staining, Test cavity, Anaesthesia (selective).
