## 1. Definition and terminology

### Explanation

The AAE Glossary defines a sinus tract as a pathway from an enclosed area of infection to an epithelial surface, with an opening or stoma (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; it usually disappears once the cause is removed by endodontic treatment, and the term fistula is often inappropriately used (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. A fistula is an abnormal communication between two internal organs or from one epithelial-lined surface to another (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC], and is "not a sinus tract" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Cohen's uses the same definitions: a chronic endodontic infection draining to the gingival surface is a sinus tract (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], running from the source to a stoma (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], and the word fistula is often used inappropriately (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. An oro-antral fistula, by contrast, is a true fistula (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. The parulis is a nodule where the tract reaches the surface (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; Sikri describes it as subacutely inflamed granulation tissue at the opening (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; the layman's "gumboil" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

> **SOURCE CONFLICT, "fistula".** Weine writes "chronic draining sinus tracts (fistulas)" as synonyms (Weine, 2004) [SRC]; the Glossary and Cohen's call that use inappropriate. Sikri's definition, "a tract leading to a suppurating cavity" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], omits the epithelial surface that the Glossary includes. Write "sinus tract" in the exam.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** A sinus tract is a pathway from an enclosed area of infection to an epithelial surface, opening at a stoma, intraorally or extraorally (AAE, 2012). A fistula joins two epithelium-lined surfaces (e.g. oro-antral fistula), so "fistula" is inappropriate for endodontic drainage (AAE, 2012; Berman and Rotstein, 2021), although Weine (2004) uses it as a synonym. The nodule at the stoma is the parulis ("gumboil").

## 2. Pathogenesis and clinical features

### Explanation

The sinus tract is the defining sign of the chronic apical abscess: gradual onset, little or no discomfort and intermittent discharge of pus through a sinus tract (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; generally symptomless (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; distinguished from asymptomatic apical periodontitis by the drainage (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Lin and Huang go further: the abscess form "always" shows a tract (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. When bacteria overwhelm the host, the abscess breaks through cortical bone, periosteum and mucosa or skin (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; pus takes the path of least resistance (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. A tooth with a tract is generally not painful (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], because drainage prevents exacerbation or swelling (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; when the stoma closes, swelling precedes the next discharge (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Weine adds that a tract is some insurance against a flare-up during treatment (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pathogenesis.** Necrotic infected pulp, then apical periodontitis, then an abscess whose pus erodes cortical bone, periosteum and mucosa or skin along the path of least resistance (Lin and Huang, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021). This is the chronic apical abscess: gradual onset, little or no pain, intermittent discharge (AAE, 2012). Drainage prevents swelling; closure of the stoma brings swelling until it reopens (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 3. Sites

### Explanation

Intraoral stomata open in the alveolar mucosa, attached gingiva, furcation or gingival crevice (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], facially or lingually according to the nearest cortical plate (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]: labially in the maxilla because the plate is thinner (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], palatally from upper lateral incisors and palatal roots (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Drainage via an isolated pocket also occurs (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC], producing a narrow pseudopocket that mimics a periodontal pocket or a vertical root fracture (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. The stoma may be adjacent to or distant from the source (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], even several teeth away (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Extraoral (cutaneous) tracts open on the skin of the face (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], particularly in young people (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]: near the symphysis from lower anteriors (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and along the inferior border of the mandible from molars (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], when the tract passes beyond the facial muscle attachments (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC]. They can be confused with facial skin lesions (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC]; many patients have been treated by physicians, dermatologists, oncologists or plastic surgeons with antibiotics or surgery before a dental cause is found (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. A skin scar is more likely than a mucosal one (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sites**
| Intraoral | Extraoral (cutaneous) |
|---|---|
| Labial/buccal alveolar mucosa or attached gingiva (commonest) | Chin, near symphysis (lower anteriors) |
| Palatal (maxillary lateral incisor, palatal root) | Inferior border of mandible (molars) |
| Gingival sulcus or furcation (pseudopocket) | Young patients; beyond muscle attachments |

The stoma may be several teeth away (Gopikrishna, 2021). Cutaneous tracts are often misdiagnosed as skin lesions and treated by dermatologists or surgeons (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

## 4. Tracing the sinus tract

### Explanation

A #25 or #30 gutta-percha cone is threaded into the stoma (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC] until resistance is felt (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], and a periapical radiograph shows the path to the tooth and the root (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019; Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. If the cone reaches the lateral root surface, Weine suggests a lateral canal lesion, a perforation or a cracked tooth (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Tracing.** Insert a #25 or #30 gutta-percha cone gently into the stoma until resistance is felt; take a periapical radiograph; the cone points to the source tooth and root (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). A cone ending on the lateral root surface suggests a lateral canal, perforation or crack (Weine, 2004).

## 5. Differential diagnosis

### Explanation

A crevicular stoma needs differentiating from a vertical root fracture and a developmental groove (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; a primary periodontal lesion has a broad coronal opening and generalised bone loss (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], whereas the endodontic tract gives a narrow defect (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Rotstein: it may mimic a periodontal abscess (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC] and probes narrow (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]. Gutmann and Lovdahl: tracts on the mucosal surface are seldom mistaken for periodontal lesions (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; pulpal tracts probe vertically only (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; chronic periodontitis can itself drain through a tract (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; multiple tracts on one tooth suggest a fracture (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Extraorally the confusion is with skin lesions (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Differential diagnosis**
- Periodontal abscess or pocket: vital tooth, broad pocket, generalised bone loss; endodontic tract probes narrow and vertical (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- Vertical root fracture (multiple tracts suggest fracture), developmental groove (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Extraoral: skin lesions (Ricucci et al, 2019); e.g. skin infection or tumour [RECALL].

## 6. Histology

### Explanation

Most tracts are not lined with epithelium throughout: in one study only 1 of 10 was epithelialised (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; in a larger study two thirds had no epithelium beyond the level of the rete ridges (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri: lined partially or completely by epithelium (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The lining does not prevent closure (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Bacteria may be present in the periapical tissue with a draining tract (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]; a long-standing tract may let biofilm on the root calcify (Ricucci, Rôças and Siqueira, 2019; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Histology.** Mostly granulation tissue; epithelium usually only near the surface (1 of 10 fully lined; two thirds no deeper epithelium) (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). Lining does not stop healing.

## 7. Management and healing

### Explanation

The tract usually disappears once the cause is removed (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; it heals regardless of location and duration once the infected canal is treated or the tooth extracted (Sedgley, Silva and Fouad, 2021) [SRC], by granulation (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], without special treatment as soon as the canal is cleaned, shaped and medicated (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. No systemic antibiotics are needed (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Failure to heal calls for re-diagnosis (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; periodontal treatment alone fails if the necrotic pulp is missed (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC].

> **SOURCE CONFLICT, time to closure.** Several days (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Weine, 2004) [SRC]; a few weeks (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]; 2 weeks in a case (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].
>
> **SOURCE CONFLICT, the extraoral tract.** Sikri: may need surgical excision (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Berman and Rotstein: generally closes after treatment, with a possible scar (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Management**
1. Root canal treatment (or extraction); intracanal medicament (Gopikrishna, 2021); no antibiotics needed (Lin and Huang, 2021).
2. Tract closes within days to weeks (Berman and Rotstein, 2021; Rotstein, 2017).
3. Extraoral: usually closes, possibly with a scar; Sikri (2019) advises excision if needed.
4. Persistence: re-diagnose (missed canal, fracture, non-endodontic lesion).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Sinus tract, not fistula (AAE Glossary).
- Trace with #25/#30 gutta-percha cone and a radiograph.
- Stoma may be several teeth away.
- Most tracts are not epithelialised; closure still occurs.
- Closes after root canal treatment; antibiotics not needed.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling it a fistula.
- Assuming the stoma marks the tooth over it.
- Prescribing antibiotics instead of root canal treatment.
- Treating a crevicular tract as a periodontal pocket.

## MNEMONIC
**"Trace, Test, Treat"**: gutta-percha tracing, sensibility test, root canal treatment.
