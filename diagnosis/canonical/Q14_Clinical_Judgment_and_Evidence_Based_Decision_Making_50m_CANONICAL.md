## 1. Introduction and definitions

### Explanation

The question borrows Kipling's line "East is East, and West is West, and never the twain shall meet" [RECALL]: it asks whether clinical judgment and evidence-based decision making are opposites or partners.

**Evidence-based dentistry.** The usual definition, attributed to Sackett and colleagues for medicine (unverified), is the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in decisions about the care of individual patients, integrating three things: the best research evidence, the clinician's expertise, and the patient's values and circumstances [RECALL]. The ADA uses a similar three-part definition for dentistry [RECALL]. No textbook read this session quotes Sackett. The nearest textbook statement is Cohen's: clinical decision making is a process that combines the best available evidence, clinical judgment and patient preferences, and treatment choices are always made under some uncertainty (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala date the "era of evidence-based medicine and dentistry" to the 1990s, driven by costs, benefits and outcomes of treatment (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

**Clinical judgment.** Kvist frames clinical knowledge in Aristotle's three forms: episteme (scientific knowledge), techne (craft skill) and phronesis (practical wisdom). Phronesis is what handles clinical uncertainty and conflicting goals (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. He states that phronesis "ensures that clinical judgment is informed by the best available scientific evidence" while leaving room for the individual patient, and that a dentist must balance all three (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. So the textbooks do not set judgment against evidence; they define each partly in terms of the other. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** "Never the twain shall meet" (Kipling) suggests two things that cannot be joined. The question asks whether the clinician's judgment and evidence-based decision making (EBDM) are rivals or partners in endodontics.

**Definitions**
- **Evidence-based dentistry (EBD):** the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about individual patients (after Sackett) [RECALL]. It rests on a triad:
  1. best available research evidence,
  2. clinical expertise (judgment),
  3. patient values, preferences and circumstances.
- Cohen's states the same triad for endodontics: clinical decision making "combines the best available evidence, clinical judgment, and patient preferences", always under some uncertainty (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021).
- **Clinical judgment:** the clinician's integration of history, findings, experience and skill to reach a decision for one patient. Kvist describes three forms of knowledge: **episteme** (science), **techne** (craft skill) and **phronesis** (practical wisdom); phronesis handles uncertainty and conflicting goals and ensures that judgment is informed by evidence (Kvist, 2025).

**Thesis of this answer.** By definition, judgment is one of the three legs of EBD, so the question is not whether they meet but how well they are joined in practice.

## 2. Hierarchy (levels) of evidence

### Explanation

The pyramid usually taught, from strongest to weakest, is: systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs; RCTs; cohort studies; case-control studies; case series and case reports; expert opinion; with animal and in-vitro research outside the clinical hierarchy [RECALL]. White and Torabinejad call systematic reviews "the highest form of clinical evidence" (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Torabinejad's group assigned levels of evidence (LOE) to 306 outcome articles on nonsurgical root canal treatment; meta-analysis of the 51 studies with at least 100 teeth gave 81.5% radiographic success at 5 years (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

Kvist explains what each design gives. RCTs assess efficacy under controlled conditions but are expensive, slow and may not reflect real practice; cohort studies show effectiveness in real practice but are open to confounding because they do not randomize; register studies use large real-world databases but may be biased or incomplete (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Any difference between two groups has four explanations: a real effect, chance, confounders, or bias (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Much of the periapical surgery literature is case series or other low-level evidence (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Levels of evidence (strongest first)** [RECALL for the pyramid order]

| Level | Design | Use in endodontics |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Systematic review / meta-analysis of RCTs | "Highest form of clinical evidence" (White and Torabinejad, 2017) |
| 2 | Randomized controlled trial | Efficacy under controlled conditions; costly, slow, may not mirror practice (Kvist, 2025) |
| 3 | Cohort (prospective/retrospective), register studies | Effectiveness in real practice; confounding, incomplete data (Kvist, 2025) |
| 4 | Case-control | Rare in endodontics |
| 5 | Case series, case reports | Much of the surgical literature (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) |
| 6 | Expert opinion, consensus | Fills gaps |
| Outside | In-vitro and animal studies | Mechanism only; never proof of clinical benefit |

- Torabinejad's group graded 306 outcome articles by level of evidence; 51 studies of 100 or more teeth gave 81.5% radiographic success at 5 years (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Any group difference may be real, chance, confounding or bias (Kvist, 2025): appraisal, not citation count, decides weight.

## 3. Why the endodontic evidence base is thin

### Explanation

Kvist's guiding reference, the Swedish SBU systematic review (2010), concluded that there is "a significant lack of robust scientific evidence" for the diagnostic and treatment methods of endodontics; later reviews largely agreed (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala explain why: a drug RCT delivers a standardized dose, but a surgical procedure such as root canal treatment varies with each operator's interpretation and execution, is multistep and sequential, and is hard to record (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Pooled outcome data are heterogeneous (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Rosenberg and Malek say outcome studies cannot be compared because of differing methods, follow-up and criteria, and call for RCTs with standardized methods (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. RCTs on number of visits lacked statistical power (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. A systematic review of magnification found no study meeting its criteria (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Kvist adds that piling up weak studies does not help, since "their quality does not improve merely by their quantity", and lists gaps: when vital pulp therapy beats pulpectomy, which protocol best prevents persistent disease, how much healing is needed, and health-economic comparison with implants (Kvist, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Why endodontics has few strong RCTs**
1. **SBU report (2010):** "a significant lack of robust scientific evidence" for endodontic diagnostic and treatment methods; later systematic reviews agree (Kvist, 2025).
2. **Operator-dependent, multistep procedure:** unlike a drug trial with a fixed dose, root canal treatment varies with each operator's execution (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021). Blinding is hardly possible [RECALL].
3. **Long follow-up:** healing takes 1 to 4 years; drop-out is high [RECALL].
4. **Heterogeneous outcome criteria** (strict versus lenient success, survival, periapical index): studies cannot be pooled or compared (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021; Rosenberg and Malek, 2021).
5. **Underpowered trials:** visit-number RCTs found no difference but lacked power (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).
6. **Ethics and funding:** no-treatment arms are hard to justify; industry funds in-vitro work [RECALL].
7. **Quantity is not quality:** systematic reviews of weak studies stay weak (Kvist, 2025).

**Open questions** (Kvist, 2025): which inflamed pulps suit vital pulp therapy versus pulpectomy; which protocol best prevents persistent disease; how much healing is enough; cost comparison with implants. Even the microscope has no qualifying outcome study (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).

## 4. The case for clinical judgment (where evidence alone cannot decide)

### Explanation

**Diagnosis is a judgment.** Diagnosis is "the art and science" of detecting deviations from health; collecting data needs "considerable clinical judgment" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. The tests themselves are imperfect: the electric pulp test response does not reflect the histologic status of the pulp; in one study cold, electric and heat tests were 86%, 81% and 71% accurate; only 50% of observers agreed on the radiographic presence of pathosis; and clinical signs correlate poorly with histopathology (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng note that the full picture may never be reached because of deficiencies in diagnostic certainty and prognostic data (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

**Case difficulty and the operator.** The AAE Case Difficulty Assessment Form grades cases as minimal, moderate or high difficulty across patient, diagnostic/treatment and additional considerations, to help the dentist judge whether the case is within his or her skill (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Evidence from expert hands does not transfer automatically.

**The individual case.** Kvist: the literature gives efficacy and effectiveness, but the real question is "whether a treatment will work in this specific situation"; literature alone cannot diagnose, choose therapy and predict prognosis (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. His example: a stable patient near the clinic may receive pulp-preserving therapy with uncertain prognosis, while a mobile patient may be more suitable for pulpectomy (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Uncertainty may be calculable risk, fundamental uncertainty (no probabilities available) or ignorance (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Prognosis "is inherently an estimate" combining judgment and knowledge (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Rosenberg and Malek: "What can be done and what should be done" differ (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and co-workers: overall percentages must be applied with caution and each case assessed individually (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Argument 1: Diagnosis cannot be outsourced to evidence**
- Diagnosis is "the art and science" of detecting deviations from health and needs "considerable clinical judgment" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Sensibility tests test nerves, not blood supply; the EPT response does not reflect pulp histology. Accuracy: cold 86%, EPT 81%, heat 71% (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). False results occur after trauma, in calcified and immature teeth, and in multirooted teeth with partial necrosis [RECALL].
- Only 50% interobserver agreement on radiographic pathosis; clinical signs correlate poorly with histology (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Diagnostic certainty and prognostic data are always incomplete (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

**Argument 2: Case difficulty and operator skill**
- The AAE Case Difficulty Assessment Form: patient, diagnostic/treatment and additional considerations; minimal, moderate, high difficulty; the dentist judges whether the case is within his or her skill or should be referred (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021).
- Trial results from specialists may not apply to a general dentist's hands [Likely].

**Argument 3: Evidence describes groups; the clinician treats one patient**
- Literature gives efficacy and effectiveness; the question is "whether a treatment will work in this specific situation" (Kvist, 2025).
- Example: pulp-preserving therapy for a stable patient living near the clinic, pulpectomy for one who moves often (Kvist, 2025).
- Uncertainty: calculable risk, fundamental uncertainty, ignorance (Kvist, 2025). Where no study exists (rare anatomy, combined lesions, medically complex patients), judgment is the only tool.
- "What can be done and what should be done" differ (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021); percentages must be applied case by case (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 5. The case against unaided clinical judgment

### Explanation

Gulabivala and Ng warn that practice patterns "may lead to the passive application of heuristic principles"; the undergraduate decision tree serves patients in the "central tendency" but not those "on the fringes of the normal distribution"; and variation in philosophy, knowledge, experience and judgment produces different plans between clinicians (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman report that treatment thresholds vary widely and recommendations may depend "more on personal values and experience than objective analysis" (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. Kvist notes that the perceived benefit of retreatment varies among dentists (Kvist, 2025) [SRC], and Gulabivala and Ng that there is no definite retreatment criterion for stable symptomless radiolucencies (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Uncertainty may cause overdiagnosis or underdiagnosis (Kvist, 2025) [SRC].

History shows the danger of opinion without evidence: the focal infection era led to widespread extraction of pulpless teeth for about 50 years, and evidence-based practice later "helped to avert irrational treatment recommendations" favouring extraction over saving teeth (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

Named cognitive biases are not described in any textbook read; the list in the exam core is [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Argument 1: Heuristics and variation**
- Routine practice may become "passive application of heuristic principles"; simple undergraduate decision trees suit typical cases but fail those "on the fringes" (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).
- Clinicians differ in philosophy, knowledge, experience and judgment, so plans differ (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014). Treatment thresholds vary widely; recommendations may rest "more on personal values and experience than objective analysis" (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021).
- The perceived benefit of retreatment varies between dentists (Kvist, 2025); no definite criterion exists for retreating a stable, symptomless radiolucency (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

**Argument 2: Cognitive biases** [RECALL]
| Bias | Endodontic example |
|---|---|
| Anchoring | Fixing on the first diagnosis (e.g. "irreversible pulpitis") and ignoring a crack or non-odontogenic pain |
| Availability | Recent dramatic failure drives the next plan |
| Confirmation | Reading a radiograph to fit the expected answer |
| Overconfidence | Underrating case difficulty; not referring |
| Commission bias | Preferring to act (retreat, extract) when monitoring is reasonable |
| Financial / technology bias | New file system or implant favoured without outcome data |

**Argument 3: History**
- The focal infection theory, an expert opinion, led to mass extraction of pulpless teeth for about 50 years; evidence-based practice later averted irrational extraction of savable teeth for implants (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).
- Uncertainty without evidence leads to overdiagnosis or underdiagnosis (Kvist, 2025).

## 6. Where outcome evidence conflicts: judgment must referee

### Explanation

**Single versus multiple visits.** Cohen's: some studies show less pain after single visits, while systematic reviews find similar pain and similar radiographic healing; "differences in research methodology explain the conflict"; and "agreement is lacking" on single-visit treatment of necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Ørstavik: individual RCTs differ, some favouring two visits and others one, but systematic reviews show virtually no difference; a CBCT-based RCT showed somewhat greater lesion volume reduction with two sessions; calcium hydroxide dressing remains the effective procedure when time or clinical conditions require it (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala: the RCTs lacked power, and operator skill, case complexity and compliance would bias the result (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

**Retreatment, surgery or implant.** Setzer and co-workers cite a systematic review that ranked implant success above endodontic treatment, but note that success criteria differ between disciplines, so "such comparisons lack validity"; survival rates are comparable, and implant survival does not exceed that of even compromised but adequately treated teeth; another review found lower outcomes for endodontic surgery than single implants (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad call the implant single crown "the replacement of choice", superior in survival to microsurgery without prior nonsurgical retreatment (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman say older surgical studies are weak by today's standards; microsurgery reaches 94% against 59% for traditional surgery; and under ideal conditions the prognoses of nonsurgical retreatment, surgery and implants "should be roughly equal" (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. Rosenberg and Malek add implant complications: more interventions, and a 48.03% cumulative complication rate at 10 to 16 years in one study; and intentional replantation is increasingly shown to be cost-effective (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC].

The authors therefore differ: White and Torabinejad favour the implant for replacement after failure; Johnson, Fayad and Berman treat the three options as roughly equal. Both are shown; neither is ranked here.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conflict 1: Single versus multiple visit**
| Source | Position |
|---|---|
| Rosenberg and Malek, 2021 | Systematic reviews: similar pain and healing; conflicts due to methodology; no agreement for necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis |
| Ørstavik, 2020 | Systematic reviews: virtually no difference; one CBCT RCT favours two visits; Ca(OH)2 dressing still indicated when time or conditions require |
| Ng and Gulabivala, 2021 | RCTs underpowered; operator skill, complexity and compliance bias results |

Verdict: the evidence permits either; judgment decides (time, symptoms, exudate, complexity, operator).

**Conflict 2: Save the tooth or place an implant**
| Source | Position |
|---|---|
| Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021 | One review ranked implant success higher, but criteria differ so "comparisons lack validity"; survival comparable; implants do not outlast even compromised, adequately treated teeth |
| White and Torabinejad, 2017 | Implant single crown is "the replacement of choice"; survives better than microsurgery done without prior retreatment |
| Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021 | Older surgical studies weak; microsurgery 94% versus 59% traditional; retreatment, surgery and implant "roughly equal" under ideal conditions |
| Rosenberg and Malek, 2021 | Implants need more interventions (48.03% complications at 10 to 16 years in one study); replantation cost-effective |

Verdict: the books disagree on ranking; the decision depends on restorability, periodontal support, cost and patient wishes, which only judgment can weigh.

## 7. Decision analysis and shared decision making

### Explanation

Ng and Gulabivala describe two ways to estimate prognosis: heuristic, intuitive weighing of dominant factors, or entering comprehensive data into an algorithmic model; "at present, the evidence base is insufficient" for the sophisticated approach (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng cite a Markov-model health-economic study: root canal treatment is highly cost-effective as first-line treatment for a maxillary central incisor, orthograde retreatment is cost-effective after failure, but surgical retreatment is not (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad cite a decision-tree and patient-utility analysis of the abscessed tooth (Balevi and Shepperd, 2007, cited by White and Torabinejad, 2017) and state that the natural tooth "has intrinsic value" (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman predict algorithms to assist decisions as evidence grows, and prefer shared decision making to a clinician-directed decision (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng describe a "mutually agreed choice of treatment" after dialogue and negotiation (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

The method of decision analysis (decision tree, probabilities, utilities, expected value, sensitivity analysis) is [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Decision analysis: the formal meeting point**
- Steps [RECALL]: (1) define the decision (e.g. retreat, surgery, extract and implant); (2) draw a decision tree; (3) attach probabilities from the best evidence; (4) attach utilities (patient values: cost, pain, time, aesthetics); (5) calculate expected value; (6) sensitivity analysis to test how robust the choice is.
- Evidence supplies the probabilities, the patient supplies the utilities, and judgment adjusts both to the case.
- Markov model: root canal treatment highly cost-effective first line for a maxillary central incisor; orthograde retreatment cost-effective after failure; surgical retreatment not (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014). A decision-tree and utility analysis of the abscessed tooth is cited by White and Torabinejad (2017); the natural tooth "has intrinsic value".
- Prognosis today is estimated heuristically because data are insufficient for algorithmic models (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021); algorithms are predicted as evidence grows (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021).

**Shared decision making**
- The clinician presents outcome probabilities, the patient states preferences, and both agree; preferable to a clinician-directed decision and increases satisfaction (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021).
- Planning ends in a "mutually agreed choice of treatment" (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

## 8. Guidelines: evidence turned into practice

### Explanation

Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang note a move from a local, experience-based to a national, evidence-based standard of care, the same for specialists and general dentists, with the AAE Case Difficulty Assessment Form as the tool for referral (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Kvist cites the AAE position statements on systemic antibiotics (2017) and vital pulp therapy (2021) and the ESE S3-level clinical practice guideline on treatment of pulpal and apical disease (Duncan et al, 2023, cited by Kvist, 2025), and observes that where evidence is lacking, recommendations rest on systematic "consensus-based statements" that also draw on the experts' own knowledge (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng say decisions "should follow the knowledge gained from research and evidence-based practice" (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. The ESE quality guidelines (2006) appear in several reference lists; their content was not read. GRADE as the grading method used in S3 guidelines is [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Guidelines bridge evidence and judgment**
- **AAE:** Case Difficulty Assessment Form; position statements on antibiotics (2017) and vital pulp therapy (2021) (cited by Kvist, 2025).
- **ESE:** quality guidelines (2006); S3-level clinical practice guideline for pulpal and apical disease (2023), using systematic reviews and graded recommendations (cited by Kvist, 2025; grading by GRADE [RECALL]).
- The standard of care is moving from local experience to a national evidence-based standard, the same for general dentists and specialists (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021).
- Where evidence is weak, guidelines are consensus-based statements that include the experts' own clinical knowledge (Kvist, 2025): **guidelines are themselves judgment and evidence combined.**
- Guidelines guide; they do not replace the decision for one patient [Likely].

## 9. Synthesis and verdict

### Explanation

The sources agree that the two meet. Kvist: literature alone is not sufficient, and a dentist must balance episteme, techne and phronesis (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman: choice should rest on best evidence, case-specific judgment and patient preference (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad: nonsurgical retreatment is the first option after failure, and comprehensive case evaluation and risk assessment of the individual is always necessary (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Deliberation**
| For "they do not meet" | For "they meet" |
|---|---|
| Thin RCT base; SBU found little robust evidence | EBD by definition includes clinical expertise |
| Group data do not fit the individual tooth | Judgment applies group data to the individual |
| Judgment is biased and variable | Evidence and guidelines check bias |
| Conflicting outcome studies | Judgment referees conflicts using case factors |

**Verdict.** The twain do meet, and must. Evidence without judgment cannot diagnose a cracked tooth, grade case difficulty or apply a percentage to one patient; judgment without evidence repeats the focal infection era and passive heuristics. In endodontics, where RCTs are few, judgment carries more weight than in pharmacology, but it must be **informed** judgment (Kvist, 2025).

**A working model**
1. Ask a focused (PICO) question [RECALL].
2. Find and appraise the best available evidence (level, bias, applicability).
3. Assess the case: diagnosis, AAE difficulty, operator skill.
4. Elicit patient values; use shared decision making.
5. Decide, record, and audit outcomes, so that each practice adds to the evidence.

**Conclusion.** Clinical decision making "combines the best available evidence, clinical judgment, and patient preferences" (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021); "a dentist must develop a balance of all three" forms of knowledge (Kvist, 2025). The two are not East and West; they are the two hands of one clinician.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Endodontics lacks high-level evidence, so clinical judgment must fill the gap."**
*Textbook:* Kvist reports the SBU conclusion of a significant lack of robust evidence and lists four unresolved areas (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala explain the methodological reason (operator-dependent multistep procedures) and say data are insufficient for algorithmic prognosis (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].
*Refinement:* the gap is uneven. For survival after primary treatment the evidence is strong: very large cohorts and systematic reviews show very high survival (White and Torabinejad, 2017; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. The gap lies in comparisons between protocols and in diagnostic accuracy. [Likely]
*Evidence versus interpretation:* the evidence shows few adequately powered RCTs; it does not show that judgment is accurate where RCTs are missing. Judgment is the default, not a proven substitute. [Certain]
*Clinical implication:* use judgment where evidence is silent, but declare it as judgment and audit its results.

**Claim 2: "Implants and endodontic treatment have equivalent outcomes."**
*Textbook:* Setzer and co-workers cite a review that ranked implant success higher, then say comparisons of success lack validity because criteria differ, and that survival is comparable (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad call the implant "the replacement of choice" after failed treatment (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman say retreatment, surgery and implants should be roughly equal under ideal conditions (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC].
*What follows:* these positions are not contradictory once the comparison is specified: survival of a tooth versus survival of an implant is similar; success definitions differ; after failure, the order is retreatment first, then surgery or replantation, then implant. [Likely]
*Limitation:* the data are mainly cohort and survival data, with different populations and operators; no head-to-head RCT was found in the books. [Certain]
*Clinical implication:* the choice cannot be read off the literature; restorability, periodontal status, cost and patient values decide.

**Claim 3: "Single-visit treatment is as effective as multiple-visit treatment."**
*Textbook:* systematic reviews show no detectable difference in healing (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021; Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC].
*Challenge:* the RCTs lacked power (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC], and one CBCT-based RCT found somewhat greater lesion reduction with two visits (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]. "No difference found" is not the same as "no difference". [Certain]
*Clinical implication:* both are acceptable; the choice rests on symptoms, time, ability to dry the canal and operator judgment.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- EBD triad: best evidence + clinical expertise + patient values (Sackett [RECALL]; same triad in Cohen's Ch.11).
- Kvist's three forms of knowledge: episteme, techne, phronesis.
- SBU 2010: significant lack of robust evidence in endodontics.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing only on evidence-based dentistry and never deliberating the opposite case.
- Treating "no significant difference" in underpowered trials as proof of equivalence.
- Comparing implant "success" with endodontic "success" as if the criteria were the same.
- Treating in-vitro or animal data as clinical evidence.
- Ending without a clear verdict; "deliberate" needs a reasoned position.
