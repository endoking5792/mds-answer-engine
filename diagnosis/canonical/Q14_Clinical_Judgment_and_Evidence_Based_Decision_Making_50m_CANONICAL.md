## 1. Introduction and definitions

### Explanation

The question borrows Kipling's line "East is East, and West is West, and never the twain shall meet" [RECALL]: it asks whether clinical judgment and evidence-based decision making are opposites or partners.

**Evidence-based dentistry.** Friedman quotes Sackett and colleagues (1991): evidence-based practice is "the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients" (Sackett et al, 1991, cited by Friedman, 2017) [SRC]. The AAE Glossary defines evidence-based endodontics as the integration of the best research evidence with clinician expertise and patient values (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Harty's states the same triad: evidence-based treatment integrates the best evidence with the patient's values, aspirations and preferences, combined with the practitioner's clinical proficiency and judgement (Chong, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's says the same in decision-making terms: clinical decision making is a process that combines the best available evidence, clinical judgment and patient preferences, and treatment choices are always made under some uncertainty (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala date the "era of evidence-based medicine and dentistry" to the 1990s, driven by costs, benefits and outcomes of treatment (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

**Clinical judgment.** Kvist frames clinical knowledge in Aristotle's three forms: episteme (scientific knowledge), techne (craft skill) and phronesis (practical wisdom). Phronesis is what handles clinical uncertainty and conflicting goals (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. He states that phronesis "ensures that clinical judgment is informed by the best available scientific evidence" while leaving room for the individual patient, and that a dentist must balance all three (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. So the textbooks do not set judgment against evidence; they define each partly in terms of the other. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** "Never the twain shall meet" (Kipling) suggests two things that cannot be joined. The question asks whether the clinician's judgment and evidence-based decision making (EBDM) are rivals or partners in endodontics.

**Definitions**
- **Evidence-based dentistry (EBD):** "the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients" (Sackett et al, 1991, cited by Friedman, 2017). It rests on a triad (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Chong, 2017):
  1. best available research evidence,
  2. clinical expertise (judgment),
  3. patient values, preferences and circumstances.
- Cohen's states the same triad for endodontics: clinical decision making "combines the best available evidence, clinical judgment, and patient preferences", always under some uncertainty (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021).
- **Clinical judgment:** the clinician's integration of history, findings, experience and skill to reach a decision for one patient. Kvist describes three forms of knowledge: **episteme** (science), **techne** (craft skill) and **phronesis** (practical wisdom); phronesis handles uncertainty and conflicting goals and ensures that judgment is informed by evidence (Kvist, 2025).

**Thesis of this answer.** By definition, judgment is one of the three legs of EBD, so the question is not whether they meet but how well they are joined in practice.

## 2. Hierarchy (levels) of evidence

### Explanation

The books give the hierarchy in slightly different forms, shown side by side. Friedman, using the Oxford CEBM scheme, ranks evidence on treatment benefit as Level 1 systematic review of randomized trials; Level 2 randomized trial (or observational study with dramatic effect); Level 3 nonrandomized controlled cohort; Level 4 case series, case-control or historically controlled studies; Level 5 mechanism-based reasoning; he adds that the RCT suits questions of intervention but the cohort study is the appropriate design for prognosis (Friedman, 2017) [SRC]. The AAE Glossary ranks multiple RCTs, single RCT, prospective cohort, case-control, case series, then expert opinion with in vitro/ex vivo bench studies, then anecdote (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Gutmann places RCTs at the peak of the pyramid and case reports and personal opinions at the base (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC], whereas White and Torabinejad call systematic reviews "the highest form of clinical evidence" (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. So the books differ on whether the systematic review or the RCT sits at the top, and on where expert opinion goes (absent from CEBM, which ends at mechanism-based reasoning). In a systematic review that assigned levels of evidence (LOE), Torabinejad and colleagues found 306 outcome articles on nonsurgical root canal treatment over 40 years; meta-analysis of the 51 studies with at least 100 teeth gave 81.5% radiographic success at 5 years (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

Kvist explains what each design gives. RCTs assess efficacy under controlled conditions but are expensive, slow and may not reflect real practice; cohort studies show effectiveness in real practice but are open to confounding because they do not randomize; register studies use large real-world databases but may be biased or incomplete (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Any difference between two groups has four explanations: a real effect, chance, confounders, or bias (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Much of the periapical surgery literature is case series or other low-level evidence (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Friedman lists the appraisal points for bias in prognosis studies: an inception cohort, at least 80% of treated subjects examined, follow-up long enough to capture the outcome, and blinded assessment, since providers of treatment may be biased towards a favourable outcome (Friedman, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Levels of evidence for treatment (strongest first)** (CEBM, after Friedman, 2017)

| Level | Design | Use in endodontics |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Systematic review of randomized trials | "Highest form of clinical evidence" (White and Torabinejad, 2017) |
| 2 | Randomized controlled trial | Efficacy under controlled conditions; costly, slow, may not mirror practice (Kvist, 2025) |
| 3 | Nonrandomized cohort, register studies | Effectiveness in real practice; confounding, incomplete data (Kvist, 2025); best design for prognosis (Friedman, 2017) |
| 4 | Case series, case-control | Much of the surgical literature (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) |
| 5 | Mechanism-based reasoning | In vitro work; never proof of clinical benefit |

- Versions differ: Gutmann (2017) puts RCTs at the peak and personal opinion at the base; the AAE Glossary (2012) ranks expert opinion with in vitro studies, above anecdote.

- A level-graded review of 306 outcome articles: 51 studies of 100 or more teeth gave 81.5% radiographic success at 5 years (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Any group difference may be real, chance, confounding or bias (Kvist, 2025): appraisal, not citation count, decides weight.

## 3. Why the endodontic evidence base is thin

### Explanation

Kvist's guiding reference, the Swedish SBU systematic review (2010), concluded that there is "a significant lack of robust scientific evidence" for the diagnostic and treatment methods of endodontics; later reviews largely agreed (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala explain why: a drug RCT delivers a standardized dose, but a surgical procedure such as root canal treatment varies with each operator's interpretation and execution, is multistep and sequential, and is hard to record (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Pooled outcome data are heterogeneous (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Rosenberg and Malek say outcome studies cannot be compared because of differing methods, follow-up and criteria, and call for RCTs with standardized methods (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. RCTs on number of visits lacked statistical power (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. A systematic review of magnification found no study meeting its criteria (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Kvist adds that piling up weak studies does not help, since "their quality does not improve merely by their quantity", and lists gaps: when vital pulp therapy beats pulpectomy, which protocol best prevents persistent disease, how long apical periodontitis takes to heal and how reliably radiographs judge it, and health-economic comparison with implants (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Even the rules for post length and diameter rest on laboratory studies or anecdotal evidence (Mannocci and Giovarruscio, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Why endodontics has few strong RCTs**
1. **SBU report (2010):** "a significant lack of robust scientific evidence" for endodontic diagnostic and treatment methods; later systematic reviews agree (Kvist, 2025).
2. **Operator-dependent, multistep procedure:** unlike a drug trial with a fixed dose, root canal treatment varies with each operator's execution (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021). Blinding is hardly possible [RECALL].
3. **Long follow-up and drop-out:** follow-up must capture completed healing and examine at least 80% of the cohort (Friedman, 2017).
4. **Heterogeneous outcome criteria** (strict versus lenient success, survival, periapical index): studies cannot be pooled or compared (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021; Rosenberg and Malek, 2021).
5. **Underpowered trials:** visit-number RCTs found no difference but lacked power (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).
6. **Ethics and funding:** no-treatment arms are hard to justify; industry funds in-vitro work [RECALL].
7. **Quantity is not quality:** systematic reviews of weak studies stay weak (Kvist, 2025).

**Open questions** (Kvist, 2025): which inflamed pulps suit vital pulp therapy versus pulpectomy; which protocol best prevents persistent disease; how long healing takes; cost comparison with implants. Even the microscope has no qualifying outcome study (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).

## 4. The case for clinical judgment (where evidence alone cannot decide)

### Explanation

**Diagnosis is a judgment.** Diagnosis is "the art and science" of detecting deviations from health; collecting data needs "considerable clinical judgment" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. The tests themselves are imperfect: the electric pulp test response does not reflect the histologic status of the pulp; in one study cold, electric and heat tests were 86%, 81% and 71% accurate; only 50% of observers agreed on the radiographic presence of pathosis; and clinical signs correlate poorly with histopathology (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng note that the full picture may never be reached because of deficiencies in diagnostic certainty and prognostic data (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

**Case difficulty and the operator.** The AAE Case Difficulty Assessment Form grades cases as minimal, moderate or high difficulty across patient, diagnostic/treatment and additional considerations, to help the dentist judge whether the case is within his or her skill (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Evidence from expert hands does not transfer automatically.

**The individual case.** Kvist: the literature gives efficacy and effectiveness, but the real question is "whether a treatment will work in this specific situation"; literature alone cannot diagnose, choose therapy and predict prognosis (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. His example: a stable patient near the clinic may receive pulp-preserving therapy with uncertain prognosis, while a mobile patient may be more suitable for pulpectomy (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Uncertainty may be calculable risk, fundamental uncertainty (no probabilities available) or ignorance (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Prognosis "is inherently an estimate" combining judgment and knowledge (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Rosenberg and Malek: "What can be done and what should be done" differ (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and co-workers: overall percentages must be applied with caution and each case assessed individually (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Where there is no clear diagnosis, Harty's advises "watch and review": the tooth is monitored until a definitive diagnosis or decision is reached (Chandler and Chong, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Argument 1: Diagnosis cannot be outsourced to evidence**
- Diagnosis is "the art and science" of detecting deviations from health and needs "considerable clinical judgment" (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Sensibility tests test nerves, not blood supply; the EPT response does not reflect pulp histology. Accuracy: cold 86%, EPT 81%, heat 71% (Berman and Rotstein, 2021). False results occur after trauma, in calcified and immature teeth, and in multirooted teeth with partial necrosis [RECALL].
- Only 50% interobserver agreement on radiographic pathosis; clinical signs correlate poorly with histology (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).
- Diagnostic certainty and prognostic data are always incomplete (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

**Argument 2: Case difficulty and operator skill**
- The AAE Case Difficulty Assessment Form: patient, diagnostic/treatment and additional considerations; minimal, moderate, high difficulty; the dentist judges whether the case is within his or her skill or should be referred (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021).
- Trial results from specialists may not apply to a general dentist's hands [Likely].

**Argument 3: Evidence describes groups; the clinician treats one patient**
- Literature gives efficacy and effectiveness; the question is "whether a treatment will work in this specific situation" (Kvist, 2025).
- Example: pulp-preserving therapy for a stable patient living near the clinic, pulpectomy for one who moves often (Kvist, 2025).
- Uncertainty: calculable risk, fundamental uncertainty, ignorance (Kvist, 2025). Where no study exists (rare anatomy, combined lesions, medically complex patients), judgment is the only tool.
- "What can be done and what should be done" differ (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021); percentages must be applied case by case (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 5. The case against unaided clinical judgment

### Explanation

Gulabivala and Ng warn that practice patterns "may lead to the passive application of heuristic principles"; the undergraduate decision tree serves patients in the "central tendency" but not those "on the fringes of the normal distribution"; and variation in philosophy, knowledge, experience and judgment produces different plans between clinicians (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman report that treatment thresholds vary widely and recommendations may depend "more on personal values and experience than objective analysis" (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. Kvist notes that the perceived benefit of retreatment varies among dentists (Kvist, 2025) [SRC], and Gulabivala and Ng that there is no definite retreatment criterion for stable symptomless radiolucencies (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Uncertainty may cause overdiagnosis or underdiagnosis (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Machtou and Ruddle report two tendencies among dentists deciding on retreatment, with the decision often modified by individual values; endodontic specialists showed the same behavioural pattern as students (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Gutmann: "Empirical decision-making in the past has been replaced by evidence-based principles" (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC].

History shows the danger of opinion without evidence: fear of focal sepsis led to widespread extraction of pulpless teeth, the theory reigning for about 50 years until around 1940; endodontics was rescued by practitioners who meticulously recorded their treatments and outcomes; and evidence-based practice later "helped to avert irrational treatment recommendations" favouring extraction of savable teeth (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

Named cognitive biases are not described in any textbook read; the list in the exam core is [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Argument 1: Heuristics and variation**
- Routine practice may become "passive application of heuristic principles"; simple undergraduate decision trees suit typical cases but fail those "on the fringes" (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).
- Clinicians differ in philosophy, knowledge, experience and judgment, so plans differ (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014). Treatment thresholds vary widely; recommendations may rest "more on personal values and experience than objective analysis" (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021).
- The perceived benefit of retreatment varies between dentists (Kvist, 2025); specialists decide like students (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019); no definite criterion exists for retreating a stable, symptomless radiolucency (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

**Argument 2: Cognitive biases** [RECALL]
| Bias | Endodontic example |
|---|---|
| Anchoring | Fixing on the first diagnosis (e.g. "irreversible pulpitis") and ignoring a crack or non-odontogenic pain |
| Availability | Recent dramatic failure drives the next plan |
| Confirmation | Reading a radiograph to fit the expected answer |
| Overconfidence | Underrating case difficulty; not referring |
| Commission bias | Preferring to act (retreat, extract) when monitoring is reasonable |
| Financial / technology bias | New file system or implant favoured without outcome data |

**Argument 3: History**
- The focal infection theory led to mass extraction of pulpless teeth for about 50 years until practitioners' recorded outcomes rescued endodontics; evidence-based practice later averted irrational extraction of savable teeth for implants (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).
- Uncertainty without evidence leads to overdiagnosis or underdiagnosis (Kvist, 2025).

## 6. Where outcome evidence conflicts: judgment must referee

### Explanation

**Single versus multiple visits.** Cohen's: some studies show less pain after single visits, while systematic reviews find similar pain and similar radiographic healing; "differences in research methodology explain the conflict"; and "agreement is lacking" on single-visit treatment of necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Ørstavik: individual RCTs differ, some favouring two visits and others one, but systematic reviews show virtually no difference; a CBCT-based RCT showed somewhat greater lesion volume reduction with two sessions; calcium hydroxide dressing remains the effective procedure when time or clinical conditions require it (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]. Ng and Gulabivala: the RCTs lacked power, and until large, properly documented RCTs exist, undocumented variables (operator skill, case complexity, patient compliance) would continue to bias the outcome (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's: the decision depends primarily on the condition of the pulp and periradicular tissues, with the clinician's experience and skills, technical difficulty and compliance also counting; noninfected vital teeth should be treated in a single visit where possible; systematic reviews show no significant difference but data are insufficient for definitive conclusions; and one large single-operator study found a significantly higher favourable outcome with two or more visits using calcium hydroxide (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017) [SRC].

**Retreatment, surgery or implant.** Setzer and co-workers cite a systematic review that ranked implant success above endodontic treatment, but note that success criteria differ between disciplines, so "such comparisons lack validity"; survival rates are comparable, and implant survival does not exceed that of even compromised but adequately treated teeth; another review found lower outcomes for endodontic surgery than single implants (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad call root canal treatment and implants "complementary therapies, not competing treatments"; among replacement options (implant, fixed or removable prosthesis) the implant single crown is "the replacement of choice", with survival equal to a root canal treated tooth and superior to failed teeth treated by microsurgery without prior nonsurgical retreatment; but the first option after failure is nonsurgical retreatment, then surgery, replantation or autotransplantation, with the implant the best alternative only when the tooth still carries unacceptable risks (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng give implants a third-line role after failed retreatment (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman say older surgical studies are weak by today's standards; microsurgery reaches 94% against 59% for traditional surgery; and under ideal conditions the prognoses of nonsurgical retreatment, surgery and implants "should be roughly equal" (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. Rosenberg and Malek add implant complications: more interventions, and a 48.03% cumulative complication rate at 10 to 16 years in one study; and intentional replantation is increasingly shown to be cost-effective (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's: implants and endodontics are complementary; an implant may be a suitable alternative in selected situations of poor prognosis, but retention of the natural tooth remains ideal because peri-implant disease is not uncommon (Chong, 2017) [SRC]; survival of treated teeth compares favourably with implant-supported crowns, and although finance should never dictate planning it remains a factor in decision-making (Mannocci and Giovarruscio, 2017) [SRC].

Where the authors actually differ is narrower than "implant versus tooth": on surgery versus implant, White and Torabinejad (implant survives better than microsurgery done without prior retreatment) and the review cited by Setzer and co-workers (lower outcomes for surgery than single implants) stand against Johnson, Fayad and Berman (older surgical studies weak; modern microsurgery 94%; options roughly equal under ideal conditions). On the sequence after failure (retreat first) the books agree. Both sides are shown; neither is ranked here. The first draft framed White and Torabinejad as favouring the implant after failure; that over-read "replacement of choice", which in their chapter refers to choosing among replacements once a tooth is to be replaced.

## 7. Decision analysis and shared decision making

### Explanation

Ng and Gulabivala describe two ways to estimate prognosis: heuristic, intuitive weighing of dominant factors, or entering comprehensive data into an algorithmic model; "at present, the evidence base is insufficient" for the sophisticated approach (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng cite a Markov-model health-economic study: root canal treatment is highly cost-effective as first-line treatment for a maxillary central incisor, orthograde retreatment is cost-effective after failure, but surgical retreatment is not (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad cite a decision-tree and patient-utility analysis of the abscessed tooth (Balevi and Shepperd, 2007, cited by White and Torabinejad, 2017) and state that the natural tooth "has intrinsic value" (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman predict algorithms to assist decisions as evidence grows, and prefer shared decision making to a clinician-directed decision (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng describe a "mutually agreed choice of treatment" after dialogue and negotiation (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

Machtou and Ruddle state that the retreatment decision can be structured as a decision tree illustrating the clinical options, giving a rational basis for deciding and a way to involve the patient (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. The formal steps of decision analysis (probabilities, utilities, expected value, sensitivity analysis) are [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Decision analysis: the formal meeting point**
- A decision tree lays out the clinical options, gives a rational basis and involves the patient (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019). Steps [RECALL]: (1) define the decision (e.g. retreat, surgery, extract and implant); (2) draw a decision tree; (3) attach probabilities from the best evidence; (4) attach utilities (patient values: cost, pain, time, aesthetics); (5) calculate expected value; (6) sensitivity analysis to test how robust the choice is.
- Evidence supplies the probabilities, the patient supplies the utilities, and judgment adjusts both to the case.
- Markov model: root canal treatment highly cost-effective first line for a maxillary central incisor; orthograde retreatment cost-effective after failure; surgical retreatment not (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014). A decision-tree and utility analysis of the abscessed tooth is cited by White and Torabinejad (2017); the natural tooth "has intrinsic value".
- Prognosis today is estimated heuristically because data are insufficient for algorithmic models (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021); algorithms are predicted as evidence grows (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021).

**Shared decision making**
- The clinician presents outcome probabilities, the patient states preferences, and both agree; preferable to a clinician-directed decision and increases satisfaction (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021).
- Planning ends in a "mutually agreed choice of treatment" (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

## 8. Guidelines: evidence turned into practice

### Explanation

Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang note a move from a local, experience-based to a national, evidence-based standard of care, the same for specialists and general dentists, with the AAE Case Difficulty Assessment Form as the tool for referral (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Kvist cites the AAE position statements on systemic antibiotics (2017) and vital pulp therapy (2021) and the ESE S3-level clinical practice guideline on treatment of pulpal and apical disease (Duncan et al, 2023, cited by Kvist, 2025), and observes that where evidence is lacking, recommendations rest on systematic "consensus-based statements" that also draw on the experts' own knowledge (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng say decisions "should follow the knowledge gained from research and evidence-based practice" (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Harty's notes that the ESE has issued quality guidelines for endodontic treatment and that practitioners must monitor their outcomes against accepted criteria and follow published guidelines (Chong, 2017) [SRC]. The PubMed abstract of the 2023 S3 guideline shows it was developed with independent methodological guidance from the Association of Scientific Medical Societies in Germany, "utilizing the GRADE process", from 14 commissioned systematic reviews, followed by a structured consensus with endodontic experts and external stakeholders that produced "evidence and expert-based recommendations" (Duncan et al, 2023) [LIT]: an explicit example of evidence and expert judgment being combined by design. The GRADE Working Group grades certainty of evidence as high, moderate, low or very low (Galicia and Khan, 2024) [SRC]; the ESE S3 guideline used GRADE (Duncan et al, 2023) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Guidelines bridge evidence and judgment**
- **AAE:** Case Difficulty Assessment Form; position statements on antibiotics (2017) and vital pulp therapy (2021) (cited by Kvist, 2025).
- **ESE:** quality guidelines (Chong, 2017); S3-level clinical practice guideline for pulpal and apical disease (2023), built on 14 commissioned systematic reviews and the GRADE process, with evidence- and expert-based recommendations agreed in a structured consensus (Duncan et al, 2023). Practitioners must audit outcomes against accepted criteria (Chong, 2017).
- The standard of care is moving from local experience to a national evidence-based standard, the same for general dentists and specialists (Johnson, Abedi and Shabahang, 2021).
- Where evidence is weak, guidelines are consensus-based statements that include the experts' own clinical knowledge (Kvist, 2025): **guidelines are themselves judgment and evidence combined.**
- Guidelines guide; they do not replace the decision for one patient [Likely].

## 9. Synthesis and verdict

### Explanation

The sources agree that the two meet. Kvist: literature alone is not sufficient, and a dentist must balance episteme, techne and phronesis (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman: choice should rest on best evidence, case-specific judgment and patient preference (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad: nonsurgical retreatment is the first option after failure, and comprehensive case evaluation and risk assessment of the individual is always necessary (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Gutmann: evidence-based decision-making has greatly advanced treatment planning, "but the impact of clinician experience should not be ignored" (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. The four evidence-based practice skills taught to dental students end in judgment: ask a focused PICO question, search for the highest level of evidence, critically evaluate it, and "make clinical judgments about the applicability" of the evidence for the patient (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Deliberation**
| For "they do not meet" | For "they meet" |
|---|---|
| Thin RCT base; SBU found little robust evidence | EBD by definition includes clinical expertise |
| Group data do not fit the individual tooth | Judgment applies group data to the individual |
| Judgment is biased and variable | Evidence and guidelines check bias |
| Conflicting outcome studies | Judgment referees conflicts using case factors |

**Verdict.** The twain do meet, and must. Evidence without judgment cannot diagnose a cracked tooth, grade case difficulty or apply a percentage to one patient; judgment without evidence repeats the focal infection era and passive heuristics. In endodontics, where RCTs are few, judgment carries more weight than in pharmacology, but it must be **informed** judgment (Kvist, 2025); evidence-based decision-making has advanced planning, but clinician experience "should not be ignored" (Gutmann, 2017).

**A working model**
1. Ask a focused PICO question (patient, intervention, comparison, outcome) (Anusavice et al, 2013).
2. Find and appraise the best available evidence (level, bias, applicability).
3. Assess the case: diagnosis, AAE difficulty, operator skill.
4. Elicit patient values; use shared decision making.
5. Decide, record, and audit outcomes, so that each practice adds to the evidence.

**Conclusion.** Clinical decision making "combines the best available evidence, clinical judgment, and patient preferences" (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021); "a dentist must develop a balance of all three" forms of knowledge (Kvist, 2025). The two are not East and West; they are the two hands of one clinician.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Endodontics lacks high-level evidence, so clinical judgment must fill the gap."**
*Textbook:* Kvist reports the SBU conclusion of a significant lack of robust evidence and lists four unresolved areas (Kvist, 2025) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala explain the methodological reason (operator-dependent multistep procedures) and say data are insufficient for algorithmic prognosis (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].
*Refinement:* the gap is uneven. For survival after primary treatment the evidence is strong: very large cohorts and systematic reviews show very high survival (White and Torabinejad, 2017; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. The gap lies in comparisons between protocols and in diagnostic accuracy. [Likely]
*Evidence versus interpretation:* the evidence shows few adequately powered RCTs; it does not show that judgment is accurate where RCTs are missing. Judgment is the default, not a proven substitute. [Certain]
*Clinical implication:* use judgment where evidence is silent, but declare it as judgment and audit its results.

**Claim 2: "Implants and endodontic treatment have equivalent outcomes."**
*Textbook:* Setzer and co-workers cite a review that ranked implant success higher, then say comparisons of success lack validity because criteria differ, and that survival is comparable (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad call the implant single crown "the replacement of choice" among replacement options, but put nonsurgical retreatment first after failure (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Johnson, Fayad and Berman say retreatment, surgery and implants should be roughly equal under ideal conditions (Johnson, Fayad and Berman, 2021) [SRC].
*What follows:* these positions are not contradictory once the comparison is specified: survival of a tooth versus survival of an implant is similar; success definitions differ; after failure, the order is retreatment first, then surgery or replantation, then implant. [Likely]
*Limitation:* the data are mainly cohort and survival data, with different populations and operators; no head-to-head RCT was found in the books. [Certain]
*Clinical implication:* the choice cannot be read off the literature; restorability, periodontal status, cost and patient values decide.

**Claim 3: "Single-visit treatment is as effective as multiple-visit treatment."**
*Textbook:* systematic reviews show no detectable difference in healing (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021; Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC].
*Challenge:* the RCTs lacked power (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]; a large single-operator study favoured two visits with calcium hydroxide (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017) [SRC]; and one CBCT-based RCT found somewhat greater lesion reduction with two visits (Ørstavik, 2020) [SRC]. "No difference found" is not the same as "no difference". [Certain]
*Clinical implication:* both are acceptable; the choice rests on symptoms, time, ability to dry the canal and operator judgment.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

- SBU 2010: significant lack of robust evidence in endodontics.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing only on evidence-based dentistry and never deliberating the opposite case.
- Treating "no significant difference" in underpowered trials as proof of equivalence.
- Comparing implant "success" with endodontic "success" as if the criteria were the same.
- Treating in-vitro or animal data as clinical evidence.
- Ending without a clear verdict; "deliberate" needs a reasoned position.
