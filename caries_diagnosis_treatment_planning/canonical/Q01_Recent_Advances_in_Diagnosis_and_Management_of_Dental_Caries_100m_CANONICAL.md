# Dental caries – Recent advances in diagnosis and management

**Marks:** 100 · Single essay

## 1. Introduction: dental caries as a disease

**Dental caries is a multifactorial, dynamic disease process resulting from dysbiosis in the dental biofilm, driven by exposure to sugars and other fermentable carbohydrates, which over time leads to demineralisation of the dental hard tissues** (Fontana and Huysmans, 2019). Lesions result from a shift in metabolic activity and a gradual change in the ecology of the biofilm; the biofilm is a prerequisite, and a lesion is a symptom of the imbalance between mineral loss and gain (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Demineralisation occurs when biofilm pH falls and remineralisation when it rises, many times a day, so lesions can progress, arrest or regress.

Two consequences shape every recent advance:

- **Disease versus lesion.** The caries lesion is the end result of caries disease (Zeller, Young and Novy, 2019). A filling treats the lesion, not the disease; drilling and filling does not cure caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Paradigm shift.** Management has moved from an operative (surgical) to a non-surgical, preventive approach, which created the demand for detailed, objective detection methods (Ferreira Zandona and Longbottom, 2019).

**Caries diagnosis today therefore has four components:** (1) detection of the lesion, (2) staging of its severity, (3) assessment of its activity, and (4) assessment of the patient's caries risk. Management is then chosen for the lesion and for the patient.

## 2. Why newer diagnostic methods were needed

- Visual, tactile and radiographic examination have **low sensitivities of 20–30%** for minute early changes (Kim, 2019).
- Bitewings detect only about 20–30% of early proximal enamel lesions (Schwendicke and Göstemeyer, 2019).
- A sharp explorer forced into a site can cavitate a remineralisable surface (Zeller, Young and Novy, 2019).
- Non-operative care can arrest only lesions detected early and judged active.

An **ideal detection method** should report both the presence of a lesion and its depth and surface status (Schwendicke and Göstemeyer, 2019): it should identify a healthy surface (specificity), detect demineralisation (sensitivity), and show whether the lesion is active and whether it is cavitated. Apart from frank cavitation, no current method does all of this accurately, so the clinician combines visual, tactile and radiographic findings with the patient's caries status (Boushell, Eidson and Shugars, 2019).

The editors stress that devices should **support visual examination and not replace it**, that teeth must be plaque-free before examination, and that greater sensitivity is for monitoring, **not a licence to increase surgical intervention** (Ferreira Zandona and Longbottom, 2019). The standard of care remains visual inspection of clean, dry, well-lit teeth with radiographs as indicated (Boushell, Eidson and Shugars, 2019).

## 3. Classification of recent diagnostic methods

Most devices work on four physical principles: optical light scattering, mineral density, fluorescence and tissue porosity; all measure tissue damage that has already occurred (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

| Group | Methods |
|---|---|
| Visual-tactile criteria | ICDAS II, Nyvad criteria, ADA CCS |
| Radiographic | Digital bitewings (CCD/CMOS sensors, phosphor plates), subtraction radiography, computer-aided detection, CBCT |
| Transillumination | FOTI, DIFOTI, NILT (DIAGNOcam), NIR imaging |
| Fluorescence | QLF, DIAGNOdent, fluorescence cameras, FACE |
| Electrical | ECM, CarieScan (ACIST) |
| Photothermal | Canary System (PTR-LUM) |
| Emerging | OCT, ultrasound, bioluminescence (CALCIVIS) |
| Risk/activity | CAMBRA, Cariogram, ICCMS |

## 4. Advances in visual-tactile assessment

### Preparing the tooth

The teeth are cleaned by toothbrushing; rotary prophylaxis is avoided because it may abrade the lesion surface. Each surface is dried with air for **10–15 s** (Machiulskiene, 2019). The probe is used only to feel texture, never to force into a fissure.

### ICDAS II

ICDAS was devised at a conference in **Dundee in 2002**, expanding the five-score Ekstrand-Kidd-Ricketts (ERK) system to **seven scores** (Ekstrand and Braga, 2019). It is a **two-digit code**: the first digit records the restoration/sealant status of the surface, the second the caries severity.

| Code | Coronal caries (second digit) | Merged |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Sound | Sound |
| 1 | First visual change in enamel | Initial |
| 2 | Distinct visual change in enamel | Initial |
| 3 | Localised enamel breakdown, no visible dentine | Moderate |
| 4 | Underlying dark shadow from dentine | Moderate |
| 5 | Distinct cavity with visible dentine | Extensive |
| 6 | Extensive distinct cavity with visible dentine | Extensive |

**Histological validation:** 95% of ICDAS 0 surfaces show no demineralisation, while 84% of ICDAS 5–6 lesions reach the inner third of dentine. Root caries uses three scores. **Activity** is scored from lesion location, visual appearance, tactile feeling and gingival bleeding; points are summed and **more than 7 points means active** (Ekstrand and Braga, 2019). **Prognosis:** initial lesions progress **2–4 times**, and microcavitated or shadowed lesions **more than 10 times**, more often than sound surfaces.

### Nyvad criteria

The Nyvad system is based on the **surface characteristics of the lesion rather than its depth**, with two severity levels (non-cavitated, cavitated) crossed with activity (Machiulskiene, 2019):

- **Active:** matt/chalky, whitish or yellowish enamel, rough to a gently moved probe, in plaque-stagnation sites, often under sticky plaque; active dentine feels soft.
- **Inactive:** **glossy and smooth**, white to brown or black, plaque-free; inactive dentine is smooth and hard, with no overhanging enamel.
- A lesion with mixed features is scored active. Active non-cavitated lesions are significantly more likely to cavitate than inactive ones, so activity assessment carries prognostic value (Machiulskiene, 2019).

### ADA Caries Classification System (2015)

The ADA CCS, released in **2015**, classifies each surface as **sound, initial, moderate or advanced**, records site and activity, maps to ICDAS and links approximal radiographic depth (E1–D3) to clinical cavitation (Zeller, Young and Novy, 2019). Its key idea is that the decision to restore should rest on the **likelihood of full cavitation through enamel**, because intact enamel keeps bacteria out of dentine.

**Conflict on the probe:**

| Author | Position |
|---|---|
| Machiulskiene (2019), Nyvad | A sharp explorer, moved gently at 20–30°, assesses texture best; no evidence that careful probing harms lesions |
| Zeller, Young and Novy (2019), ADA CCS | Use a rounded explorer or ball probe; forcing an explorer can cause cavitation |

Both agree that pressure must never be used; the difference is in the instrument tip.

## 5. Advances in radiographic diagnosis

### Bitewings and depth scoring

Bitewing sensitivity is **about 0.2–0.3 for early lesions**, rising to 0.4–0.6 for dentine lesions, with specificity around or above 0.9 (Schwendicke and Göstemeyer, 2019). Depth is scored **E1, E2 (outer/inner enamel) and D1–D3 (outer, middle, inner dentine)**. **E1–D1 lesions are managed mainly non-restoratively**, D2–D3 usually by restoration, because restoring proximal surfaces starts the costly cycle of re-restoration ("death spiral").

### Digital radiography

Digital receptors are **solid-state sensors (CCD or CMOS)** or **photostimulable phosphor plates** (Göstemeyer and Schwendicke, 2019). Advantages include immediate images, easy storage and transfer, and a dose reduction of **30–80%** per image, although total patient dose falls less because more images are taken. Recent studies find digital bitewings **as accurate as film**. Newer tools:

- **Image processing:** zoom, contrast, inversion, edge enhancement.
- **Digital subtraction radiography:** subtracting corresponding pixels on serial images from the same projection removes unchanged anatomy, showing progression or arrest; it improves inter-observer agreement on lesion activity.
- **Computer-aided detection:** at best as accurate as human observers at present.

### Cone beam CT

CBCT detects **cavitation** more accurately than standard methods (Göstemeyer and Schwendicke, 2019). Reported sensitivity is **0.44–0.61** against **0.31–0.33** for 2D imaging, but scatter and **beam-hardening artefacts** around restorations limit recurrent-caries diagnosis, and the higher dose means **CBCT cannot be recommended as a primary method for caries**; caries is read only as an incidental finding (Price, 2019).

## 6. Transillumination

- **FOTI:** high-intensity white light through the tooth; porous lesions **appear dark on a lighter background**, dentine lesions as an orange-brown or bluish shadow (Hogan, Pretty and Ellwood, 2019). It is **non-invasive, non-ionising and can be repeated without limit**, but subjective; sensitivity ranges from 21–96% and specificity from 74–88% for non-cavitated lesions. FOTI is an adjunct to visual examination and helps to decide whether a radiograph is justified.
- **DIFOTI:** a **CCD camera replaces the human eye**, allowing image storage and monitoring; interpretation remains subjective.
- **NILT (DIAGNOcam, CariVu):** introduced in **2012**; **~780 nm** near-infrared light passes through the alveolar bone and tooth and is captured occlusally (Kühnisch, 2019). Demineralised enamel scatters light and appears dark. For proximal dentine caries, sensitivity was **99%**, comparable to digital bitewings, without radiation; it suits children and orthodontic patients.
- **NIR imaging:** enamel is **most transparent near 1300 nm**, and at longer wavelengths stains do not interfere, so stain can be told from demineralisation (Fried, 2019a).

## 7. Fluorescence-based methods

### Quantitative light-induced fluorescence (QLF)

QLF measures **mineral loss as fluorescence loss and bacterial activity as red fluorescence at the same time** (Kim, 2019). **Principle:** **405 nm** blue light excites fluorophores at the enamel–dentine junction; the porous white spot scatters this light and appears dark. Software gives:

- **ΔF:** fluorescence loss, representing **lesion depth**, correlating with transverse microradiography at **r = 0.88**.
- **Lesion area (mm²) and ΔQ (volume).**
- **ΔR:** red fluorescence from porphyrins of mature plaque (at least 3 days old) and active lesions.

QLF is non-destructive and quantitative, so it suits **monitoring of remineralisation** and patient motivation; devices have progressed from the intraoral Inspektor camera to the QLF-D and handheld Qray systems.

### Laser fluorescence (DIAGNOdent)

A **655 nm** diode laser induces fluorescence that is read as a **value of 0–99**, with a peak-hold function; separate tips exist for occlusal and approximal sites (Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019).

| Reading | DIAGNOdent, occlusal | Pen, occlusal | Pen, approximal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sound | 0–13 | 0–12 | 0–7 |
| Enamel caries | 14–20 | 13–25 | 8–16 |
| Dentine caries | >20 | >25 | >16 |

**Limitations:** prophylaxis paste and calculus give false positives; it does not help for secondary caries (Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019) and cannot separate active from inactive lesions (Caneppele et al., 2020). Its greater sensitivity is offset by false positives, so it is not a principal diagnostic method (Boushell, Eidson and Shugars, 2019).

**Conflict on what it measures:**

| Author | Source of the signal |
|---|---|
| Neuhaus and Lussi (2019) | Fluorescence of bacterial porphyrins, which correlates with lesion depth |
| Caneppele et al. (2020) | Chromophores; the lower the mineral content, the greater the fluorescence |
| Banerjee and Watson (2015) | Both light scattering and fluorescence, to a varying degree, so readings must be interpreted cautiously |

### Fluorescence cameras and FACE

Intraoral cameras such as Soprolife and VistaCam use blue or violet LEDs to show carious areas in red (Caneppele et al., 2020). **FACE (fluorescence-aided caries excavation)** illuminates the cavity with **405 nm violet light** through a high-pass filter: **infected dentine fluoresces red, non-infected dentine green** (Lennon and Buchalla, 2019). The red signal comes from bacterial porphyrins, so no dye is needed. FACE identifies infected dentine during excavation, preserving bacteria-free softened dentine and guiding selective excavation over the pulp.

## 8. Other new technologies

- **Electrical conductance (ECM, CarieScan/ACIST):** porous, fluid-filled demineralised enamel conducts current better than intact enamel (Caneppele et al., 2020; Boushell, Eidson and Shugars, 2019).
- **Canary System:** pulsed laser light is converted into heat (**photothermal radiometry**) and **luminescence**; as a lesion grows **PTR increases and LUM decreases**, combined into a Canary Number (Amaechi, 2019). It examines **up to 5 mm** below the surface, raises temperature by **less than 1–2 °C**, works around restorations and under sealants, and monitors remineralisation. Caneppele et al. (2020) note a lack of independent studies.
- **Optical coherence tomography:** cross-sectional images of reflectivity to **3–5 mm in enamel and 1–2 mm in dentine** at better than 10 μm resolution; polarisation-sensitive OCT quantifies lesion severity (Fried, 2019b).
- **Ultrasound:** **18 MHz** pulse-echo waves distinguish intact from demineralised enamel (Yanıkoğlu and Tağtekin, 2019).
- **Bioluminescence (CALCIVIS):** a calcium-sensitive photoprotein glows with free calcium ions, giving **live visualisation of active demineralisation**; it is one of the few devices aimed directly at activity (Longbottom and Vernon, 2019).

## 9. Lesion activity and caries risk assessment

**Activity assessment** (Section 4) decides whether a lesion needs active care. **Risk assessment** determines the probability of new lesions and of progression (Fontana and Huysmans, 2019).

- A **risk factor** directly increases the probability of disease and is part of the causal chain; a **risk indicator** is an existing sign of disease, such as active lesions or restorations placed in the past 3 years (Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson, 2019).
- **Past caries experience is the best predictor**. Diet analysis and clinical examination form the foundation; salivary tests add support, while bacterial counts and ATP tests have little predictive value.
- **CAMBRA** is a medical model: (1) assess risk, (2) reduce pathological factors, (3) enhance protective factors, (4) minimally invasive restoration when needed (Zeller, Young and Novy, 2019). It is evolving into the standard of care; the **Cariogram** is validated for children (Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson, 2019).
- **Categories:** low risk (no active lesions in 2–3 years) versus caries-active patients with more than two new or progressing lesions a year (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **ICCMS** links ICDAS staging and activity with risk to give **risk-adjusted preventive care, control of non-cavitated lesions, conservative restoration of cavitated ones and a risk-based recall interval** (Caneppele et al., 2020).

**Diagram:** ICCMS-type pathway. Patient risk (low/moderate/high) beside lesion staging (sound, initial, moderate, extensive) and activity (active/inactive), arrows leading to preventive care, non-operative control, minimally invasive restoration and a risk-based recall.

## 10. Recent advances in management

### Decision-making by lesion and patient

Expert consensus uses **cavitation, cleansability and activity** (Fontana and Huysmans, 2019):

1. **Arrested lesions** need no intervention except for aesthetics, function or pulpal risk.
2. **Non-cavitated active lesions** receive evidence-based non-restorative treatment.
3. Occlusal lesions that look non-cavitated but reach dentine radiographically may be sealed, but the sealant must be monitored because of a possible **"trampoline" effect**.
4. **Most cavitated lesions are non-cleansable, and thus active**, and need restoration.

Decisions are **shared with the patient**. Ekstrand and Braga (2019) align the merged ICDAS stages with a graded menu: initial lesions can be arrested non-operatively, whereas extensive and most moderate lesions need restoration or sealing. The options run from toothbrushing instruction and local fluoride, through sealing, infiltration and silver diamine fluoride, to stepwise excavation, Hall crowns, restorations, endodontics and extraction.

### Non-operative (non-invasive) management

**Fluoride.** Fluoride is incorporated into the apatite lattice and inhibits bacterial enolase (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Toothpaste of **at least 1000 ppm** is advised under 3 years and **1350–1500 ppm** family paste afterwards, with advice to spit out, not rinse, after brushing. Varnishes added to toothpaste give further reduction, and pastes up to **5000 ppm** exist for high-risk adults (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Sturdevant's protocol prescribes 5000 ppm paste and fluoride varnish at **3-month** recall for high-risk adults (Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson, 2019).

**Silver diamine fluoride (SDF 38%).** A caries-arresting and desensitising solution; silver is antibacterial. After biofilm removal it is applied with a microbrush for **3 min**, with petroleum jelly protecting gingiva and lips. It suits large cavitated lesions where restorative care is unavailable, and root caries. **Drawbacks:** arrested dentine stains black, and **up to 50%** of lesions reactivate by **24 months** after one application, so re-application every **6 months** or restoration is advised (Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson, 2019).

**Remineralising agents.** **CPP–ACP**: the milk-derived casein phosphopeptide stabilises amorphous calcium phosphate and keeps it supersaturated at the tooth surface; it is contraindicated in milk-protein allergy (Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson, 2019). Bioactive glasses are an alternative (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**Fissure sealants.** Sealing is indicated where **active fissure caries has not arrested** despite control, or where an erupting molar cannot be cleaned; sealing every tooth is **over-treatment** (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Resin and high-viscosity glass ionomer are **equally effective**; glass ionomer is preferred for erupting teeth and poor cooperation. Sealed bacteria **die or become dormant** (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016), and sealants can arrest initial lesions within limits (Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson, 2019).

**Resin infiltration (micro-invasive).** An extremely low-viscosity resin infiltrates non-cavitated smooth-surface and proximal lesions after etching. Authors disagree:

| Author | Position |
|---|---|
| Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson (2019) | A practice-based RCT found infiltration more effective than oral hygiene and fluoride instruction at preventing progression |
| Ekstrand and Braga (2019) | Lists sealing/infiltration among options for approximal lesions |
| Kidd and Fejerskov (2016) | Trials have yet to show success comparable with occlusal sealants; the technique is demanding and costly, and even deep non-cavitated lesions arrest with cleaning and fluoride |

### Minimally invasive operative management

Restoration is required when the patient **cannot clean an active cavitated lesion** (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Minimally invasive dentistry removes **only unrepairable enamel and dentine**, keeps cavities small and uses adhesive materials that seal residual bacteria from nutrients (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**Carious tissue removal by depth** (Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson, 2019):

1. **Moderate lesions** (not into the inner third, no exposure risk): excavate to a **caries-free DEJ and firm dentine**.
2. **Deep lesions** in a vital, symptomless tooth: **selective removal to soft dentine** — clean periphery to a sound DEJ; centrally, soft dentine is left within about **1 mm of the pulp**; seal with glass ionomer or a definitive restoration.
3. **No re-entry:** temporisation and re-entry do not improve outcomes; calcium hydroxide liners do not improve outcomes; the marginal seal is critical.

Kidd and Fejerskov (2016) state that complete removal risking exposure **should be considered unethical**. Caries-affected dentine gives better indirect pulp protection than any artificial material (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Detector dyes stain demineralised matrix, not bacteria, and lead to over-preparation, so they are no longer recommended (Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson, 2019; Banerjee and Watson, 2015); FACE is the fluorescence alternative (Lennon and Buchalla, 2019).

**Stepwise excavation** removes part of the soft dentine, seals temporarily and re-enters later. Complete removal exposed the pulp in up to **53%** of controls, and re-entry itself exposed **17%** (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).

| Point | Kidd and Fejerskov (2016) | Banerjee and Watson (2015) |
|---|---|---|
| Re-entry interval | 25 days to 2 years in trials | 6–9 months in the original method |
| Is re-entry needed? | No proven benefit; the authors would not re-enter | One-step sealing gives significantly fewer exposures |

**Atraumatic restorative treatment (ART).** Developed for areas without electricity or running water: hand excavation of soft dentine, restoration and sealing of adjacent fissures with **high-viscosity glass ionomer**, usually without anaesthesia (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Occlusal ART succeeds; approximal ART tends to fail by loss or fracture. Worn ART restorations can be cut back and laminated with composite (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**Hall technique.** A stainless steel crown is cemented with glass ionomer on a cavitated, symptomless primary molar with **no anaesthetic, no caries removal and no tooth reduction**; the occlusion re-establishes in **2–4 weeks** (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).

**Non-restorative cavity control.** Primary-tooth cavities are opened so the parent can brush them; the lesions arrest, and several teeth can be opened in about **10 minutes** (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).

### Recall and monitoring

Recall follows risk: **3-monthly** with varnish and bitewings every **6–12 months** for high risk, **9–12 months** for low risk (Ferreira Zandoná, Ritter and Eidson, 2019). Quantitative tools (QLF, Canary, subtraction radiography) document whether non-operative care is arresting lesions.

## 11. Conclusion

Recent advances have changed caries care from detecting holes to diagnosing a disease: standardised visual criteria that stage severity and activity, low-dose and non-ionising adjuncts that quantify and monitor lesions, and risk assessment that personalises care. Management follows the same logic: arrest non-cavitated lesions with fluoride, SDF, remineralising agents, sealants or infiltration, and restore cavitated lesions with minimally invasive, selective and sealed techniques.
