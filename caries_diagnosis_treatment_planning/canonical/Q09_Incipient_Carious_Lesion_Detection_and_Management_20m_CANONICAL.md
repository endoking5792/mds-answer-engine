# What is an incipient carious lesion? How will you detect and manage such lesions?

**Marks:** 20 · Long essay

**Answer blueprint:** Definition → Histopathology → Clinical features and differential diagnosis → Detection → Activity assessment → Principles of management → Management options → Monitoring and prognosis → Conclusion.

## Definition

An **incipient carious lesion** (white spot, non-cavitated or initial lesion) is "the first evidence of caries activity in enamel": chalky white and opaque when air-dried, seeming to disappear when wet, with a **hard, intact, smooth** surface; it may be **reversible** (Ferreira Zandoná and Ritter, 2019). It is **subsurface demineralisation under an intact surface** (Mount et al., 2016), corresponding to ICDAS codes 1 and 2, the "initial" stage (Ekstrand and Braga, 2019).

## Histopathology

On a smooth surface the lesion is a **cone with its apex towards the DEJ** (Singh, 2020). Four zones are seen in polarised light (Ferreira Zandoná and Ritter, 2019):

| Zone | Feature |
|---|---|
| Surface zone | Relatively intact, pore volume below 5%; 20-100 µm thick (Singh, 2020); richer in fluoride than sound enamel (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) |
| Body of lesion | Largest pore volume, 5% at the periphery to 25% at the centre |
| Dark zone | Pore volume 2%-4%; enlarges with remineralisation |
| Translucent zone | Deepest; advancing front |

Sturdevant numbers zones from the surface, Singh from the advancing front; the zones are the same. The intact surface is a **barrier to bacterial invasion** (Ferreira Zandoná and Ritter, 2019) and must never be broken by probing.

## Clinical features and differential diagnosis

Caries white spots lie in **plaque stagnation areas**, are matt and rough when dried, and may take up stain. **Developmental hypomineralisation** is smooth and glossy, lies on self-cleansing areas and is often bilateral on corresponding teeth (Amaechi et al., 2013); hypoplasia is visible both wet and dry (Singh, 2020).

## Detection

1. **Prepare:** clean, dry, well-lit surface (Longbottom and Ferreira Zandona, 2019); air-dry about **5 s** (Cortés, Martignon and Douglas, 2019).
2. **Wet-then-dry inspection:** a lesion seen only after drying is less than halfway through enamel and may fully reverse; one seen wet is deeper and may only arrest (Amaechi et al., 2013). Of ICDAS code 1 lesions, 95% are confined to the outer half of enamel (Ekstrand and Braga, 2019).
3. **Gentle tactile check:** use a **CPITN probe with a 0.5 mm ball** (Boushell and Shugars, 2019); forceful probing can irreversibly damage a non-cavitated lesion (Longbottom and Ferreira Zandona, 2019).
4. **Proximal surfaces:** bitewings show subsurface radiolucency, confirmed by transillumination; separation for at least 24 hours allows direct inspection (Mount et al., 2016). Bitewing sensitivity for early lesions is only 0.2-0.3 (Schwendicke and Göstemeyer, 2019).
5. **Adjuncts:** QLF and laser fluorescence give numerical readings to monitor change (Kim, 2019).

## Activity assessment

**Active** lesions are matt, chalky, whitish-yellowish, rough to gentle probing and near the gingiva; **inactive** lesions are glossy, smooth and away from it (Machiulskiene, 2019). An ICDAS activity score above 7 points means active (Ekstrand and Braga, 2019).

## Principles of management

- **Arrested lesions** need no intervention apart from aesthetics; **active non-cavitated lesions** need non-restorative treatment (Fontana and Huysmans, 2019).
- Cutting a cavity in an intact lesion is "neither necessary nor ethical" (Mount et al., 2016).

## Management options

1. **Plaque control:** toothbrushing and interdental cleaning.
2. **Diet:** reduce the amount and frequency of sucrose; **saliva:** chewing to raise flow (Mount et al., 2016).
3. **Topical fluoride:** varnish (5% NaF, 22,600 ppm F) two to four times a year by risk, or every 3 months until activity is controlled; APF 1.23% or neutral 2% NaF gels (Amaechi et al., 2013).
4. **Calcium phosphate agents:** ACP-based rinses promote remineralisation (Amaechi et al., 2013).
5. **Fissure sealants:** for incipient enamel lesions not reaching the DEJ (Ferreira Zandoná and Ritter, 2019); sealed lesions do not progress (Amaechi et al., 2013).
6. **Resin infiltration:** etch with hydrochloric acid, dry with ethanol and air, apply infiltrant for 3 minutes, cure, then for 1 minute, cure; the resin blocks lesion pores as a diffusion barrier but is radiolucent (Amaechi et al., 2013).

## Monitoring and prognosis

Review at recall ("watchful waiting" with active prevention) using activity signs and standardised bitewings (Amaechi et al., 2013).

| Source | Expected outcome |
|---|---|
| Ferreira Zandoná and Ritter (2019); Singh (2020) | Reversible |
| Mount et al. (2016) | Even if not fully remineralised, the sealed surface acts as a natural, permanent restoration |
| Kidd and Fejerskov (2016) | Reversal is rare; most remain as a scar, and reversal is possible only while the lesion is active |

Arrest is always achievable; visible regression needs early treatment.

## Conclusion

The incipient lesion can be controlled without a drill: clean, dry detection, activity assessment and risk-based non-operative care preserve the intact surface.
