## 1. Definition and parts

### Explanation

The retainer has four prongs and two jaws joined by a bow (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. It anchors the dam to the most posterior isolated tooth (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and can also retract gingiva (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; the distal clamp has to resist the pull of the dam towards the frame (Borges et al, 2020) [SRC], and it usually sits on the distalmost exposed tooth (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. The bow must be springy enough to grip (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Posterior clamps have one bow; anterior ones have two (Borges et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** A metal retainer that anchors the rubber dam to the most posterior isolated tooth and can also retract gingiva (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Parts:** bow (spring), two jaws, four prongs (points), forceps holes, wings in winged clamps (Ritter et al, 2019; Borges et al, 2020).

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

Summitt's three basic types are winged, wingless and butterfly (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. A "W" prefix means wingless (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]; wings let the dam be attached before the clamp is seated (Summitt, 2013) [SRC] and give extra retraction (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Wingless clamps need less space (Kunzelmann, 2001) [SRC]; winged ones are bulkier (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and can interfere with matrices and wedges (Borges et al, 2020) [SRC].

By jaw design, Marzouk separates four-point contact blades (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], indicated when the tooth lacks retentive curvature (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] but risky for undermined tooth structure (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], from circumferential blades (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], which are less retentive and less traumatic (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Gingivally directed (inverted) prongs suit partly erupted teeth (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; No. 14A is an example (Kunzelmann, 2001) [SRC].

Retracting clamps: the 212 series are double-bowed and retract gingiva from Class V preparations (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; the 212 butterfly is a retractor only (Summitt, 2013) [SRC], may rock and damage the root (Summitt, 2013) [SRC], and is stabilised with compound (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]; 212R and 212L allow adjacent teeth to be restored together (Borges et al, 2020) [SRC]. The Schultz series is split to a single bow (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; cervical retracting clamps have movable jaws (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; some clamps carry cheek and tongue guards (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Asymmetric clamps such as 12A/13B have a larger buccal jaw (Kunzelmann, 2001) [SRC] for tapered maxillary molars (Kunzelmann, 2001) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**
1. **By wings:** winged (dam attached before placement, extra retraction) vs wingless "W" (less bulk) (Summitt, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
2. **By jaw contact:** four-point (retentive; for teeth lacking convexity) vs circumferential (less retentive, less traumatic) (Marzouk et al, undated).
3. **By prong direction:** horizontal vs gingivally inclined (14A, partly erupted teeth) (Kunzelmann, 2001).
4. **By function:** anchoring vs retracting (212 butterfly, 212R/212L, Schultz, cervical clamps with movable jaws) (Marzouk et al, undated; Borges et al, 2020).
5. **By symmetry:** symmetric vs asymmetric (12A/13B for tapered maxillary molars) (Kunzelmann, 2001).

## 3. Common clamps

### Explanation

Sturdevant's Table 8.1 suggests W56 for most molars (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and W27 for terminal mandibular molars with distal preparations (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's limits the kit to a few clamps (Summitt, 2013) [SRC], with supplements 00, W14A and W1A (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Kunzelmann's set is Ivory 7, 8, 8A, 14A for molars, 0, 1, 2 for premolars and 212 for anteriors (Kunzelmann, 2001) [SRC]. The UK letter system uses BW, JW, K and AW for molars (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC], AW for partly erupted crowns (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC], GW and EW for smaller teeth (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC] and the Ferrier clamp for anterior gingival retraction (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC]. Hu-Friedy-style 200-205 molar, 206-209 premolar and 210-211 incisor clamps appear in Modern Operative Dentistry (Borges et al, 2020) [SRC]. For children, No. 27 suits primary teeth (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and W14 young permanent teeth (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

## 4. Principles of selection and use

### Explanation

A properly selected clamp touches the four line angles (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], which stops rocking (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; only the jaw points should touch, giving four-point contact (Summitt, 2013) [SRC], because the dam, not the clamp, makes the seal (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Contact elsewhere on the jaw lets it rotate (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]; two contacts on one side and one on the other also lets it rotate (Kunzelmann, 2001) [SRC]. An overexpanded clamp grips weakly (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Jaws should not pass the line angles, or they block matrix and wedge (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The clamp is tied with floss (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] because the bow can fatigue and break (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], making fragments retrievable (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC]; once the dam is on, a dislodged clamp is either thrown out or trapped by the dam (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Rocking clamps can injure gingiva and cause soreness (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; poorly fitted clamps chip ceramic crowns (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principles** (Ritter et al, 2019; Summitt, 2013)
- Four-point contact at the line angles prevents rocking; the dam, not the clamp, seals.
- Jaws must not extend beyond line angles (interfere with matrix/wedge).
- Expand only enough to pass the height of contour; overexpanded clamps lose grip.
- Always tie with about 30 cm of floss: the bow can fatigue and fracture.
- Avoid placing on ceramic crowns or veneers (edge fracture) (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

## 5. Modification and forceps

### Explanation

Jaws can be recontoured with a stone (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; W8A jaws should be nearly horizontal (Summitt, 2013) [SRC] and, once trimmed, the points must be blunted (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. To bend a 212, Marzouk heats it red hot and works it with pliers (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Sturdevant's lets it bench-cool to regain hardness (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], whereas Modern Operative Dentistry quenches in water, bends, then tempers by reheating and quenching in oil (Borges et al, 2020) [SRC]. Forceps: Ivory forceps have stabilisers that stop rotation (Summitt, 2013) [SRC] and stops that stop the tips going too deep (Kunzelmann, 2001) [SRC]; Stokes forceps allow rotation for angled teeth (Summitt, 2013) [SRC]; Palmer and Brewer forceps are also used (Borges et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Modification:** grind jaws with a stone and blunt the points; bend 212 jaws after heating red hot (bench-cool, or temper in oil) (Ritter et al, 2019; Borges et al, 2020).

**Forceps:** Ivory (stabilisers), Stokes (allow rotation), Palmer, Brewer (Summitt, 2013; Borges et al, 2020).
