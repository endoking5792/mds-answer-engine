## STUDY MODE

### S1. What "adhesion" has to explain [basic science]
Most restoratives hold on by mechanical locking (composite into etched enamel and collagen). GIC is different: Summitt calls polyalkenoate cements the only dental materials whose primary adhesion to tooth is a true chemical bond (Hilton et al, 2013), although Craig lists zinc polycarboxylate, resin-modified GIC and self-adhesive resin cements as also capable of chemical interaction with hydroxyapatite (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Sturdevant 5th ed gives the mechanism a larger mechanical share: adhesion is achieved in part by mechanical retention and in part chemical chelation, and good mechanical bonding is much more important in most cases (Roberson et al, 2006). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed (adapted from the 7th ed) says instead that glass ionomer forms weak chemical (chelation) bonds that are predictable, effective and durable, and that chemical bonding to hydroxyapatite is an important part of the mechanism for materials that need no etching; the "mechanical bonding is much more important" statement is not covered in the 2nd South Asia edition (Ritter et al, 2019). Goldstein says glass ionomers bond to dental tissue through combined micromechanical and chemical mechanisms (Goldstein et al, 2018). The question therefore has three parts: how the bond forms, how to prepare the tooth so it forms, and how strong it really is.

### S2. How the bond forms [mechanism]
The polyacid in the freshly mixed cement is a high-molecular-weight chain with many carboxyl (COOH) groups. Hydroxyapatite is the mineral of enamel and dentine, and carries phosphate and calcium. The carboxyl groups displace surface phosphate ions, and each displaced phosphate takes a calcium ion with it, so an ion-enriched layer forms and is bound to enamel and dentine (Mount, 1994). As the cement sets, the mineral released buffers the acid and raises the pH, and calcium and phosphate re-precipitate at the interface as a calcium or strontium phosphate and polyalkenoate layer; this is "diffusion-based adhesion" (Mount et al, 2016). Wilson's SEM work shows this ion-exchange layer (Mount, 1994). Sikri describes the same mechanism and adds micromechanical self-etching as a second part (Sikri, 2020).

On dentine there is an extra route: hydrogen bonding or metal-ion bridging between carboxyl groups and collagen (Mount et al, 2016). Phillips adds that a short polyalkenoic acid pretreatment removes the smear layer and exposes collagen fibrils to a depth of about 0.5 to 1 micron, so interdiffusion gives a micromechanical component (Anusavice et al, 2013), and Summitt says the cement then acts as a self-etchant by hybridising the partly demineralised dentine (Hilton et al, 2013). McCabe lists two competing theories, chelation of calcium to give an interfacial calcium polyalkenoate salt (supported by stronger bonds to enamel than dentine), and dissolution and reprecipitation of apatite; he says the exact mechanism is unclear (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Nakabayashi and Pashley give the pKa of phosphoric acid as 2.12 and of polyacrylic acid as 4.25, so phosphoric acid has the higher potential to demineralise dentine, although total etching also depends on dissociation, concentration, contact time and buffering; they report that smear layers are 0.5 to 2.0 micrometres thick, that acidic cements including glass ionomer leave a slightly demineralised dentine subsurface without dissolving all smear plugs, and that such cements are "in a sense" self-etching (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).

### S3. Preparing the tooth [clinical decision-making]
- **Why condition at all.** A smear layer of debris, pellicle or polishing paste blocks the surface. Brief 10% polyacrylic acid dissolves the smear layer in 10-15 seconds (Mount, 1994). It is the cement's own acid, so residue does not interfere with setting; it improves wetting and pre-activates calcium and phosphate ions (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Time and strength.** Left longer than 20 seconds it is likely to demineralise dentine and open tubules (Mount, 1994); van Noort prefers 20% polyacrylic acid and warns that citric acid should not be used because it opens tubules and may compromise the bond (van Noort and Barbour, 2013), and McCabe found no improvement in bond strength from citric acid (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Sikri lists 50% citric acid, 25% tannic acid and 20% polyacrylic acid as suggested conditioners, and says polyacrylic acid is commonly applied for 10 to 20 seconds (Sikri, 2020).
- **Alternatives.** Causton's ITS (mineralising) solution or 25% tannic acid unites the smear layer with the tooth and seals the tubules (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016); useful before luting on vital teeth to avoid hydraulic pressure and sensitivity (Mount, 1994).
- **Never acid-etch** the tooth before GIC (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Clinical effect.** Conditioned restorations lasted longer, but the difference in longevity was not statistically significant in the evidence Bonsor quotes (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- **When not needed.** Under amalgam or gold, where chemical adhesion is not required, conditioning is unnecessary (Mount, 1994).

### S4. How strong is the bond? [debated]
Measured values are low and vary by source (see synthesis). The reason is that in most tests the cement fails inside itself, because its tensile strength is low (about 7 MPa in van Noort, about 12 MPa in McCabe, and only 2-4 MPa for luting cements in Mount), leaving the ion-exchange layer attached to the tooth (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount et al, 2016). That is why the cement's own strength limits its apparent adhesion, and why a stronger cement gives better adhesion provided the surface is clean (Mount, 1994).

### S5. Modified materials [classification]
- **Resin-modified GIC** bonds by the same chelation, with only a small micromechanical contribution because resin is a minor part of the mix (Mount et al, 2016). Summitt instead says RMGIs bond by an ionic bond plus mechanical interlocking with collagen and the resin monomer (Hilton et al, 2013), so the books differ on the micromechanical share. Fewer free carboxyl groups and polymerisation shrinkage stress limit the bond, while water uptake may allow the cement to readapt to the tooth (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); quoted bond strengths are similar to or slightly better than conventional GIC (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013), and conditioning and priming can raise the shear bond to 8-24 MPa (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Phillips says hybrid-layer formation is not conclusively shown (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Compomers and giomers:** restorative compomers have no useful intrinsic adhesion and must be used with a dentine bonding system (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Anusavice et al, 2013); powder-liquid compomer cements for luting are self-adhesive because the liquid contains water (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **GIC to composite** (sandwich): two interfaces, cement to dentine by ion exchange and cement to composite mechanically or chemically; auto-cure cement is etched 15 seconds with 37% phosphoric acid, while dual-cure retains resin bonds and does not need etching (Mount, 1994). Books disagree on timing: Bonsor says etching within 5 minutes removes the matrix in 15 seconds and works only after about 24 hours (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013), whereas Sikri advises a delay of about 20 minutes (2-5 minutes for fast-setting cement), 15-20 seconds of etching, and warns that more than 30 seconds degrades the cement (Sikri, 2020); Goldstein says it is not necessary to etch glass ionomer but some clinicians use a 5-second final etch over the base (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- **To metals:** GIC bonds to stainless steel and to tin-oxide-plated gold alloys (Craig, 2002).

### S6. Clinical meaning
Adhesion means no mechanical undercut is needed, erosion lesions need not be cut, and microleakage is reduced (Mount, 1994). Even with low measured values, retention in cervical erosion lesions has been better than with composite, with 75% retention at four years (Craig, 2002); Sturdevant 5th ed cautions that little clinical evidence shows GIC restorations to be better than composites (Roberson et al, 2006), whereas Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed quotes a 2014 review in which GIC-based materials had the lowest annual failure rate (2%) in non-carious cervical lesions and calls them still the standard for retention (Ritter et al, 2019).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

### Claim. What is the bond strength of GIC to dentine?
- **Textbook position.** Mount says the strength of the ion-exchange layer "has yet to be measured", because tests actually measure the cement's own tensile strength (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). Others give numbers (each book's value is shown, none is ranked): Craig 11th ed. reports a tensile bond to dentine of 1-3 MPa in the cements chapter and 2-3 MPa in the preventive-materials chapter (Craig, 2002); McCabe 1-3 MPa and about 5 MPa cohesive failure at enamel (McCabe and Walls, 2008); Bonsor about 5 MPa (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); van Noort 2-7 MPa shear, and tensile bond to dentine of 3.1 MPa untreated, 3.7 MPa after citric acid and 6.8 MPa after polyacrylic acid (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); Bhat about 12 MPa to enamel and about 1.23 MPa to dentine (Bhat et al, 2019); Sturdevant 5th ed macroshear 6-12 MPa against 22-35 MPa for modern dentine bonding agents (Roberson et al, 2006; these figures are not covered in the 2nd South Asia edition). Summitt says values equal one-step self-etching adhesives on dentine but are hard to interpret because of cohesive failure (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Retrieved evidence on conditioning (laboratory studies, abstracts only).** The effect of 10% polyacrylic acid on shear bond strength depended on the material and the substrate and was often not significant (Cai et al, 2021); the best conditioner depended on the type of cement, polyacrylic acid for self-cure high-viscosity GIC and phosphoric acid plus a resin-modified GIC adhesive for light-cure high-viscosity GIC, in a model of silver-diamine-fluoride-treated simulated carious dentine (Koh et al, 2024); for a resin-modified GIC bonded to caries-affected dentine, polyacrylic acid gave the highest micro-shear bond strength (14.26 MPa) and 37% phosphoric acid gave more microleakage (Alrefeai et al, 2022).
- **Interpretation and limit.** The sources disagree because they use different tests, and because the bond usually outlasts the cement, so the number reflects the cement. The statement that fits all the sources is a range of about 1-12 MPa depending on test, lower than modern adhesives, and not a true measure of the interface. [Likely] No clinical or microtensile journal study of conventional GIC was retrieved; the three laboratory abstracts above are the only journal evidence.
- **Conclusion.** Quote the range and the cohesive failure, not a single figure. Craig reports clinical retention in cervical erosion lesions better than composite despite the low number, while Sturdevant 5th ed doubts clinical superiority (Craig, 2002; Roberson et al, 2006) and Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed gives GIC-based materials the lowest annual failure rate in non-carious cervical lesions (Ritter et al, 2019). Mount et al say never acid-etch conventional GIC (Mount et al, 2016); Koh et al (2024) found the best conditioner for light-cure high-viscosity GIC differed, which is laboratory evidence only (abstract only).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 43 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Summitt describes glass ionomer cement as the only dental material whose main adhesion to tooth is a true chemical bond (Hilton et al, 2013). It bonds to enamel and dentine without acid etching, which gives retention without mechanical undercuts and reduces microleakage (Mount, 1994).

### 2. Mechanism of adhesion
- **Step 1, ion exchange.** Carboxyl groups of the polyacid displace phosphate ions from hydroxyapatite; each phosphate takes a calcium ion, forming an ion-enriched layer bound to tooth (Mount, 1994).
- **Step 2, diffusion layer.** As the cement sets, released mineral buffers the acid, and calcium or strontium phosphate and polyalkenoate re-precipitate at the interface; this is diffusion-based adhesion (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Step 3, dentine extras.** Hydrogen bonds or metal-ion bridges between carboxyl groups and collagen (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Two parts.** Micromechanical self-etching plus chemical ionic bonding (Sikri, 2020).
- **Failure.** Cohesive within the cement, leaving the ion-exchange layer on the tooth (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2A. Chemical bond compared with micromechanical bond
| Feature | Glass ionomer | Composite (adhesive system) |
|---|---|---|
| Main mechanism | chemical (ionic) bond with hydroxyapatite (Hilton et al, 2013) | micromechanical, hybrid layer (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| Tooth preparation | polyacrylic acid conditioning, 10 seconds (Mount et al, 2016) | acid etch and primer or self-etch (Sturdevant 5th ed; Roberson et al, 2006) |
| Bond to enamel | weaker than a modern composite bond, but performs well clinically (Mount et al, 2016) | highest |
| Moisture | needs a clean surface, no dehydration (Mount, 1994) | needs control of wet or dry dentine |

### 3. Tooth preparation (conditioning)
1. Clean with plain pumice slurry, since most polishing pastes leave a smear layer (Mount, 1994); Mount and Hume add that scrubbing is optional and may abrade the gingival margin (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds (Mount et al, 2016); Mount (1994) gives 10-15 seconds to remove the smear layer, and over 20 seconds demineralises and opens tubules (Mount, 1994). Sikri gives 20% polyacrylic acid for 10-20 seconds (Sikri, 2020).
3. Wash for 10 seconds; dry lightly without dehydrating (Mount et al, 2016).
4. Never acid-etch before GIC (Mount et al, 2016).
5. Alternatives: Causton's ITS solution or 25% tannic acid for 1-2 minutes (Mount et al, 2016); avoid citric acid (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
Reasons for polyacrylic acid: it is the cement's own acid, raises wettability, and pre-activates calcium and phosphate (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

### 3A. Protocols by situation
- **Class V erosion lesion:** scrub with plain pumice slurry for 5 seconds (optional in Mount et al, 2016), condition 10-15 seconds, wash, dry gently (Mount, 1994).
- **Luting on a vital tooth:** considerable hydraulic pressure is possible, so do not condition dentine with 10% polyacrylic acid; use a mineralising solution or 25% tannic acid (Mount, 1994); some patients have severe sensitivity on seating from hydraulic displacement of dentinal fluid, so seal tubules with a low-viscosity resin or a remineralising solution (Sikri, 2020).
- **Fissure sealant:** condition 10 seconds with 10% polyacrylic acid, wash with water only, dry lightly; this also removes biofilm (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Sandwich technique:** condition dentine, place cement, then etch auto-cure GIC for 15 seconds before composite (Mount, 1994).

### 3B. Ways to improve or extend adhesion
- New dentine bonding agents containing polyalkenoic acid (especially maleic acid) form a hybrid layer similar to the ion-exchange layer (Mount, 1994).
- A 2-5 micrometre layer of tin oxide on the fitting surface gives some adhesion to noble metals (Mount, 1994).
- A mineralising solution or tannic acid unites the smear layer with the tooth instead of removing it (Mount, 1994).

### 4. Bond strength
| Source | Value |
|---|---|
| Craig 11th ed. | tensile to dentine 1-3 MPa (cements chapter) or 2-3 MPa (preventive chapter) (Craig, 2002) |
| McCabe | dentine 1-3 MPa; enamel cohesive failure about 5 MPa (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Bonsor | about 5 MPa (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) |
| van Noort | shear 2-7 MPa; tensile to dentine 3.1 (untreated), 3.7 (citric), 6.8 (polyacrylic acid) MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Sturdevant 5th ed | macroshear 6-12 MPa (modern bonding agents 22-35 MPa) (Roberson et al, 2006); not covered in 2nd South Asia ed |
Mount: the strength of the union has not yet been measured, because failure is cohesive within the cement (Mount et al, 2016). The values differ because the tests differ.

### 5. Factors affecting adhesion
- Clean surface free of saliva, pellicle, plaque and blood (Mount, 1994).
- Conditioning and its time (Mount, 1994).
- Moisture control: dehydration and early water contact damage the cement (Sikri, 2020).
- Cement strength: a stronger cement gives better apparent adhesion (Mount, 1994).
- Placing the mix while it is still glossy: the gloss shows free polyacid for bonding, and a dull mix has reacted too much for good bonding (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Type of cement: metal-filled cements show less ion-exchange adhesion (Mount, 1994).

### 5A. The interface after the bond forms
- The ion-exchange layer seals the dentinal tubules and prevents microleakage (Mount et al, 2016).
- Polyacrylic acid is mild and of high molecular weight, so it does not penetrate tubules; after 10 seconds tubule plugs remain and limit dentinal fluid flow (Mount et al, 2016).
- With a dual-cure cement, separation occurred inside dentine or enamel and not at the ion-exchange layer, presumably because the cement was stronger in tension; this suggests the weakest point is normally the cement, not the interface (Mount, 1994).
- When a restoration fractures, the cement often leaves an acid-resistant surface layer on the tooth (Sikri, 2020).
- Adhesion reduces marginal leakage, so harmful micro-organisms cannot enter the tooth-restoration interface (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Adhesion of modified materials
- **RMGIC:** same chelation; micromechanical share minor (Mount et al, 2016) or substantial (Summitt: ionic bond plus interlocking with collagen and resin) (Hilton et al, 2013); limited by fewer free carboxyl groups and polymerisation shrinkage (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- **Compomer and giomer:** restorative types need a bonding agent (McCabe and Walls, 2008); luting compomers are self-adhesive (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **To composite in a sandwich:** etch auto-cure GIC 15 seconds with 37% phosphoric acid; dual-cure needs no etching (Mount, 1994).
- **To metal:** stainless steel, tin-oxide-plated gold (Craig, 2002).

### 7. Clinical significance
- No cavity undercuts, minimal preparation of erosion lesions (Mount, 1994).
- Retention in cervical erosion lesions better than composite despite low bond values (Craig, 2002); Sturdevant 5th ed doubts clinical superiority overall (Roberson et al, 2006); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed: lowest annual failure rate, 2% (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Seals dentine and reduces microleakage (Mount et al, 2016).
- Luting GIC improves seal but not retention; retention must come from the preparation (Mount et al, 2016).

### 8. Conclusion
GIC bonds by ion exchange and diffusion, with conditioning by 10% polyacrylic acid. Measured values are low (about 1-12 MPa by test) because failure is within the cement, but clinical retention is good. The practical rules are a clean surface, brief conditioning, no etching, no dehydration, and immediate protection of the setting cement.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Writing "acid-etch with phosphoric acid before GIC" without saying that Mount et al advise against it (Mount et al, 2016) while Phillips lists 34-37% phosphoric acid among conditioners (Anusavice et al, 2013).
2. Giving one bond strength value without saying the tests differ and failure is cohesive.
3. Describing the bond as only "mechanical" or only "chemical" without attribution: Summitt gives a true chemical bond as the primary adhesion (Hilton et al, 2013), while Sturdevant 5th ed gives part mechanical retention and part chelation (Roberson et al, 2006), Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed gives weak chelation bonds that are predictable and durable (Ritter et al, 2019) and Goldstein gives combined micromechanical and chemical bonding (Goldstein et al, 2018).
4. Forgetting that restorative compomers and giomers do not bond without an adhesive.
