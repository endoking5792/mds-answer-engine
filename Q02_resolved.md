## STUDY MODE

### S1. How to think about the question
"Role" means: what jobs does this material do that others do worse, and where should it not be used? GIC does five jobs: it **restores** (where load is low or moisture control is poor), **seals** (dentine sealant, liner, base, fissure sealant), **lutes** (cementation), **builds** (transitional restorations, limited cores, undercut block-out) and **prevents** (fluoride reservoir for high-risk patients). Its limits are its brittleness, early water sensitivity and wear. Every use below follows from two properties: chemical adhesion to tooth and fluoride release and recharge (Sikri, 2020; Mount, 1994).

### S2. The role by situation [clinical decision-making]
- **Cervical lesions (Class V, erosion, abrasion, abfraction).** The ion-exchange bond and the cement's flexibility let it survive tooth flexure at the neck of the tooth, and the lesion needs little or no preparation (Sikri, 2020). Summitt notes one study with 80% retention at 10 years with no mechanical retention (Hilton et al, 2013), and a trial in which 96.4% of resin-modified GIC restorations were retained at 5 years against 51.5% for bonded composite (Hilton et al, 2013). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed quotes a 2014 review in which GIC-based materials had the lowest annual failure rate (2%) and calls them still the standard for retention (Ritter et al, 2019), whereas Mosquim et al say resin materials resist toothbrush abrasion and erosion better, and that recommendation of ionomers has declined because of roughness and colour change (Mosquim et al, 2024).
- **Root caries and the high-risk or xerostomic patient.** Summitt calls GIC the material of choice for most root caries lesions, because it bonds, releases fluoride for a long time and can be recharged from toothpaste or mouthrinse (Hilton et al, 2013). Fluoride-releasing materials are described as essential in the high-caries-risk patient (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Transitional and stabilising restorations.** GIC or resin-modified GIC strengthens the remaining tooth and removes the carious biomass that acts as a bacterial reservoir in rampant caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); ART and the interim therapeutic restoration use the same idea, with maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013); the interim therapeutic restoration is applicable mainly to single-surface or small two-surface cavities, where success is greatest (Hilton et al, 2013). Mount calls GIC the material of choice for transitional restorations, as it is relatively insoluble and strong enough for reasonable occlusal load (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Liner and base.** Mount says GIC gives the most effective long-term seal to dentine and is best used as the primary dentine sealant against microleakage (Mount, 1994). Over calcium hydroxide or MTA it gives an acid-resistant, adhesive covering and a fluoride source in case of leakage (Hilton et al, 2013). A liner is a layer 0.2-1 mm thick and a base is more than 1 mm (Torres, 2020); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed limits a liner to 0.5 mm and uses a light-cured resin-modified GIC base only at the deep site where remaining dentine is 0.5-2 mm (given for amalgam), covering any calcium hydroxide with it (Ritter et al, 2019). Under composite, a very deep floor with calcium hydroxide over the pink area is covered by a thin coat of GIC, which stops the etchant dissolving the calcium hydroxide (Torres, 2020).
- **Primary teeth.** Many deciduous teeth can be restored with glass ionomer alone (Mount et al, 2016); however, Mount names stainless steel crowns as the restoration of choice for carious or hypoplastic primary molars in young children and lists GIC or composite for small cavities (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Luting.** Crowns, bridges and orthodontic bands: low solubility, low film thickness, adhesion and fluoride; but its tensile strength is too low to add retention, so retention must come from the preparation (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Fissure sealing and prevention.** GIC sealants bond to enamel by ion exchange and are more technique-tolerant; some evidence suggests better caries protection than resin sealants, but retention was poor in some studies and most publications still name a resin-based sealant as first choice (Mount et al, 2016). Neither material flows into a fissure narrower than about 200 micrometres, so retention depends on enamel adhesion at the entrance (Mount, 1994). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed calls the efficacy of GI for pit-and-fissure sealants questionable on clinical trials and properties (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Special uses.** Sandwich restorations under composite or amalgam (Sikri, 2020); undercut block-out for inlays (Hilton et al, 2013); repair and endodontic sealing (Sikri, 2020), although resin-modified GIC should not be used to repair a root perforation (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); Sikri also lists dentine substitute, splinting of periodontally weak teeth and glazing of old GIC fillings (Sikri, 2020). A 1 mm GI barrier over the remaining gutta-percha, or a 1-2 mm intracoronal barrier, reduced coronal leakage in laboratory studies (Barsness and Roach, 2016). In xerostomia or Sjogren syndrome, Mount confines GIC to replacing lost dentine and laminates a resin-based material over it (Mount et al, 2016).

### S3. The limits [treatment decision]
- **Load and wear.** Conventional GIC and resin-modified GIC should not be used in occlusal load-bearing areas of permanent teeth (Hilton et al, 2013), and Sikri likewise says composites and glass ionomers are not preferred in stress-bearing areas (Sikri, 2020); one wear measurement was 73 micrometres in a year for GIC against under 10 for composite (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Cores.** GIC is not strong enough for a core unless two intact walls and 1-2 mm of sound coronal tooth remain (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says reinforced GIs (Equia, Fuji IX) should not be used for core build-ups (Ritter et al, 2019), Goldstein lists conventional GI only for blocking out undercuts and resin-modified GI for a partial core with adequate tooth structure (Goldstein et al, 2018), and Badr et al quote a fatigue test where all GIC core crowns failed at 20,000 cycles (Badr et al, 2016); resin-modified GIC cores or bases should not be used under all-ceramic restorations because hygroscopic expansion could fracture the ceramic (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011), and acid-based cements including glass polyalkenoate are best avoided as luting agents under porcelain (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011). Craig lists conventional GIC for cementing high-strength ceramic crowns and notes some manufacturers advise against resin-modified GIC luting under low-strength silica-based ceramics (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- **Lifespan.** The Pickard Manual gives often-quoted, approximate average replacement ages of 10-15 years for amalgam, 5-8 for composite and 3-5 for GIC, saying a precise figure is impossible and that not all GICs fail after five years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- **Aesthetics.** Opaque and dull with time; resin-modified or light-cure grades are better (Sikri, 2020).
- **Technique.** Moisture control, immediate sealing and delayed finishing decide success (see Q1 and Q15).

### S4. Choosing GIC against composite or amalgam
Choose GIC when the lesion is small to moderate, low-load, near the gingival margin or on the root, when moisture control is uncertain, or when caries risk is high. Choose composite or amalgam when the load is high, the restoration is large, or aesthetics and polish matter most. A layered (sandwich) restoration combines the two, but see Q14 for its evidence.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

### Claim. Can GIC serve as a permanent restoration in load-bearing posterior teeth?
- **Textbook position.** Summitt and Mount restrict it to areas without heavy occlusal load (Hilton et al, 2013; Mount et al, 2016); Pickard gives a shorter lifespan than composite or amalgam (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- **Retrieved evidence.** A systematic review of 14 RCTs (only 2 at low risk of bias) found ART with high-viscosity GIC did not differ significantly from conventional restorations in primary teeth and in single-surface permanent cavities, and found no significant difference for multi-surface permanent restorations either way (Frencken et al, 2021).
- **Reviews that differ.** Ruengrungsom et al (2018) report better survival for the conventional technique than for ART in single-surface occlusal and multi-surface GIC restorations, while Frencken et al (2021) report no significant difference in single-surface cavities and primary molars at most time points and a significant difference favouring ART/HVGIC at 4.3 and 6.3 years. Panetta et al (2024) graded the evidence conflicting and the medium and long-term performance questionable. All abstract only.
- **Interpretation and limit.** The textbooks restrict GIC in load-bearing areas (Hilton et al, 2013; Mount et al, 2016); the reviews above reach different results for single-surface cavities and primary molars, and are reported side by side without choosing between them. Abstract-level reading only; most trials were small. [Likely]
- **Caries-prevention caveat.** A systematic review quoted by Sturdevant 5th ed found no conclusive evidence for or against GIC inhibiting secondary caries (Roberson et al, 2006), which is not covered in the 2nd South Asia edition; that edition instead says the fluoride burst is complete within 24 h, that protection needs fluoride exposure about three times a day, that a luting film, base or liner is unlikely to take up enough fluoride, and that only restorative GI have legitimate potential to prevent secondary caries (Ritter et al, 2019), while Goldstein says the anticariogenic effect promoted for fluoride-releasing GI luting cement has not been documented clinically and that post-cementation sensitivity is more likely due to dentine desiccation or contamination than to the cement (Goldstein et al, 2018); in primary teeth a meta-analysis quoted by Hill and Gillam found high-viscosity GIC more effective than silver diamine fluoride at 12 months, but the difference was not statistically significant (Oliveira et al, 2019, quoted in Hill and Gillam, 2024). Both are textbook-quoted summaries.
- **Conclusion.** The textbooks place GIC in low-load, high-risk or single-surface situations (Hilton et al, 2013; Mount et al, 2016); the reviews above differ on survival against conventional restorations, so state each with its author.

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement (glass polyalkenoate cement) is a water-based cement formed by reaction between ion-leachable fluoroaluminosilicate glass and polyalkenoic acid, first reported by Wilson and Kent in 1972 (Mount et al, 2016; 1971 in Sikri's Ch.1, Sikri, 2020), first product ASPA 1976 (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Its two defining properties are chemical adhesion to enamel and dentine and fluoride release with recharge (Sikri, 2020). These give it a role in restoring, sealing, luting, building and preventing.

### 1A. Why GIC suits these roles (the two properties)
- **Adhesion:** polyacrylic acid releases phosphate, which takes up calcium from the tooth to form an ion-exchange layer; bonding to dentine also involves hydrogen bonds and metal-ion bridges to collagen; failure is cohesive, within the cement (Sikri, 2020).
- **Fluoride:** high release in the first week, falling over months to a constant low level; Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed: burst complete in 24 h (Ritter et al, 2019); the cement takes up fluoride from toothpaste and rinses and releases it again, acting as a reservoir (Sikri, 2020).
- **Also useful:** thermal expansion close to dentine and acceptable biocompatibility, because the large polyacid molecules do not travel through dentinal tubules (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Role as a restorative material
- **Class V, erosion, abrasion, abfraction:** ion-exchange bond and flexibility; little or no preparation (Sikri, 2020); 80% retention at 10 years in one study (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Class III:** resin-modified (light-cure) GIC for aesthetics; hidden lesions with auto-cure cement, which releases more fluoride (Sikri, 2020).
- **Root caries and high-risk patients:** material of choice, as it bonds, releases fluoride and recharges (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Class I and II:** only small, low-load cavities, or as part of a sandwich (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- **Primary teeth:** GIC alone is often enough for small cavities, but stainless steel crowns are Mount's choice for carious or hypoplastic primary molars in young children (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2A. Which type of GIC for which role (Wilson and McLean)
| Role | Type | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Luting crowns, bridges, bands | Type I | low powder:liquid ratio for flow (Sikri, 2020) |
| Aesthetic restoration (Class III, V) | Type II.1 | light-cure or dual-cure for translucency (Sikri, 2020) |
| Posterior, ART, transitional | Type II.2 / high-viscosity | strongest, auto-cure, high powder:liquid ratio (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Liner, base, dentine substitute | Type III | weak lining grades cannot support composite (Sikri, 2020) |

### 3. Role as a liner, base and dentine substitute
- Gives the most effective long-term seal to dentine (Mount, 1994).
- Over calcium hydroxide or MTA after pulp capping or indirect pulp capping: adhesion, acid resistance, fluoride source (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Blocks out undercuts for inlays and onlays (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Type III lining cement is too weak as a base for composite lamination (Sikri, 2020).

### 4. Role as a luting cement
Crowns, bridges, orthodontic appliances; low solubility, thixotropic flow, low film thickness, adhesion, fluoride; retention must come from preparation (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016). Seal dentine tubules first to avoid post-cementation sensitivity (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Role in minimal intervention and prevention
- ART and interim therapeutic restoration: hand excavation and GIC; must be followed by maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Transitional restoration in rampant caries; reduces the bacterial reservoir (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- Fissure sealant, especially in partially erupted molars; most publications still name resin the first-choice sealant (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013).
- Fluoride reservoir for high-risk and xerostomic patients (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 5A. Role in special groups
- **Children:** small cavities and enamel defects restored with GIC or composite; hypoplastic first permanent molars can be held with GIC while they erupt, then crowned (Mount et al, 2016); Class I to VI and nursing-bottle caries are listed uses (Sikri, 2020).
- **Elderly and xerostomic patients:** root and cervical lesions need a material that bonds and releases fluoride; resin-modified GIC and compomer are recommended aesthetic choices for Class V in high-risk patients (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Orthodontic patients:** bands cemented with GIC show better retention and less demineralisation than zinc phosphate (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Endodontics and core:** 1-2 mm GI barrier over root filling (Barsness and Roach, 2016); reinforced GI not for cores (Ritter et al, 2019); coronal seal over gutta-percha before non-vital bleaching, and a GIC dentine-replacement layer under a composite cap in root-filled teeth (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011; Alani and Patel, 2025).

### 6. Role as a bilayered (sandwich) and repair material
GIC base with composite cap; preferred base in Class II with subgingival cervical extension (Sikri, 2020). Repair of defects, perforation and resorption cavities (Sikri, 2020); Bonsor and Pearson advise against resin-modified GIC for root perforation (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### 7. Limitations
- Not for heavy occlusal load (Hilton et al, 2013); wear 73 micrometres a year against under 10 for composite in one measurement (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Not a core unless two walls and a 1-2 mm ferrule remain (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011); no resin-modified GIC core or base under all-ceramic, because of hygroscopic expansion (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011).
- Often-quoted, approximate average replacement age 3-5 years against 5-8 for composite and 10-15 for amalgam (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- Opaque, moisture-sensitive in the first 24 hours (Sikri, 2020).

### 7A. GIC compared with composite and amalgam
| Feature | GIC | Composite | Amalgam |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adhesion | chemical (ion exchange), polyacrylic acid conditioning instead of etching (Sikri, 2020) | micromechanical, needs bonding system | none (retention form) |
| Fluoride | release and recharge | very low, in fluoride-releasing types only (Hilton et al, 2013) | none |
| Strength and wear | low, wear 73 micrometres a year in one measure (Hilton et al, 2013) | higher | highest |
| Moisture tolerance | needs control (moisture-sensitive early), though less technique-sensitive near the gingiva than composite (Sikri, 2020) | very sensitive | tolerant |
| Aesthetics | opaque, dulls | best | none |
| Average replacement age (often-quoted, approximate) | 3-5 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) | 5-8 years | 10-15 years |

### 7B. Technique points that decide the role
Isolate (rubber dam or cord) (Sikri, 2020); condition with polyacrylic acid, 10% for 10 seconds (Mount et al, 2016) to 20% for 10-20 seconds (Sikri, 2020); place under a matrix, avoiding voids (Sikri, 2020); seal at once with resin, because varnish and petroleum jelly wash away and an unprotected surface cracks (Sikri, 2020), Mount prefers a very low-viscosity unfilled resin as varnish turns porous (Mount, 1994); finish conventional GIC after about 24 hours (Sikri, 2020; Sturdevant 5th ed, Roberson et al, 2006), Type II.2 shortly after matrix removal (Mount, 1994), resin-modified GIC at once after curing (Sturdevant 5th ed, Roberson et al, 2006); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed: most current GICs and RMGIs may be finished immediately (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 8. Present evidence
For single-surface cavities and primary molars, ART with high-viscosity GIC does not differ significantly from conventional restorations up to 5 years (Frencken et al, 2021), whereas Ruengrungsom et al (2018) report better conventional survival and Panetta et al (2024) grade the evidence conflicting.

### 9. Conclusion
GIC is a bioactive, adhesive, fluoride-recharging material best used where those properties matter and load is low: cervical and root lesions, high-risk patients, transitional restorations, liners and bases, luting and sealing. It complements, and does not replace, composite and amalgam.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Five roles: restore, seal (liner/base/sealant), lute, build (transitional/limited core), prevent (fluoride).
- Material of choice: root caries and indirect pulp capping cover (Hilton et al, 2013); cervical lesions and transitional restorations (Mount et al, 2016).
- Not for: heavy occlusal load (Hilton et al, 2013), cores without two walls, resin-modified GIC cores or bases under all-ceramic (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011).
- Often-quoted, approximate replacement age: GIC 3-5, composite 5-8, amalgam 10-15 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- ART and ITR need maintenance care to work (Hilton et al, 2013).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing uses without saying why GIC suits each one (adhesion, fluoride, low load).
2. Claiming GIC is suitable for all posterior restorations.
3. Writing "GIC bonds, so it needs acid etching" instead of polyacrylic acid conditioning (10% in Mount et al, 2016; 20% in Sikri, 2020).
4. Forgetting the limits and the replacement-age data.
5. Calling resin-modified GIC simply "light-cured GIC": it sets partly by the acid-base reaction and partly by photo and chemical polymerisation (Mount et al, 2016).
