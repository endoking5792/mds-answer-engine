## STUDY MODE

### S1. How to think about the question
"Role" means: what jobs does this material do that others do worse, and where should it not be used? GIC does five jobs: it **restores** (where load is low or moisture control is poor), **seals** (dentine sealant, liner, base, fissure sealant), **lutes** (cementation), **builds** (transitional restorations, limited cores, undercut block-out) and **prevents** (fluoride reservoir for high-risk patients). Its limits are its brittleness, early water sensitivity and wear. Every use below follows from two properties: chemical adhesion to tooth and fluoride release and recharge (Sikri, 2020; Mount, 1994).

### S2. The role by situation [clinical decision-making]
- **Cervical lesions (Class V, erosion, abrasion, abfraction).** The ion-exchange bond and the cement's flexibility let it survive tooth flexure at the neck of the tooth, and the lesion needs little or no preparation (Sikri, 2020). Summitt notes one study with 80% retention at 10 years with no mechanical retention (Hilton et al, 2013), and a trial in which 96.4% of resin-modified GIC restorations were retained at 5 years against 51.5% for bonded composite (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Root caries and the high-risk or xerostomic patient.** Summitt calls GIC the material of choice for most root caries lesions, because it bonds, releases fluoride for a long time and can be recharged from toothpaste or mouthrinse (Hilton et al, 2013). Fluoride-releasing materials are described as essential in the high-caries-risk patient (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Transitional and stabilising restorations.** GIC or resin-modified GIC strengthens the remaining tooth and removes the carious biomass that acts as a bacterial reservoir in rampant caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); ART and the interim therapeutic restoration use the same idea, with maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013). Mount calls GIC the material of choice for transitional restorations, as it is relatively insoluble and strong enough for reasonable occlusal load (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Liner and base.** Mount says GIC gives the most effective long-term seal to dentine and is best used as the primary dentine sealant against microleakage (Mount, 1994). Over calcium hydroxide or MTA it gives an acid-resistant, adhesive covering and a fluoride source in case of leakage (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Primary teeth.** Many deciduous teeth can be restored with glass ionomer alone (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Luting.** Crowns, bridges and orthodontic bands: low solubility, low film thickness, adhesion and fluoride; but its tensile strength is too low to add retention, so retention must come from the preparation (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Fissure sealing and prevention.** GIC sealants bond to enamel by ion exchange and are more technique-tolerant; some evidence suggests better caries protection than resin sealants, but retention is poorer (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Special uses.** Sandwich restorations under composite or amalgam (Sikri, 2020); undercut block-out for inlays (Hilton et al, 2013); repair and endodontic sealing (Sikri, 2020).

### S3. The limits [treatment decision]
- **Load and wear.** Conventional GIC and resin-modified GIC should not be used in occlusal load-bearing areas of permanent teeth (Hilton et al, 2013); one wear measurement was 73 micrometres in a year for GIC against under 10 for composite (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Cores.** GIC is not strong enough for a core unless two intact walls and 1-2 mm of sound coronal tooth remain (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011); it is a poor base under all-ceramic restorations (low strength, and RMGIC expands) (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011).
- **Lifespan.** The Pickard Manual gives average replacement ages of 10-15 years for amalgam, 5-8 for composite and 3-5 for GIC, while saying not all GICs fail after five years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- **Aesthetics.** Opaque and dull with time; resin-modified or light-cure grades are better (Sikri, 2020).
- **Technique.** Moisture control, immediate sealing and delayed finishing decide success (see Q1 and Q15).

### S4. Choosing GIC against composite or amalgam
Choose GIC when the lesion is small to moderate, low-load, near the gingival margin or on the root, when moisture control is uncertain, or when caries risk is high. Choose composite or amalgam when the load is high, the restoration is large, or aesthetics and polish matter most. A layered (sandwich) restoration combines the two, but see Q14 for its evidence.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

### Claim. Can GIC serve as a permanent restoration in load-bearing posterior teeth?
- **Textbook position.** Summitt and Mount restrict it to areas without heavy occlusal load (Hilton et al, 2013; Mount et al, 2016); Pickard gives a shorter lifespan than composite or amalgam (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- **Retrieved evidence.** A systematic review of 14 RCTs (only 2 at low risk of bias) found ART with high-viscosity GIC did not differ significantly from conventional restorations in primary teeth and in single-surface permanent cavities, and found no significant difference for multi-surface permanent restorations either way (Frencken et al, 2021).
- **Interpretation and limit.** The textbooks' caution fits multi-surface permanent teeth; for single-surface cavities and primary molars newer high-viscosity materials do acceptably. Abstract-level reading only; most trials were small. [Likely]
- **Conclusion.** Use GIC for low-load, single-surface or high-risk situations; do not promise composite-like survival in large permanent posterior restorations.

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 43 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement (glass polyalkenoate cement) is a water-based cement formed by reaction between ion-leachable fluoroaluminosilicate glass and polyalkenoic acid, developed by Wilson and Kent in the early 1970s (Sikri, 2020). Its two defining properties are chemical adhesion to enamel and dentine and fluoride release with recharge (Sikri, 2020). These give it a role in restoring, sealing, luting, building and preventing.

### 1A. Why GIC suits these roles (the two properties)
- **Adhesion:** polyacrylic acid releases phosphate, which takes up calcium from the tooth to form an ion-exchange layer; bonding to dentine also involves hydrogen bonds and metal-ion bridges to collagen; failure is cohesive, within the cement (Sikri, 2020).
- **Fluoride:** high release in the first week, falling over months to a constant low level; the cement takes up fluoride from toothpaste and rinses and releases it again, acting as a reservoir (Sikri, 2020).
- **Also useful:** thermal expansion close to dentine and acceptable biocompatibility, because the large polyacid molecules do not travel through dentinal tubules (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Role as a restorative material
- **Class V, erosion, abrasion, abfraction:** ion-exchange bond and flexibility; little or no preparation (Sikri, 2020); 80% retention at 10 years in one study (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Class III:** light-cure GIC for aesthetics; hidden lesions with conventional cement (Sikri, 2020).
- **Root caries and high-risk patients:** material of choice, as it bonds, releases fluoride and recharges (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Class I and II:** only small, low-load cavities, or as part of a sandwich (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- **Primary teeth:** often GIC alone is enough (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2A. Which type of GIC for which role (Wilson and McLean)
| Role | Type | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Luting crowns, bridges, bands | Type I | low powder:liquid ratio for flow (Sikri, 2020) |
| Aesthetic restoration (Class III, V) | Type II.1 | light-cure or dual-cure for translucency (Sikri, 2020) |
| Posterior, ART, transitional | Type II.2 / high-viscosity | strongest, auto-cure, high powder:liquid ratio (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Liner, base, dentine substitute | Type III | weak lining grades cannot support composite (Sikri, 2020) |

### 3. Role as a liner, base and dentine substitute
- Gives the most effective long-term seal to dentine (Mount, 1994).
- Over calcium hydroxide or MTA after pulp capping or indirect pulp capping: adhesion, acid resistance, fluoride source (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Blocks out undercuts for inlays and onlays (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Type III lining cement is too weak as a base for composite lamination (Sikri, 2020).

### 4. Role as a luting cement
Crowns, bridges, orthodontic appliances; low solubility, thixotropic flow, low film thickness, adhesion, fluoride; retention must come from preparation (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016). Seal dentine tubules first to avoid post-cementation sensitivity (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Role in minimal intervention and prevention
- ART and interim therapeutic restoration: hand excavation and GIC; must be followed by maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Transitional restoration in rampant caries; reduces the bacterial reservoir (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- Fissure sealant, especially in partially erupted molars (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013).
- Fluoride reservoir for high-risk and xerostomic patients (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 5A. Role in special groups
- **Children:** small cavities and enamel defects restored with GIC or composite; hypoplastic first permanent molars can be held with GIC while they erupt, then crowned (Mount et al, 2016); Class I to VI and nursing-bottle caries are listed uses (Sikri, 2020).
- **Elderly and xerostomic patients:** root and cervical lesions need a material that bonds and releases fluoride; resin-modified GIC and compomer are recommended aesthetic choices for Class V in high-risk patients (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Orthodontic patients:** bands cemented with GIC show better retention and less demineralisation than zinc phosphate (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Endodontics and core:** coronal seal over gutta-percha before non-vital bleaching, and a GIC dentine-replacement layer under a composite cap in root-filled teeth (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011; Alani and Patel, 2025).

### 6. Role as a bilayered (sandwich) and repair material
GIC base with composite cap; preferred base in Class II with subgingival cervical extension (Sikri, 2020). Repair of defects, perforation and resorption cavities (Sikri, 2020).

### 7. Limitations
- Not for heavy occlusal load (Hilton et al, 2013); wear 73 micrometres a year against under 10 for composite in one measurement (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Not a core unless two walls and a 1-2 mm ferrule remain (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011); avoid under all-ceramic (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011).
- Average replacement age 3-5 years against 5-8 for composite and 10-15 for amalgam (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- Opaque, moisture-sensitive in the first 24 hours (Sikri, 2020).

### 7A. GIC compared with composite and amalgam
| Feature | GIC | Composite | Amalgam |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adhesion | chemical, no etching | micromechanical, needs bonding system | none (retention form) |
| Fluoride | release and recharge | none | none |
| Strength and wear | low, wear 73 micrometres a year in one measure (Hilton et al, 2013) | higher | highest |
| Moisture tolerance | needs control, but less technique-sensitive near the gingiva (Sikri, 2020) | very sensitive | tolerant |
| Aesthetics | opaque, dulls | best | none |
| Average replacement age | 3-5 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) | 5-8 years | 10-15 years |

### 7B. Technique points that decide the role
Isolate (rubber dam or cord) (Sikri, 2020); condition with 20% polyacrylic acid 10-20 seconds (Sikri, 2020); place under a matrix, avoiding voids (Sikri, 2020); seal at once with resin, because varnish and petroleum jelly wash away and an unprotected surface cracks (Sikri, 2020); contour and polish after 24 hours in moist conditions (Sikri, 2020).

### 8. Present evidence
For single-surface cavities and primary molars, ART with high-viscosity GIC does not differ significantly from conventional restorations up to 5 years; evidence for multi-surface permanent restorations is not settled (Frencken et al, 2021).

### 9. Conclusion
GIC is a bioactive, adhesive, fluoride-recharging material best used where those properties matter and load is low: cervical and root lesions, high-risk patients, transitional restorations, liners and bases, luting and sealing. It complements, and does not replace, composite and amalgam.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Five roles: restore, seal (liner/base/sealant), lute, build (transitional/limited core), prevent (fluoride).
- Material of choice: root caries, cervical lesions, transitional restorations, indirect pulp capping cover (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Not for: heavy occlusal load, cores without two walls, under all-ceramic (Hilton et al, 2013; Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011).
- Average replacement age: GIC 3-5, composite 5-8, amalgam 10-15 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- ART and ITR need maintenance care to work (Hilton et al, 2013).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing uses without saying why GIC suits each one (adhesion, fluoride, low load).
2. Claiming GIC is suitable for all posterior restorations.
3. Writing "GIC bonds, so it needs acid etching" instead of 10% polyacrylic acid conditioning.
4. Forgetting the limits and the replacement-age data.
