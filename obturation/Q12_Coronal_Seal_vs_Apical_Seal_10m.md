## STUDY MODE

### Apical seal
- **Aim:** prevent percolation of periapical fluid into the canal and entomb residual bacteria (Azim and Aksel, 2022; Sikri, 2019).
- **Location and extent:** the root filling should end at the dentinocemental junction, about 0.5 mm (range 0.2-2.5 mm) from the radiographic apex; the filling should lie 0-2 mm from the radiographic apex for the best outcome (Azim and Aksel, 2022). Success falls by 12-14% for every millimetre of working length lost (Azim and Aksel, 2022).
- **Components:** core plus sealer; sealer mainly provides the seal in the apical area (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). At least 5 mm of apical gutta-percha was needed for a seal in one study (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- **Failure:** short, void-filled or overextended fillings leave unfilled infected areas or irritate the periodontal ligament (Azim and Aksel, 2022). Radiographic appearance does not correlate well with leakage (Berman et al, 2021).

### Coronal seal
- **Aim:** prevent saliva and bacteria diffusing down a gutta-percha filling to the periapex (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- **Evidence:** coronal leakage can happen within a short time through seemingly well-obturated canals; Cohen's states research has shifted from the apical to the coronal seal (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Cavit/Super EBA at 3.5 mm** reduced bacterial leakage by 85% and 65% (45 days); a bonded lining over the chamber floor gave no leakage at 60 days (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Practice:** place the permanent restoration soon after obturation, under rubber dam, with no gap between filling and obturation; remove gutta-percha and sealer from the clinical crown to avoid discolouration (Azim and Aksel, 2022; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- A retreatment rule of re-doing treatment if not restored in 3 months came from in vitro leakage; its clinical significance has been challenged (Berman et al, 2021).

### Comparison
| Feature | Apical seal | Coronal seal |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Block periapical fluids; entomb bacteria | Block saliva and bacteria from mouth |
| Provided by | Core + sealer within 0-2 mm of apex | Sealer-gutta-percha plus orifice barrier and permanent restoration |
| Evidence | Outcome falls with short or long fills (12-14% per mm) | Meta-analysis: poor RCT and poor coronal restoration have similar odds of impairing healing (Berman et al, 2021) |
| Control by clinician | Technique, length, sealer | Timing and quality of restoration |
| Most controllable failure | Partly | "Probably the most common, but best controllable" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |

### Evidence conflict
Berman et al (2021): type of coronal restoration had no significant influence on healing if good quality, yet cast restorations improved survival over temporary ones; Walton and Torabinejad (2002): composites and glass ionomer give the best seal.

### Introduction
A root-filled tooth can be reinfected from two directions: periapical tissue fluids can percolate into the canal through the apex, and saliva and bacteria can leak down the canal from the mouth. The **apical seal** and the **coronal seal** guard against these two routes. Early work emphasised the apical seal; more recent work stresses the coronal seal.

### Apical seal
- **Aim:** prevent percolation of periapical exudate into the canal and entomb residual bacteria.
- **Extent:** the filling should end at the dentinocemental junction, on average 0.5 mm (range 0.2-2.5 mm) from the radiographic apex; a filling 0-2 mm from the radiographic apex gives the best outcome. Success falls 12-14% for each millimetre of working length lost.
- **Components:** core plus sealer; the seal is mainly entrusted to the sealer, and in one study at least 5 mm of apical gutta-percha was needed.

### Coronal seal
- **Aim:** prevent saliva and bacteria diffusing down the gutta-percha to the periapex.
- **Evidence:** coronal leakage can occur within a short time through seemingly well-obturated canals. There is a definite correlation between poorly restored crowns and leakage of dye, bacteria or endotoxin and a poorer prognosis; lack of a coronal seal is probably the most common but most controllable cause of failure.
- **Barriers:** 3.5 mm of Cavit or Super EBA reduced bacterial leakage by 85% and 65% against unsealed controls at 45 days; a bonded lining of the chamber floor gave no leakage at 60 days.
- **Practice:** place the permanent restoration soon after obturation under rubber dam with no gap between filling and obturation; remove gutta-percha and sealer from the clinical crown to avoid discolouration.

### Comparison
| Feature | Apical seal | Coronal seal |
|---|---|---|
| Threat | Periapical fluid | Saliva and bacteria |
| Provided by | Core and sealer within 0-2 mm of the apex | Orifice barrier and permanent restoration |
| Evidence | Outcome falls with short or long fills | A meta-analysis found poor root canal treatment and poor coronal restoration have similar odds of impairing healing |
| Control | Partly | Most controllable |

### Controversy
The 3-month retreatment rule for teeth without a definitive restoration came from in vitro leakage studies; its clinical significance has been challenged.

### Conclusion
Both seals are needed. The coronal seal is now regarded as at least as important as the apical seal and, being under the clinician's control, deserves prompt attention.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Apical: 0-2 mm from radiographic apex; 12-14% per mm.
- Coronal: leakage soon after obturation; restore promptly.
- Cavit 3.5 mm: 85% reduction.
