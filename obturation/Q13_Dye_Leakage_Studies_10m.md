## STUDY MODE

### Purpose
In vitro leakage tests compare the sealing of obturation techniques, sealers and root-end materials. Dye leakage studies place a tracer dye around a filled canal and measure how far it penetrates [LIT].

### Method (general) [LIT]
Root-filled teeth are coated with nail varnish except at the apex, immersed in dye (commonly methylene blue, India ink or silver nitrate), then sectioned or cleared and the linear penetration measured under a microscope.

### What the textbooks say
- All obturation materials and techniques leak to some degree; there are **no standardised techniques to evaluate and measure microleakage**; radiographs correlate poorly with leakage (Berman et al, 2021).
- Cold lateral condensation showed more apical dye leakage than heated multiphase gutta-percha in vitro (Gilhooly et al, cited in Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). Studies on apical gutta-percha length used dye penetration to suggest 5 mm was needed (Nixon and colleagues, in Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- MTA leaked significantly less than amalgam and Super EBA in dye and bacterial leakage studies (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019); several dye leakage studies on MTA Angelus gave conflicting results (Bioceramics in Endodontics).
- Coronal leakage: poor restorations correlate with dye, bacterial or endotoxin leakage and poorer prognosis (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- **Clinical significance:** "It is not known whether experimental leakage differences based on bacterial leakage or dye penetration are significant clinically" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002). In vitro coronal leakage studies underpin the 3-month retreatment rule, yet its clinical significance is challenged (Berman et al, 2021).

### Other leakage methods named in the textbooks
Bacterial leakage, endotoxin, electrochemical testing (Chong, 2017), fluid filtration and glucose penetration (reference lists in Harty's and Cohen's; the text of those chapters does not describe the methods).

### Limitations of dye studies [LIT]
Dye molecules are small and may not behave like bacteria; sectioning may smear dye; air bubbles trapped in voids; results depend on dye, pH and time; no standard method; destructive and not repeatable on the same tooth.

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count 385; about 18 min at 22 words/min)**

### Definition
A dye leakage study is an in vitro test of the seal of a root filling or root-end material in which extracted teeth are exposed to a tracer dye and the distance the dye travels along the filling is measured.

### Method [LIT: Jafari and Jafari, 2017]
1. Prepare and fill extracted teeth with the material or technique under test.
2. Coat the root with nail varnish, leaving the area under test (for apical leakage, the apical 3 mm) exposed.
3. Immerse in dye: 0.5-2% methylene blue, 0.5-1% India ink, 5% eosin, Procion brilliant blue, rhodamine B, fuchsin or silver nitrate.
4. Section the tooth longitudinally or transversely, or demineralise and clear it to give a three-dimensional view.
5. Measure the **linear depth** of penetration under a stereomicroscope (×4 to ×30) or with image software;

### Uses in the textbooks
Cold lateral condensation leaked more than heated gutta-percha in vitro; MTA leaked significantly less than amalgam and Super EBA as a root-end filling; coronal dye leakage correlated with poor restorations and worse prognosis.

### Limitations
- **No standardised method:** textbooks state that no standardised technique exists to measure microleakage and that radiographs correlate poorly with leakage.
- **Dye behaviour:** India ink particles are larger than some bacterial components and may give false results in either direction; methylene blue can dissolve during demineralisation; dye size, pH and immersion time alter the result [LIT].
- **Sectioning:** longitudinal sectioning depends on a random cut axis and can miss the deepest penetration, underestimating leakage; clearing is more accurate but demineralisation may be incomplete [LIT].
- **Air bubbles** trapped in voids block dye movement [LIT].
- **Poor correlation between tests:** fluid filtration favoured lateral condensation, the bacterial test favoured Thermafil, and dye and electrochemical tests showed no difference; the authors found poor correlation between methods [LIT: Karagenc et al, 2006, abstract level].
- **Clinical relevance is uncertain:** it is not known whether differences in dye or bacterial leakage are clinically significant.

### Alternatives
Fluid filtration (air bubble movement in a capillary), bacterial and endotoxin leakage (clinically relevant but strain-dependent), glucose penetration (sensitive but slow), radioisotope and electrochemical tests, and CBCT or SEM imaging [LIT].

### Conclusion
Dye studies are simple and cheap and can rank materials, but they are not standardised, correlate poorly with other leakage tests, and cannot by themselves predict clinical success.
