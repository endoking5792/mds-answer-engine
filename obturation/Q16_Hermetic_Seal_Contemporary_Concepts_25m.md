## STUDY MODE

### Why a hermetic seal is the aim
Obturation entombs residual bacteria and blocks coronal and periapical routes of reinfection; leakage correlates with poor obturation but there is no standardised leakage test and radiographs do not correlate with leakage (Berman et al, 2021; Azim and Aksel, 2022).

### Contemporary concepts
1. **Disinfect first, then seal.** The seal cannot kill what disinfection leaves; a dry canal is needed (Azim and Aksel, 2022).
2. **Accurate working length and an apical limit of 0-2 mm** using apex locators; each millimetre lost lowers success 12-14% (Azim and Aksel, 2022).
3. **Three-dimensional filling of the whole system.** Dense, void-free filling to seal portals of exit and entry (Azim and Aksel, 2022).
4. **Sealer-centred obturation.** Leakage appears to depend on the sealer more than the core (Chugal and Lin, in Jain, 2016); sealers must be insoluble, non-shrinking and biocompatible (see `endodontic_sealers/`).
5. **Bonding / monoblock.** Resin-bonded systems (Resilon) failed clinically and were withdrawn in 2014; the monoblock remains theoretical (Azim and Aksel, 2022).
6. **Hydraulic bioceramic sealing.** Calcium silicate sealers expand slightly, set with tubular moisture, and are bioactive; used with single-cone, coneless (foramen up to 0.7 mm) or warm techniques, and synchronised hydraulic condensation with coated cones (Azim and Aksel, 2022; Chopra, Bioceramics, Ch.12; Common Complications).
7. **Sealing the apex when no stop exists.** MTA/bioceramic apical plug, custom cone, collagen matrix (Q10).
8. **Coronal seal.** Prompt permanent restoration with rubber dam; orifice barrier or bonded chamber-floor lining; Cavit/Super EBA 3.5 mm reduced bacterial leakage 85%/65% (Berman et al, 2021).

### Techniques that improve adaptation
Warm vertical compaction/continuous wave, injectable thermoplastic gutta-percha, carrier-based (Q8). Sealer placement with ultrasonic activation gave better distribution in one study (Berman et al, 2021).

### Moisture
Resin sealers need slightly moist dentin; bioceramic sealers use residual moisture; alcohol as final irrigant dehydrates dentin and may delay bioceramic set (Berman et al, 2021; Azim and Aksel, 2022).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim — current concepts narrow, but do not close, the leakage gap.** Cohen's: all materials and techniques leak to some extent and radiographic quality does not predict leakage. Endodontic Prognosis: older sealer classes give the same clinical outcome and bioceramic sealers lack sufficient clinical data. Tennert and others: the risk of extrusion differs by technique. INTERPRETATION: a "hermetic seal" is a laboratory ideal; clinically what matters is disinfection, density to the correct length and an early coronal seal. ## EXAM MODE
**(Word count 864; about 39 min at 22 words/min)**

### Introduction
A hermetic seal is a leak-proof barrier that stops bacteria and fluids passing through or around the root filling. It is the aim of obturation, yet Cohen's states that all materials and techniques result in some degree of leakage, and that no standardised test measures it. The question therefore asks what contemporary practice does to come closest. The answer rests on eight linked concepts.

### 1. Disinfection and a dry canal first
A seal cannot kill bacteria left by poor cleaning; obturation reflects the quality of cleaning and shaping. The canal should be dry before filling; if exudate persists, use an intracanal medicament for at least one week.

### 2. Correct length
Use an apex locator with radiographs. Fill 0-2 mm from the radiographic apex; success falls 12-14% for each millimetre of working length lost; fillings beyond the apex irritate the ligament and may compromise the apical seal.

### 3. Dense, three-dimensional filling
The filling should be dense with no voids from the orifice to the dentinocemental junction, sealing all portals of entry and exit. Warm vertical compaction with continuous wave and thermoplastic back-fill, injectable gutta-percha and carrier-based systems adapt better to canal irregularities than cold lateral compaction in the laboratory.

### 4. Sealer as the critical interface
Leakage appears to depend more on the sealer than on the core, because gutta-percha does not bond. Sealers should be insoluble, dimensionally stable, biocompatible and radiopaque. Epoxy resin (AH Plus) is low in solubility and adhesive; calcium silicate sealers are non-shrinking, slightly expanding and bioactive.

### 5. Hydraulic bioceramic techniques
Calcium silicate sealers set with moisture and expand slightly. They permit the single-cone technique (cone as a piston in a canal of constant taper), the coneless or BioConeless technique when no cone fits (foramen up to about 0.7 mm), and synchronised hydraulic condensation with coated cones. BC HiFlow tolerates heat; BioRoot RCS should not be used with warm techniques. Moisture must be managed: flooding or alcohol drying impairs setting.

### 6. Bonded systems and the monoblock
Resilon with Epiphany tried to bond core, sealer and dentin, but polycaprolactone degrades, the C-factor in a canal is about 1,000 against 5 in a Class I cavity, and it was withdrawn in 2014 after 56% against 88% success at six years. Resin-, glass-ionomer- and bioceramic-coated cones continue the idea without proven benefit.

### 7. Sealing when no apical stop exists
An MTA or bioceramic putty plug of 3-6 mm, a custom cone, or dentin chips create the barrier in open apices or resorbed foramina.

### 8. Coronal seal
Coronal leakage can occur within a short time through seemingly well-obturated canals, and research emphasis has moved from the apical to the coronal seal. Place the permanent restoration soon after obturation under rubber dam with no gap between filling and restoration. A 3.5 mm Cavit or Super EBA barrier reduced bacterial leakage by 85% and 65% at 45 days, and a bonded lining over the chamber floor gave no leakage at 60 days.

### What a clinician actually does to improve the seal
1. **Before filling:** complete cleaning and shaping, final irrigation protocol compatible with the sealer, and a dry or slightly moist canal as the sealer requires.
2. **Choose the sealer first:** epoxy resin for a conventional low-solubility seal and easy retreatment; calcium silicate for hydraulic sealing in tapered canals with a well-fitting cone; do not use paraformaldehyde sealers.
3. **Choose the technique for the anatomy:** cold lateral or single cone for simple canals; continuous wave, injectable or carrier-based filling for curved, oval, C-shaped or resorbed canals; coneless filling when no cone fits.
4. **Control the apex:** apex locator, 0-2 mm limit, injection needle 3-6 mm from the preparation, apical plug when there is no stop.
5. **Keep sealer volume small** because most sealers dissolve to some degree; the core should form the bulk of the filling.
6. **Seal the coronal end at once:** remove gutta-percha and sealer from the crown, place an orifice barrier and a permanent restoration without delay.
7. **Review:** healing on follow-up is the real test of the seal, because radiographs cannot show a seal.

### Techniques and materials in summary
| Concept | Technique / material |
|---|---|
| Dense filling | Continuous wave, injectable, carrier |
| Stable interface | Epoxy or calcium silicate sealer |
| Hydraulic sealing | Single cone, coneless, coated cones |
| Apical barrier | MTA, bioceramic putty, custom cone |
| Coronal seal | Cavit / Super EBA, bonded lining, early permanent restoration |

### Critical appraisal
Radiographs do not correlate well with leakage and no standard method measures it. Older sealer classes give the same clinical outcome and bioceramic sealers lack sufficient clinical data. Poor-quality coronal restoration has odds of impairing healing similar to poor-quality root canal treatment.

### Why a hermetic seal is difficult to prove
There is no standardised method to measure microleakage; radiographs correlate poorly with it; and laboratory tests (dye, fluid filtration, bacterial) disagree with one another. A claim that a technique gives a hermetic seal should therefore be read as a laboratory finding, not a clinical result.

### Conclusion
A truly hermetic seal remains an ideal rather than a clinical fact. Contemporary practice approaches it by combining thorough disinfection, correct length, a dense filling with a stable, biocompatible sealer, an apical barrier where there is no stop, and an early permanent coronal seal.
