## STUDY MODE

### 1. Introduction, definition and aims
Success of root canal treatment depends on cleaning and shaping, three-dimensional sealing and a fluid-tight coronal restoration (Sikri, 2019). Obturation is "the three-dimensional filling of the entire root canal system as close to the cementodentinal junction as possible" with minimal, biocompatible sealer and a core material (Sikri, 2019). Aims: prevent reinfection, entomb residual bacteria, stop periapical fluid seeping into the canal, create a barrier between mouth and periapex, and give a favourable biological environment for healing (Azim and Aksel, 2022; Sikri, 2019).

### 2. Timing
Obturate an asymptomatic tooth with a dry canal, no sinus tract, no foul odour and an intact temporary filling (Sikri, 2019). Vital pulps: one visit is usually acceptable; necrotic pulps and apical periodontitis carry higher bacterial loads and may need multiple visits with a medicament; if the canal will not dry, leave a medicament at least one week (Azim and Aksel, 2022).

### 3. Apical extent
Fill to the dentinocemental junction, on average 0.5 mm (range 0.2-2.5 mm) from the radiographic apex; 0-2 mm from the apex gives the best outcome; success falls 12-14% per millimetre of working length lost; use an electronic apex locator (Azim and Aksel, 2022). Overextension: material beyond the apex with an inadequately filled canal; overfilling: well-filled canal with surplus beyond the apex (Sikri, 2019; Azim and Aksel, 2022). Extruded sealer occurs in about 50% of cases without affecting outcome (Azim and Aksel, 2022); 96% of overextended cases showed radiographic repair (Augsburger and Peters, in Azim and Aksel, 2022); healing may be delayed (about 14 months) when gutta-percha is extruded (Azim and Aksel, 2022). Grossman's reports extruded material can reduce healing probability by as much as 25% (Gopikrishna, 2021); Harty's calls the evidence "equivocal" (Chong). Prevent overextension with accurate working length, avoid over-preparing the constriction and use plasticised gutta-percha judiciously (Sikri, 2019).

### 4. Ideal obturating material
Grossman (Cohen's Box 9.2): easily manipulated with ample working time; dimensionally stable; seals laterally and apically; non-irritating; moisture-impervious; unaffected by tissue fluid; inhibits bacteria; radiopaque; non-staining; sterile; easily removed (Berman et al, 2021). Ingle's cites West's seventeen (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

### 5. Materials
**Core materials** (Q1, Q2): gutta-percha is the standard (trans-polyisoprene; zinc oxide filler; beta/alpha phases; ISO and greater-taper cones). Silver cones: obsolete. Resilon: withdrawn 2014. Coated cones, GuttaCore, GuttaFlow, SmartSeal: newer. Pastes: N2 contraindicated. MTA and bioceramic putty for apical plugs. **Sealers** (see `endodontic_sealers/`): zinc oxide-eugenol (long record, resorbs if extruded), calcium hydroxide (soluble), epoxy resin AH Plus (low solubility, adhesion), methacrylate resin (bonded, moisture-sensitive), glass ionomer, silicone, calcium silicate (bioceramic). Paraformaldehyde sealers are contraindicated (Berman et al, 2021).

### 6. Pre-obturation procedures
Master cone: tip matched to the master apical file, tug-back, radiographic check, cut or replaced if short or loose; cone standardisation tools exist because ISO tolerances vary (Azim and Aksel, 2022). Dry canal with paper points; calcium silicate sealers need moisture, and alcohol final irrigant may delay set (Azim and Aksel, 2022). Disinfect the cone in 5.25% NaOCl for 1 min (Q3). Place sealer (cone, file, lentulo, injection tip, ultrasonic activation) (Azim and Aksel, 2022; Berman et al, 2021).

### 7. Techniques (see Q8-Q10 for detail)
A. **Cold lateral compaction.** Spreader 1-2 mm short of working length (20 s hold; Sikri, 2019); accessory cones; flexible spreaders in curved canals; inverted cone and roll cone in open apices. Simple, good length control; weaker fill of irregularities (Berman et al, 2021).
B. **Single cone.** Cone matched to final file; calcium silicate sealer; "sealer-dependent" (Azim and Aksel, 2022).
C. **Chemically softened.** Chloroform, eucalyptol, halothane; custom cone; shrinkage on evaporation (24% volume in Callahan-Johnston); abandoned except custom cones (Sikri, 2019; Berman et al, 2021).
D. **Warm vertical compaction (Schilder 1967)** and **continuous wave (Buchanan)**; System B, Touch 'n Heat, Elements (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021).
E. **Warm lateral:** Endotec, DownPak/EndoTwinn, Thermapact.
F. **Thermomechanical:** McSpadden (Q9).
G. **Ultrasonic plasticising** (anteriors; Sikri, 2019).
H. **Injectable:** Obtura II/III, Calamus, Ultrafil, PAC-160.
I. **Carrier-based:** Thermafil, Successfil, Densfil, GuttaCore; **Trifecta** (Q6, Q7).
J. **Apical plug techniques:** warm sectional gutta-percha, dentin chips, MTA, SimpliFill, FiberFill (Sikri, 2019).
K. **Cold flowable:** GuttaFlow; EZ Fill; SmartSeal.
L. **Silver cones** (historical; Q1).

### 8. Latest advances
Calcium silicate (bioceramic) sealers and hydraulic condensation; single cone, coneless and BioConeless; heat-tolerant BC HiFlow; coated cones; GuttaCore; Resilon (abandoned); nanodiamond gutta-percha (trial). See Q4, Q14.

### 9. Complications
Overextension and overfilling; underfilling; voids; vertical root fracture from compaction force; thermal injury (root surface rise above 10 °C believed damaging; flame-heated carriers and Touch 'n Heat in mandibular incisors exceeded it) (Berman et al, 2021); sealer or paraformaldehyde toxicity and paresthesia; stripping of carriers; blocked orifices in multi-canal teeth when using carriers (Sikri, 2019).

### 10. Evaluation and coronal seal
Quality by length and density; radiographic quality does not correlate well with leakage (Berman et al, 2021). Restore soon after obturation; remove gutta-percha and sealer from the clinical crown (Azim and Aksel, 2022). Cavit or Super EBA 3.5 mm reduced bacterial leakage (Berman et al, 2021).

### 11. Evidence
Little evidence supports any method over another; Toronto data hint at warm vertical compaction (Berman et al, 2021). Clinical studies: no significant difference between techniques using gutta-percha and sealer (Azim and Aksel, 2022). Older sealer classes give the same outcome; bioceramics lack sufficient clinical data (Endodontic Prognosis).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim 1 — technique has less influence on outcome than disinfection, length and coronal seal.** Cohen's, Azim and Aksel and Endodontic Prognosis all find no clear outcome advantage of one technique or sealer class; Walton and Torabinejad and Cohen's identify coronal restoration quality as an equal determinant of healing. **Claim 2 — extruded material does not always impair healing.** Azim and Aksel: extrusion in about 50% without affecting outcome and 96% repair; Grossman's: up to 25% reduction; Harty's: equivocal; Bioceramics in Endodontics (citing Ricucci): outcome related to coexisting infection rather than material toxicity. **Claim 3 — newer is not proven better.** Resilon, the most widely promoted bonded system, did worse at 6 years (56% vs 88%). INTERPRETATION: choose techniques to suit anatomy and operator skill, control the apical extent, keep sealer volume small and restore the crown early. LIMITATION: textbook evidence rests largely on in vitro leakage and short clinical series; heat, temperature and set-time figures differ between books. [Likely]

### Introduction
Success of root canal treatment depends on three things: meticulous cleaning and shaping, a three-dimensional seal, and a fluid-tight coronal restoration. **Obturation** is the three-dimensional filling of the entire root canal system as close to the cementodentinal junction as possible, using a core material together with a minimal amount of biocompatible sealer. This answer covers aims, timing, extent, materials, pre-obturation steps, each technique with its merits and demerits, recent advances, complications, evaluation, coronal seal and the evidence.

### Historical perspective
Many substances have been used in root canals, from plaster of Paris and bamboo to gold and platinum, and none met the ideal. Hill (1847) made "Hill's stopping" from gutta-percha, Bowman (1867) demonstrated gutta-percha root fillings and S.S. White began manufacturing points in 1887. Jasper introduced silver points in 1933 and Schilder introduced warm vertical compaction in 1967. Martin introduced the Obtura injection gun in 1977, Johnson the Thermafil carrier in 1978 and McSpadden the compactor in 1979. Resilon appeared in the 2000s and was withdrawn in 2014; calcium silicate sealers brought the single-cone technique back into use.

### Aims of obturation
1. Prevent recontamination of the canal by bacteria left after preparation, or entering from the mouth or lateral communications.
2. Entomb residual bacteria and remove their nutrient supply by stopping periapical fluid seeping into the canal.
3. Provide a barrier between the oral cavity and the periapical tissues.
4. Create a favourable biological environment for healing.
5. Fill the canal with a dense, void-free filling from orifice to the dentinocemental junction, sealing all portals of entry and exit.

### When to obturate
Obturate an asymptomatic tooth with a dry canal, no sinus tract, no foul odour and an intact temporary filling; a negative culture is preferred. Vital teeth can usually be completed in one visit. Necrotic teeth and retreatment cases with apical periodontitis carry high bacterial loads and may need several visits with an intracanal medicament. If the canal cannot be dried, leave a medicament for at least one week.

### Apical extent of the filling
The accepted limit is the dentinocemental junction, on average about 0.5 mm (range 0.2-2.5 mm) from the radiographic apex. A filling 0-2 mm from the radiographic apex gives the best outcome, and success falls by 12-14% for every millimetre of working length lost. An electronic apex locator is used with the radiograph to find the limit.
- **Overextension:** material beyond the apex while the canal is inadequately filled.
- **Overfilling:** an adequately filled canal with surplus material beyond the apex.
- **Underfilling:** material short of the apex.
Extruded sealer occurs in about 50% of cases without affecting outcome, and 96% of overextended cases showed radiographic repair in one study; gutta-percha extruded beyond the apex delayed healing by about 14 months. Grossman's states that extruded material can reduce the probability of healing by as much as 25%, while Harty's calls the evidence equivocal. Prevention: accurate working length, no over-preparation of the apical constriction, care with rotary instruments, judicious use of plasticised gutta-percha.

### Requirements of an ideal obturating material (Grossman)
Easily manipulated with ample working time; dimensionally stable with no shrinkage; seals laterally and apically; non-irritating to periapical tissues; impervious to moisture and non-porous; unaffected by tissue fluids; inhibits bacterial growth; radiopaque; does not discolour the tooth; sterile; easily removed if necessary. No material satisfies all.

### Materials
**A. Core materials**
| Material | Features |
|---|---|
| Gutta-percha | Trans-polyisoprene from *Palaquium* trees. Composition: gutta-percha 18-22%, zinc oxide 59-76% (filler), radiopacifier (barium sulfate) 1-18%, wax and resin 1-4%. Beta phase at room temperature (solid, compactable), alpha when heated (tacky, flowable, shrinks on cooling). Cones: ISO 0.02 taper, greater taper (0.04, 0.06), non-standardised, pellets, carriers. Compactable, inert, tissue tolerant, radiopaque, removable; no adhesion, so a sealer is always needed |
| Silver cones | Rigid, easy length control; do not fill irregular canals; corrode with cytotoxic by-products; hard to remove. Obsolete |
| Resilon | Polycaprolactone core with Epiphany dual-cure sealer and primer; monoblock attempt; withdrawn in 2014 (56% vs 88% success at 6 years) |
| Coated cones | Gutta-percha coated with resin (EndoREZ), glass ionomer (Activ GP) or bioceramic nanoparticles to bond to sealer |
| GuttaCore | Cross-linked gutta-percha carrier, removable |
| GuttaFlow, SmartSeal | Cold flowable gutta-percha-silicone; hydrophilic point that swells in the first 4 hours |
| Pastes | N2 (paraformaldehyde, lead): toxic, banned |
| MTA, bioceramic putty | Apical plugs, open apices, resorptions |

**B. Sealers** (fill gaps between core and wall; lubricate; Grossman's criteria)
| Class | Example | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc oxide-eugenol | Pulp Canal Sealer, Tubli-Seal | Longest record, antimicrobial, resorbs if extruded; soluble, shrinks; cytotoxic in culture |
| Calcium hydroxide | Sealapex | Needs solubility to act; no proven benefit |
| Epoxy resin | AH Plus | Adhesive, low solubility, working time about 4 h |
| Methacrylate resin | EndoREZ, Epiphany | Hybrid layer; moisture-sensitive; more cytotoxic than AH Plus |
| Glass ionomer | Ketac-Endo | Bonds to dentin; hard to remove |
| Silicone | RoekoSeal, GuttaFlow | Slight expansion; no antibacterial effect |
| Calcium silicate | iRoot SP, BioRoot RCS | Hydraulic, bioactive, pH about 12; retreatment debated |
| Paraformaldehyde | N2, Endomethasone | Contraindicated |

**Sealer details worth stating.**
- *Zinc oxide-eugenol (Grossman's non-staining formula):* zinc oxide 42, Staybelite resin 27, bismuth subcarbonate 15, barium sulfate 15, sodium borate 1 (parts); sets by chelation to zinc eugenolate; Grossman's cement hardens in about 2 h at 37 °C and begins to set in the canal within 10-30 min. Sikri prints 40/30 for the first two. Toxic when fresh because of free eugenol, much less when set; resorbs if extruded.
- *Epoxy resin:* AH-26 released formaldehyde on setting and was reformulated as AH Plus (epoxy-amine; working time about 4 h, set about 8 h; no formaldehyde; addition polymerisation with no residual monomer; soluble in chloroform; allergy to epoxy possible).
- *Calcium silicate:* tricalcium and dicalcium silicate with zirconium oxide or tantalum oxide radiopacifier; sets with water to calcium silicate hydrate and calcium hydroxide, pH about 12, expansion under 0.1%; initial set about 165 min (set time varies from 3 to 24 h between products and books); solubility and radiopacity may fall outside ISO limits for some products.
- *ISO 6876:* flow over 20 mm, film thickness under 50 μm, solubility under 3%, radiopacity over 3 mm aluminium.
- *Paraformaldehyde sealers* are strongly contraindicated; the American Dental Association classed N2 as "unacceptable" in 1974.

**C. Disinfection of gutta-percha.** Cones cannot be heat sterilised; they are immersed in 5.25% sodium hypochlorite for one minute and rinsed.

### Pre-obturation procedures
1. **Master cone:** tip matched to the master apical file, with "tug-back"; check radiographically; trim or replace if short or loose; cone standardisation tools exist because ISO tolerances vary.
2. **Dry the canal** with paper points; calcium silicate sealers need moisture, and alcohol as final irrigant may delay their set.
3. **Disinfect the cone.**
4. **Place sealer** with the master cone, a file, a lentulo spiral, an injection tip or ultrasonic activation.

### Obturation techniques
**1. Cold lateral compaction.** The most accepted and widely used method.
- *Steps:* fit and verify the master cone; coat the canal and the cone with sealer (wipe the apical 2 mm of sealer from the cone); seat the cone; insert a finger spreader to 1-2 mm short of working length and hold for about 20 s; rotate and remove; place an accessory cone one size smaller than the spreader in the space; repeat until the whole canal is filled with well-compacted gutta-percha; sever the cones at the orifice with a hot instrument and compact vertically; radiograph.
- *Modifications:* flexible spreaders and master cone 2 mm short for curved canals; inverted cone (serrated butt cut off) or roll-cone (heated cones rolled and chilled) for wide open apices.
- *Merits:* simple, good length control, any sealer, no special equipment.
- *Demerits:* does not fill irregularities as well as warm techniques; sealer voids; excessive spreader force can fracture the root.

**2. Single-cone technique.** A cone matching the final file and taper, plus a non-shrinking sealer (calcium silicate). The cone is seated with passive sealer placement, seared at the orifice and condensed. *Merits:* simple, less technique-sensitive, high success reported. *Demerit:* "sealer-dependent"; weak in oval canals or when the cone does not fit.

**3. Chemically softened gutta-percha.** Chloroform, eucalyptol or halothane softens the cone tip for one second; in the old Callahan-Johnston method too much solvent was used and evaporation shrank the mass by about 24%. Chloropercha and eucapercha are solvent-gutta-percha mixtures. Largely abandoned because of shrinkage, voids and poor length control; the custom cone for open apices is the surviving use.

**4. Warm vertical compaction (Schilder, 1967).** Three pluggers of graded size; a master cone is fitted, coated with sealer and placed; the coronal gutta-percha is seared and compacted with the largest plugger; a second heat wave removes a segment and the middle plugger compacts the warmed mass; repeated heat and compaction cycles advance apically in 3-4 mm increments until the apical 4-5 mm is sealed; the rest is back-packed in 5 mm segments. *Merits:* dense, homogeneous, three-dimensional filling of lateral canals. *Demerits:* difficult, time consuming, needs a more tapered preparation, risk of vertical root fracture and extrusion, less length control than lateral compaction.

**5. Continuous wave compaction (Buchanan).** Tapered NiTi preparation, tapered cone and pluggers (.06 to .12). A System B plugger heated to about 200 °C is advanced in a single motion to 3-4 mm from the binding point, heat is stopped and apical pressure held for 5-10 s as the mass cools, then a 1-second burst of heat separates the plugger; the remainder is back-filled with injected gutta-percha. Warm vertical compaction in this form is the most commonly used technique among specialists. Related devices: Touch 'n Heat (keep heat 4-5 mm from the terminus), Elements (System B plus extruder in one unit).

**6. Warm lateral compaction.** Endotec (heated spreader up to 350 °C; ZAP and TAP technique for C-shaped canals), DownPak/EndoTwinn (heat plus ultrasonic vibration), Thermapact. More gutta-percha, less sealer; the heated tips do not compact.

**7. Thermomechanical compaction (McSpadden, 1979).** A reverse Hedström-type compactor at 8,000-20,000 rpm creates frictional heat that softens gutta-percha and drives it apically and laterally. Modified NiTi MicroSeal condensers run at 1,000-4,000 rpm with low-fusing alpha-phase gutta-percha. *Demerits:* instrument fracture, extrusion, gouging, unsuitable for curved canals, heat.

**8. Injectable thermoplasticised gutta-percha.** Gutta-percha heated outside the canal and injected through a fine needle: Obtura II/III (heated about 160-200 °C; 20 or 23 gauge needle), Ultrafil (low-fusing, about 70-90 °C, three viscosities), Calamus Dual, PAC-160. The needle is placed 3-6 mm from the apical preparation, injection is passive, and compaction continues while the mass cools. *Indications:* curved, C-shaped and webbed canals, internal resorption, lateral canals, back-fill, unnegotiable canals filled before root-end surgery. *Demerits:* extrusion, poor apical control, voids unless pressure is applied, heat.

**9. Carrier-based gutta-percha (Thermafil, Johnson 1978).** Alpha-phase gutta-percha on a plastic (formerly metal) or cross-linked gutta-percha carrier. *Steps:* verify size; dry the canal; thin coat of sealer; heat the obturator in the oven until the coating is shiny; insert to working length with firm uniform pressure without rotation; allow 2-4 minutes to cool; sever the carrier at the orifice; radiograph. *Merits:* quick, flexible, fills fins and resorptive defects, low condensation force. *Demerits:* gutta-percha may strip off the carrier, extrusion, post space and retreatment difficulty.

**10. Trifecta.** Successfil carrier places a 1-2 mm alpha-phase apical mass, rotated counter-clockwise and withdrawn; the coronal canal is back-filled with Ultrafil.

**11. Ultrasonic plasticising.** In anterior teeth an ultrasonic file plasticises gutta-percha, then a spreader makes space for further cones.

**12. Apical plug techniques.** Warm sectional gutta-percha, dentin chips, MTA 3-6 mm, SimpliFill and FiberFill; used for open apices and when a post is planned.

**Comparison**
| Technique | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Cold lateral | Simple, length control | Voids, irregularities |
| Single cone + bioceramic | Simple, quick | Sealer-dependent |
| Warm vertical / continuous wave | Densest fill | Skill, fracture, extrusion |
| Injectable | Complex anatomy | Apical control |
| Carrier-based | Fast | Stripping, post space |
| Thermomechanical | Simple kit | Fracture, heat |

### Selection of technique
No technique suits every canal. A practical guide drawn from the textbooks:
| Situation | Suitable technique |
|---|---|
| Straight, round, well-tapered canal | Cold lateral compaction or single cone with bioceramic sealer |
| Curved canal | Lateral compaction with flexible spreaders; thermoplasticised gutta-percha or flexible carrier if severe |
| Oval, C-shaped canal, fins, isthmus | Warm vertical compaction, injectable gutta-percha, carrier; Endotec ZAP and TAP in C-shaped molars |
| Internal resorption, webbings, accessory canals | Injectable gutta-percha, carrier-based |
| No cone fits, apical delta, abrupt curve | Coneless or BioConeless technique with warm back-fill |
| Open apex or apical resorption | MTA or bioceramic plug, custom cone, dentin chips |
| Post space needed | Apical sectional plug; avoid carriers that are hard to remove |
| Retreatment likely | Gutta-percha with epoxy or ZOE sealer; glass ionomer sealer hardest to remove |

### Obturation in special situations
- **Open apex:** an apical barrier of MTA (3-6 mm) with a collagen matrix, then gutta-percha back-fill; avoid bismuth oxide staining near the crown, avoid etching over MTA.
- **Multi-canal teeth:** when a carrier is used in one canal, close the other orifices with damp cotton so gutta-percha does not block them.
- **Vital teeth:** the apical area may be blocked with dentin chips; instrumentation to the terminus is less critical than in necrotic teeth.

### Post-obturation
Remove excess gutta-percha and sealer from the chamber to below the cementoenamel junction to avoid staining; place a base or orifice barrier; take a final radiograph; place the permanent restoration without delay; review the tooth for healing at follow-up.

### Single-visit obturation
Completing treatment and obturating in one visit is not new. Favourable criteria (Grossman's Box 16.12): an experienced operator, a cooperative patient, good access and mouth opening, normal and patent canal anatomy, easily negotiable curvatures, no periradicular lesion, easy anaesthesia and primary treatment. Unfavourable criteria: an inexperienced operator, anatomical variations, calcifications, ledges or obstructions, acute curvature, a periradicular lesion, a hot tooth and retreatment. Indications include vital teeth, medically compromised or apprehensive patients, fractured anteriors and non-vital teeth with a sinus tract; contraindications include temporomandibular disorders, limited access and retreatment. A dry canal and the absence of persistent exudate remain essential before filling.

### Placing the sealer
Mix according to the manufacturer to a smooth, homogeneous paste. Methods: coat the master cone and use it to carry sealer along the canal wall; rotate a file or paper point coated with sealer; use a lentulo spiral (hazardous at high volume or with engine-driven devices); inject through a tip; or activate ultrasonically, which gave the best distribution when used circumferentially. Large volumes should be avoided because most sealers are soluble to some extent and the core should form the bulk of the filling.

### Periradicular reactions to obturating materials
No cementing medium or core is entirely innocuous. Any foreign body extruded into the periradicular tissue can be highly irritating. Zinc oxide-eugenol cements irritate because of eugenol, epoxy resin sealers are more compatible, and calcium silicate sealers are described as the most biocompatible in Grossman's, although Cohen's reports one study in which EndoSequence BC sealer took longer than AH Plus to become non-cytotoxic. Unset sealers are more toxic than set sealers, and cell-culture tests exaggerate clinical harm because they lack lymphatics and host defences. All filling materials should be confined to the canal.

### Obturation-related causes of failure
- Inadequate apical density or voids, which often go unnoticed on a two-dimensional radiograph.
- Underfilling short of the apex, leaving infected canal unfilled, or extrusion beyond it.
- Leakage through a deficient coronal restoration; in vitro, lack of a coronal seal led to reinfection with *Staphylococcus* within 19 days.
- Vertical root fracture from excessive compaction force.

### Radiographic criteria of an acceptable filling
A dense, three-dimensional filling with a continuous taper that reflects the canal preparation, extending to 0-2 mm from the radiographic apex, with no voids between cones or along the walls, filling lateral canals where present, and a coronal restoration without a gap. A single two-dimensional view can hide voids, and a good appearance does not prove a seal; views from more than one angulation are advisable [RECALL].

### Recent advances
- **Hydraulic bioceramic obturation:** single cone with calcium silicate sealer; coneless / BioConeless (foramen up to about 0.7 mm; apical deltas, abrupt curves); synchronised hydraulic condensation with coated cones; heat-tolerant BC HiFlow for warm techniques.
- **Coated cones** forming a bonded unit: resin, glass-ionomer, bioceramic nanoparticle.
- **GuttaCore**, GuttaFlow, EZ Fill (bi-directional spiral, room temperature), SmartSeal.
- **Resilon:** abandoned after poorer long-term results.
- **Nanodiamond-embedded gutta-percha:** in clinical trials with no adverse events reported.

### Monoblock concept
A monoblock is core, sealer and dentin acting as one cohesive unit (concept of Tay). Primary monoblocks have one interface (gutta-percha alone), secondary two (sealer plus core), tertiary three (a coating on the core bonding to the sealer). Prerequisites: mutual bonding and a modulus close to dentin (about 14,000 MPa). Polymerisation shrinkage in a canal (C-factor about 1,000 against 5 in a Class I cavity) and degradation of polycaprolactone prevent a true bonded unit; no filling material reinforces the root.

### Complications
- Overextension, overfilling, underfilling, voids.
- Vertical root fracture from excessive compaction force.
- Thermal injury: root surface rise above 10 °C is believed to risk bone damage; flame-heated carriers and Touch 'n Heat in mandibular incisors exceeded it.
- Carrier stripping; blocked orifices in multi-canal teeth when a carrier is used in one canal.
- Toxicity of sealers, especially paraformaldehyde (neurotoxicity, paresthesia, osteomyelitis).

### Evaluation and coronal seal
The filling is judged by length and density; radiographic appearance does not correlate well with leakage. Place the permanent restoration soon after obturation under rubber dam; remove gutta-percha and sealer from the clinical crown; Cavit or Super EBA 3.5 mm, or a bonded lining of the chamber floor, reduced bacterial leakage.

### Evidence
Little evidence supports one method over another; the Toronto studies suggest warm vertical compaction may be superior to lateral compaction but not definitively. Clinical studies show no significant difference among gutta-percha-and-sealer techniques. Older sealer classes give the same outcome and bioceramic sealers lack clinical data for comparison.

### Summary of the evidence by source
| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| Cohen's | Little evidence supports one method over another; Toronto studies suggest warm vertical compaction may be better than lateral compaction, but definitive evidence is lacking; all materials and techniques leak to some extent |
| Azim and Aksel | Adequate filling can be achieved by any technique; no significant outcome difference among gutta-percha-and-sealer techniques; warm vertical compaction most used by specialists; single cone has a high success rate |
| Endodontic Prognosis | ZOE, epoxy, calcium hydroxide-salicylate, silicone and composite sealers give the same clinical outcome; bioceramics lack clinical data for comparison |
| Ingle's | In vitro, cold lateral condensation may leak more than other techniques; Thermafil fills lateral canals as well as vertical compaction and better than lateral condensation |
| Grossman's | Extruded material can reduce healing probability by as much as 25% |
| Harty's | Evidence that extruded sealer lowers success is equivocal; only limited research suggests that sealer selection influences outcome |

### Practical sequence summary
Disinfect, dry, confirm working length, fit and disinfect the master cone, place sealer, fill with the chosen technique, check the radiograph for length and density, remove excess from the chamber, place a barrier and restore promptly, and review for healing.

### Conclusion
Gutta-percha with a sealer remains the standard core, placed by cold or warm techniques according to canal anatomy. Newer hydraulic, bonded and flowable systems improve handling and adaptation in the laboratory but have not shown better healing; the one bonded system followed long term, Resilon, was withdrawn. Success depends on disinfection, correct length, a dense filling with a small sealer volume, and an early permanent coronal seal.
