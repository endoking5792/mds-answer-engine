## STUDY MODE

### S1. Why casting defects matter [orientation]
- Phillips says defects can nearly always be avoided by following fundamental rules, and that failures are seldom due to anything but the operator's carelessness or ignorance (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th ed says every step must be handled properly or the casting may not fit the prepared tooth (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Marzouk calls wax-pattern fabrication the most empirical step and the cause of most casting failures (Marzouk, year not printed).
- Phillips says porosity cannot be prevented entirely but can be minimised by proper technique (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S2. Classification [classification]
- Phillips: four headings, namely distortion, surface roughness and irregularities, porosity, and incomplete or missing detail (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Bhat: seven groups, namely distortion, discolouration, surface roughness with unevenness and nodules, solidification shrinkage porosities, air inclusion porosities, incomplete castings, and fins (Bhat et al, 2019).
- McCabe and Walls: four types, namely finning and bubbling, incomplete casting, porosity, and oversized or undersized casting (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Singh: six groups, namely under or oversized casting, distortion, surface roughness and irregularities, incomplete casting, contaminated casting and porosities (Singh, 2020).
- Craig 11th ed gives no list; it describes suckback porosity, black castings, rounded margins and air-bubble nodules as common problems (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Sikri tabulates incomplete castings, rounded margins, pits, distortion, surface roughness and irregularities, fins or spines, and discoloration (Sikri, 2020).

### S3. Distortion [defect]
- Phillips: marked distortion of a casting is probably related to distortion of the wax pattern, which proper handling prevents (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Wax distortion arises from occluded air, physical deformation, stresses trapped during cooling, long storage and temperature change (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Waxes tend to return partially to their shape after manipulation, called elastic memory, and a pattern stored too long distorts by stress relaxation (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Inlay wax may contract 0.35% when cooled from 37 °C to 25 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Phillips advises removing an MOD pattern with a staple-like wire and floss and investing at once (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Investment also distorts the pattern: proximal walls move outward, gingival margins are forced apart, and distortion increases as the pattern gets thinner (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Bhat adds vibration, thick investment, delayed investing, uneven expansion and a long sprue or ring, with a 3 mm liner-free gap as the remedy (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Craig 11th ed: the longer a pattern stands, the greater the deformation, and it should not stand more than 20 to 30 minutes (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Marzouk: invest at once, or refrigerate if investment cannot follow within 5 minutes (Marzouk, year not printed).

### S4. Surface roughness and irregularities [defect]
- Phillips separates roughness (finely spaced imperfections) from irregularities (isolated imperfections such as nodules) (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Causes in Phillips: air bubbles, water films, high liquid/powder ratio, coarse silica, foreign bodies, impact of metal on the mould wall, patterns too close, carbon (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Vacuum investing: 95% of vacuum-invested castings were free of nodules, whereas only 17% of hand-invested castings were entirely free of defects (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- A nodule on a margin or internal surface cannot be removed without altering fit (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Bhat lists a rough wax pattern, coarse powder, high water/powder ratio, poor wetting and a high casting force (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Craig 11th ed: air bubbles on the outside are theoretically removable, often at great expense of time and lost metal, and do not affect fit; on margins or inside they often force a recast (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Fins form when investment cracks from rapid heating (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Singh, 2020).

### S5. Porosity [defect]
- Phillips classifies porosity as solidification defects (localised shrinkage, microporosity, suck-back) and trapped gases (pinhole, gas inclusions, subsurface, back pressure) (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- The detail of each type with cause and prevention is in the Exam Mode tables.
- Sikri lists localised shrinkage, microporosity, pinhole, gas inclusion, subsurface and back-pressure porosity (Sikri, 2020).
- McCabe and Walls describe cooling-shrinkage voids at the sprue base, surface pitting and gaseous porosity (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Rudolf et al, writing on cast Co-Cr alloys, give trapping of inert gas in the molten metal as the main cause of porosity; Phillips, Sikri and McCabe and Walls describe solidification shrinkage and dissolved or trapped gas (Rudolf et al, 2024; Anusavice et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020; McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### S6. Incomplete casting, size errors, contamination [defect]
- Phillips: causes are insufficient venting and high viscosity of the metal; pressure should be applied for at least 4 seconds (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Incomplete wax elimination gives rounded but shiny margins (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th ed: a delay of more than a minute after the ring leaves the oven gives an undersized casting (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- McCabe and Walls: low-density base-metal alloys give less thrust in centrifugal casting (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Phillips: gypsum investment heated above 700 °C with carbon releases sulphur dioxide that makes gold castings brittle (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th ed lists four causes of black castings (Craig and Powers, 2002).

### S7. Roles of wax, investment, burnout, alloy and technique [mechanism]
- Wax: pattern quality fixes the starting accuracy; softer wax gives a larger cast because it resists investment expansion less (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Investment: liquid/powder ratio, vacuum mixing, permeability and thickness behind the pattern control roughness, nodules and back pressure (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Burnout: rapid heating gives steam spalling and fins; incomplete wax elimination gives carbon and incomplete margins (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Alloy: copper, silver, platinum and palladium take up gases in the melt (Anusavice et al, 2013; McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Technique: sprue size, position and direction, mould and melt temperature, torch zone and casting pressure (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002).

### S8. Detection and effect on fit and outcome [clinical]
- Microporosity is not detectable unless the casting is sectioned (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- A finished casting should be checked under magnification before fitting on the die (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Irregularities on the cavity surface prevent seating of an otherwise accurate casting (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Porosity weakens the casting and can cause discoloration, plaque accumulation, secondary caries and periodontal disease (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Rough surfaces trap food debris and corrode (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Marginal openings accepted by dentists in a laboratory study averaged 21 µm occlusal and 74 µm gingival (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Singh notes that microporosities are fine irregular voids that weaken the casting (Singh, 2020).
- Rudolf et al: narrow, long surface notches in cast Co-Cr are vulnerable to corrosion and metallic fatigue (Rudolf et al, 2024).
- Craig 11th ed: overheating a Co-Cr alloy in an oxygen-acetylene flame raises its carbon content, precipitates carbides and makes clasps liable to fracture (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Phillips: a soft wax pattern may give a slightly larger and rougher casting than a hard wax pattern (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S9. Shrinkage compensation in brief [mechanism]
- Craig 11th ed: wax shrinkage plus metal shrinkage equals wax expansion plus setting, hygroscopic and thermal expansion of the investment (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Alloy shrinks in three stages; only the contraction after the casting has gained strength affects fit (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Linear shrinkage: 1.56% (type 1 gold), 1.37% (type 2), 1.42% (type 3) and 2.30% for Ni-Cr and Co-Cr (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Full treatment is in Q16.

### S10. Recent advances [advances]
- Phillips lists sintering of foil, CAD-CAM, copy milling, electroforming and 3D printing with metal powder as ways to make prostheses without the challenges of casting (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- CAD/CAM titanium has no bulk casting defects or alpha case (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 14th ed: direct metal laser sintering makes Co-Cr crowns and frameworks (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Rudolf et al: milling gives fewer inconsistencies and porosity but wastes up to 80% of the block (Rudolf et al, 2024).

### S11. Spruing and the order of solidification [mechanism]
- Craig 11th ed: ideally the alloy farthest from the sprue button freezes first and the sprue and button feed the rest of the pattern (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- The change from liquid to solid is accompanied by a large shrinkage that investment expansion cannot offset; it is compensated only by correct casting technique (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- If alloy in the sprue freezes before the alloy in the casting, suckback porosity occurs (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- A single sprue aimed at the pulpal floor of a full crown heats the investment there, keeps that alloy molten longer, and leaves a large void under the sprue (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Phillips explains the same sequence in terms of the sprue freezing across its section before the casting is complete, which leaves a localised void in the last part to solidify (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- McCabe and Walls describe the wave of solidification that should move toward the sprue, with a reservoir that freezes last (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Craig 11th ed: the sprue should be attached to the bulkiest part, flared, and directed toward the margins; a Y-sprue is often used on MOD inlays (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Craig 11th ed: sprue diameter ranges from 6 to 12 gauge (4.1 to 2.0 mm), and the pattern lies about 6 mm from the end of the ring (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Bhat: a reservoir 1 mm from the pattern and a sprue thicker than the thickest part of the pattern supply liquid to the last region to solidify (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Marzouk calls the sprue diameter the most important factor in the speed of filling; that speed should not exceed the speed of gas escape (Marzouk, year not printed).

### S12. Investment, burnout and casting temperature [mechanism]
- Phillips: gypsum investment burnout is 500 °C for the hygroscopic technique and 700 °C for the thermal technique; phosphate investment goes from 700 to 1030 °C depending on the alloy (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Rapid heating generates steam that causes flaking or spalling of the mould wall, and radial cracks give fins or spines (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Gypsum heated above 700 °C with carbon releases sulfur dioxide that contaminates and embrittles gold castings (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th ed: prolonged heating of gypsum investment above 650 °C breaks it down and roughens the casting (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Phillips: the investment contracts as it cools, so approximately 1 minute can pass before a noticeable loss in dimension (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th ed: a delay of more than a minute gives an undersized casting, and reheating a cooled ring gives a casting that is too small (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Phillips: too high a liquid/powder ratio gives a rougher casting, while too little water makes the mix unmanageably thick (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Phillips: a lower liquid/powder ratio is associated with less porosity of the investment (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S13. Where the books differ [comparison]
- Classification: Phillips four headings, Bhat seven, McCabe and Walls four, Singh six, Craig 11th ed none (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008; Singh, 2020; Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Suck-back: Phillips places it inside the crown near the sprue from a hot spot; Craig 11th ed ties it to the sprue freezing first; Bhat places it opposite the sprue (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002; Bhat et al, 2019).
- Microporosity: Phillips ties it to low mould or casting temperature; Bhat advises a higher casting temperature; Singh says the casting cools too rapidly (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019; Singh, 2020).
- Subsurface porosity: Phillips says the reasons are not well understood but names simultaneous nucleation of solid grains and gas bubbles as a possible cause; Bhat also names simultaneous nucleation and advises a higher liquid temperature, whereas Phillips Table 10-4 shows it increasing with melt and mould temperature; Singh adds casting in a cold mould (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019; Singh, 2020).
- Pinhole porosity: Phillips gives the cause (dissolved gas expelled on freezing) and advises premelting and a reducing flame for the larger gas-inclusion voids; Singh says nothing much can be done for pinhole (Anusavice et al, 2013; Singh, 2020).
- Distance of pattern from ring end: Phillips 6 mm or less; Singh 6-8 mm (Anusavice et al, 2013; Singh, 2020).

### S14. Critical evidence synthesis [critical]
- The books agree that porosity weakens the casting, but they give no incidence figures (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019).
- The figures of 95% nodule-free (vacuum) and 17% defect-free (hand-invested) come from one study quoted by Phillips (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Subsurface porosity is explained differently in three books, and Phillips calls its reasons not well understood (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019; Singh, 2020).
- Craig 11th ed gives numerical mould-temperature data for suckback in one alloy, which other books do not match (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Craig 14th ed leaves the topic to a website (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- The books state their own limits: Craig 11th ed says a complete description is beyond its scope (Craig and Powers, 2002).

### 1. Definition and scope
- Writer's working definition: a casting defect is any fault in a lost-wax cast restoration that spoils its shape, size, surface or soundness; no book defines the term, but Bhat lists exact shape, size, fit, smooth surface and no flaws as the aim of a precision casting (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Defects can nearly always be avoided by strict observance of procedure; Phillips attributes them to the carelessness or ignorance of the operator (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Marzouk calls wax-pattern fabrication the cause of most casting failures (Marzouk, year not printed).

### 2. Casting process in brief
- A sprued wax pattern is invested in a ring, the wax is burned out, and molten metal is forced through the sprue into the mould by centrifugal force, pressure or vacuum (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Steps where defects arise: waxing and removal of the pattern, spruing, investing, burnout, melting, casting, cleaning and pickling (Craig and Powers, 2002; Bhat et al, 2019).

### 4. Distortion
- **Cause, wax:** occluded air, deformation in moulding or removal, release of trapped stress, long storage, temperature change (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Waxes show elastic memory, returning partly to their original shape (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Inlay wax may contract 0.35% on cooling from 37 °C to 25 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Cause, investment:** setting and hygroscopic expansion move the proximal walls outward and force the gingival margins apart; the thinner the pattern, the greater the distortion (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Bhat adds vibration, thick investment, delay in investing and a long sprue or ring (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Prevention:** invest immediately after removal; remove an MOD pattern with a staple-like wire and floss parallel to the axial walls; avoid touching the pattern (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th ed: a pattern should not stand more than 20 to 30 minutes, and a Y-sprue is used on MOD patterns (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Bhat: a 3 mm gap free of liner at the ring ends restricts longitudinal expansion (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Marzouk: refrigerate the pattern if investing cannot follow within 5 minutes (Marzouk, year not printed).

### 5. Surface roughness and irregularities
| Defect | Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Roughness (general) | High liquid/powder ratio; coarse silica; wrong binder to quartz ratio (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Measure accurately; fine silica (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Nodules (air voids) | Air bubbles on the pattern; excess wetting agent dilutes investment (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Vacuum investing; vibration; thin air-dried wetting agent (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Ridges and veins (water film) | Wax repels water; investment separates from pattern (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Do not jar the pattern; paint investment in intimate contact (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Foreign bodies, bright concavities | Investment pieces; carbon or flux carried in (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Clean crucible former; careful sprue removal (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Raised area on casting | Metal strikes the mould wall at 90° and abrades it (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Direct the sprue so the metal glances (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Cracked investment | Patterns less than 3 mm apart (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Space and stagger patterns (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Fins | Rapid heating; weak investment; high casting force (Bhat et al, 2019) | Heat slowly; fewer arm turns (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Rough from heat | Gypsum investment held above 650 °C breaks down (Craig and Powers, 2002) | Cast promptly after burnout (Craig and Powers, 2002) |
- Phillips: 95% of vacuum-invested castings were free of nodules, whereas only 17% of hand-invested castings were entirely free of defects (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th ed: external air bubbles do not affect fit but are removable only at great expense of time and metal; margin or inside bubbles often need a recast (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Phillips: steam from rapid heating causes spalling, and radial cracks give fins or spines, especially with cristobalite (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 6. Porosity: every type
| Type | Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Localised shrinkage | Sprue freezes before the casting is fed; linear contraction at least 1.25% (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Reservoir 1 mm from pattern and thicker sprue (Bhat et al, 2019); Sikri adds flaring the junction, avoiding a hot spot, no excessively long sprue, and lowering casting temperature about 30 °C (Sikri, 2020); flaring also in Craig 11th (Craig and Powers, 2002) |
| Microporosity | Too rapid solidification; mould or casting temperature too low; found only on sectioning (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Raise temperature (Bhat et al, 2019; Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Suck-back | Hot spot where hot metal strikes the mould wall, often at an unrounded line angle (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Flare the sprue; lower casting temperature about 30 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Pinhole | Gas dissolved in the melt is expelled on freezing (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Phillips gives no separate remedy; Singh: nothing much can be done (Singh, 2020) |
| Gas inclusion | Larger voids; mechanically trapped gas or oxidising zone (Anusavice et al, 2013); gas unable to escape through investment pores (Singh, 2020) | Reducing zone; adjusted torch; premelt reused alloy on graphite (Anusavice et al, 2013); reducing zone (Singh, 2020) |
| Subsurface | Simultaneous nucleation of solid grains and gas bubbles at the mould wall (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Control rate of metal entry (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Back pressure | Air cannot escape through the investment pores (Anusavice et al, 2013) | Full burnout; enough temperature and pressure; 6 mm or less behind pattern (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
- Pontic porosity: a bulky pontic holds heat; one or more small chill-set sprues (for example 18-gauge) within 5 mm of the ring edge solve it (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Copper and silver dissolve large amounts of oxygen when molten, and platinum and palladium take up hydrogen and oxygen (Anusavice et al, 2013); McCabe and Walls also list gold among the metals dissolving oxygen, as does Singh (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Singh, 2020).
- Pattern position differs in what is measured: Phillips gives investment thickness of 6 mm or less behind the pattern, Singh 6-8 mm, Craig 11th about 6 mm from the ring end, and Bhat under 6 mm of investment (Anusavice et al, 2013; Singh, 2020; Craig and Powers, 2002; Bhat et al, 2019).
- Back-pressure risk rises with dense modern investments, vacuum investing and carbon-clogged moulds (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th ed on suckback: expansion of the investment cannot compensate for it (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Craig 11th ed prevention: Y-sprue with widely separated arms; wider legs or an extra 1 to 2 g of alloy; mould temperature raised from 500 °C to 650 or 700 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Raising alloy temperature or adding a turn on the centrifuge does not help and may increase suckback (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Craig 11th ed figures: with the mould at 500 °C the investment near the pulpal floor was within 40 °C of the alloy solidification temperature, near the sprue 355 °C below it; at 700 °C the gaps were 30 to 35 °C and 140 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Craig 11th ed problem 7: a smaller or longer sprue, a single sprue aimed at the pulpal floor, or a small button gives suckback; use a shorter, larger-diameter sprue (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Bhat: pinhole, large air bubbles and subsurface voids are "air inclusion" porosities, and all porosities decrease strength (Bhat et al, 2019).
- McCabe and Walls: gaseous porosity from dissolved oxygen is reduced by avoiding overheating or casting in inert gas or vacuum (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Marzouk: palladium takes up hydrogen and silver oxygen, released on solidification as porosity and roughness (Marzouk, year not printed).
- Rudolf et al, on cast Co-Cr alloys: the main cause of porosity is trapped inert gas, set beside the shrinkage and dissolved-gas causes above without ranking (Rudolf et al, 2024).
- Detail of each porosity: see Q14 and Q15.

### 7. Incomplete casting and rounded margins
- Phillips: causes are insufficient venting and high viscosity of the metal; pressure should be applied at least 4 seconds (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Incomplete wax elimination fills pores and gives rounded margins that are shiny, from carbon monoxide (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Underheated metal is too viscous; raise it higher than the liquidus temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Sikri Table 16.5 gives casting at least 150 °F (57 °C) above the fusion temperature, casting within one minute of removing the ring, and a 10 gauge sprue about 6.0 mm long; the figures are printed as stated and the pair is not reconciled here (Sikri, 2020).
- Craig 11th ed: insufficient pressure or underheating rounds the margins because the alloy freezes before gases leave; also a thick layer of investment, a glassy phosphate coat, or an underwound machine (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Bhat: insufficient alloy, incomplete melting, low force, blocked sprue, incomplete dewaxing, premature solidification (Bhat et al, 2019).
- McCabe and Walls: a casting under back pressure is rounded at the edges; low-density base-metal alloys give less thrust (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Sikri (Table 16.5): incomplete burnout of wax gives shiny rounded margins (Sikri, 2020).

### 8. Other defects
- **Undersize or oversize:** a delay of more than a minute after the ring leaves the oven gives an undersized casting (Craig and Powers, 2002); McCabe and Walls link fit to the balance of shrinkage against expansion (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- **Black or discoloured castings:** four causes with calcium sulfate investment: wax not eliminated, mould too long in the oven, oxidising flame, no deoxidiser (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Carbon black (wax not eliminated) is not cleaned by routine pickling, whereas black from copper oxide (oxidising flame, deoxidiser lost) is cleaned by normal pickling (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Sulphur contamination gives a black or gray brittle layer (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Gypsum investment heated above 700 °C with carbon gives sulfur dioxide that makes gold castings extremely brittle (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Mercury contamination of gold loses ductility (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Base metals:** overheating with oxygen-acetylene raises carbon, precipitates carbides and risks clasp fracture (Craig and Powers, 2002).

### 9. Role of wax, investment, burnout, alloy and technique
| Factor | Effect on defects |
|---|---|
| Wax pattern | Distortion, size error; soft wax gives a larger cast (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Investment | Roughness, nodules, fins, back pressure (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Burnout | Spalling, fins, carbon, sulfur contamination (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Alloy | Gas uptake; white gold alloys with higher liquidus are more susceptible to porosity (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Technique | Sprue, temperatures, torch zone, pressure (Craig and Powers, 2002) |
- Marzouk: unevaporated wax clogs investment pores, giving back-pressure porosity or incomplete casting, and a large melt and mould temperature difference gives subsurface or generalised porosity (Marzouk, year not printed).
- **Spruing (Craig 11th ed):** the type, number, position, diameter, length and direction of the sprue are all important to success (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Attach the sprue to the bulkiest part of the pattern, away from fine margins, and flare the junction; an unflared junction leaves sharp investment projections that can break off and enter the metal (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Direct the sprue toward the margins to reduce turbulence (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Sprue diameter may vary from 6 to 12 gauge (4.1 to 2.0 mm); too small a sprue lets the metal freeze before the mould fills, giving an incomplete casting (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Place the pattern about 6 mm from the end of the ring so the casting freezes first and the sprue metal stays liquid to feed it (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- A Y-sprue prevents warpage of a distortion-prone pattern and is often used on MOD inlays; small vent sprues help gas escape (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- McCabe and Walls: attach sprues at points of greatest bulk to create a heat sink and prevent premature solidification; thicken a section of the sprue near the casting as a reservoir that freezes last and holds the shrinkage porosity (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Bhat: place a reservoir 1 mm from the pattern and use a sprue thicker than the thickest part of the pattern; too long a sprue and ring distorts the casting (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Phillips: phosphate investment has low permeability, so casting pressure should be greater than for a gypsum mould (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Phillips: begin burnout while the mould is still wet, so that water vapour flushes wax from the mould; for gypsum investments heat slowly to 650-700 °C in 60 minutes (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Phillips: cast within about 1 minute of removing the ring, because the investment contracts as it cools (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Phillips: the longer the delay before the ring is immersed in the water bath, the less the hygroscopic expansion, so mould size varies with technique (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Phillips: a low-heat hygroscopic mould may retain fine carbon, so back-pressure porosity is a greater hazard than with the high-heat technique (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 10. Detection and effect on fit and clinical outcome
- Check the casting under magnification before fitting on the die (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Microporosity is found only by sectioning, and pinholes appear on polishing (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Irregularities on the cavity surface stop an accurate casting from seating (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Porosity weakens the casting and may cause discoloration, plaque build-up, secondary caries and periodontal disease (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Rough surfaces collect food debris and corrode (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Distortion and size error cause misfit, as do incomplete casting and fins (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Surface notches in cast Co-Cr are open to corrosion and fatigue (Rudolf et al, 2024).
- A laboratory study accepted mean marginal openings of 21 µm occlusal and 74 µm gingival (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 11. Shrinkage compensation in brief (see Q16)
- Wax shrinkage plus metal shrinkage equals wax expansion plus setting, hygroscopic and thermal expansion (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Alloy shrinks in three stages; stage three after the casting gains strength governs fit (Anusavice et al, 2013); Craig 11th ed describes two stages, liquid to solid and contraction of the solid alloy, with a typical cooling shrinkage of 1.25% to 2.5% (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Linear shrinkage: 1.56%, 1.37% and 1.42% for type 1, 2 and 3 gold; 2.30% for Ni-Cr and Co-Cr (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- The hygroscopic low-heat method draws expansion from the 37 °C bath, hygroscopic expansion and thermal expansion (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Too much expansion makes the crown too large, as much as 1.7% (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Metal-ceramic alloys need a total expansion of 2% or more (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 13. Critical evidence synthesis
- Phillips alone gives a table of technical variables for solidification porosity (Table 10-4) (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- For subsurface porosity Bhat advises a higher liquid temperature, while Phillips Table 10-4 shows higher melt and mould temperatures increasing it; neither is ranked here (Bhat et al, 2019; Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Phillips calls the cause of subsurface porosity not well understood, and Sikri repeats that the exact reason is not established (Anusavice et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020).
- The one comparative figure, 95% nodule-free against 17% defect-free, comes from a single study quoted without detail (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- The fit figures come from a laboratory study of 10 dentists (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th ed's suckback figures describe one alloy and mould; they do not apply to every alloy (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- In the pages read, none of the books gives a clinical incidence of casting defects; Phillips illustrates porosity with photomicrographs credited to individual researchers, not with incidence data (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Marzouk's statement that wax-pattern fabrication causes most failures gives no incidence data, while Phillips blames operator carelessness; both are reported without ranking (Marzouk, year not printed; Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 14. Recent advances
- Phillips lists foil sintering, CAD-CAM, copy milling, electroforming and 3D printing with metal powder as methods that avoid casting procedures and shrinkage (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Direct laser metal sintering (DLMS) fuses metal powder layer by layer; the powder may be titanium, Co-Cr or noble metal (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- In one study of three-unit Co-Cr FDPs, laser sintering gave the best fit, then milled wax with casting, conventional casting, then metal milling (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- CAD/CAM titanium has no bulk casting defects or alpha case (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 14th ed: direct metal laser sintering makes Co-Cr crowns and frameworks (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Rudolf et al: milling gives fewer inconsistencies and less porosity but wastes up to 80% of the block, and a printed Co-Cr framework is stress-relieved at 750-800 °C (Rudolf et al, 2024).
- Bhat: CAD/CAM helps overcome many casting defects (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Craig 11th ed and McCabe and Walls give no metal laser sintering (Craig and Powers, 2002; McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**Conclusion:** casting defects are classified as distortion, roughness and irregularities, porosity and incomplete casting, each traced to wax, investment, burnout, alloy or technique and prevented by controlled procedure (Anusavice et al, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Phillips: four headings; porosity in solidification and trapped-gas groups (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Contraction at least 1.25% (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Suck-back: flare, lower casting temperature 30 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Back pressure: 6 mm or less of investment, pressure at least 4 seconds (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Nodules: vacuum investing; 95% nodule-free against 17% defect-free (hand) (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Gypsum above 700 °C with carbon: brittle gold (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th: suckback is not cured by hotter alloy (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Craig 14th ed has no defects text (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Giving one classification as if all books agree.
2. Describing suck-back and localised shrinkage as the same without noting the books differ: Phillips and Sikri fold suck-back into localised shrinkage, while Bhat, Singh and Craig 11th list it separately (Anusavice et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020; Bhat et al, 2019; Singh, 2020; Craig and Powers, 2002).
3. Citing Craig 14th ed for defects (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
4. Raising alloy temperature as the cure for suckback against Craig 11th ed (Craig and Powers, 2002).
5. Leaving out recent advances and shrinkage compensation.
