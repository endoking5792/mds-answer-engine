## STUDY MODE

### S1. Forms [classification]
Gold is used as direct filling gold (foil, electrolytic precipitate, granular) and as cast gold alloy (Anusavice et al, 2013). Direct gold is supplied in three forms, with purity 99.99% or higher except platinized foil and alloyed electrolytic precipitate (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S2. Properties [properties]
Pure gold is the noblest dental metal, rarely tarnishing or corroding, and two clean sheets weld at room temperature, so it can be built up by cold welding (Anusavice et al, 2013). It is soft (22 VHN) and the most ductile and malleable metal in restorative dentistry (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S3. Cast gold [use]
Hilton et al say gold alloy is considered, if used with care, to have the greatest longevity of any restorative material (disputed by other studies), wears at a rate similar to enamel, casts accurately and resists corrosion (Hilton et al, 2013). Type 1 alloys are soft and designed for inlays; type 4 suits bridges and partial denture frameworks (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S4. Limits [limits]
Higher initial cost, with at least two appointments and laboratory costs (Hilton et al, 2013). Allergy is relatively rare but documented, and galvanic effects occur against zinc-containing amalgam (Hilton et al, 2013). Direct gold is used on a very limited basis (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 8 minutes.)*

### 1. Forms
- Direct filling gold: foil, electrolytic precipitate, granular, 99.99% pure (except platinized foil, alloyed precipitate) (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Cast gold alloy: inlays, onlays, crowns (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 2. Properties
- Noblest dental metal; rarely tarnishes or corrodes (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Pure gold is soft (22 VHN), very ductile and malleable (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Clean pieces cold-weld at room temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Wears like enamel (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 3. Advantages
- Greatest longevity if used with care; disputed by other studies (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Casts accurately; type I or II margins can usually be improved after casting (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Among the most biocompatible materials; allergy relatively rare but documented (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 4. Limitations
- Higher cost, two appointments (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Galvanic effects against zinc-containing amalgam (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Direct gold: very limited use (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 5. Conclusion
Gold is chosen for durability and biocompatibility, at higher cost (Hilton et al, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Cold welding of clean gold at room temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Wear similar to enamel (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Three forms of direct gold (Anusavice et al, 2013).
