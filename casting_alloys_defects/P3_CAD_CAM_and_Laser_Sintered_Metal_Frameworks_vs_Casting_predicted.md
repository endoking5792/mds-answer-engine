## STUDY MODE

### S1. Alternatives to casting [overview]
Phillips: technologies exist for metal prostheses without the challenges of casting and casting shrinkage: sintered burnished foil, CAD-CAM of metal blocks, copy milling, electroforming and three-dimensional printing with metal powder followed by sintering (Anusavice et al, 2013). Powers and Wataha say machining is a growing alternative, but most prostheses are still cast (Powers and Wataha, 2013).

### S2. Milling [subtractive]
Milling machines under numerical control reproduce geometries from scanners and process cobalt-chromium, titanium, zirconium and plastics; five-axis machining makes embrasures and undercuts without manual trimming (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Rudolf et al: digital techniques exclude impression, waxing and casting as error sources; the framework is milled from an industrial blank with fewer inconsistencies and less porosity; disadvantage, milling may remove up to 80% of the block (Rudolf et al, 2024). Soft milling uses wax-bound Co-Cr powder blocks whose binder is about 1 to 2% and is burnt away on sintering (Rudolf et al, 2024). Phillips: the optical scanning procedure eliminates the need for an impression, and a prosthesis can be placed in 10 minutes to 1 hour (Anusavice et al, 2013). Bhat lists CAD-CAM advantages of a single sitting within an hour, easier finishing of titanium because no hard alpha case forms, and limited technical skill (Bhat et al, 2019). Phillips: CAD/CAM titanium has no bulk casting defects or alpha case (Anusavice et al, 2013). Bhat: no precautions against casting defects are needed, but the equipment is very expensive and a trained technician is needed (Bhat et al, 2019).

### S3. Laser sintering [additive]
Sakaguchi et al: direct metal laser sintering uses a high-power laser to fuse successive 0.02-mm layers of powdered metal to make cobalt-chrome copings and frameworks, which are then sandblasted and polished (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Phillips: a laser fuses metal powder layer by layer (direct laser metal sintering); the powder can be titanium, Co-Cr or a noble metal; electron beam melting is a related process (Anusavice et al, 2013). Rudolf et al distinguish SLS, in which powder particles are joined by sintering or partial melting and which is usually used for polymers and ceramics although possible for metals, from SLM and EBM, in which particles are completely melted and which are used predominantly for metals (Rudolf et al, 2024). SLM gives fully dense metal objects with mechanical properties comparable to bulk materials (Rudolf et al, 2024). Additive manufacturing is stated to reduce material waste and fabrication time greatly (Rudolf et al, 2024). Rudolf et al: sintered frameworks carry residual stresses and should be annealed at 750 to 800 C in argon, mandatory for four or more units (Rudolf et al, 2024).

### S4. Fit [evidence]
One study of three-unit Co-Cr bridges found the best fit with laser sintering, then CAD/CAM wax milling followed by casting, then conventional casting, then CAD/CAM metal milling; these were initial results (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 1. Introduction
Conventional casting involves impression, waxing and casting steps that are sources of error (Rudolf et al, 2024), and casting shrinkage (Anusavice et al, 2013). Milling and laser sintering are digital alternatives.

### 2. Milling (subtractive)
- Blank milled under numerical control from scans; Co-Cr, titanium, zirconium processed (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Five-axis machining makes fine geometry without manual trimming (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Industrial blank gives fewer inconsistencies and less porosity (Rudolf et al, 2024).
- Titanium has no alpha case or bulk casting defects (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Disadvantage: up to 80% of the block may be wasted (Rudolf et al, 2024).

### 3. Laser sintering (additive)
- Laser fuses 0.02-mm layers of metal powder (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Metals: titanium, Co-Cr or noble metal; electron beam melting is related (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- SLS partly melts (mainly polymers, ceramics); SLM and EBM fully melt (mainly metals) (Rudolf et al, 2024).
- Residual stresses: anneal at 750 to 800 C in argon (Rudolf et al, 2024).

### 4. Comparison
| Feature | Casting | Milling | Laser sintering |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steps | impression, waxing, casting (Rudolf et al, 2024) | digital; casting steps excluded (Rudolf et al, 2024) | layer-by-layer from powder (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) |
| Shrinkage problem | casting shrinkage (Anusavice et al, 2013) | casting shrinkage avoided (Anusavice et al, 2013) | casting shrinkage avoided (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Waste | not stated | up to 80% of block (Rudolf et al, 2024) | waste reduced greatly (Rudolf et al, 2024) |
| Residual stress | not stated | not stated | anneal 750 to 800 C (Rudolf et al, 2024) |
| Fit in one study | third (Anusavice et al, 2013) | fourth (Anusavice et al, 2013) | best (Anusavice et al, 2013) |

### 5. Cost (different items)
Equipment: very expensive for CAD-CAM (Bhat et al, 2019). Fabrication: time and costs reduced (Rudolf et al, 2024).

### 6. Conclusion
Digital methods avoid casting error and shrinkage; they cost equipment and, for milling, material (Rudolf et al, 2024; Bhat et al, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Milling may remove up to 80% of the block (Rudolf et al, 2024).
- Laser sintering builds 0.02-mm layers (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Stress-relief anneal 750 to 800 C for sintered frameworks (Rudolf et al, 2024).
- One study: best fit with laser sintering (Anusavice et al, 2013).
