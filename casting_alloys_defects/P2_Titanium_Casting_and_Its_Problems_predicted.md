## STUDY MODE

### S1. Background [definition]
Titanium has a passivating oxide film about 10 nm thick and is considered the most biocompatible metal used for dental restorations (Anusavice et al, 2013). Successful casting was not achieved until the 1970s because of its reactivity with oxygen (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S2. Problems [problems]
Melting point: Phillips 1668 C (Anusavice et al, 2013); van Noort about 1670 C (van Noort, 2013); Craig 11th about 1700 C (Craig and Powers, 2002); Craig 14th 1700 C for CP Ti (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). These are melting points; casting temperatures are separate figures: van Noort tabulates about 1700 C and casting shrinkage 3.5% (van Noort, 2013), Craig 14th 1760 to 1860 C (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). High melting point makes compensation of cooling contraction difficult (van Noort, 2013).
Reactivity: it needs vacuum or inert atmosphere and special equipment, so few laboratories are set up (van Noort, 2013). Without a controlled vacuum, an oxygen-enriched hardened "case" forms, up to 100 micrometres thick, which reduces strength and ductility (Craig and Powers, 2002). Phillips gives the alpha case as approximately 150 micrometres with Vickers hardness rising from nearly 200 in the bulk to approximately 650 at a depth of 25 micrometres below the surface (Anusavice et al, 2013). van Noort gives a reaction layer of 50 to 100 micrometres (van Noort, 2013); Craig 14th says the hardened layer can be as thick as 100 micrometres (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
Investment: standard investments cannot be used; oxides more stable than titanium oxide are needed (Craig and Powers, 2002). Magnesium oxide, side by side: Phillips lists MgO, ZrO2 or Y2O3 as oxides that ensure acceptable castability (Anusavice et al, 2013); Bhat lists Al2O3, ZrO2 and MgO (Bhat et al, 2019); Craig 14th lists ZrO2, Al2O3 and MgO as inert fillers that achieve this goal (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); Craig 11th states that with magnesium oxide-based investments internal porosity results (Craig and Powers, 2002). The molten alloy leaves a surface scale that affects fit (van Noort, 2013).
Machine: low density makes centrifugal force inadequate or casting difficult in conventional centrifugal machines (Bhat et al, 2019; Craig and Powers, 2002; Sakaguchi et al, 2019). An electric arc machine in argon can reach 2000 C; crowns cast this way fitted worse than Pd-Ag and better than Ni-Cr, and multiple microcracks were seen at margins (Craig and Powers, 2002).
Porosity: internal porosity is often seen (van Noort, 2013); pressure-difference casting gave larger pores (Anusavice et al, 2013).
Finishing: machinability is poor (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S3. Solutions [management]
Arc melting with argon, special investments and machines (Anusavice et al, 2013); CAD/CAM to avoid the effect of oxygen (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 1. Introduction
Titanium is highly biocompatible but difficult to cast (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 2. Problems encountered
1. High melting point (books differ):

| Book | Value |
|---|---|
| Anusavice et al, 2013 | 1668 C |
| van Noort, 2013 | about 1670 C |
| Craig and Powers, 2002 | about 1700 C |
| Sakaguchi et al, 2019 | 1700 C |

Compensating the cooling contraction is then difficult (van Noort, 2013).
2. Reactivity: needs vacuum or inert atmosphere and special equipment (van Noort, 2013).
3. Alpha case: Craig 11th up to 100 micrometres, reducing strength and ductility (Craig and Powers, 2002); van Noort 50 to 100 (van Noort, 2013); Phillips approximately 150 (Anusavice et al, 2013).
4. Investment: usual materials cannot be used (Craig and Powers, 2002). MgO: listed by Phillips (Anusavice et al, 2013), Bhat (Bhat et al, 2019) and Craig 14th (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); Craig 11th says MgO-based investments give internal porosity (Craig and Powers, 2002). The alloy leaves a surface scale that affects fit (van Noort, 2013).
5. Casting machine: low density makes centrifugal force inadequate (Bhat et al, 2019; Craig and Powers, 2002).
6. Porosity: internal porosity is often seen (van Noort, 2013). Craig 14th reports surface porosity to a depth of 75 micrometres with old machines and silica-containing investments, and internal porosity up to 30% of the cross-section (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Pressure-difference casting gave larger pores (Anusavice et al, 2013).
7. Finishing and machining are poor (Anusavice et al, 2013); difficult to weld, solder, machine, finish and adjust (Craig and Powers, 2002).

### 3. Remedies
- Arc melting with argon, special investment (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Grind and polish away the case and porosity (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- CAD/CAM milling avoids the oxygen effect (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Porcelain bonding: poor above 800 C; reaction layer interferes (van Noort, 2013).

### 4. Conclusion
Cast titanium needs special melting, investment and machines, and its surface layer must be removed (Anusavice et al, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Melting point 1668 C (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Alpha case: hard, oxygen-rich layer (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Oxides more stable than titanium oxide needed for investment (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- CAD/CAM as an alternative (Anusavice et al, 2013).
