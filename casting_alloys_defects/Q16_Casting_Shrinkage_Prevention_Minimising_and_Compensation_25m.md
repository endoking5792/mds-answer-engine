## STUDY MODE

### S1. What shrinks [definition]
Anusavice et al state that all metals of dental interest shrink on changing from liquid to solid and on cooling from casting temperature to room temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013). Casting shrinkage therefore has two sources, wax and metal (Craig and Powers, 2002). Bhat et al write casting shrinkage as wax shrinkage plus alloy shrinkage (Bhat et al, 2019).

### S2. Alloy shrinkage [numbers by book]
Anusavice et al divide it into three stages: contraction of the liquid, the liquid-to-solid change, and contraction of the solid from solidus to room temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013). The first stage does not matter and the second is fed from the reservoir; contraction from the point the casting gains strength to move independently of the mold affects fit, because the alloy contracts more than the investment (Anusavice et al, 2013). Craig and Powers describe two stages, the change of state and the cooling of the solid (Craig and Powers, 2002). They call the change of state a large shrinkage that investment expansion cannot offset and that is compensated only by proper casting technique (Craig and Powers, 2002). Values: Anusavice et al, Table 16-14: 1.56, 1.37 and 1.42% for three gold types and 2.30% for Ni-Cr and Co-Cr (Anusavice et al, 2013). Craig and Powers: 1.25 to 2.5% typical, up to 2.5% (Craig and Powers, 2002). van Noort: 1.25 to 1.80% for gold alloys (van Noort, 2013). Bhat et al: 1.3 to 1.6% for low-fusing alloys and 2.0 to 2.3% for high-fusing alloys (Bhat et al, 2019). McCabe and Walls: 1.4% gold, 2.0% Ni-Cr, 2.3% Co-Cr (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Powers and Wataha: 1 to 2.5% (Powers and Wataha, 2013).

### S3. Wax shrinkage [numbers by book]
Anusavice et al: inlay wax may contract as much as 0.35% cooling from 37 to 25 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013). Craig and Powers: 0.4% for a fall of 12 to 13 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002). Bhat et al: 0.3 to 0.4% (Bhat et al, 2019). McCabe and Walls: 0.1 to 0.5% for a direct pattern (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### S4. Prevention and minimising [methods]
Wax: invest the pattern immediately (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002). Metal: keep the casting fed by the sprue and button so the casting freezes toward the sprue (Craig and Powers, 2002; McCabe and Walls, 2008). Craig and Powers use a Y-sprue, an extra 1 to 2 g of alloy or a mold at 650 to 700 °C against suck-back (Craig and Powers, 2002). Anusavice et al use chill-set sprues for pontics (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S5. Compensation [methods]
Craig and Powers give the balance wax shrinkage + metal shrinkage = wax expansion + setting expansion + hygroscopic expansion + thermal expansion (Craig and Powers, 2002). van Noort gives the same balance (van Noort, 2013). Anusavice et al name the hygroscopic (low-heat) and thermal (high-heat) methods (Anusavice et al, 2013). Low-heat expansion has three sources: the 37 °C bath expanding the wax, hygroscopic expansion, and thermal expansion (Anusavice et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020). Anusavice et al add that the 37 °C bath effect may be caused mainly by heating and expanding the wax rather than by hygroscopic expansion alone (Anusavice et al, 2013). Other controls: liners, liquid/powder ratio, die spacer and a Type V die (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002; Sikri, 2020).

### S6. Numbers for expansion [by book]
Anusavice et al: for Type I investments, which rely principally on thermal expansion, thermal expansion not less than 1% nor more than 1.6%; for Type II investments, thermal expansion 0% to 0.6% at 500 °C, and setting expansion in water 1.2% minimum and 2.2% maximum (Anusavice et al, 2013). Bhat et al: normal setting expansion up to 0.3 to 0.5%, hygroscopic 1.0 to 2.0% (Bhat et al, 2019). McCabe and Walls: gypsum setting about 0.3%, about 1.3% with hygroscopic expansion (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### S7. Critical evidence note [one claim]
The claim is that the investment expansion can be set to equal total shrinkage. Craig and Powers state the balance as an equation and say the relative contribution of each expansion does not matter if the total is sufficient (Craig and Powers, 2002). Anusavice et al state that the allowable tolerance is not known and that the casting procedure is partly empirical (Anusavice et al, 2013). They also note that a cristobalite thermal expansion of 1.3% plus 0.3 to 0.4% setting expansion can give up to 1.7%, above the average gold shrinkage, so a crown may be too large (Anusavice et al, 2013). Craig and Powers give an accuracy tolerance of plus or minus 0.05% for an inlay (Craig and Powers, 2002).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 42 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Casting shrinkage is the net dimensional loss between wax pattern and finished casting, from shrinkage of the wax and of the alloy (Craig and Powers, 2002). If uncorrected the casting is too small and does not seat (Craig and Powers, 2002). The aim is to prevent avoidable shrinkage, minimise the rest, and compensate for it by enlarging the mold (Craig and Powers, 2002).

van Noort gives 1.25 to 1.80% for gold alloys (van Noort, 2013). Powers and Wataha give 1 to 2.5% (Powers and Wataha, 2013). The books differ on which stage affects fit. Anusavice et al: contraction after the casting gains strength affects fit, the liquid-to-solid change being fed from the reservoir (Anusavice et al, 2013). Craig and Powers: the liquid-to-solid change is a large shrinkage compensated only by proper casting technique (Craig and Powers, 2002).

### 3. Prevention and minimising
**Wax shrinkage.**
- Invest the pattern immediately after it is made (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Form the pattern with warmed instruments and a warmed die to reduce stress (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Expanding the wax in warm water before investing was once used but distorted the pattern and is not practised (Bhat et al, 2019); Craig and Powers call it impractical for the same reason (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Metal shrinkage (feeding).**
- Freeze the casting toward the sprue, with the sprue and button feeding it; investment expansion cannot compensate suck-back porosity (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Use a Y-sprue, an extra 1 to 2 g of alloy, or a mold temperature of 650 to 700 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Add a reservoir near the casting, which freezes last (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Place a small chill-set sprue on a pontic (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 4. Compensation
**Principle.** Wax shrinkage + metal shrinkage = wax expansion + setting expansion + hygroscopic expansion + thermal expansion (Craig and Powers, 2002; van Noort, 2013).

**Methods (Anusavice et al, 2013).**
1. Thermal (high-heat) method: investment expansion on heating to 650 to 700 °C for gypsum-bonded and up to 1050 °C for phosphate-bonded investment (Anusavice et al, 2013).
2. Hygroscopic (low-heat) method: a 37 °C bath expands the wax, water entering gives hygroscopic expansion, and thermal expansion supplies the rest (Anusavice et al, 2013). Sikri adds that a higher water/powder ratio and a longer delay before immersion reduce the expansion (Sikri, 2020).
3. Controls: two liners and a lower liquid/powder ratio give more expansion, and a liner 3.25 mm short of the ring ends evens it (Anusavice et al, 2013).
4. Die spacer: Craig and Powers describe the 10 to 30 µm spacer as space for cement, not a cure for improper manipulation, and not for the margins (Craig and Powers, 2002). For base metals they paint a spacer short of the margins for extra compensation (Craig and Powers, 2002). Sikri says the spacer compensates solidification shrinkage of large crown and bridge patterns (Sikri, 2020). A Type V die gives larger expansion for base metals (Anusavice et al, 2013).

Sakaguchi et al state that setting and thermal expansion together compensate casting shrinkage (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Marzouk states that the investment must change dimension opposite to, and so compensate, shrinkage of the wax pattern, the binder and the alloy (Marzouk, year not printed). Powers and Wataha state that present investments rely mainly on thermal expansion (Powers and Wataha, 2013).

### 5. Base-metal alloys
They shrink more, so they need more mold expansion: a die spacer and two liners (Craig and Powers, 2002). Bhat et al add colloidal silica with phosphate investment (Bhat et al, 2019).

### 6. Limits
Total expansion can exceed shrinkage: cristobalite thermal expansion of 1.3% with 0.3 to 0.4% setting expansion can reach 1.7%, so a crown may be too large (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 7. Conclusion
Shrinkage is prevented by feeding and prompt investing, and compensated by balancing investment expansion against wax and alloy shrinkage (Craig and Powers, 2002).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Balance: wax shrinkage + metal shrinkage = wax + setting + hygroscopic + thermal expansion (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Three stages of alloy shrinkage; the third matters for fit (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Low-heat method: 37 °C bath, hygroscopic, thermal (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Suck-back is not cured by investment expansion (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Wax expansion before investing is not practised (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Over-compensation can give a crown that is too large (Anusavice et al, 2013).
