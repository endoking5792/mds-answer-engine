## STUDY MODE

### S1. Scope and terms [definition]
Cast restorations are made by the lost wax casting technique (van Noort, 2013). Phillips says the noble metals are gold, palladium, platinum, rhodium, ruthenium, iridium and osmium, and that silver is reactive in the oral cavity and is not considered noble (Anusavice et al, 2013); Craig 11th agrees that silver is not considered noble in dentistry (Craig and Powers, 2002), whereas van Noort calls silver and palladium "precious" (van Noort, 2013). Bhat records that alloys with Pd above 50 wt% were once named semiprecious and predominantly base metal alloys nonprecious, "but these terms are not used now" (Bhat et al, 2019). Bhat elsewhere says silver-palladium alloys are sometimes known as semiprecious metals (Bhat et al, 2019), whereas Sikri's glossary defines a semi-precious alloy as one composed of precious and base metals in different ratios (Sikri, Indirect Restorations, year not printed).

### S2. Classification, as each book prints it [classification]
- Phillips: in 1932 gold alloys were classed by Vickers hardness as type I (50 to 90), II (90 to 120), III (120 to 150) and IV (150 and above) (Anusavice et al, 2013); the ADA 1984 scheme has three categories, high noble, noble and predominantly base metal (Anusavice et al, 2013); the ISO draft table lists four types by yield strength 80, 180, 270 and 360 MPa with elongation 18, 10, 5 and 3% (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Craig 11th: in the old ADA No. 5 the noble metal content of Types I to IV ranged from 83 to 75 wt% and all were gold based; the current ADA specification has high-noble, noble and predominately base metal groups (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Craig 14th: Table 10.3 lists high-noble, titanium and titanium alloys (titanium 85% or more), noble and predominantly base groups, with Types I-IV set by yield strength (annealed) below 140, 140 to 200, 201 to 340 and above 340 MPa and elongation 18, 18, 12 and 10% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- van Noort: high-gold alloys have precious metal content not less than 75% and gold above 60%, in four types, soft to extra hard (van Noort, 2013).
- Bhat: the earlier ADA No. 5 set minimum gold at 85, 78, 78 and 75% with hardness 50-90, 90-120, 120-150 and above 150 VHN, and its Table 18.2 gives minimum yield of 80, 180, 240 or 270 and 300 or 360 MPa for the ADA 1997 or 2002 columns (Bhat et al, 2019).
- McCabe and Walls: ISO 1562 Types 1-4, with noble metal at least 75% and at least 65% of it gold; alloys with 25% up to but not including 75% noble metal fall under ISO 8891 (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Powers and Wataha: three ADA groups only (Powers and Wataha, 2013). Rudolf et al: Table 1.5 lists four types of gold alloys (1927) by VHN, and Table 1.6 lists five types by properties, not composition (Rudolf et al, 2024).

### S3. Gold alloys, composition and properties [composition]
van Noort gives ranges of Au 80-90, 75-78, 62-78 and 60-70% for Types I to IV (van Noort, 2013); McCabe and Walls give typical Au 85, 75, 70 and 65% (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Hardness, strength and proportional limit rise from Type 1 to 4 and corrosion resistance falls as gold falls (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Phillips: copper in enough amount makes the alloy heat-treatable, zinc is an oxygen scavenger, and a Pd content of about 1.5 wt% improves tarnish resistance (Anusavice et al, 2013). van Noort: copper is limited to 16%, and at least 11% copper is needed for order hardening, so Types I and II cannot be hardened (van Noort, 2013).

### S4. Variations and limitations [comparison]
- Gold Types: Type I and II have high elongation and are easily burnished but suit low stress only (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Type IV cannot be burnished once hardened and is unsuitable for inlays (van Noort, 2013). For inlays van Noort says Type I suits single-surface low-stress inlays, Type II most inlays and Type III all inlays and onlays (van Noort, 2013); McCabe and Walls say Type 2 is the most widely used for inlays and Type 3 serves crowns and inlays in high-stress class II molars (McCabe and Walls, 2008); Phillips says Type 1 and 2 are used for inlays and Type 3 for crowns and onlays (Anusavice et al, 2013). Phillips says a hardening treatment is not usually required for small structures such as inlays (Anusavice et al, 2013); McCabe and Walls say it is hardly ever done for type 3 (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Medium- and low-gold alloys: lower ductility and high yield make burnishing hard (van Noort, 2013); McCabe and Walls state some contain as little as 10% gold but more normally around 45-50% (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Silver-palladium: at least 25% Pd; castability reported poor, though acceptable castings are possible with close control of casting and mould temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013); some compare favourably with gold alloys and are a suitable alternative if care is taken (McCabe and Walls, 2008); long spans contraindicated, tendency to work-harden rapidly, and one alloy (Palliag W) suits only low-stress inlays (van Noort, 2013).
- Base metal: about half the density of gold (Anusavice et al, 2013); most difficult to cast and polish (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- Titanium: melting point 1668 °C, special casting machine needed (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S5. Hazards [safety]
Phillips: nickel is a moderate allergen (Anusavice et al, 2013). Craig 14th: nickel sensitivity 5% to 8% of females, with no correlation found between intraoral nickel restorations and sensitivity, and beryllium limited to 0.02 wt% by ISO (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); the ADA Council recommends avoiding beryllium-containing alloys where possible (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Phillips also reports no correlation between nickel sensitivity and intraoral nickel-alloy restorations (Anusavice et al, 2013). McCabe and Walls: beryllium is a known animal carcinogen (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### S6. Critical evidence note: what "Types I-IV" means [critical appraisal]
The books use the same labels for two different systems. Craig 11th says the old ADA types were set by composition and gold content, and that the present types are set by mechanical properties, so a Type III label gives no indication of composition (Craig and Powers, 2002; Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Rudolf et al add that since 1989 any composition may be used if the tests are passed (Rudolf et al, 2024). The yield limits also differ between books: Phillips lists 80, 180, 270 and 360 MPa in an ISO draft and remarks that ADA No. 5 (1997) and ISO 1562 use similar values (Anusavice et al, 2013); Craig 14th lists below 140, 140-200, 201-340 and above 340 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); Bhat lists 80, 180, 240 or 270 and 300 or 360 MPa (Bhat et al, 2019). The books were not compared against the standards themselves, so no value is judged correct here.

### 1. Introduction
Inlays are made by the lost wax casting technique (van Noort, 2013). Metals: gold, lower-gold and palladium alloys, base metals, titanium (Anusavice et al, 2013).

High noble: noble metals 60 wt% or more and gold 40% or more; noble: 25% or more; predominately base metal: below 25% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 3. Gold alloys: composition and properties
| Type | Au, Ag, Cu % (van Noort) | VHN; elongation % (McCabe and Walls) |
|---|---|---|
| I | 80-90, 3-12, 2-5 (van Noort, 2013) | 50-90; 35 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| II | 75-78, 12-15, 7-10 (van Noort, 2013) | 90-120; 25 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| III | 62-78, 8-26, 8-11 (van Noort, 2013) | 120-160; 20 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| IV | 60-70, 4-20, 11-16 (van Noort, 2013) | 150-230; 15 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |

Type 1 is soft and designed for inlays, Type 2 is widely used for inlays (Anusavice et al, 2013); Type 3 serves crowns and inlays in high-stress class II molars (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### 4. Variations and limitations of each metal
1. **Gold Types I and II:** easily burnished, low stress only (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). They cannot be hardened; at least 11% copper is needed (van Noort, 2013).
2. **Gold Types III and IV:** age hardening raises strength but lowers ductility; Type IV cannot be burnished and is unsuitable for inlays (van Noort, 2013). Hardening is not usually required for small inlays (Anusavice et al, 2013) and is hardly ever done for type 3 (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
3. **Medium and low gold:** gold 40-60% and 10-20% (van Noort, 2013); some as little as 10%, more normally 45-50% (McCabe and Walls, 2008); Marzouk's low-gold class has gold under 50%, some as little as 5% (Marzouk, year not printed). Limitation: lower ductility, burnishing difficult (van Noort, 2013).
4. **Silver-palladium:** at least 25% Pd; poor castability reported, yet acceptable castings with close control (Anusavice et al, 2013); some castability compares favourably with gold, but molten Pd dissolves oxygen (McCabe and Walls, 2008); work-hardens rapidly, and Palliag W suits only low-stress inlays (van Noort, 2013).
5. **Palladium-copper-gallium:** strongest noble alloy, yield 1145 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); difficult to use, higher corrosion (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
6. **Cobalt-chromium and nickel-chromium:** ANSI/ADA No. 14 minima for removable-denture alloys: Cr 20 wt% or more, Cr+Co+Ni 85 or more, yield 500 MPa, elongation 1.5% (Craig and Powers, 2002; Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Half the density of gold (Anusavice et al, 2013); most difficult to cast and polish (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
7. **Titanium:** melting point 1668 °C, special casting machine (Anusavice et al, 2013); McCabe and Walls say its use is mainly restricted to preformed implants (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
8. **Not for casting:** bronze "technique alloys" (Bhat et al, 2019).

### 5. Hazards
Nickel is a moderate allergen (Anusavice et al, 2013); 5% to 8% of females are sensitive, but no correlation was found with intraoral nickel restorations (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Anusavice et al, 2013). Beryllium is a known animal carcinogen (McCabe and Walls, 2008); the ADA Council advises avoiding it where possible (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 6. Conclusion
Per van Noort Type I suits single-surface low-stress inlays, II most inlays and III all inlays (van Noort, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Three ADA groups: high noble (60% noble, 40% gold), noble (25%), predominately base metal (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Old Types I-IV by gold content; present types by yield and elongation (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Copper at least 11% is needed to harden gold (van Noort, 2013).
- Silver is not noble in dentistry (Anusavice et al, 2013).
