## 1. Introduction and classification

### Explanation

Grossman's classifies shaping instruments by method of use: group I hand-operated (broaches, K-type reamers and files, H-files); group II low-speed stainless steel latch instruments (Gates-Glidden, Peeso); group III sonic and ultrasonic; group IV engine-driven NiTi (rotary, reciprocating, canal-adaptive) (Gopikrishna, 2021). Hülsmann and Schäfer call the earlier automated devices "conventional rotary systems" (Giromatic, Canal Finder, Excalibur) and note that "these systems lost popularity after the introduction of rotary NiTi systems" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's Table 14.6 lists the hand techniques chronologically (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Root canals can be prepared with hand instruments, manipulated by the fingers, or with automated (engine-driven) instruments powered by a handpiece or motor. Hand instrumentation was the only method for most of the twentieth century; automated preparation became practical only when nickel-titanium (NiTi) allowed files to rotate in curved canals without fracturing (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).

**Classification (Gopikrishna, 2021)**
- *Hand instruments:* barbed broaches; stainless steel reamers, K-files, K-Flex, Flex-R; Hedström and S-files; NiTi hand files; greater-taper NiTi hand files.
- *Automated:* (a) low-speed stainless steel (Gates-Glidden, Peeso); (b) conventional reciprocating and vertical handpieces with stainless steel files (Giromatic, Canal Finder, Excalibur), now abandoned; (c) sonic and ultrasonic; (d) engine-driven NiTi: continuous rotation, reciprocation, canal-adaptive (SAF, XP-endo).

## 2. Hand instrumentation

### Explanation

Hülsmann and Schäfer review each hand instrument. Reamers produce round preparations in straight canals but transport curved ones; "the sole use of reamers to prepare root canals is not recommended" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. K-files give conflicting results; a combination of reaming and filing appears effective. H-files cut most aggressively and "should not be used in the apical part of curved root canals", but they are useful "following rotary NiTi preparation of root canals having an oval cross-section" and for isthmuses, which flexible NiTi files cannot be steered into (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Flexible stainless steel files with non-cutting tips are the best of the stainless steel group, "out of all stainless steel instruments for hand use" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC], but still limit apical enlargement in severe curves.

NiTi hand files have about three times the elastic flexibility of stainless steel (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]; stainless steel deforms permanently at about 1% strain (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. But because they are superelastic, "NiTi hand instruments are not suitable for probing and scouting" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]; they are ground, not twisted (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC], and cut less efficiently, one NiTi Hedström type cutting about 60% less (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Greater-taper NiTi files used by hand "should be preferably used as engine-driven instruments" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC].

The decisive advantage of stainless steel hand files is precurving and tactile feedback: Schäfer, "stainless steel hand instruments are best suited for creating a glide path" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]; Grossman's establishes the glide path with "a precurved ISO size 10 or ISO size 8" file (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Of hand techniques, balanced force "creates the least canal aberrations" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC] and is "the most effective hand instrumentation technique" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Hand instrumentation**

*Instruments and their place (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019)*
- **Reamers:** round preparations in straight canals; transport curved canals; not recommended alone.
- **K-files:** most versatile; used in watch-winding, reaming, filing or balanced force; easily precurved.
- **Flexible stainless steel files (K-Flexofile, Flex-R) with non-cutting tips:** best stainless steel instruments for curved canals, but apical enlargement in severe curves stays small.
- **Hedström and S-files:** most efficient cutting; should not be used apically in curved canals; ideal for circumferential filing of oval canals and isthmuses after rotary preparation.
- **NiTi hand files:** about three times more flexible; centre better in curves, especially above size 30; but cannot be precurved, give less tactile feedback and cut less efficiently.

*Hand techniques:* standardized, step-back, passive step-back, crown-down, balanced force (the best hand technique for centring) and hybrid (Q01).

*Strengths of hand instrumentation*
1. Excellent tactile feedback: the operator feels calcifications, curves, ledges and canal contents.
2. Stainless steel files can be precurved to negotiate curves, bypass ledges and find calcified canals; they are best for scouting and the glide path (Schäfer, 2017).
3. Low cost, no motor, no file fracture from cyclic fatigue.
4. Circumferential filing reaches oval recesses that rotary files miss.

*Weaknesses*
1. Stainless steel stiffens as size increases and tends to straighten curved canals, producing ledges, zips, elbows and perforations.
2. Slow and tiring; many instruments; operator skill influences results more than with NiTi (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).
3. Apical enlargement in curved canals limited to small sizes, compromising irrigation.
4. Step-back preparations with Gates-Glidden drills are larger than necessary coronally (Peters and Arias, 2025).

## 3. Automated (engine-driven) instrumentation

### Explanation

Conventional automated handpieces with stainless steel files straightened curves and caused blockage, perforation and fracture, and "lost popularity" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Haapasalo and Chen's five generations of NiTi are reported by Hülsmann and Schäfer (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]; they prefer the term "NiTi systems rather than NiTi instruments" because each is used in a defined sequence (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues explain the mechanical basis: a no. 45 NiTi file bends with the load needed for a no. 25 stainless steel file (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]; Schäfer: recovery from strain "up to 10%" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]. The weakness is fracture, mostly through cyclic fatigue (Jain, 2016) [SRC]; reported rates are 0.4-5% for rotary and 1-6% for hand files (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], so NiTi rotaries do not break more often than hand files in these figures.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Automated instrumentation**
1. **Conventional handpieces with stainless steel files** (Giromatic 1964, Canal Finder, Excalibur): rigid motions that straightened curves and caused blockages and perforations; abandoned after NiTi (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).
2. **Low-speed stainless steel drills:** Gates-Glidden and Peeso for coronal flaring and post space; now largely replaced by NiTi orifice shapers.
3. **Sonic and ultrasonic:** effective for irrigant activation, not for shaping (Q08).
4. **Engine-driven NiTi systems** (five generations: constant taper with radial lands; active blades; heat-treated alloys; reciprocation; asymmetric cross-sections) used as crown-down, single-length or single-file systems (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).

*Strengths:* superelasticity (a #45 NiTi file bends like a #25 stainless steel file) gives centred preparations with less transportation and fewer errors (Siqueira et al, 2022); greater tapers give a continuous funnel with few instruments in less time; consistent shapes with less dependence on operator skill.

*Weaknesses:* fracture without warning by cyclic fatigue or torsion (Jain, 2016); cost of motors and single-use files; poor tactile feedback; cannot be precurved, so cannot bypass ledges; round preparations leave oval recesses untouched; possible debris extrusion and dentinal cracks with some systems.

## 4. Comparison and critical evaluation

### Explanation

Clinical evidence: Siqueira and colleagues report that NiTi hand files were "five times more likely to achieve healing" than stainless steel files, and rotary NiTi healed 77% against 60% for stainless steel hand files (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]; one study found no difference. The ESE guideline, as reported by Peters and Arias, recommends "contemporary engine-driven techniques with NiTi root canal instruments" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer add a caution from micro-CT: even with greater-taper NiTi used by hand, 28-83% "of the root canal surfaces were not prepared" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]; and they raise Gambill's point that NiTi's lower transportation may partly reflect lower cutting efficiency (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang: "no consensus or clinical evidence on which technique or instrument design or type is clinically superior" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Comparison**

| Feature | Stainless steel hand | NiTi hand | Engine-driven NiTi |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flexibility | Low, falls with size | About 3× SS | High; heat-treated alloys higher |
| Precurving and tactile feel | Excellent | Poor | Poor |
| Centring in curves | Poor above size 25-30 | Good | Very good |
| Transportation and ledges | Frequent | Less | Least |
| Speed and number of files | Slow, many | Slow | Fast, few |
| Fracture | Deforms visibly first | Without warning | Without warning (fatigue) |
| Oval canals | Good with circumferential filing | Poor | Poor (round shapes) |
| Cost | Low | Moderate | High |
| Best use | Scouting, glide path, ledges, calcified canals, oval recesses | Curved canals by hand | Routine shaping of most canals |

**Critical evaluation**
- Engine-driven NiTi gives better-shaped canals faster with fewer errors, and outcome studies favour NiTi: 77% healing against 60% with stainless steel hand files (Siqueira et al, 2022). The ESE guideline recommends engine-driven NiTi (Peters and Arias, 2025).
- But neither method cleans the whole canal: 28-83% of walls remained untouched in one micro-CT study (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019); irrigation remains essential.
- Some of NiTi's centring may come from lower cutting efficiency, not flexibility alone (Gambill, in Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).
- The two methods are complementary, not rivals: stainless steel hand files scout, negotiate and create the glide path; NiTi shapes; Hedström files clean oval recesses. This hybrid use, adapting the tool to the canal (Peters and Arias, 2025), is the current standard.

**Conclusion.** Hand instruments remain indispensable for negotiation, glide path, bypassing ledges and difficult anatomy; engine-driven NiTi has become the method of choice for shaping. The best results come from combining them.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

- Stainless steel deforms permanently at about 1% strain; NiTi recovers 8-10%.
- NiTi hand files about 3× as flexible as stainless steel but cannot be precurved.
- Reamers alone not recommended; H-files not apically in curves; H-files best for oval recesses.
- Balanced force: best hand technique.
- Healing 77% with rotary NiTi vs 60% with SS hand files (one study).
- Fracture rates: rotary 0.4-5%, hand 1-6%.
- ESE S3: engine-driven NiTi recommended.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

- Treating "automated" as meaning only rotary NiTi; mention conventional handpieces, sonic/ultrasonic and reciprocation.
- Claiming rotary NiTi cleans the canal completely.
- Omitting the indispensable role of stainless steel hand files for scouting and glide path.
- Stating that NiTi breaks more often than hand files without data.

## MNEMONIC

**"FEEL" for why hand files survive:** **F**eel (tactile); **E**ngage curves by precurving; **E**xploration and glide path; **L**edge bypass.
