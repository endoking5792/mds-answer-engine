## 1. Introduction, definitions and terminology

### Explanation

Grossman's defines the procedure as "removing the pulp tissue and debris from the canal and shaping the canal to receive an obturating material", and keeps the word debridement for the removal of food, debris and necrotic pulp that has accumulated in the pulp cavity (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer give the more modern definition: preparation uses "mechanical and chemical means to remove pulpal tissue, reduce microbial load, and enable appropriate obturation" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. The two definitions differ in emphasis, not substance: Grossman's makes obturation the end point, Ingle's makes microbial reduction the first aim.

Three names are used for the same procedure, and examiners like the distinction:
- **Biomechanical preparation (BMP):** Schilder's term, which carries both biological and mechanical objectives (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
- **Chemomechanical preparation:** because canal fins, lateral canals and the apical delta cannot be reached by files, a chemical agent is needed; Sikri calls this the "concept of chemomechanical preparation" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues use this as their chapter title for the same reason.
- **Shaping and cleaning:** Peters, Peters and Basrani argue that reversing the usual order "may more correctly reflect the fact that enlarged canals direct and facilitate the cleaning action of irrigants" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri makes the same point, and Grossman's 14th edition titles its chapter "Shaping and cleaning".

Schäfer places the procedure inside the traditional endodontic triad, noting that "cleaning, shaping and filling remains relevant" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Cleaning and shaping (biomechanical preparation) is the phase of root canal treatment that removes the pulp tissue, debris and microorganisms from the root canal system and gives the canal a shape that can be disinfected and filled (Gopikrishna, 2021). It uses mechanical and chemical means to remove pulp tissue, reduce microbial load and allow obturation (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019). It forms the middle part of the endodontic triad of access, cleaning and shaping, and obturation (Schäfer, 2017).

**Definitions and terms**
- **Cleaning:** removal of all organic and inorganic contents (vital or necrotic pulp, debris, microorganisms and their products) from the root canal system.
- **Shaping:** enlargement of the canal into a tapered form that admits irrigants, medicaments and the obturating material.
- **Debridement:** removal of accumulated debris and necrotic pulp from the pulp cavity (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Biomechanical preparation:** Schilder's term, combining biological and mechanical objectives (Sikri, 2019).
- **Chemomechanical preparation:** the same procedure stressing that irrigants are needed for areas files cannot reach (Sikri, 2019; Siqueira et al, 2022).
- **"Shaping and cleaning":** the order is sometimes reversed because the canal must be enlarged before irrigants can clean it (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

## 2. Rationale: why cleaning and shaping matter

### Explanation

Every textbook read makes the same argument against the older view that obturation decides outcome. Grossman's calls shaping and cleaning "the most important phase of endodontic treatment" and states that obturating an improperly cleaned canal "would still lead to an endodontic failure irrespective of the quality of obturation" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang say the early belief that failures came from incomplete obturation "proved fallacious as obturation merely reflects the adequacy of the cleaning and shaping" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues trace that belief to the Washington study, in which "more than 60% of teeth with posttreatment disease were associated with inadequate root canal fillings", and point out that a poor filling and a poor outcome occurring together are not cause and effect; the filling "merely reflects the aptness of chemomechanical procedures" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Grossman's adds the histological evidence: examination of failed pulpless teeth "often shows that the canals were not completely cleaned" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri expresses it as the axiom "what comes out is more important than what goes in" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's opens its chapter with a similar line.

Peters, Peters and Basrani compare treatment to "a chain of events, wherein the chain is only as strong as each individual link", and treat shaping as the decisive link because it determines how well irrigation and obturation can work (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. They report favourable outcomes of up to 95% for teeth with irreversible pulpitis (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

A newer element in the rationale comes from Peters, Arias and Shabahang: vertical fracture and other non-endodontic causes are "the major reasons for the eventual loss" of root-filled teeth (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. This adds a second aim to the classical one: shape enough to disinfect, but not so much that the root is weakened. Sections 3, 7 and 9 return to this tension.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Rationale**
1. Shaping and cleaning is the most important phase of root canal treatment; obturation of an improperly cleaned canal leads to failure whatever the quality of the filling (Gopikrishna, 2021).
2. The old belief that failures are due to poor obturation "proved fallacious": obturation merely reflects the adequacy of cleaning and shaping (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). In the Washington study more than 60% of failures had inadequate fillings, but those canals had also been poorly cleaned (Siqueira et al, 2022).
3. Histological examination of failed root-filled teeth often shows canals that were not completely cleaned (Gopikrishna, 2021). "What comes out is more important than what goes in" (Sikri, 2019).
4. Well-prepared canals heal predictably: favourable outcomes of up to 95% are reported for irreversible pulpitis (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
5. Treatment is a chain of events only as strong as its weakest link; shaping decides how well irrigation and obturation can work (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
6. Most root-filled teeth are now lost to vertical fracture and other non-endodontic causes, so preparation must also preserve tooth structure (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

## 3. Objectives of cleaning and shaping

### Explanation

**Schilder's objectives (1974).** Hülsmann and Schäfer date them to Schilder's 1974 paper, which set out five mechanical and five biological objectives, and remark that "some of these objectives are still valid more than 40 years later" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. The textbooks do not reproduce the list identically, and the differences should be shown rather than smoothed over:

| Source | Mechanical objectives | Biological objectives |
|---|---|---|
| Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021) | Continuous tapering cone, narrowest apically; walls taper evenly and are confluent with the access; "quality of flow", a shape that lets plasticized gutta-percha flow without impedance; apical foramen as small as practical; no transportation of the foramen [SRC] | Confinement of instrumentation to the roots; necrotic debris not forced beyond the foramen; removal of all tissue; "creation of sufficient space for optimal obturation" (four items) [SRC] |
| Ingle's (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) | Continuously tapering funnel; cross-section narrower at every point apically; preparation flows with the original canal; foramen in its original position; foramen as small as practical | Confinement to the roots; no debris beyond the foramen; removal of all tissue and debris; sufficient space for intracanal medicaments; "completion of preparation in one appointment" (five items) [SRC] |
| Sikri (2019) | Same five as Ingle's | Confinement; no debris beyond the foramen; "removal of substrate harboring microorganisms"; space for intracanal medicaments (four items) [SRC] |

So Grossman's has the "flow" concept among the mechanical aims and space for obturation as a biological aim, Ingle's has space for medicaments and single-appointment completion, and Sikri has removal of the microbial substrate. A student should give Schilder's five plus five as in Ingle's and mention the variants. Peters, Peters and Basrani add a critical note: Schilder's uniform continuous taper "was aimed at facilitating obturation rather than targeting antimicrobial efficacy" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Contemporary objectives.** Cohen's Box 8.1 reduces the aims to four: remove infected soft and hard tissue; give disinfecting irrigants access to the apical canal space; create space for medicaments and obturation; and "retain the integrity of radicular structures" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer's list adds neutralization of microbial toxins and byproducts and preservation of sound root dentine (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's states one overriding objective, to debride the canal "without compromising the structural integrity of the root" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and gives four reasons for widening canals: mechanical removal of microorganisms, complete removal of pulp tissue (because "tags of pulp tissue and odontoblasts cling to the canal wall"), room for irrigants to reach the apical third, and space for gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Peters, Arias and Shabahang name "elimination (or significant reduction) of inflamed or necrotic pulp tissue and microorganisms" as "the most critical factors" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang list the criteria of canal preparation as a continuously tapered funnel, the original canal shape maintained, the foramen kept in its original position, the apical opening as small as possible, and "glassy smooth walls" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani define the ideal mechanical objective as "complete and centered incorporation of the original canals into the prepared shape", then admit "this goal is unlikely to be met with current techniques" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; they also cite 0.3 mm of remaining wall as "considered critical by some authors" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Schilder's objectives (1974): five mechanical and five biological (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019)**

| Mechanical objectives | Biological objectives |
|---|---|
| 1. Continuously tapering funnel from apex to access cavity | 1. Confine instrumentation to the roots themselves |
| 2. Cross-sectional diameter narrower at every point apically | 2. Do not force necrotic debris beyond the foramen |
| 3. Preparation flows with the shape of the original canal | 3. Remove all tissue and debris from the canal space |
| 4. Apical foramen kept in its original position | 4. Create sufficient space for intracanal medicaments |
| 5. Apical foramen kept as small as practical | 5. Complete preparation in one appointment |

Textbooks list these slightly differently. Grossman's adds "quality of flow" (a shape that lets plasticized gutta-percha flow against the walls) and replaces the last two biological items with space for optimal obturation (Gopikrishna, 2021); Sikri lists "removal of substrate harboring microorganisms" (Sikri, 2019). Peters, Peters and Basrani note that Schilder's uniform taper was designed to ease obturation, not to maximise disinfection (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**Contemporary objectives (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019)**
1. Remove vital and necrotic pulp and infected hard tissue.
2. Eliminate or substantially reduce microorganisms and biofilm, and neutralise their toxins.
3. Give irrigants access to the apical canal space.
4. Create space for medicaments and obturation.
5. Preserve the position and integrity of the apical anatomy and avoid iatrogenic damage.
6. Retain sound radicular dentine so the tooth survives long term.

**Why canals are widened (Gopikrishna, 2021):** to remove microorganisms mechanically; to remove pulp tags and odontoblasts clinging to the walls; to let irrigants reach the apical third; to receive gutta-percha.

**Most critical factor:** elimination, or significant reduction, of inflamed or necrotic pulp tissue and microorganisms; adequate cleaning and shaping plus a coronal seal matter more for short-term success than the obturation itself (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

**Criteria of a correct preparation (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021):** continuously tapered funnel, original canal shape maintained, foramen in its original position, apical opening as small as possible, glassy smooth walls. The ideal of incorporating the whole original canal into the preparation is rarely met with current instruments (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

## 4. Pretreatment assessment and the limits of mechanical preparation

### Explanation

Siqueira and colleagues summarise the anatomical problem: whatever technique, instrument and irrigant is used, "chemomechanical procedures alone are unable to achieve thorough cleaning of the root canal system", because recesses, isthmuses, lateral canals and apical ramifications remain untouched (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang therefore set a realistic target: "a practical objective of cleaning is to significantly reduce the irritants, not totally eliminate them", and conclude that mechanical shaping and irrigation must be used "in synergy" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

Hülsmann and Schäfer insist that "each root canal has to be addressed individually", planned from careful radiographic evaluation, with CBCT where anatomy is complex (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang list the anatomical features that raise difficulty and recommend the AAE "Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]: long roots, narrow curved apical roots, mid-root concavities, curvatures that are not seen because "canals are seldom straight, and curvatures in a buccolingual direction are normally not" visible (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; "roots with an S-shape or bayonet configuration are very difficult to treat"; mineralization that "generally occurs in a coronal to apical direction" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; and internal or external resorption. They add that "small, long, and curved canals are the most difficult and tedious to enlarge" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer add that the operator's skill has a larger effect on shape, working time and errors with stainless steel instruments and a smaller one with rotary NiTi (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pretreatment assessment**
- Read the preoperative radiograph (and CBCT where needed) for root and canal number, length, curvature (degree, radius, site, S-shape), canal width, calcification, resorption and periapical status; assess case difficulty.
- Each canal is planned individually (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019); grade difficulty with the AAE Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Difficulty rises with long roots, narrow curved apical thirds, mid-root concavities (strip perforation risk), buccolingual curves invisible on a radiograph, S-shaped or bayonet roots, coronal-to-apical mineralization, and internal or external resorption (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). Small, long, curved canals are the hardest to enlarge and the most prone to loss of length and transportation.

**Limits of mechanical preparation**
- Instruments do not reach fins, isthmuses, lateral canals and apical ramifications; chemomechanical procedures alone cannot fully clean the canal system (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- The practical aim is to significantly reduce irritants, not to eliminate them; shaping and irrigation must act in synergy (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Operator skill matters more with stainless steel than with rotary NiTi (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).

## 5. Steps of preparation: coronal pre-enlargement, glide path, patency and working length

### Explanation

**Sequence.** Grossman's divides shaping into "five phases": glide path determination, coronal pre-enlargement, working length measurement, root canal shaping techniques and working width (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer list six parts starting with the coronal access cavity and ending with the middle and apical thirds. Peters, Arias and Shabahang describe preparation in stages: "Stage 1 is a defined preflaring, before bringing any hand file to the apical third", with Stages 2 and 3 reaching working length according to curvature (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

**Coronal pre-enlargement.** Grossman's: enlarging the orifice and 2-3 mm of the coronal third "reduces the stresses on subsequent shaping instruments", and working length "should be determined only after coronal pre-enlargement" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; it also claims that pre-enlargement reduces "the potential for extrusion of debris beyond the working length" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani rename the step "orifice modification" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC] and are more cautious: early irrigant access and less apical pushing of debris "has not been confirmed in experiments", while the documented benefit is "mitigation of WL changes" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. The two books therefore agree on doing it but disagree on what it is proven to achieve.

The instrument for it is also disputed. Grossman's says Gates-Glidden drills are "currently not recommended" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and Peters, Arias and Shabahang call them "an unsuitable instrument for modern endodontics" because of the dentine they remove (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer, in contrast, describe the drill as "safe, relatively inexpensive" and effective, noting that "fracture usually occurs high at the shaft, making removal easy" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Both views are current; the newer textbooks lean towards NiTi orifice shapers. Peters, Arias and Shabahang ground the caution in the finding that preserving pericervical dentine "(4 mm above and below the crestal bone)" is crucial for stress distribution (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

**Glide path.** Grossman's defines it as creating "a patent path up to the working length without altering or blocking the original root canal anatomy" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The books differ on technique: Grossman's uses "a precurved ISO size 10 or ISO size 8" K-file (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], whereas Peters, Arias and Shabahang say hand files for glide path preparation "should not be precurved", so that rotaries can follow the path predictably (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani set the end point as the moment "a straight size #15 K-file can passively and smoothly travel to WL", and explain that the glide path "minimizes the so-called screw-in or threading-in effect" and torsional failure (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. A rotary glide path "has demonstrated better preservation of the canal anatomy with fewer shaping aberrations" and "has shown a less incidence of postoperative pain" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], though manual glide paths also perform acceptably. Schäfer favours stainless steel for the glide path because of its tactile feedback (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]. The two statements on precurving are reconciled by purpose: precurving helps negotiation past an impediment, but the verified glide path for rotaries is checked with a straight file [Likely].

**Patency.** Peters, Peters and Basrani define the patency file as a #10 or #15 K-file "passively extended just through the apical foramen" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. How far beyond is stated differently: Sikri says "2.0 mm beyond the apical constriction" with a size 08 or 10 file (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Schäfer, "usually set at 1 mm longer than the final working length" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]; Siqueira and colleagues, "up to 1 mm longer than the canal terminus" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. On whether it is safe, Sikri lists the objection that it "may push microorganisms into periapical area" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], while Peters, Arias and Shabahang, after reviewing outcome data and a systematic review on pain, conclude "the benefits of maintaining apical patency seem to outweigh possible risks" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sequence of preparation**
Access → coronal pre-enlargement (orifice modification) → glide path and patency → working length → shaping of the middle and apical thirds → apical gauging and final apical enlargement (Gopikrishna, 2021; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

**Coronal pre-enlargement**
- Enlarge the orifice and the coronal 2-3 mm before working length; this reduces stress on later instruments (Gopikrishna, 2021) and reduces working-length change during shaping (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Advantages: removes coronal interferences and dentine shelves, gives straighter access and better tactile control apically, allows early irrigant entry, reduces the curvature the files must negotiate.
- Grossman's also claims less apical extrusion of debris; Peters, Peters and Basrani say this benefit has not been confirmed experimentally.
- Instruments: NiTi orifice shapers. Gates-Glidden drills are "currently not recommended" (Gopikrishna, 2021) and "unsuitable" for modern endodontics (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021), but Hülsmann and Schäfer still describe them as safe and inexpensive. Preserve pericervical dentine, 4 mm above and below the crestal bone (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

**Glide path**
- A patent path to working length without altering the original anatomy (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Made with #08-#10 then #15 stainless steel K-files in watch-winding, or with rotary glide path files (ProGlider, Scout RaCe).
- Confirmed when a straight #15 K-file slides passively to working length; it prevents the screw-in effect and torsional fracture of rotaries (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Rotary glide paths preserve canal anatomy better and cause less postoperative pain; manual glide paths remain acceptable (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Grossman's precurves the negotiating file; Peters, Arias and Shabahang say the glide path file used before rotaries should not be precurved.

**Apical patency**
- A small #10 or #15 K-file passed passively through the foramen to stop debris packing and loss of length (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Distance: 2 mm beyond the constriction (Sikri, 2019); 1 mm beyond working length (Schäfer, 2017); up to 1 mm beyond the terminus (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- For: maintains working length, prevents blockage, improves irrigant delivery. Against: may push debris and microorganisms periapically (Sikri, 2019). Current view: benefits outweigh risks (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

## 6. Apical limit of preparation (working length)

### Explanation

Grossman's recommends terminating instrumentation and obturation "within 0.5-1.0 mm short of the radiographic apex" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani report a split by diagnosis: 0.5-1 mm short in necrotic cases and 1-2 mm short in irreversible pulpitis (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues agree for vital teeth ("1 to 2 mm short of the radiographic apex") (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC] but argue for infected canals that, because the apical canal is "critical territory" for bacteria, host and clinician, it is "prudent to extend the apical limit of instrumentation to the canal terminus" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], with the patency file reaching the terminus and the enlarged preparation sculpted "short of the canal terminus (0.5 to 1 mm) by using larger file sizes" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

The anatomy behind the debate: the classic constriction was found in "only 46% of the teeth" in one study (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. A prospective study found the odds of success "reduced by 12% for every 1 mm of the canal short of the terminus remaining" uninstrumented, yet the same authors conclude the exact point "remains a matter of debate" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's places the cementodentinal junction "0.5-0.75 mm short of the anatomical apex" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], applies Weine's rule that with bone resorption "the working length should be reduced to 1.5 mm short of the radiographic apex" (2 mm with root resorption) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and recommends combining the electronic apex locator with Ingle's radiographic method (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Peters, Peters and Basrani agree that combined use gives "greater accuracy" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang note that working lengths "tend to shorten" during preparation as the canal straightens, so length must be rechecked (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Working length methods themselves belong to the Working Length topic.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Apical limit**
- Traditional target: the apical constriction (minor diameter), taken as 0.5-1.0 mm short of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- By diagnosis: necrotic pulp 0.5-1 mm short, irreversible pulpitis 1-2 mm short (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); vital pulp 1-2 mm short (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Infected canals: Siqueira and colleagues advise cleaning to the canal terminus with a patency file, and enlarging with larger files 0.5-1 mm short, because the apical canal is "critical territory" where residual bacteria cause persistent disease.
- Evidence: the classic constriction exists in only 46% of teeth; odds of success fall by 12% for every 1 mm of uninstrumented canal; the exact point remains debated (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- The cementodentinal junction lies about 0.5-0.75 mm short of the anatomical apex; with periapical bone resorption reduce working length to 1.5 mm short, and with root resorption to 2 mm short (Weine, in Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Determine length with an electronic apex locator combined with a radiograph, which is more accurate than either alone (Gopikrishna, 2021; Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Working length shortens as the canal is straightened during shaping, so re-check it after coronal flaring and at the end (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

## 7. Apical size (width) of preparation

### Explanation

All the books state that there is no fixed size. Peters, Arias and Shabahang: "there is no standardized apical canal size" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's: the minimum size "cannot be standardized and varies from case to case" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Peters, Peters and Basrani: final apical size "remains a much-disputed entity" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC] and there is "an ongoing debate" between small tapered and large apical preparations (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

Grossman's rejects the traditional rules (three sizes beyond the first file to bind; enlarging until white shavings appear), because "the color of dentinal shavings is no indication" of infected dentine (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Its Box 14.9 sets out the trade-off: a small master apical file conserves dentine and avoids transportation but risks "leaving behind infected tissues"; a large one gives "increased probability of physical disruption of bacterial biofilms" but more risk of transportation, ledges and extrusion (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

The books do not weigh the two sides equally:
- Siqueira and colleagues favour larger sizes for infected canals: "the larger the apical preparation of infected root canals, the greater the reduction of the bacterial populations" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].
- Schäfer sets a floor for irrigation: at least ISO 35-40 at .02 taper or 25 at .04 (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC].
- Peters, Arias and Shabahang caution that with stainless steel hand files "apical transportation occurs in many curved canals enlarged beyond a No. 20" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], and limit small curved canals to "three sizes larger than the IAF" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. They make irrigant delivery, not the obturation technique, the criterion (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

Gauging is the practical problem. The initial apical file is "the largest file to bind slightly at the corrected WL after straightline access" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], but gauging with fine files "is likely to result in underestimation of the diameter" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri reports that "the first file that bind may not reflect the diameter at working length" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues describe a "planned apical preparation principle", in which the canal diameter at 1-4 mm from the foramen "is measured based on cone beam computed tomographic scans and used to determine the ideal master apical instrument size and taper" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani add the irrigation reason for width: passively delivered solutions "progress only about 1 mm farther than the tip of the needle" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Apical width**
- No standard size; depends on initial canal diameter, tooth, curvature, vital or infected status, resorption and anatomy (Gopikrishna, 2021; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Old rules (three sizes beyond the first binding file; clean white shavings) are no longer valid: shaving colour does not indicate infected dentine (Gopikrishna, 2021).

| | Small apical size | Large apical size |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Conserves dentine; maintains anatomy; less transportation; less irrigant extrusion | Removes more infected dentine and biofilm; better irrigant penetration (Gopikrishna, 2021); deeper needle placement (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) |
| Disadvantages | Infected tissue left behind; poor apical irrigation; not ideal for lateral compaction | Transportation, ledges, extrusion, not ideal for thermoplastic obturation (Gopikrishna, 2021); weakened root (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) |
(Gopikrishna, 2021)

- Siqueira and colleagues: larger preparations give greater bacterial reduction in infected canals. Schäfer: at least 35-40/.02 or 25/.04 for effective irrigation. Peters, Arias and Shabahang: stainless steel transports curved canals beyond size 20; limit small curved canals to three sizes above the initial apical file.
- Initial apical file = largest file binding slightly at working length after straight-line access; it underestimates the true diameter (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Sikri, 2019).
- Planned apical preparation: canal diameters 1-4 mm from the foramen measured on CBCT to choose the master apical size and taper (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Irrigants act only about 1 mm beyond the needle tip, so the canal must be wide enough for the needle (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

## 8. Instrument motions

### Explanation

Grossman's, Peters, Arias and Shabahang and Sikri describe the same manual motions with small variations: watch-winding (back-and-forth rotation to negotiate), reaming (clockwise rotation, a quarter to half turn in Grossman's and Sikri, 180-360 degrees in Torabinejad's), filing (push-pull against a wall) and circumferential filing. Siqueira and colleagues warn that "filing motion should not be used for apical preparations", because it produces irregular apical shapes and ledges (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], and describe the "pecking or pumping motion" that lowers apical force for rotaries (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues add a removal motion (advance, one or two clockwise turns, pull) that "is used to remove vital pulps, free debris, and cotton pellets or paper points from the canal" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang note that circumferential filing "is not very effective beyond the coronal third" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Anticurvature filing "balances the cutting flutes against the safer part of the root canal" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Instrument motions**
- **Removal motion:** advance, rotate clockwise one or two turns, withdraw; extirpates pulp and removes debris, cotton or paper points (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- **Watch-winding:** back-and-forth rotation with light apical pressure; negotiation and working files to length.
- **Reaming:** clockwise rotation (quarter to half turn by hand) then withdrawal; round preparations in straight canals.
- **Filing:** push-pull rasping against the wall; tends to straighten curves and should not be used apically (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- **Circumferential filing:** filing around all walls of wide or oval canals; effective mainly in the coronal third (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- **Anticurvature filing (Abou-Rass):** files directed away from the furcal danger zone towards the bulkier safety zone (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Balanced force:** see section 9.
- **Rotary and reciprocating:** engine-driven, used with a light pecking motion (Siqueira et al, 2022).

## 9. Preparation techniques

### Explanation

**Classification.** Grossman's Table 14.6 lists the techniques with their originators and years (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]: Ingle 1961 standardized; Clem, Weine and Schilder 1969-74 step-back; Abou-Rass 1980 anticurvature filing; Marshall and Pappin 1980 crown-down pressureless; Goerig 1982 step-down; Fava 1983 double flare; Roane 1985 balanced force; Lim and Stock 1987 circumferential filing; Torabinejad 1994 passive step-back; Yared 2008 reciprocation. Sikri divides techniques into apical-coronal and coronal-apical groups (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Peters, Peters and Basrani into crown-down, step-back, apical widening and hybrid (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Standardized technique.** Every file goes to working length, so the canal copies the last instrument. In small curved canals "procedural errors were identified with the technique" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri: "the technique is no longer followed" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Step-back (telescopic, serial).** Grossman's: the apical third is enlarged first "to at least a No. 25 or 30" and larger files then shape the middle and coronal parts (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], for example sizes 30, 35, 40 and 45 at 1, 2, 3 and 4 mm short, with recapitulation (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani: 1 mm and 0.5 mm steps give tapers of about .05 and .10 (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang judge it "superior to standardized serial filing and reaming techniques in débridement and maintaining the canal shape" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's lists its limitations: debris extrusion, "tendency to straighten in the canal" and loss of working length (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Passive step-back (Torabinejad) inserts progressively larger hand files "as deep as they can be passively placed" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's modified step-back starts stepping back 2-3 mm from the end, which "gives a parallel and round form in the apical area" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Hülsmann and Schäfer describe Tronstad's apical box, which gives the apical 2-5 mm "a cylindrical rather than a conical shape" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC].

**Coronal-apical techniques.** Goerig first advocated instrumenting the coronal third first (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. In the crown-down pressureless technique, "straight K-files are then used in a large to small sequence with a reaming motion and no apical pressure" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], beginning at the depth to which "no. 35 file penetrates to its point of first resistance" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Fava's double flare is crown-down followed by step-back (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's lists the advantages, among them that it "minimizes the extrusion of debris" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Balanced force (Roane, Sabala and Duncanson, 1985).** The principle is agreed: preload the file with a clockwise turn, then cut with a counterclockwise turn under apical pressure (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The rotation angles are not agreed, and a student should know that:

| Source | Clockwise (placement) | Counterclockwise (cutting) |
|---|---|---|
| Gopikrishna, 2021 (Grossman's) | 90° | 180-270° [SRC] |
| Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021 (Cohen's) | about 90° | 180-270° [SRC] |
| Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021 | less than 180° | at least 120° [SRC] |
| Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019 (Ingle's) | 180° | 120° [SRC] |
| Sikri, 2019 | one quarter turn | three-quarter turn [SRC] |
| Schäfer, 2017 (Harty's) | quarter turn | half turn [SRC] |

All agree on a final clockwise withdrawal to load the debris. Peters, Peters and Basrani report "general agreement" that it "creates the least canal aberrations with K-files" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Peters, Arias and Shabahang call it "the most effective hand instrumentation technique" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's records Roane's recommended minimum apical sizes, 45 in curved canals and 80 in single-rooted teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri describes Roane's "apical control zone" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Hybrid.** Grossman's: step-down followed by step-back, "recommended by this textbook for use with stainless steel instruments" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani use the word more widely: any combination of techniques or NiTi systems "to eliminate or reduce the shortcomings of individual instruments" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Note a terminology conflict: Sikri describes the crown-down/step-back combination under step-down and calls it "modified step-back technique", a name he also uses for the apical-parallel technique above.

**Comparative evidence.** Hülsmann and Schäfer: there is "no evidence that step-down techniques are superior to step-back techniques or vice versa with the exception of the BF technique" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC], and one comparison found crown-down "produced more ledges" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC], which sits uneasily with Grossman's list of crown-down advantages. Peters, Arias and Shabahang: "There is no consensus or clinical evidence on which technique or instrument design or type is clinically superior" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

**Engine-driven NiTi.** "Most rotary nickel–titanium systems employ crown-down instrumentation" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], because crown-down reduces torsional load (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Schäfer groups NiTi use into crown-down, single-length and single-file systems (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]. In reciprocation the counterclockwise engaging angle is "five times the clockwise disengaging angle" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], greater in the cutting direction so the file advances (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]; Schäfer reports conflicting studies on cracks and on "an increased tendency of apical debris extrusion" with reciprocation (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]. Canal-adaptive instruments (SAF, XP-endo Shaper) aim "to conserve radicular dentin by emphasizing more on cleaning" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; newer "3D conforming instruments" address non-round canals (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], because a rotating file "can produce a round canal at best" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

The case for NiTi is mechanical and clinical: a no. 45 NiTi file bends with the load of a no. 25 stainless steel file (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]; NiTi recovers from strain "up to 10%" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues cite two outcome studies favouring NiTi: hand NiTi files were "five times more likely to achieve healing", and rotary NiTi healed 77% against 60% for stainless steel hand files (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]; they also cite one study with no difference. Peters, Arias and Shabahang give handling rules: three to five movements per step, "not exceed 10 to 15 seconds" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

**Oval canals.** Peters, Peters and Basrani advise that canals with a larger buccolingual dimension be shaped as if they were two canals: "consider shaping as two canals" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Siqueira and colleagues add rotary instruments "in brushing motion" or circumferential hand filing towards the recesses (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

**Other methods.** Sonic and ultrasonic devices "are not indicated for shaping" and serve cleaning (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Lussi's "non-instrumentation technique" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC] "cannot be considered a viable alternative" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification of techniques (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019)**

| Group | Technique (originator, year) |
|---|---|
| A. Apical-coronal | Standardized (Ingle 1961); step-back/telescopic (Clem, Weine, Schilder 1969-74); passive step-back (Torabinejad 1994); modified step-back / apical box (Tronstad) |
| B. Coronal-apical | Step-down (Goerig 1982); crown-down pressureless (Marshall and Pappin 1980); double flare (Fava 1983) |
| C. Balanced force | Roane 1985 |
| D. Combination | Hybrid (crown-down + step-back) |
| E. Engine-driven NiTi | Rotary crown-down; single-length; single-file reciprocation (Yared 2008); canal-adaptive (SAF, XP-endo) |
| F. Adjuncts / others | Anticurvature filing (Abou-Rass 1980); circumferential filing (Lim and Stock 1987); sonic and ultrasonic; non-instrumentation technique (Lussi) |

**1. Standardized technique (Ingle 1961).** Each file to full working length; the canal copies the last instrument; suits a single-cone filling. Causes ledges, zips and perforations in curved canals and is no longer followed (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Sikri, 2019).

**2. Step-back technique.** After working length, the apical third is enlarged to at least size 25-30 (master apical file); each larger file is then used 1 mm shorter (e.g. 30, 35, 40, 45 at 1, 2, 3, 4 mm short), recapitulating with the master apical file between files; the coronal part is flared and the steps smoothed (Gopikrishna, 2021). Steps of 1 mm or 0.5 mm give about .05 or .10 taper (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Better debridement and shape than standardized preparation (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). Limitations: debris extrusion, canal straightening, loss of length (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Passive step-back (Torabinejad):** progressively larger files placed passively to their binding point, giving gradual taper.
- **Modified step-back / apical box:** apical 2-3 mm (Sikri) or 2-5 mm (Tronstad) prepared parallel, for a tight apical cone fit.

**3. Step-down / crown-down techniques.** Coronal two-thirds prepared first (Goerig), then apical. Crown-down pressureless (Marshall and Pappin): find the depth a #35 file reaches; then K-files large to small, reaming without apical pressure, to the provisional and then final working length; then apical enlargement (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019). Double flare (Fava): crown-down with K-files, then step-back. Advantages: early removal of coronal debris and bacteria, less extrusion and pain, straighter access, better irrigant penetration, less zipping, working length changes less (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**4. Balanced force technique (Roane, 1985).** Flex-R or K-file with non-cutting tip, not precurved:
- Insert passively; rotate clockwise to engage dentine with light apical pressure.
- Rotate counterclockwise with enough apical pressure to keep the file at depth; dentine is cut with a clicking sound.
- Repeat and advance; withdraw with a clockwise turn to load debris.
- Angles differ between books: Grossman's and Cohen's 90° clockwise and 180-270° counterclockwise; Sikri quarter turn and three-quarter turn; Harty's quarter and half turn; Ingle's 180° and 120°.
- It gives the best centring and least canal aberration of any hand technique (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). Roane advised a minimum apical size of 45 (1.5 mm short of the foramen) in curved canals and 80 in single-rooted teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**5. Hybrid technique.** Patency with #10; passive #15-25 to binding; coronal enlargement; working length; apical enlargement to master apical file; step-back through the middle third (Gopikrishna, 2021). In the wider sense, combining instruments or NiTi systems to overcome each one's limits (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**6. Engine-driven NiTi techniques**
- **Rotary crown-down:** descending tip sizes at constant taper, or descending tapers at constant tip; reduces torsional load (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- **Single-length:** each instrument to full working length (ProTaper, Mtwo) (Schäfer, 2017).
- **Single-file reciprocation (WaveOne Gold, Reciproc Blue):** counterclockwise cutting angle about five times the clockwise release angle (Gopikrishna, 2021); faster, with conflicting data on debris extrusion and cracks (Schäfer, 2017).
- **Canal-adaptive (SAF, XP-endo Shaper and Finisher):** conserve dentine and clean oval canals (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Advantages of NiTi: flexibility (a #45 NiTi bends like a #25 stainless steel file), centred preparations, fewer errors and shorter time (Siqueira et al, 2022). Better healing in two outcome studies (77% vs 60%), no difference in one (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Oval and flattened canals: shape as two canals (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021), with brushing strokes or circumferential filing towards the recesses, and supplement with adaptive files and activated irrigation (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Rules: glide path first; flood the canal with NaOCl; light pecking strokes, 3-5 movements, no more than 10-15 seconds; clean flutes; single use in difficult canals (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

**Comparison**
- No technique or system has been shown clinically superior (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Except for balanced force, there is no evidence that step-down is better than step-back or the reverse; one study found more ledges with crown-down (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).
- Sonic and ultrasonic devices are for cleaning, not shaping (Gopikrishna, 2021); the non-instrumentation technique is not a viable alternative (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).

## 10. Chemical adjuncts and recapitulation during shaping

### Explanation

Grossman's identifies the apical 3-4 mm as "the most critical area for canal disinfection" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and recommends "2 mL of the irrigant per canal per instrument change" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Recapitulation prevents "the packing of dentin filings" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and, in Peters, Arias and Shabahang, "is performed between each successive enlarging instrument regardless of the cleaning and shaping technique" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. For rotaries, aqueous NaOCl rather than paste lubricants should be used "to reduce torque" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Only stainless steel files are precurved (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. A deformed file "must be discarded" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang's general principles add that binding or cutting on insertion "should be avoided" (files are teased to length) and that instruments, irrigants, debris and filling materials "should be contained within the canal" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Chemical adjuncts and recapitulation**
- Irrigate copiously throughout: about 2 mL per canal per instrument change; the apical 3-4 mm is the most critical zone (Gopikrishna, 2021). NaOCl dissolves tissue and kills bacteria; EDTA removes the smear layer at the end.
- Lubricants (glycerine, EDTA pastes) help negotiate narrow canals by hand; with NiTi rotaries use NaOCl rather than pastes to reduce torque (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Recapitulate with a small file to working length between every enlarging instrument to prevent dentine packing and loss of length (Gopikrishna, 2021; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Clean and inspect file flutes; discard deformed files (Gopikrishna, 2021); precurve only stainless steel files.
- Never force or lock a file; tease it to length with watch-winding and remove dentine on the outstroke (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Keep instruments, irrigants, debris and filling materials within the canal; all are periradicular irritants (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

## 11. Shape required for obturation, evaluation of the preparation and errors to avoid

### Explanation

The required shape depends partly on the filling method. Peters, Arias and Shabahang: for lateral compaction, the spreader should reach "within 1 to 2 mm of the working length", and for warm vertical compaction the pluggers "within 3 to 5 mm" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; Hülsmann and Schäfer require "a conicity of 7% or more" for warm vertical compaction (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. But both books then subordinate obturation to biology: Hülsmann and Schäfer, "preparation should dictate the obturation technique rather than the obturation technique dictating a certain preparation technique" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, "the degree of shaping should be tooth dependent" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], and "the more dentin is removed from the canal walls, the less resistant to fracture the root becomes" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

The apical configuration is classified by gauging: if a file one or two sizes smaller than the master apical file also stops at working length, "the apical configuration is called an apical stop" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; if it passes, a seat; if the master apical file passes, an open apex. The apical size reached "should be verified by apical gauging" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

End points: "glassy smooth" walls and no debris (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; no turbidity or effervescence after NaOCl (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; tug-back of the master cone and a symmetric shape on the radiograph (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. A well-shaped canal is defined by "the absence of procedural errors", achieved disinfection and retained tooth structure (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues are blunt that none of the classic clinical criteria is reliable, and that preparation is complete "when the canal is enlarged to diameters that are compatible with the root anatomy and large enough to be safe and antibacterially effective" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

Errors: "canal transportation is at the root of all these clinical problems" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and about 100-150 μm of transportation "may be clinically acceptable" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Overzealous preparation causes "unnecessary weakening of the tooth, and susceptibility to fracture" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Errors are answered in full in the Procedural Errors topic.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Shape for obturation**
- Lateral compaction: spreader to within 1-2 mm of working length; warm vertical compaction: plugger to within 3-5 mm and a taper of about 7% or more (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).
- Preparation should dictate the obturation technique, not the reverse (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019); shaping should be tooth dependent, since more dentine removed means a weaker root (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

**Apical configuration (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021):** apical stop (a smaller file also stops at working length), apical seat (a smaller file passes), open apex (master apical file passes).

**Criteria of a completed preparation**
1. Continuous taper from orifice to apex, original canal incorporated, foramen not moved.
2. Apical narrowing confirmed by gauging; patency maintained.
3. Glassy smooth walls; no debris; no turbidity or effervescence after NaOCl (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
4. Master cone fits with tug-back; radiograph shows a symmetric shape with no thinned walls.
5. No procedural errors and maximum tooth structure retained (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
6. Clinical signs are unreliable; preparation is complete when the canal is as large as anatomy allows and large enough to be antibacterially effective (Siqueira et al, 2022).

**Errors to avoid:** loss of working length, blockage, ledge, transportation and zip, apical and strip perforation, instrument separation, debris and irrigant extrusion, overpreparation and root weakening. Transportation underlies most; about 100-150 μm may be acceptable (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

## 12. Conclusion

### Explanation

Peters, Peters and Basrani close their chapter with the line that "cleaning and shaping are important, interdependent steps in root canal treatment" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]: cleaning needs enough shape for the irrigation needle, and shaping is limited by the need to avoid errors and fractures.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** Cleaning and shaping are interdependent: the canal must be shaped wide enough for irrigants to reach the apical third, yet conservatively enough to avoid errors and root fracture (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Schilder's objectives still guide the shape, but the modern aim is biological: maximum microbial reduction with minimum loss of dentine. No single technique is proven superior; a glide path, coronal pre-enlargement, an individual apical size, flexible NiTi or balanced force hand instrumentation, copious irrigation and recapitulation, chosen for each canal's anatomy, give predictable results. Preparation should be planned from the diagnosis (vital or infected), the anatomy and the instruments available, and should dictate the obturation technique rather than be dictated by it (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019). Performed to these principles, root canal treatment is a predictable way of retaining teeth with healthy periradicular tissues.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

1. **Claim:** cleaning and shaping, not obturation, determines outcome. **Textbooks:** Gopikrishna, 2021; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Siqueira et al, 2022 all agree. **Refinement:** the Washington-study link between poor fillings and failure is correlation, not cause (Siqueira et al, 2022). **Strength:** strong agreement across books. **Conclusion:** [Certain].
2. **Claim:** larger apical preparation improves disinfection. **Textbooks:** Siqueira et al, 2022 favour larger sizes in infected canals; Schäfer, 2017 sets a floor of 35-40/.02. **Challenge:** Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021 cite studies finding no difference in bacterial counts between size 25 and 40, and transportation risk rises with size. **Strength:** in vitro and ex vivo data, few clinical outcome studies. **Conclusion:** [Likely] for infected canals within the limits of anatomy and instrument flexibility; there is no single correct size.
3. **Claim:** balanced force is the best hand technique. **Textbooks:** Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019 (the only technique with evidence of less straightening). **Challenge:** Hülsmann and Schäfer also report perforations, fractures and some studies showing displacement of the canal axis; the angles differ between books. **Conclusion:** [Likely].
4. **Claim:** crown-down is superior to step-back. **Textbooks:** Gopikrishna, 2021 and Sikri, 2019 list its advantages. **Challenge:** Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019 find no evidence either way and one study with more ledges after crown-down; the claimed reduction in extrusion is "not confirmed" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). **Conclusion:** [Guessing] for clinical superiority; it is mechanically logical and needed for rotaries.
5. **Claim:** NiTi improves outcome. **Textbooks:** Siqueira et al, 2022 report two studies favouring NiTi (77% vs 60%; five times more healing) and one showing no difference. **Conclusion:** [Likely]; the mechanical advantage (fewer errors) is [Certain].
6. **Claim:** apical patency is safe and useful. **Textbooks:** Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021 (benefits outweigh risks; no increase in pain); Siqueira et al, 2022 (higher success when patency achieved). **Challenge:** Sikri, 2019 lists the risk of pushing debris and microorganisms. **Conclusion:** [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

- Schilder 1974: five mechanical and five biological objectives; "concept of flow".
- Modern aims add: neutralise toxins, retain radicular dentine, preserve pericervical dentine (4 mm above and below the crest).
- "Shaping and cleaning": shape first so irrigants can clean.
- Sequence: coronal pre-enlargement → glide path (#15 straight K-file to length) → working length → shaping → gauging → final apical enlargement.
- Working length: 0.5-1 mm short (necrotic), 1-2 mm short (vital); odds of success fall 12% per uninstrumented mm.
- No standard apical size; initial apical file underestimates diameter; irrigant acts about 1 mm beyond the needle tip.
- Step-back steps of 1 mm or 0.5 mm give about .05 or .10 taper.
- Balanced force: clockwise to engage, counterclockwise with apical pressure to cut, clockwise withdrawal; best centring of hand techniques.
- Reciprocation: counterclockwise cutting angle about five times the clockwise release angle.
- No technique proven clinically superior.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

- Writing that obturation quality decides success; examiners expect the "obturation reflects cleaning and shaping" argument.
- Listing Schilder's objectives without separating mechanical from biological, or presenting one textbook's list as the only version.
- Quoting the "three sizes beyond the first binding file" or "white dentine shavings" rule as current.
- Giving one set of balanced force angles as fact without noting that books differ.
- Claiming crown-down is proven superior to step-back.
- Forgetting coronal pre-enlargement and glide path before rotary NiTi.
- Omitting preservation of dentine (pericervical dentine, fracture risk) as a modern objective.
- Describing ultrasonics as a shaping technique; it is a cleaning adjunct.

## MNEMONIC

**"FUNNEL" for Schilder's mechanical objectives:** **F**unnel-shaped continuous taper; **U**nchanged foramen position; **N**arrowest at the apex; **N**atural canal shape followed (flow); **E**ntrance confluent with the access; **L**ittle apical opening (as small as practical).

**"CRAMS" for the biological objectives:** **C**onfine instruments to the root; **R**emove all tissue and debris; **A**void forcing debris apically; **M**edicament space; **S**ingle appointment completion.
