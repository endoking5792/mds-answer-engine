## 1. Definition and concept

### Explanation

The AAE Glossary gives the general surgical sense of the word: "removal of foreign matter, devitalized tissue and microorganisms from an area" of injury (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Grossman's applies it narrowly to the pulp cavity: when food or other debris has accumulated with necrotic pulp, "the removal of this material from the pulp cavity is referred to as debridement", and keeps "shaping and cleaning" for the whole procedure (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Current textbooks use the word more broadly, as the combined result of mechanical preparation and chemical irrigation: Hülsmann and Schäfer describe preparation as using "mechanical and chemical means to remove pulpal tissue, reduce microbial load" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani stress that shaping comes first because enlarged canals "direct and facilitate the cleaning action of irrigants" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

For the essay, debridement is best defined as the removal of the canal contents (vital or necrotic pulp, microorganisms, their products, dentine debris and foreign material) by four complementary means: instruments, irrigants (with or without activation), interappointment medicaments, and, where necessary, surgery or extraction. The essay below covers the first three; surgical debridement belongs to the Surgical Endodontics topic.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Debridement is the "removal of foreign matter, devitalized tissue and microorganisms from an area" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). Root canal debridement is the "elimination of organic and inorganic substances as well as microorganisms from the root canal by mechanical and/or chemical means" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012); Grossman's uses the term more narrowly for removing accumulated debris and necrotic pulp from the pulp cavity (Gopikrishna, 2021). It is achieved by mechanical and chemical means acting together (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019): canals are shaped so that irrigants can reach and clean them, which is why "shaping and cleaning" is the more exact order (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**Means of debridement**
1. Mechanical: hand and engine-driven instrumentation.
2. Chemical: irrigants (NaOCl, chelators, chlorhexidine, combination products).
3. Physical activation of irrigants: manual, sonic, ultrasonic, negative pressure, multisonic, laser.
4. Interappointment medicaments: calcium hydroxide and others.
5. Surgical: periradicular surgery, root resection or extraction when orthograde debridement fails.



**Debridement versus disinfection.** Debridement is the physical removal of tissue, debris and microorganisms; disinfection is the killing or inactivation of the microorganisms that remain. In the root canal the two happen together during chemomechanical preparation, because NaOCl both dissolves tissue and kills bacteria, and the flushing action of every irrigant removes debris mechanically.

## 2. Evolution of debridement

### Explanation

Hülsmann and Schäfer trace the history: "Pierre Fauchard described instruments for root canal preparation" in 1733, when the basic treatment was cauterisation of the pulp; in the 19th century "enlargement of root canals was performed with broaches"; "in 1885, the Gates Glidden drill was introduced" and the K-file followed in 1904 (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's standardized technique (1961), the first ISO specifications (1974), the "introduction of ultrasonics and sonics systems by Richman in 1957", nickel-titanium instruments (1988) and the self-adjusting file ("in 2010, the SAF system was introduced") mark the later stages (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. On the chemical side, Coolidge introduced NaOCl in 1919 (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], Walker in 1936 "introduced double-strength chlorinated soda solution (5% NaOCl)" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], Nygaard-Østby introduced EDTA in 1957 (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and Hermann introduced calcium hydroxide in 1920 (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Two minimally invasive ideas, Lussi's non-instrumentation technique and, more recently, the GentleWave system, attempted debridement by fluid dynamics with little or no instrumentation (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Evolution of debridement (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019; Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Siqueira et al, 2022)**

| Year | Development |

| 19th century | Canals enlarged with broaches |

The direction of change has been from mechanical removal alone, through chemomechanical preparation, to minimally invasive shaping with activated irrigation.

## 3. Biological rationale

### Explanation

Mechanical enlargement "dramatically decreases the presence of microorganisms" but "cannot render the canal sterile" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues show why: whatever instruments and irrigants are used, "chemomechanical procedures alone are unable to achieve thorough cleaning of the root canal system", because isthmuses, recesses, lateral canals and apical ramifications remain unprepared (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Bacteria also enter dentinal tubules; in teeth with apical periodontitis they "may penetrate the tubules to the periphery of the root" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues call the apical canal "critical territory" for bacteria, host and clinician (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

The consequence is the working aim set by Peters, Arias and Shabahang: "to significantly reduce the irritants, not totally eliminate them", using mechanical shaping and irrigation "in synergy" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; the instruments themselves generate debris "that is typically pushed into accessory anatomy" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer add "neutralization of microbial toxins and byproducts" as a goal (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Biological rationale**

Pulpal and periapical diseases are caused by microorganisms. In necrotic and previously treated teeth they live as biofilms on the canal walls, in the apical canal, in isthmuses, fins and lateral canals, and inside dentinal tubules; in teeth with apical periodontitis they may reach the periphery of the root through the tubules (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). The apical canal is "critical territory" for bacteria, host and clinician, because there the bacteria are in direct contact with the periradicular tissues (Siqueira et al, 2022).

Instrumentation greatly reduces the number of bacteria but cannot render the canal sterile (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021), and chemomechanical procedures alone cannot fully clean the root canal system, whatever instruments and irrigants are used (Siqueira et al, 2022). Instruments also create dentine debris that is pushed into accessory anatomy and blocks it (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

Therefore:
- The realistic aim is a significant reduction of irritants, not total elimination, using mechanical shaping and irrigation in synergy (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Microbial toxins and byproducts (endotoxin) must also be neutralised (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).
- Because no single method is sufficient, debridement combines instruments, irrigants, activation and medicaments.

## 4. Objectives of debridement

### Explanation

Cohen's Box 8.1 lists the objectives of cleaning and shaping: remove infected soft and hard tissue, "give disinfecting irrigants access to the apical canal space", create space for medicaments and obturation, and retain radicular structure (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. For irrigation specifically, the objectives "are mechanical, chemical, and biological" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]: to "flush out debris, (ii) lubricate the canal, (iii) dissolve organic and inorganic tissue", prevent or remove the smear layer and disrupt biofilms (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; the biological function "is related to their antimicrobial effects" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues add the purely mechanical value of flushing, "mechanical removal of bacteria and pulpal and dentinal debris by flushing" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Objectives (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021)**
- Remove vital and necrotic pulp, infected dentine and microorganisms.
- Give irrigants access to the apical canal space.
- Create space for medicaments and obturation.
- Retain the integrity of radicular structure.

**Objectives of irrigation**
- Mechanical: flush out debris and bacteria; lubricate instruments.
- Chemical: dissolve organic tissue (NaOCl) and inorganic tissue (chelators); prevent or remove the smear layer; detach and disrupt biofilms.
- Biological: kill planktonic and biofilm microorganisms and inactivate endotoxin, without harming vital tissue (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Siqueira et al, 2022).


The objectives must be met without harming the patient: instruments, irrigants and debris are kept within the canal, the original canal anatomy and apical constriction are preserved, and as much radicular dentine as possible is retained so that the tooth does not fracture later (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

## 5. Mechanical debridement (instrumentation)

### Explanation

Grossman's gives four reasons for widening canals: to eliminate microorganisms mechanically, to remove pulp tissue completely (since "tags of pulp tissue and odontoblasts cling to the canal wall"), to let irrigants reach the apical third, and to receive gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues show that irrigation itself has a mechanical component: "approximately 70% more debris appears to remain" without irrigant, and the flushing effect reduces bacteria "irrespective of the type of irrigant used" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

The parts of instrumentation that matter most for debridement are summarised from Q01: a tapered shape (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], judged by its ability to deliver irrigant rather than by the obturation technique (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], because passive irrigant progresses only about 1 mm beyond the needle tip (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; apical width, where larger preparations in infected canals give greater bacterial reduction (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], balanced against root weakening (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC] in "an ongoing debate" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; apical length, extended to the terminus in infected canals (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], with preparation 0.5-1 mm short (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], since each uninstrumented millimetre reduces the odds of success by 12% (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; a glide path to #15 (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; and NiTi instruments, with which healing was 77% against 60% for stainless steel in one study (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

The weakness of rotary instruments for debridement is geometric: "a rotating file can produce a round canal at best" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Oval canals are shaped "as two canals" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC] or with brushing motions (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], and canal-adaptive files aim at "cleaning that canal rather than just enlarging the canal" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's describes the SAF as removing a uniform layer of dentine "(60-75 μm thick)" while preserving the canal shape, adds that "the SAF system is no longer commercially available", and describes the XP-endo Finisher assuming "a spoon shape of 1.5 mm depth in the final 10 mm of its length" at body temperature (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Mechanical debridement**

*Reasons for widening canals (Gopikrishna, 2021):*
1. To remove microorganisms from the canal walls mechanically.
2. To remove pulp tissue completely, because pulp tags and odontoblasts cling to the walls and later necrose, giving a substrate for bacteria.
3. To increase the capacity of the canal so that irrigants reach the apical third.
4. To shape the canal to receive the filling.

*Principles that govern mechanical debridement*
- **Sequence:** straight-line access, removal of chamber contents, coronal pre-enlargement, then a glide path, confirmed when a straight #15 K-file slides to working length (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- **Length:** infected canals are cleaned to the canal terminus with a patency file and enlarged with larger files 0.5-1 mm short (Siqueira et al, 2022); the odds of success fall by 12% for every millimetre left uninstrumented (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- **Width:** larger apical preparations remove more infected dentine and give greater bacterial reduction in infected canals (Siqueira et al, 2022), but more dentine removal leaves a weaker root (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021); the correct size is still debated (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- **Shape:** a continuous taper wide enough for the irrigating needle; passive irrigant acts only about 1 mm beyond the needle tip, so shaping is judged by its ability to deliver irrigant (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- **Technique:** no technique is proven superior; balanced force is the best hand technique and NiTi instruments give centred, error-free preparations (details in Q01). One outcome study found 77% healing with rotary NiTi against 60% with stainless steel hand files (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- **Limits:** a rotating file can cut a round canal at best (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); oval canals are shaped as two canals with brushing or circumferential strokes (Siqueira et al, 2022).

*Supplementary mechanical instruments (Gopikrishna, 2021)*
- Self-adjusting file: a hollow compressible NiTi lattice that adapts to the canal cross-section, removes a uniform 60-75 μm layer of dentine and irrigates continuously; no longer marketed.
- XP-endo Shaper and Finisher: thermally active files that expand at body temperature (the Finisher into a 1.5 mm spoon shape over 10 mm) to touch walls that conventional files miss.

*Irrigation as mechanical debridement:* about 70% more debris remains in canals instrumented without irrigant, and the flushing effect reduces bacteria regardless of the irrigant used (Siqueira et al, 2022).

## 6. Debridement according to pulp diagnosis

### Explanation

Siqueira and colleagues base the strategy on the presence or absence of infection. "A diseased vital pulp is irreversibly inflamed but free of bacteria colonizing the root canal" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], so the task is asepsis and complete pulp removal; the recommended length is 1-2 mm short (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], and keeping a vital pulp stump "is unpredictable" and apparently not essential for healing (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. With necrosis, "it is advisable to treat root canals containing necrotic pulps as infected canals" whether or not a lesion is visible (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], so debridement extends to the terminus with patency and larger apical sizes. Retreatment cases "almost always harbor a persistent or secondary intraradicular infection" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], so filling material must be removed first and the same principles applied.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Debridement according to diagnosis (Siqueira et al, 2022)**

| | Vital pulp (irreversible pulpitis) | Necrotic pulp / apical periodontitis | Retreatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem | Inflamed but uninfected radicular pulp | Intraradicular infection, biofilm, often to the foramen | Persistent or secondary infection beyond or around old filling |
| Aim | Asepsis; complete pulp removal | Maximal bacterial reduction | Remove filling and residual bacteria |
| Length | 1-2 mm short of radiographic apex | To canal terminus with patency; preparation 0.5-1 mm short | As for necrotic |
| Apical size | Moderate | As large as anatomy allows | As large as anatomy allows |
| Medicament | Usually not needed (single visit) | Ca(OH)2 between visits in Siqueira's two-visit protocol; single visit without Ca(OH)2 is an option in asymptomatic teeth (ESE guideline, in Peters and Arias, 2025) | Ca(OH)2 often used |

Necrotic pulps are treated as infected even without a visible lesion; preservation of a vital pulp stump is unpredictable and not required for healing (Siqueira et al, 2022).

## 7. Chemical debridement: irrigants

### Explanation

**General.** Cohen's list of ideal properties includes the ability "to completely remove the smear layer, and be able to disinfect", to "be active in the presence of blood, serum, and protein", and to "have no adverse effects on the sealing ability of filling materials" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. "Presently, no solution can be regarded as optimal" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Peters, Arias and Shabahang agree that "no solution meets all the requirements" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], and "the process of delivery is as essential as the antibacterial characteristics of the irrigants" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Sodium hypochlorite.** "NaOCl is the most commonly used irrigating solution" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; "in 1919 Coolidge" introduced it to endodontics (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang list its advantages: flushing, dissolution of vital and necrotic tissue, antimicrobial and lubricating action (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Mechanism: Cohen's describes Estrela's dynamic balance, with saponification ("the solution produces glycerol and soap") (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], neutralisation, and chloramination, in which chlorine combines with amino groups "to form chloramines" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Siqueira and colleagues add the "irreversible oxidation of sulfhydryl groups of essential bacterial enzymes" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], with HOCl "the main active moiety" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], while endodontic solutions are made "at pH 12 for increased chemical stability", which puts the chlorine mainly in the less active OCl- form (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

Concentration is a genuine disagreement: Cohen's gives a range "between 0.5% and 6%" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Peters, Arias and Shabahang call 2.5% "a common concentration" that limits toxicity (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Both agree that "a high volume of irrigation and frequent exchanges can compensate" for a lower concentration (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], because the chlorine "is unstable and consumed rapidly (probably within the first 2 minutes)" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. NaOCl "should be used throughout the instrumentation, and for 1 to 2 minutes after" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Heating increases tissue dissolution in vitro, but the irrigant reaches body temperature as soon as it enters the canal (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and "there are no clinical studies" supporting heated NaOCl (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Its main limitation: "it does not remove inorganic tissue" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

**Factors governing NaOCl efficacy.** "Retention time and the volume of irrigation are essential factors" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Siqueira and colleagues agree that "frequency and volume of irrigation" compensate for concentration (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Organic matter matters: "the presence of dentin caused marked delays in the killing of E. faecalis by 1% NaOCl", whereas without confounders NaOCl kills "even at low concentrations of less than 0.1%" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], because in vivo "the presence of organic matter consumes NaOCl and weakens its effect" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. "Disinfection by hypochlorites is optimal at around pH 6" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

**Chlorhexidine.** The books disagree on its value. In favour: Peters, Arias and Shabahang state that 2% CHX "has similar antimicrobial action as 5.25% NaOCl" and is more effective against *E. faecalis*, and that the two are "synergistic" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; Siqueira and colleagues conclude that "from a microbiologic point of view, it makes no difference whether NaOCl or CHX is used" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], and count its substantivity, detected in dentine "up to 120 days" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], as "an important advantage of CHX over NaOCl" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Against: Peters, Peters and Basrani cite Ng and colleagues' finding that "abstaining from using 2% CHX as an adjunct irrigant to NaOCl was associated with superior periapical healing", and conclude that because "CHX lacks a tissue-dissolving property", "NaOCl is still considered the primary irrigating solution" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Peters, Arias and Shabahang also note that clinical studies do not confirm better outcomes with CHX (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. NaOCl and CHX together form a precipitate containing "4-chloroaniline (PCA)" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], so the canal should be "dried using paper points before the final CHX rinse" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Chelators.** EDTA was introduced "in 1957 by Nygaard-Østby" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; it binds calcium until equilibrium, so "EDTA is selflimiting" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Because EDTA strips NaOCl of free chlorine, "EDTA and NaOCl should be used separately" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; EDTA is used "once the cleaning and shaping are completed for around 1 minute" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Its effect is "diminished in the apical third" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], and exposure "over 10 minutes" erodes peritubular and intratubular dentine (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. HEBP (etidronate) is a weak chelator with "no short-term reactivity with NaOCl", so it can be mixed with it (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Adding surfactants does not help: they "do not enhance the ability of NaOCl to dissolve pulp tissue" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Combination products.** MTAD contains "3% doxycycline", "4.25% citric acid" and "0.5% polysorbate 80 detergent" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Again the books differ: Peters, Arias and Shabahang say "MTAD may be superior to NaOCl in antimicrobial action" while it "has not been shown to be clinically beneficial" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; Peters, Peters and Basrani cite a clinical trial in which MTAD "did not reduce the bacterial counts in infected canals beyond levels achieved" with NaOCl alone (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. QMiX was "introduced in 2011" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani summarise an intraoral biofilm study: chelators containing antimicrobials could not remove biofilm; "dissolution ability is mandatory" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. IKI is used "in concentrations ranging from 2% to 5%" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Chemical debridement**

**Ideal properties of an irrigant:** broad antimicrobial spectrum against planktonic and biofilm bacteria; dissolution of necrotic and vital tissue; inactivation of endotoxin; ability to remove the smear layer and disinfect; activity in the presence of blood, serum and protein; low toxicity; no adverse effect on dentine or on the seal of filling materials (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). No current solution meets all requirements (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021), and delivery is as important as chemistry (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**1. Sodium hypochlorite (Coolidge, 1919)**
- The most commonly used irrigant. It flushes debris, dissolves vital and necrotic tissue, kills microorganisms and lubricates instruments; it is cheap and available (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Mechanism: saponification of fatty acids into soap and glycerol, neutralisation of amino acids, and chloramination, in which chlorine combines with amino groups to form chloramines that impede cell metabolism (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); irreversible oxidation of the sulfhydryl groups of essential bacterial enzymes, with HOCl as the main active moiety (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Concentration: 0.5-6% (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021), commonly 2.5% to limit toxicity (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Use: throughout instrumentation and for 1-2 minutes after it. Preheated NaOCl cools to body temperature once in the canal, so some advise heating it in situ; no clinical studies support heated NaOCl (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Limitations: does not remove the inorganic part of the smear layer; toxic and hypertonic if extruded; weakens dentine at high concentration.

**Factors governing NaOCl efficacy**
- Retention time and volume are essential (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); frequency and volume compensate for low concentration (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Chlorine is consumed within about 2 minutes, so the solution must be constantly replenished (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Organic matter and dentine consume NaOCl and delay killing; in their absence NaOCl kills bacteria even below 0.1% (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- pH: disinfection is optimal at about pH 6, but commercial solutions are made at pH 12 for stability (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Contact: the solution must reach the apical third, which depends on shaping and needle depth.

**2. Chlorhexidine (2%)**
- Cationic bisbiguanide; broad spectrum; substantive, retained in dentine up to 120 days; low toxicity. It cannot dissolve tissue or remove the smear layer.
- The books disagree on its role. Peters, Arias and Shabahang (2021) find 2% CHX similar to 5.25% NaOCl, more effective against *E. faecalis* and synergistic with NaOCl. Siqueira and colleagues (2022) find no microbiological difference from NaOCl and value its substantivity. Peters, Peters and Basrani (2021) cite a systematic review showing better periapical healing when CHX was not added to NaOCl, and conclude NaOCl remains the primary irrigant.
- NaOCl + CHX forms a precipitate containing 4-chloroaniline (PCA); dry the canal with paper points before a final CHX rinse (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**3. Chelators**
- 17% EDTA (Nygaard-Østby, 1957) binds calcium and removes the inorganic smear layer; it is self-limiting. It is used for about 1 minute after shaping and separately from NaOCl, because it removes NaOCl's free chlorine (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Its effect is weaker apically, and exposure beyond 10 minutes erodes dentine (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- 10% citric acid has a similar effect.
- HEBP (etidronate) is a weak chelator that does not react with NaOCl in the short term and can be mixed with it during preparation (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**4. Combination products**
- MTAD (3% doxycycline, 4.25% citric acid, 0.5% Tween 80), used as a final rinse after 1.3% NaOCl: may be more antimicrobial than NaOCl in vitro but not shown to be clinically beneficial (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021); a clinical trial found no bacterial reduction beyond NaOCl alone (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- QMiX (2011): a CHX analogue, EDTA and a surfactant; removes the smear layer as well as EDTA.
- On intraorally infected dentine, chelators with antimicrobials neither removed nor killed biofilm; tissue-dissolving ability is mandatory (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**5. Others:** iodine potassium iodide 2-5%; superoxidized water; saline (flushing only). Adding surfactants does not improve NaOCl's tissue dissolution (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

## 8. Smear layer

### Explanation

Cohen's quotes the AAE definition: "a surface film of debris retained on dentin or other surfaces after instrumentation" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues give its dimensions: "approximately 1- to 2-μm thick", with smear plugs packed into tubules "to depths of up to 40 μm" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. On whether to remove it, Cohen's notes "there is still a controversy" over its effect on outcome (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], whereas Peters, Arias and Shabahang state that "evidence generally supports removing the smear layer before obturation" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; the only clinical outcome evidence Siqueira and colleagues cite is that removal with EDTA and NaOCl "positively influenced the outcome of retreatment cases" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Their final-flush protocol is "5 to 10 ml of 17% EDTA ... followed by 5 to 10 ml of 2.5% NaOCl" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]; Cohen's recommends the same sequence with a final NaOCl flush (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Smear layer**
- Definition: a surface film of debris retained on dentine after instrumentation, containing dentine particles, pulp remnants, bacteria and irrigant (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Thickness 1-2 μm, with smear plugs up to 40 μm into the tubules (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Removal: opens tubules to irrigants and medicaments, improves sealer adaptation, removes a bacterial substrate. Retention: may block bacterial penetration of tubules.
- Evidence: still controversial (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021), but generally supports removal before obturation (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021); removal improved retreatment outcome in one study (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Protocol: 5-10 ml 17% EDTA for 1-3 minutes, then 5-10 ml 2.5% NaOCl (Siqueira et al, 2022).


**Sequence in practice.** Shaping with NaOCl creates the smear layer; EDTA (or another chelator) removes its inorganic part; NaOCl then dissolves the organic remnant. Because EDTA is weaker in the apical third, the final rinse should be delivered close to working length and, where possible, activated. In a two-visit case the smear layer re-forms when the medicament is removed by recapitulation, so the EDTA-NaOCl sequence is repeated before obturation (Siqueira et al, 2022).

## 9. Irrigant delivery: needles and fluid dynamics

### Explanation

With passive syringe irrigation "the actual exchange of irrigant is restricted to 1 to 1.5 mm apical to the needle tip" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues give the practical rules: side-vented and open-ended needles "should be inserted up to 1 or 2 mm short of the WL, respectively" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], with at least 2 ml of NaOCl after each instrument through a 30-gauge needle (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC] and a flow "of 4 to 15 ml/min" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Binding the needle with no backflow "should be avoided under all circumstances" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Volume helps, but "there may be a point of saturation" beyond which more irrigant no longer improves bacterial elimination (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Irrigant delivery**
- Irrigant is exchanged only 1-1.5 mm beyond the needle tip (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021), so the needle must reach the apical third.
- Use 30-gauge needles: side-vented 1 mm and open-ended 2 mm short of working length; at least 2 ml NaOCl after each instrument; gentle flow of 4-15 ml/min; never bind the needle; keep it moving (Siqueira et al, 2022; Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Volume matters up to a saturation point; retention time and irrigant renewal matter beyond it (Siqueira et al, 2022).


**Why this matters.** The zone of irrigant exchange is so short that a canal shaped too narrow for the needle is never cleaned apically, however long it is irrigated. Shaping therefore determines how deep the needle can go, and the needle, the volume and the frequency of renewal determine how much fresh NaOCl reaches the apical biofilm. Slow delivery with constant back-and-forth movement of the needle gives turbulence at the wall while keeping the pressure low enough to avoid extrusion (Siqueira et al, 2022).

## 10. Activation of irrigants

### Explanation

Cohen's states that "effective cleaning of the root canal must include intermittent agitation of the canal content" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Manual dynamic activation (MDA):** "manual push-pull movements using a fitted master gutta-percha cone" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Sonic:** the EndoActivator "uses safe, noncutting polymer tips" in a subsonic handpiece (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. "Sonic devices range between 1500 and 6000 Hz, and ultrasonic equipment requires vibrations greater than 20,000 Hz" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Ultrasonic.** Grossman's describes two physical processes: cavitation, "the growth and collapse of bubbles" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and acoustic streaming, "small but intense eddy currents" around the file (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], while noting that "the root canal diameter does not permit cavitation significantly" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and that the apical third should first be enlarged "to at least the size of a No. 30 to No. 40 file" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang name "acoustic microstreaming" as the main mechanism (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. PUI "was first described by Weller" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; "passive" refers to "the noncutting action" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; it is used "once the root canal system has a final apical size and taper" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Ultrasonic instrumentation with wall contact "may result in the uncontrolled cutting" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and ultrasonic shaping of curved canals "is no longer recommended" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. PUI improves debridement "in vitro; the results in vivo present some controversy" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Continuous ultrasonic irrigation "allows simultaneous continuous irrigant delivery and ultrasonic activation" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Apical negative pressure (EndoVac).** A macrocannula "equivalent to an ISO size #55,.02 taper" removes coronal debris and a microcannula "equivalent to a size #32,.02 taper" works near working length (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Its strength is safety: it "is unlikely to extrude irrigation solution" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; its weakness is passive apical flow, generating "the lowest wall shear stress" in one study (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and in a clinical study its antimicrobial efficacy "was not significantly better" than needle irrigation (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

**Multisonic (GentleWave).** The regimen "begins with 3% NaOCl followed by 8% EDTA" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; reported 12-month success was 97% (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], but the studies come from one group and "more independent studies on GW are essential" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Lasers and light.** Er:YAG at 2940 nm "has the highest absorption in water" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; PIPS keeps the tip in the pulp chamber (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Nd:YAG is "not an alternative, but a possible supplement" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Photodynamic therapy "is a two-step procedure that involves the introduction of a photosensitizer" followed by light (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Experimental.** Nanoparticles of chitosan, zinc oxide and silver (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; ozone, where in vivo results "are inconclusive" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang add that for all these devices "cost-effectiveness still needs to be scientifically demonstrated" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], and mention cold saline irrigation to reduce postoperative pain (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Activation of irrigants:** effective cleaning requires agitation of the canal contents (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

| Method | Principle | Comments |
|---|---|---|
| Manual dynamic activation | Push-pull of a fitted gutta-percha cone | Simple, cheap, effective in vitro |
| Sonic (EndoActivator) | Noncutting polymer tips at 1500-6000 Hz | Minimal extrusion |
| Passive ultrasonic irrigation (Weller) | Noncutting file or wire at more than 20 kHz after final shape; acoustic streaming and cavitation | Better debridement in vitro; in vivo results controversial (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) |
| Continuous ultrasonic irrigation | Energised needle delivering and activating at once | Better debris removal |
| Apical negative pressure (EndoVac) | Macrocannula (#55) and microcannula (#32) suck irrigant to working length | Safest against extrusion; low wall shear stress; not more antimicrobial clinically (Siqueira et al, 2022) |
| Multisonic (GentleWave) | Hydrodynamic cavitation with 3% NaOCl and 8% EDTA in a sealed system | 97% success at 12 months in one group's studies; needs independent trials |
| Lasers (Er:YAG, PIPS, Nd:YAG) | Laser-activated irrigation; photoacoustic streaming | Supplement, not alternative |
| Photodynamic therapy | Photosensitizer + light | Adjunct, in vitro promise |
| Ozone, nanoparticles | Oxidation; antimicrobial particles | In vivo results inconclusive |

- Ultrasonics: cavitation is limited by canal diameter; enlarge the apex to #30-40 before use (Gopikrishna, 2021); ultrasonic shaping of curved canals is no longer recommended (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Cost-effectiveness of all devices remains to be shown (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).


**Choosing an activation method.** Activation is used after the canal has reached its final size and taper, so that the tip oscillates freely. Ultrasonic and sonic activation clean isthmuses and fins better than syringe irrigation in vitro; negative pressure is chosen where extrusion is a concern, such as open apices or proximity to the sinus; multisonic and laser systems are newer, costly, and supported mainly by laboratory data or by single research groups (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Whichever is chosen, it supplements and does not replace chemomechanical preparation with NaOCl.

## 11. Interappointment medicaments

### Explanation

The aims of intracanal medication "are restrict bacterial regrowth, supply continued disinfection, and create a physical barrier" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Calcium hydroxide was "introduced by Hermann" in 1920 (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. The evidence conflicts within one chapter: one study found that one week of Ca(OH)2 "resulted in a 92.5% reduction" after irrigation alone had achieved 61.9% (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and Ca(OH)2 can "hydrolyze the lipid moiety of bacterial lipopolysaccharides" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; yet newer reviews conclude that it "has limited effectiveness in eliminating bacteria from human root canals" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and removal leaves "a residue covering 20% to 45% of the canal wall surfaces" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Phenolics and formaldehyde are obsolete: "there is no clinical reason to use formocresol" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Interappointment medicaments**
- Aims: restrict bacterial regrowth, continue disinfection, act as a physical barrier (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Calcium hydroxide (Hermann, 1920): high pH, slow-acting, broad spectrum; detoxifies LPS. One study showed 92.5% bacterial reduction after one week against 61.9% after irrigation alone, but systematic reviews show limited effectiveness, and it leaves residue on 20-45% of the walls (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Chlorhexidine gel, Ca(OH)2/CHX mixtures, Ledermix and antibiotic pastes (regenerative cases): adjuncts with variable evidence.
- Phenolics and formocresol: toxic, no clinical justification (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).


**When a medicament is used.** Ca(OH)2 is placed when treatment cannot be completed in one visit, for example with persistent exudate, severe symptoms or limited time. It must fill the canal homogeneously to working length as a thick but moist slurry, placed with a lentulo spiral or syringe and confirmed radiographically, under a sound temporary seal; it must not be extruded towards the sinus or mandibular canal (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

## 12. Complications of chemical debridement

### Explanation

Extrusion of NaOCl causes "severe pain, edema of neighboring soft tissues", bleeding, ecchymosis and chlorine taste (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; part of the rapid swelling is osmotic, since 3-6% solutions reach "2800 mOsm/kg" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Management includes analgesia, a nerve block rather than infiltration when swollen, and "extraoral cold compresses" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], then warm compresses after a day. The accident itself "did not adversely affect the endodontic prognosis" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Full management is in the Irrigants topic.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Complications**
- NaOCl accident: immediate severe pain, rapid oedema (hypertonic solution), haemorrhage, ecchymosis, chlorine taste, possible paraesthesia (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Management: reassure and inform; nerve-block anaesthesia and analgesics; cold compresses first day, warm compresses after; antibiotics only if infection risk; daily review; refer if worsening. The accident does not itself worsen endodontic prognosis (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Prevention: loose needle, side-vented tip, rubber stop, gentle flow, extra care in open apices.
- Others: PCA precipitate (NaOCl + CHX), dentine erosion (prolonged EDTA), Ca(OH)2 extrusion into nerve or sinus.


**Prevention is the key.** Most accidents follow a needle that binds, excessive pressure, a wide or resorbed apex, or a perforation that was not recognised. Checking the needle length with a rubber stop, keeping the needle loose and moving, and using side-vented needles or negative pressure in high-risk teeth prevent nearly all of them.

## 13. Debridement protocol and evaluation

### Explanation

Cohen's summary protocol: "NaOCl is the irrigant of choice"; "EDTA or other chelators should be used at the end of a procedure to remove the smear layer, followed by a final flush with" NaOCl for 1 minute (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Cleaning is judged indirectly: after NaOCl there should be "an absence of visible turbidity and effervescence" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC] and "glassy smooth" walls (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues warn that none of these signs is reliable and define completion as enlargement "compatible with the root anatomy and large enough to be safe and antibacterially effective" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A contemporary debridement protocol**
1. Rubber dam; disinfect the field; access; remove chamber contents; flood with NaOCl.
2. Coronal pre-enlargement; glide path; working length with apex locator and radiograph; patency.
3. Shape with NiTi or balanced force, irrigating with at least 2 ml NaOCl after every instrument and recapitulating.
4. Gauge and complete the apical preparation to a size compatible with anatomy.
5. Final irrigation: NaOCl activated (sonic, PUI or negative pressure); 17% EDTA 5-10 ml for 1 minute; final NaOCl flush (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Siqueira et al, 2022).
6. Dry; obturate in the same visit, or place Ca(OH)2 and a sound temporary seal.

**Evaluation:** no turbidity or effervescence after NaOCl, glassy smooth walls, master cone tug-back (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). Clinical signs are unreliable; debridement is complete when the canal is as large as anatomy allows and large enough to be antibacterially effective (Siqueira et al, 2022).

## 14. Conclusion

### Explanation

Peters, Peters and Basrani's summary applies here more than anywhere: "cleaning and shaping are important, interdependent steps in root canal treatment" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** Debridement is achieved by instruments, irrigants, activation and medicaments working together; none alone can clean the root canal system, because its anatomy of isthmuses, fins, lateral canals and tubules is beyond the reach of any file. Shaping makes space for the needle; NaOCl dissolves tissue and kills bacteria; EDTA removes the smear layer; activation carries irrigants into areas files cannot touch; calcium hydroxide may continue disinfection between visits. The evidence for most newer devices and irrigants is still largely in vitro, so NaOCl-based chemomechanical preparation remains the standard. The aim is the greatest possible reduction of the microbial load and its products, compatible with the anatomy of the root, with the least loss of tooth structure, followed by a sound obturation and coronal seal.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

1. **Claim:** chemomechanical preparation cannot sterilise the canal. **Textbooks:** Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Siqueira et al, 2022. **Strength:** consistent histological, micro-CT and microbiological data. **Conclusion:** [Certain].
2. **Claim:** CHX is as good an irrigant as NaOCl. **For:** Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021 (similar to 5.25% NaOCl, synergistic); Siqueira et al, 2022 (no microbiological difference; substantivity). **Against:** Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021 (systematic review: better healing without adjunct CHX; no tissue dissolution). **Conclusion:** [Likely] that CHX matches NaOCl in killing bacteria; [Certain] that it cannot replace NaOCl for tissue dissolution.
3. **Claim:** activation devices improve outcome. **Textbooks:** better debridement in vitro for PUI, sonic and negative pressure (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). **Challenge:** in vivo results controversial; EndoVac no better clinically (Siqueira et al, 2022); GentleWave outcome data from one group only. **Conclusion:** [Likely] for cleaner canals; [Guessing] for better healing.
4. **Claim:** MTAD and QMiX are superior final rinses. **For:** in vitro antimicrobial data (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). **Against:** no clinical benefit (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021); no extra bacterial reduction in a trial; no biofilm removal on intraorally infected dentine (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). **Conclusion:** [Guessing].
5. **Claim:** smear layer should be removed. **Textbooks:** generally supported (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021); improved retreatment outcome (Siqueira et al, 2022); still controversial (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). **Conclusion:** [Likely].
6. **Claim:** Ca(OH)2 completes debridement between visits. **For:** 92.5% reduction after one week in one study; LPS detoxification. **Against:** systematic reviews show limited effectiveness (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). **Conclusion:** [Guessing] for consistent added benefit.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

- Writing debridement as purely mechanical instrumentation; examiners expect the chemical, activation and medicament arms.
- Claiming that any irrigant or device sterilises the canal.
- Mixing EDTA with NaOCl, or recommending prolonged EDTA exposure.
- Stating that CHX is superior to NaOCl without noting it cannot dissolve tissue.
- Presenting GentleWave or lasers as proven to improve healing.
- Omitting needle depth and the 1-1.5 mm exchange zone, which explains why shaping is needed.
- Forgetting the PCA precipitate when using CHX after NaOCl.
