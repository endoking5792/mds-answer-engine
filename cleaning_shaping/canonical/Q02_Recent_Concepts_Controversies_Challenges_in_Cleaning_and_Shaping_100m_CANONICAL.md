## 1. Introduction: the changing aims of cleaning and shaping

### Explanation

Peters and Arias set out the modern double aim: to "preserve the natural dentition for the lifetime of the patient (‘retention’) and treat or prevent apical periodontitis (‘healing’)" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. The second aim is classical; the first is new in emphasis, because "vertical fracture or other nonendodontic causes are the major reasons for the eventual loss" of root-filled teeth (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], and "root fracture and non-restorable carious defects are most frequently cited" reasons for extraction (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

The central paradox of recent decades is stated plainly by Peters and Arias: "despite substantial improvements in endodontic technology in recent years, healing outcomes after root canal treatments remain substantially similar" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. In the Ng, Mann and Gulabivala prospective study they use as the chapter's guiding reference, success "was about 83% and 80% for initial treatment and re-treatment" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. They still regard "preparation and disinfection of the root canal as the single most important factor" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. Jain places the predictability of endodontics on knowledge, skill and desire: "three major elements determine the predictability of successful endodontics" (Jain, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Since Schilder's 1974 concept of "cleaning and shaping", root canal preparation has changed more than any other step of endodontic treatment: nickel-titanium (NiTi) alloys, heat treatment, reciprocation, adaptive instruments, electronic apex locators, magnification, CBCT and activated irrigation. The aims have also changed. Treatment now has two goals: healing (treating or preventing apical periodontitis) and retention (keeping the tooth for the patient's lifetime) (Peters and Arias, 2025). Most root-filled teeth are now lost to vertical root fracture and non-restorable caries rather than to persistent infection (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Peters and Arias, 2025), so preservation of dentine has become an objective in its own right.

The paradox is that, despite better technology, healing outcomes have remained substantially similar, about 83% for initial treatment and 80% for retreatment in the Ng, Mann and Gulabivala prospective study (Peters and Arias, 2025). The reason is the complexity of root canal anatomy, which no instrument fully cleans. Preparation and disinfection nevertheless remain the single most important factors in treating endodontic disease (Peters and Arias, 2025).

## 3. Minimally invasive shaping and preservation of dentine

### Explanation

Peters, Arias and Shabahang report that "preserving pericervical dentin (4 mm above and below the crestal bone) is crucial for the distribution of functional stresses" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Peters and Arias add that "excessive coronal flaring reduces dentinal wall thickness and therefore structural strength", and recommend instruments "with a limited maximum flute diameter (MFD)" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; Grossman's describes TruNatomy's 0.8 mm maximum flute diameter as making it "one of the thinnest rotary files available" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Peters and Basrani cite an experiment suggesting that "more conservative access cavities, in particular in premolars, may increase fracture resistance and permit similar shaping outcomes" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

Gates-Glidden drills illustrate the change. Peters and Arias: they "have largely been superseded by dedicated NiTi rotaries" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC], and using one on an MB2 canal before scouting it "is a common practice among inexperienced clinicians" that creates ledges (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; Peters, Arias and Shabahang call them "unsuitable" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer still call them "safe, relatively inexpensive" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. Peters and Arias also criticise the old stainless steel and step-back routine: "the resulting shape is larger than needed in the coronal third, potentially debilitating the tooth" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Minimally invasive shaping**
- The pericervical dentine, 4 mm above and below the crestal bone, distributes functional stresses; its preservation improves fracture resistance (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- Excessive coronal flaring thins the walls and weakens the root (Peters and Arias, 2025). Old stainless steel step-back preparations with Gates-Glidden drills produced coronal shapes larger than needed (Peters and Arias, 2025).
- Modern practice: conservative access, scouting with a #10 K-file before flaring, orifice modification with small NiTi orifice shapers rather than Gates-Glidden drills, and instruments with a small maximum flute diameter (for example TruNatomy, 0.8 mm) (Peters and Arias, 2025; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Conservative access cavities may increase fracture resistance with similar shaping outcomes (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**Controversy:** Gates-Glidden drills are "superseded" (Peters and Arias, 2025) and "unsuitable" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021), but Hülsmann and Schäfer (2019) still describe them as safe and inexpensive. The benefit of minimally invasive access for long-term survival rests mostly on laboratory and finite element studies.


**Rationale.** Because a root-filled tooth is now more likely to be lost by fracture than by persistent infection, every millimetre of coronal and cervical dentine removed during access and flaring must be justified by a gain in disinfection. The shift is from "straight-line access at any cost" to selective removal of only the restrictive dentine that prevents files and needles from reaching the apical third.

## 4. Advances in NiTi alloys

### Explanation

**Basic alloy.** "Nitinol" is "an acronym for the elements from which the material was composed" (nickel, titanium, Naval Ordnance Laboratory) (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Composition is reported differently: Jain gives "approximately 56% nickel and 44% titanium by weight", "generically known as 55 Nitinol" (Jain, 2016) [SRC], while Grossman's gives "55% nickel and 45% titanium" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Schäfer agrees with 55/45 for conventional NiTi and reports that "M-wire consists of 55.8% nickel" and CM-wire "a lower percentage weight of nickel (52.1%)" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]. Recoverable strain is also stated differently: "as much as 8%" (Jain, 2016) [SRC] against "up to 10%" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC].

Jain describes the mechanism as "a stress-induced thermoelastic transformation" from austenite to martensite (Jain, 2016) [SRC], giving the two clinically important properties, "superelasticity and high resistance to cyclic fatigue" (Jain, 2016) [SRC]; a #15 NiTi file is "two to three times more flexible" than stainless steel (Jain, 2016) [SRC].

**Heat treatment.** Studies around 2000 found that heat treatment "was effective in increasing the flexibility" of NiTi instruments (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. "M-wire was introduced in 2007" and R-phase files in 2008 (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Grossman's describes M-wire as containing "deformed and microtwinned martensite, premartensitic R phase, and austenitic phase" and CM wire as having "an austenitic finish temperature of approximately 55°C" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Schäfer notes that deformed CM-wire files "will regain their original shape when heated during sterilization" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]. Jain reports better "centering ability" and less transportation with these files, and that "up to seven sterilization cycles" did not affect them (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Peters and Arias note that flexible martensitic alloy also "cut more rapidly" in lateral action (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. A caution from Cohen's: the fatigue advantage of martensitic alloys at room temperature "was greatly reduced at body temperature" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advances in NiTi metallurgy**

*Conventional NiTi (Nitinol, Naval Ordnance Laboratory):* about 55-56% nickel and 44-45% titanium by weight (Jain, 2016; Gopikrishna, 2021). Under stress the austenite transforms to martensite (stress-induced thermoelastic transformation) and back when the stress is removed, giving superelasticity and resistance to cyclic fatigue (Jain, 2016). NiTi recovers 8% (Jain, 2016) to 10% (Schäfer, 2017) strain, against less than 1% for stainless steel; a #15 NiTi file is two to three times more flexible.

*Heat-treated (thermomechanically processed) alloys*

| Alloy | Introduced / features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| M-wire | 2007; mixed martensite, R-phase and austenite; 55.8% Ni; more flexible and fatigue resistant | ProTaper Next, WaveOne, Reciproc, Vortex |
| R-phase | 2008; rhombohedral intermediate phase; allows twisting instead of grinding | Twisted File, K3XF |
| Controlled memory (CM) | Austenite finish about 55°C; 52.1% Ni; martensitic at body temperature; no shape memory, regains shape on autoclaving | HyFlex CM |
| Gold and Blue | Micro-milled, then given specific annealing and cooling steps that make them more martensitic (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); flexible, fatigue resistant | ProTaper Gold, WaveOne Gold, Reciproc Blue |
| MaxWire | Martensite-austenite change with temperature; expands in the canal | XP-endo Shaper and Finisher |
(Jain, 2016; Gopikrishna, 2021; Schäfer, 2017)

*Benefits:* better centring and less transportation (Jain, 2016); higher fatigue resistance; martensitic alloys cut faster with light touch (Peters and Arias, 2025); unaffected by up to seven sterilisation cycles (Jain, 2016).

*Controversy:* figures for composition and recoverable strain differ between books; the fatigue advantage of martensitic alloys measured at room temperature is greatly reduced at body temperature (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**Generations of NiTi files (Gopikrishna, 2021):** first, constant taper with radial lands (ProFile, GT, LightSpeed); second, active cutting edges without lands (ProTaper, K3, Mtwo, RaCe); third, heat-treated alloys (ProTaper Gold, Twisted File, HyFlex CM); fourth, reciprocation (WaveOne, Reciproc); fifth, asymmetric or off-centred cross-sections (ProTaper Next, One Shape, TruNatomy).



**Why heat treatment helps.** Heat treatment raises the transformation temperatures so that more of the file is martensitic at body temperature. Martensite bends with little force and resists fatigue, so heat-treated files follow curves with less restoring force, transport less, and survive more cycles; files that are not superelastic can be precurved and regain shape on autoclaving (Jain, 2016; Schäfer, 2017).

## 5. Advances in instrument design, manufacture and motors

### Explanation

Design has changed alongside the alloy. Grossman's notes that rotary files are made "with modified noncutting tips as these noncutting tips cause less canal transportation" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; radial lands were added "to reduce canal transportation" and support the cutting edge (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; later files have variable, then "regressive" taper (TruNatomy) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang describe files "with more recent instruments featuring smaller maximum fluted diameters of less than 1 mm" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC] and new manufacturing methods replacing grinding: "twisting, shape-setting, and electric discharge machining" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; the thermal treatments "allows dentin preservation in the coronal third of many cases with a minimal orifice modification" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Martensitic rotaries "work better with higher speeds, for example, 500 rpm" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

Motors: most rotary files work "between 1.5 and 4.0 N cm" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; electric motors offer auto-stop and "an optional auto reverse function" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; integrated motors with apex locators stop and reverse "the moment the file tip extends beyond the working length" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. CBCT "may also help to determine the best strategy to shape the most difficult canal anatomies" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advances in instrument design and manufacture**
- **Tips:** non-cutting (Batt) tips reduce transportation (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Taper:** from ISO .02 to greater fixed tapers (.04-.12), then variable taper along one file (ProTaper), and regressive taper with a small maximum flute diameter (TruNatomy) to spare coronal dentine (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Cross-section:** U-file with radial lands (ProFile, GT), convex triangular without lands (ProTaper), S-shaped (Mtwo, Reciproc), off-centred rectangular giving swaggering motion (ProTaper Next), parallelogram (TruNatomy).
- **Maximum flute diameter:** under 1 mm in recent files, to limit coronal enlargement (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- **Manufacture:** grinding replaced or supplemented by twisting (Twisted File), shape-setting (TRUShape, XP-endo) and electric discharge machining (HyFlex EDM) (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021); electropolishing, e.g. RaCe (Jain, 2016).
- **Motors:** torque-controlled electric motors (most files 1.5-4.0 N cm) with auto-stop and auto-reverse; combined rotary/reciprocating and adaptive motors; motors with integrated apex locators that stop and reverse at working length (Gopikrishna, 2021). Martensitic files may run faster (about 500 rpm) than austenitic files (about 250 rpm) (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
- **Imaging and vision:** CBCT helps plan shaping of difficult anatomy (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021); the operating microscope helps locate and negotiate canals.

## 6. Instrument fracture: the main mechanical challenge

### Explanation

Jain calls "repeated cyclic metal fatigue" possibly "the most important factor in instrument separation" (Jain, 2016) [SRC]; files break "most commonly at the point of maximum flexion of the shaft", usually the midpoint of the curve (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Torsional load is reduced by crown-down sequences (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and Jain advises "right-torque" motors (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Incidence: hand files 1-6%, rotary 0.4-5% (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; and the current evidence "does not suggest" that a retained instrument significantly raises failure when treated by specialists (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Instrument fracture**
- Two mechanisms: cyclic (flexural) fatigue from repeated tension and compression in a curve, and torsional failure when the tip locks while the shank rotates. Cyclic fatigue may be the most important; files usually break at the point of maximum flexion, the midpoint of the curvature (Jain, 2016).
- Incidence: hand files 1-6%, rotary files 0.4-5%; a retained fragment does not necessarily worsen outcome in specialist hands (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Prevention: glide path; crown-down sequence to avoid taper lock (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); light pecking strokes; slow speed and right-torque motors (Jain, 2016); heat-treated alloys; single use in difficult canals; no instrumentation in a dry canal (Peters and Arias, 2025).


**Speed and torque.** High speed improves cutting but reduces tactile sense and raises the risk of distortion and breakage; high torque makes a file aggressive and more likely to lock, while too low a torque stops it cutting. Each instrument has its own correct torque, which modern motors store in their library (Jain, 2016; Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 7. Kinematics: rotation, reciprocation and adaptive motion

### Explanation

Reciprocation is old: "Reciprocation is defined as any repetitive back-and-forth motion and has been used clinically to drive stainless steel files since 1958" (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. The modern form uses unequal angles, "lower than the angle at which the elastic limit" of the alloy develops (Jain, 2016) [SRC]; Grossman's: the counterclockwise engaging angle is "five times the clockwise disengaging angle" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The date of Yared's ProTaper F2 reciprocation technique is given as 2007 by Jain ("Yared in 2007 described") (Jain, 2016) [SRC] and as 2008 in Grossman's Table 14.6 (Gopikrishna, 2021); the year differs between books.

Advantages: reduced torsional and bending stress and longer fatigue life (Jain, 2016); shorter working time, as reciprocating systems "considerably reduce the working time" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]. Disadvantages and doubts: Jain concludes "research to date has not indicated they are superior to files used in continuous rotation" (Jain, 2016) [SRC]; Schäfer reports studies showing more incomplete dentinal cracks and "an increased tendency of apical debris extrusion" alongside studies showing no difference (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]; Grossman's warns that misuse can cause debris extrusion and "increased postoperative pain" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Schäfer's rule: withdraw "after three ‘pecks’" (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC].

Adaptive motors run in rotation and switch to reciprocation "once the file exceeds a certain stress inside the canal" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Single file.** Peters and Arias accept single-file, single-length shaping in simple canals but state that "a single-file technique is typically not appropriate in molar cases of moderate difficulty" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Kinematics**

| | Continuous rotation | Reciprocation | Adaptive motion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Principle | 360° clockwise rotation | Unequal clockwise and counterclockwise angles below the elastic limit; counterclockwise cutting about five times the release angle | Rotation that switches to reciprocation under load |
| Examples | ProTaper Gold, HyFlex, TruNatomy | WaveOne Gold, Reciproc Blue | TF Adaptive |
| Advantages | Augers debris coronally; good tactile control | Less torsional and cyclic stress; longer fatigue life; single file; shorter working time | Combines both |
| Disadvantages | Higher fatigue in curves | Possible dentinal cracks and debris extrusion; one size does not fit all | Motor cost |
(Jain, 2016; Gopikrishna, 2021; Schäfer, 2017)

- History: stainless steel reciprocation since 1958 (Racer handpiece 1958, Giromatic 1964, both with equal 90° angles; Jain, 2016; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019); Yared's ProTaper F2 technique (2007 in Jain, 2016; 2008 in Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Use reciprocating files with gentle in-and-out pecks of up to 3 mm, withdrawing after three pecks to clean and irrigate (Schäfer, 2017).

**Controversy:** reciprocation has not been shown superior to continuous rotation (Jain, 2016); studies on cracks and extrusion conflict (Schäfer, 2017). A single file is acceptable in simple canals but not in molars of moderate difficulty (Peters and Arias, 2025).

## 8. Canal-adaptive and 3D conforming instruments

### Explanation

Because "a rotating file can produce a round canal at best" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], several designs try to touch more of an oval canal. The SAF "relies on the gentle abrasive action of a flexible mesh lattice" (Jain, 2016) [SRC], removing a uniform 60-75 μm (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; it "is no longer commercially available" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The XP-endo Finisher becomes a spoon of 1.5 mm depth at body temperature (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Jain reports that TRUShape files contact "up to 75% of canal walls" (Jain, 2016) [SRC]; these figures read like manufacturer data [Guessing on independent confirmation]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang call the newest group "3D conforming instruments" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; Peters and Arias describe shape-set files (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC] and, as the simple alternative, preparing "two canal pathways" in buccal and lingual extensions (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Canal-adaptive and 3D conforming instruments**
- Problem: rotating files cut round canals, leaving the buccal and lingual extensions of oval canals untouched (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Self-adjusting file: hollow, compressible NiTi lattice that adapts to the canal, abrades a uniform 60-75 μm layer with vibration and continuous irrigation (Jain, 2016; Gopikrishna, 2021); now withdrawn from the market.
- XP-endo Shaper and Finisher: MaxWire alloy that expands at body temperature (the Finisher into a 1.5 mm spoon) to scrape walls conventional files miss (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- TRUShape: S-shaped, shape-set files reported to contact up to 75% of canal walls and to remove less dentine (Jain, 2016; figures from manufacturer-type data).
- Alternative without special files: prepare two canal paths in the far buccal and far lingual parts of an oval canal (Peters and Arias, 2025).

## 9. Glide path and apical patency

### Explanation

Jain quotes West's definition, "a smooth radicular tunnel from the orifice of the canal to the physiologic terminus" (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Peters and Arias's test: "a straight, not precurved size #10 K-file can passively and smoothly travel to working length", checked from 1-2 mm and from 5-6 mm off length (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; Peters, Peters and Basrani use a #15 file for the same test (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Rotary glide path files "should be used only after a size #10 K-file is placed to working length" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; they preserve canal anatomy better with "less incidence of postoperative pain" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], while Jain argues that manual glide paths keep "greater tactile control, lower risk of endodontic fracture" (Jain, 2016) [SRC].

Patency has moved from controversy to recommendation. Peters and Arias: accumulating evidence "strongly suggests that keeping canals patent is associated with better outcomes", and achieving patency was "a prognostic factor for periapical healing" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. Sikri's objection that patency "may push microorganisms into periapical area" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC] is the older view.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Glide path**
- Definition: a smooth radicular tunnel from the orifice to the physiological terminus (West, in Jain, 2016).
- Confirmed when a straight, unbent #10 K-file (Peters and Arias, 2025) or #15 K-file (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) slides passively to working length.
- Manual glide path: better tactile control and lower fracture risk, but stainless steel can ledge curved canals (Jain, 2016). Rotary glide path (ProGlider, PathFile, TruNatomy Glider) after a #10 reaches length: better preservation of anatomy and less postoperative pain (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**Apical patency**
- Formerly disputed because of possible extrusion of debris and bacteria (Sikri, 2019).
- Now supported: patency is associated with better outcomes and is a prognostic factor for healing, with less postoperative pain and no increase in irrigation accidents (Peters and Arias, 2025).


**Benefits of patency listed by Peters and Arias (2025):** lower risk of losing length; less canal transportation and fewer preparation errors; better tactile sense during apical shaping; fewer gas bubbles blocking irrigant from reaching working length; better delivery of irrigants to the apical third.

## 10. Working length, apical limit and apical size

### Explanation

**Apical limit.** Electronic apex locators allow "safe canal shaping as close as 0.5 mm to the canal terminus" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; for infected canals "the best healing results are achieved when the working length is between 0 and 0.5 mm from the tip of the root" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. This is closer than the traditional 0.5-1 mm short (necrotic) and 1-2 mm short (vital) (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and supports Siqueira and colleagues' advice to clean to the terminus in infected teeth (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. For vital pulps, Peters and Arias note "recent paradigms advocate the preservation of the vital pulp tissue" through vital pulp therapy rather than pulpectomy (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

**Planned preparation.** Siqueira and colleagues describe a "planned apical preparation principle" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC] in which canal diameters 1-4 mm from the foramen are measured on CBCT "to determine the ideal master apical instrument size and taper" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

**Apical size: the main controversy.** Peters and Arias: "no specific apical size leads to better clinical outcomes" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; size should instead permit the irrigation needle: "#25 with a minimum taper of 0.06" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC], or, in their disinfection section, "#25 with a 0.08 or a size #30 with a taper of 0.06" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; the two minima in the same chapter differ slightly. Cohen's records both camps: "#50 or greater" for disinfection, while "others found no difference whether the selected final size was small or large" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues favour larger sizes in infected canals (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Peters and Arias add a danger of large sizes: "dentin shavings compacted into canal portions" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Working length and apical limit**
- Electronic apex locators confirmed by radiograph allow shaping to within 0.5 mm of the terminus; in infected canals the best healing is at 0-0.5 mm from the radiographic apex (Peters and Arias, 2025), closer than the traditional 0.5-1 mm short (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Vital pulps: recent paradigms favour vital pulp therapy (pulpotomy) over complete pulpectomy where possible (Peters and Arias, 2025).
- Planned apical preparation: CBCT measurement of canal diameter 1-4 mm from the foramen to choose the master apical size and taper (Siqueira et al, 2022).

**Apical size**
| View | Source |
|---|---|
| Large (#50 or more) for disinfection | Reported by Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; favoured for infected canals by Siqueira et al, 2022 |
| No difference between small and large | Reported by Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021 |
| No size predicts outcome; minimum size is the one that admits a 30-gauge needle: #25/.06 or #25/.08 or #30/.06 | Peters and Arias, 2025 |
| Large sizes may pack dentine debris into inaccessible anatomy | Peters and Arias, 2025 |

Current consensus: choose the apical size from curvature, root wall thickness and irrigant delivery, not from a fixed number (Peters and Arias, 2025).


**Why the limits are debated.** Working short in an infected canal leaves bacteria and debris in the critical apical millimetres and can block the canal; working long risks extrusion of debris, irrigant and filling material, which also lowers success. The apex locator has narrowed this margin, allowing infected canals to be cleaned close to the terminus while preparation and filling stay within the canal.

## 11. Shaping for disinfection: irrigation-centred concepts

### Explanation

Peters and Arias set the realistic aim as "to significantly reduce the irritants more than totally eliminate them" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. They emphasise time: dissolution of organic matter needs "~30-40 mins" of contact (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC], "a minimum contact time of up to 40 min has been discussed" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. "Continuous chelation", combining "etidronic acid with sodium hypochlorite during the shaping procedure", is a new strategy (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC], possible because HEBP has "no short-term reactivity with NaOCl" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. On chlorhexidine, the Ng study found "irrigation with chlorhexidine is not beneficial" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC], echoing Cohen's (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], whereas Siqueira and colleagues see no microbiological difference (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Of the newer devices, PUI improves debridement in vitro but in vivo results "present some controversy" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; GentleWave reports 97% 12-month success (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC] but "more independent studies on GW are essential" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; for all devices "cost-effectiveness still needs to be scientifically demonstrated" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Lussi's non-instrumentation technique (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC] "cannot be considered a viable alternative" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Shaping for disinfection**
- Shaping is now judged by whether it lets irrigants work: the canal must admit a 30-gauge side-vented needle 1-2 mm from working length, and NaOCl needs long contact (up to 30-40 minutes) with frequent replenishment, so preparation is done through a reservoir of NaOCl (Peters and Arias, 2025).
- Continuous chelation: etidronate (HEBP) mixed with NaOCl during shaping, since it does not inactivate NaOCl (Peters and Arias, 2025; Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Final rinse: 17% EDTA for 1 minute followed by NaOCl; activation by sonic, ultrasonic, negative pressure or laser (details in Q15).
- Chlorhexidine irrigation was not beneficial in the Ng outcome study (Peters and Arias, 2025).
- Minimally instrumented cleaning (GentleWave) reports 97% success at 12 months but needs independent trials; earlier non-instrumentation techniques failed clinically (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019).


**Consequence for shaping.** If irrigation, not the instrument, does most of the cleaning in fins and isthmuses, the shape need only be large and tapered enough to carry the needle and allow irrigant exchange; anything larger removes dentine without a proven biological return.

## 12. Evidence-based guidance: the ESE S3 guideline

### Explanation

Peters and Arias report the European Society of Endodontology S3-level clinical practice guideline (2023): root canal preparation "should be performed using contemporary engine-driven techniques with NiTi root canal instruments" in mature teeth (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; NaOCl followed by EDTA may be considered; and the guideline recommends "a single-visit approach without the use of inter-appointment calcium hydroxide" in asymptomatic teeth where procedures are adequate (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. They also report that single-cone obturation "when executed to high technical standards may have appropriate clinical outcomes" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC], which changes the shaping target, since the shape no longer has to accommodate spreaders or pluggers.

#### ✎ Exam core

**ESE S3-level guideline (2023), as reported by Peters and Arias (2025)**
- Use contemporary engine-driven NiTi instruments for root canal preparation in mature teeth.
- Irrigate with NaOCl (1-5.25%) followed by EDTA and NaOCl.
- In asymptomatic mature teeth with adequate procedures, a single visit without interappointment calcium hydroxide is recommended.
- Gutta-percha with epoxy-resin, zinc oxide-eugenol or calcium silicate sealer by lateral, warm vertical, carrier-based or single-cone technique may be used; obturation options are listed as "may be considered".
- Separately, Peters and Arias (2025) note that well-executed single-cone obturation may give appropriate clinical outcomes; if so, shapes need not be widened for spreaders or pluggers [Likely; inference].


## 13. A contemporary shaping protocol

### Explanation

Peters and Arias give a systematic sequence. Scout "with a #10 K-file, not with the intention of negotiating the canal to its full length", but to orient the orifice shaper (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; modify the orifice; negotiate with a "viscous chelator ... to prevent pulp tissue blockage" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC], an apex locator on the file, and "the shortest file that can realistically reach patency" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. Tactile feedback distinguishes impediments: "a loose resistance indicates the presence of a ledge or acute curve, while so-called rubbery resistance suggests the presence of compacted soft tissue" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. After glide path and NiTi shaping through NaOCl, apical size is gauged: after reaching #25, "a set of flexible K-files sizes #25, 30 and 35 is used" at working length (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; finally "patency is again verified with a precurved size #10 K-file" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A contemporary shaping protocol (Peters and Arias, 2025)**
1. Analyse the preoperative radiographs (and CBCT where indicated) for curvature, length and anatomy; choose the target apical size and taper.
2. Conservative access; scout each orifice with a #10 K-file only to orient the next instrument.
3. Orifice modification with a small NiTi orifice shaper; avoid Gates-Glidden drills and overflaring.
4. Negotiate with a #08-#10 K-file in a viscous chelator, with the apex locator attached, using watch-winding; precurve only if an impediment is felt (loose resistance = ledge or curve; rubbery resistance = packed tissue).
5. Establish working length electronically, verify radiographically; confirm patency.
6. Glide path: straight #10 (or #15) K-file to length, then rotary glide path file if desired.
7. Shape with heat-treated NiTi through a reservoir of NaOCl, never in a dry canal; clean flutes; recapitulate.
8. Gauge the apical size with K-files (#25, 30, 35); enlarge further if larger files reach length.
9. Recheck patency with a precurved #10 K-file; final activated irrigation and EDTA.

## 14. Major controversies (summary)

### Explanation

The disagreements below are collected from the sections above and from Q01. None is resolved by randomised outcome evidence; "there is no consensus or clinical evidence on which technique or instrument design or type is clinically superior" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], and "there is no evidence that step-down techniques are superior to step-back techniques or vice versa" except balanced force (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. On coronal flaring, Peters, Peters and Basrani accept only that it reduces working-length change (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; its claimed effect on extrusion "has not been confirmed in experiments" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Controversies in cleaning and shaping**

| Issue | One view | Other view |
|---|---|---|
| Apical size | Large (#50+) for disinfection | No size predicts outcome; minimum to admit the needle (Peters and Arias, 2025) |
| Apical limit | 0.5-2 mm short of radiographic apex | Clean to the terminus in infected canals; best healing 0-0.5 mm (Peters and Arias, 2025; Siqueira et al, 2022) |
| Patency | May push debris and bacteria out | Associated with better outcomes and less pain (Peters and Arias, 2025) |
| Coronal flaring | Essential for control and safety | Weakens the root if excessive; benefit on extrusion unproven (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) |
| Gates-Glidden drills | Safe and inexpensive (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) | Superseded and unsuitable (Peters and Arias, 2025; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) |
| Crown-down vs step-back | Crown-down advantages listed (Gopikrishna, 2021) | No evidence of superiority except balanced force (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) |
| Rotation vs reciprocation | Reciprocation safer and faster | Not superior; possible cracks and extrusion (Jain, 2016; Schäfer, 2017) |
| Single file vs multiple files | Simplicity in easy canals | Not appropriate for moderately difficult molars (Peters and Arias, 2025) |
| Chlorhexidine irrigation | Equal to NaOCl microbiologically (Siqueira et al, 2022) | Not beneficial in outcome study (Peters and Arias, 2025) |
| Interappointment Ca(OH)2 | Improves bacterial reduction | Single visit without Ca(OH)2 in asymptomatic teeth (ESE guideline) |
| Minimal vs conventional shaping | Preserve dentine for retention | Smaller shapes may leave infection; long-term data lacking |


**Why these issues stay unresolved:** most comparisons are in vitro (plastic blocks, extracted teeth, micro-CT), outcome studies are few and retrospective, and a single change of instrument is too small to alter healing rates measurably when canal anatomy, microbial load and coronal restoration have larger effects.

## 15. Challenges

### Explanation

Peters and Arias list the anatomical limits: lateral canals, curvatures, fins, cul-de-sacs and isthmuses "make total debridement virtually impossible" (Peters and Arias, 2025). For difficult canals they give strategies: "consider adapting the tools to the anatomy of the specific canal system, rather than adapting the canal to the tool" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC], and divide severely curved canals "in two" stages (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. They prefer a tapered apical "resistance form" to the old apical stop (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. Canal transportation remains "at the root of all these clinical problems" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Challenges in cleaning and shaping**
1. **Anatomical complexity:** oval and C-shaped canals, isthmuses, lateral canals, apical deltas, MB2, S-shaped and severely curved canals; no instrument prepares all walls, and micro-CT shows large uninstrumented areas even after modern preparation (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
2. **Microbial persistence:** biofilm in untouched areas and tubules; the realistic aim is reduction, not elimination (Peters and Arias, 2025).
3. **Balancing disinfection against dentine preservation:** larger shapes clean more but weaken the root; long-term outcome data for minimal shapes are lacking.
4. **Procedural errors:** transportation underlies ledges, zips and perforations (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); instrument fracture; dentinal cracks; extrusion of debris and irrigant.
5. **Calcified and severely curved canals:** need magnification, CBCT, precurved small files, staged preparation dividing the canal in two, and adapting the tool to the canal rather than the canal to the tool (Peters and Arias, 2025).
6. **Evidence gap:** most data on new instruments and devices are in vitro; manufacturer claims run ahead of independent clinical trials; outcomes have not improved in step with technology (Peters and Arias, 2025).
7. **Cost and learning curve:** single-use heat-treated files, motors, microscopes, CBCT and activation devices.

## 16. Future directions and conclusion

### Explanation

Peters and Arias close: "only long-term observation will really disclose what matters" (Peters and Arias, 2025); meanwhile adherence to sound practice and avoiding gross errors "goes a long way". Their summary of the field is that the twin goals of healing and retention should guide the choice of every instrument and step (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Future directions:** CBCT-guided planned preparation; instruments designed to individual canal anatomy; smarter motors that sense load; minimally invasive shaping combined with more efficient activated irrigation; bioactive hydraulic sealers allowing single-cone obturation of conservative shapes; independent long-term clinical trials and patient-centred outcomes such as tooth survival.

**Conclusion.** Cleaning and shaping has moved from Schilder's mechanical objectives towards a biological and conservative philosophy: shape enough to deliver irrigants and remove infection, but no more than needed, so that the tooth survives. Heat-treated NiTi, new kinematics, adaptive instruments, glide paths, patency and electronic length control have made preparation safer and more predictable. Many issues remain controversial, above all apical size, kinematics and the value of new devices, because outcome evidence lags behind innovation. The clinician should adapt the instruments to the canal, not the canal to the instruments (Peters and Arias, 2025), and judge every new concept by its effect on both healing and retention.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

1. **Claim:** NiTi instruments improve outcomes. **Textbooks:** ESE guideline recommends NiTi (Peters and Arias, 2025); outcome studies favouring NiTi reported by Siqueira et al, 2022. **Challenge:** overall healing rates have stayed similar despite new technology (Peters and Arias, 2025). **Conclusion:** [Likely] fewer errors and better process quality; [Likely] a modest outcome benefit.
2. **Claim:** heat-treated alloys prevent fracture. **Textbooks:** better fatigue resistance and centring (Jain, 2016; Schäfer, 2017). **Challenge:** much of the advantage disappears at body temperature (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). **Conclusion:** [Likely].
3. **Claim:** reciprocation is superior. **Challenge:** not shown superior; conflicting data on cracks and extrusion (Jain, 2016; Schäfer, 2017). **Conclusion:** [Guessing].
4. **Claim:** minimally invasive shaping improves tooth survival. **Textbooks:** pericervical dentine and finite element data (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Peters and Arias, 2025). **Challenge:** no long-term clinical trials cited. **Conclusion:** [Likely] biomechanically; [Guessing] clinically.
5. **Claim:** larger apical preparation improves healing. **Challenge:** "no specific apical size leads to better clinical outcomes" (Peters and Arias, 2025). **Conclusion:** [Guessing]; size should be anatomy- and irrigation-driven.
6. **Claim:** patency improves outcome. **Textbooks:** prognostic factor in a prospective study (Peters and Arias, 2025). **Conclusion:** [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

- Twin goals: healing and retention (Peters and Arias, 2025).
- Pericervical dentine: 4 mm above and below the crestal bone.
- Outcomes about 83% (initial) and 80% (retreatment) despite new technology.
- M-wire 2007; R-phase 2008; CM wire Af about 55°C; 52.1% Ni.
- Reciprocation: stainless steel since 1958; Yared 2007/2008; counterclockwise angle about 5× clockwise; not proven superior.
- Glide path: straight #10 (Peters and Arias) or #15 (Cohen's) K-file to length; rotary glide path only after #10 reaches length.
- Infected canals: best healing with working length 0-0.5 mm from the apex.
- Minimum apical size for a 30-gauge needle: #25/.06 (or #25/.08, #30/.06).
- ESE S3 (2023): engine-driven NiTi; NaOCl + EDTA; single visit without Ca(OH)2 in asymptomatic mature teeth.
- "Adapt the tools to the canal, not the canal to the tool."

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW

1. **NiTi phase transformation:** austenite (cubic) ⇄ martensite under stress and temperature, with R-phase between, labelled stress-induced and thermal transformation.
2. **Pericervical dentine:** root outline with a band 4 mm above and below the crestal bone; contrast an overflared and a conservative shape.
3. **Reciprocation:** circle with a large counterclockwise cutting arc and a small clockwise release arc, showing net advance after several cycles.
4. **Oval canal cross-section:** round rotary preparation leaving buccal and lingual recesses untouched, versus two-path or adaptive preparation.
5. **Glide path test:** straight #10 K-file sliding to working length from 1-2 mm and from 5-6 mm off length.
6. **Controversy balance:** a see-saw with "disinfection (larger size)" against "dentine preservation (smaller size)".

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

- Listing brand names without explaining the concept each represents (alloy, design, kinematics).
- Presenting reciprocation, heat-treated alloys or GentleWave as proven to improve healing.
- Omitting the retention goal and pericervical dentine, which is the central recent concept.
- Writing a single "correct" apical size.
- Ignoring the controversies section; this question explicitly asks for controversies and challenges.
- Treating Gates-Glidden drills as current routine without noting that recent books advise against them.
- Forgetting the ESE S3 guideline, the newest evidence-based statement.
