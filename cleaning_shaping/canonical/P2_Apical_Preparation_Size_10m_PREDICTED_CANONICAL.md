## 1. Apical preparation size

### Explanation

Final apical size "remains a much-disputed entity" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; "an ongoing debate" exists (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; some argued for "#50 or greater" while "others found no difference" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. For larger: bacterial reduction increases with size in infected canals (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]; biofilm disruption and irrigant penetration (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Schäfer's floor of 35-40/.02 or 25/.04 (Schäfer, 2017) [SRC]; apical canals often correspond to "size 30-40, or even larger" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. For smaller: infected tissue may remain but dentine and anatomy are conserved (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; stainless steel transports curves beyond #20 (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], limit three sizes above IAF (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; enlarging oval canals round "would require overenlargement" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]; "larger, however, is not always better" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC], and a #40 seat in a moderately curved mesiobuccal canal will zip it (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Gauging underestimates (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Peters and Arias: no size predicts outcome; minimum #25/.06 for the needle; large sizes pack debris (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. Grossman's rejects the "three sizes beyond" rule (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** The apical size (master apical file) determines how much infected apical dentine is removed and how deep irrigants can reach, but also how much the canal is transported and the root weakened. Final apical size "remains a much-disputed entity" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**Evidence for larger sizes**
- Bacterial reduction and apical cleanliness increase with apical size in infected canals (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- More biofilm removed; deeper needle placement and irrigant exchange (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Natural apical diameters are often #30-40 or larger, and initial gauging underestimates them (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**Evidence for smaller sizes**
- Some studies found no difference in bacterial counts between small and large sizes (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Larger sizes transport curved canals (stainless steel beyond #20), create zips and ledges, weaken roots and increase extrusion (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- In oval canals, making the apex round would need overenlargement (Siqueira et al, 2022); large sizes may pack debris into isthmuses (Peters and Arias, 2025).

**Appraisal**
- No apical size has been shown to give better clinical outcomes (Peters and Arias, 2025); most data are in vitro or microbiological.
- The old rules (three sizes beyond the first binding file; white shavings) have no biological basis (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Taper can substitute for diameter: a variably tapered #30 file may reach 9% taper apically (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- NiTi allows larger sizes safely in curves.

**Factors deciding size in a given canal (Gopikrishna, 2021; Siqueira et al, 2022):** initial canal width (gauged with hand files after coronal flaring, or measured on CBCT 1-4 mm from the foramen), vital versus infected pulp, periapical lesion or resorption, degree and radius of curvature, root width and wall thickness, canal configuration (oval, C-shaped, isthmus), instrument flexibility and taper, and the obturation technique.

**Conclusion.** Neither "larger" nor "smaller" is correct as a rule. The apical size should be the largest compatible with root anatomy, curvature and wall thickness, and at least large enough to admit a 30-gauge needle (#25/.06 or similar), judged by gauging and, where available, CBCT measurement (Peters and Arias, 2025; Siqueira et al, 2022).
