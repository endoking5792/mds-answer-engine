## 1. Introduction: the problem the question poses

### Explanation

Peters, Peters and Basrani state the overall aim: "Endodontic therapy is directed toward one specific set of aims: to cure or prevent periradicular periodontitis" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. They agree that "a major biologic aim of root canal therapy is to address apical periodontitis by disinfection and subsequent sealing", but add that "considerable disagreement exists over how this goal should be achieved" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. That disagreement is the subject of the question: whether the shape of the canal (mechanical objectives) or the removal of infection (biological objectives) should govern preparation, and what happens where they conflict.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** The purpose of root canal treatment is to cure or prevent apical periodontitis (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Schilder in 1974 described root canal preparation in terms of two sets of objectives: mechanical objectives, which define the shape of the prepared canal, and biological objectives, which define what preparation must achieve and avoid in relation to the tissues (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019). The two are usually presented as equal partners. A critical evaluation shows that they are not: the mechanical objectives were designed largely to make obturation possible, while modern evidence places biological control of infection first and adds a third objective, the preservation of tooth structure for long-term retention (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Peters and Arias, 2025). Although everyone agrees on the aim, there is "considerable disagreement" on how to achieve it (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

## 2. The objectives as classically stated

### Explanation

Schilder's list, as given by Hülsmann and Schäfer, has five mechanical and five biological objectives, of which "some of these objectives are still valid more than 40 years later" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. The textbooks differ in the wording, as Q01 shows: Grossman's has the "quality of flow" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and, among biological objectives, "confinement of instrumentation to the roots themselves" and "creation of sufficient space for optimal obturation" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's includes space "for intracanal medicaments" and "completion of preparation in one appointment" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri, "removal of substrate harboring microorganisms" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Jain reduces Schilder's shaping to "four design objectives" (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Jain also separates biological goals ("to eliminate microorganisms from the root canal system", remove pulp tissue that supports growth, avoid forcing debris out) from technical goals, which "are directed toward shaping the canal so as to achieve the biological objectives and to facilitate placement of a root filling" (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Cohen's similarly lists mechanical, biologic and technical objectives (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Schilder's objectives (1974) (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019)**

| Mechanical objectives | Biological objectives |
|---|---|
| 1. Continuously tapering funnel from apex to access | 1. Confine instrumentation to the root canal |
| 2. Cross-section narrowest apically | 2. Do not force necrotic debris beyond the foramen |
| 3. Preparation flows with the original canal | 3. Remove all tissue and debris |
| 4. Foramen kept in its original position | 4. Create space for intracanal medicaments |
| 5. Foramen kept as small as practical | 5. Complete preparation in one appointment |

Grossman's adds the "quality of flow" for plasticised gutta-percha and lists space for obturation as a biological aim (Gopikrishna, 2021); Sikri lists removal of the substrate harbouring microorganisms (Sikri, 2019).

**A modern framing (Jain, 2016; Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021)**
- *Biological goals:* eliminate microorganisms, remove the pulp tissue that supports their growth, avoid forcing debris beyond the foramen.
- *Technical (mechanical) goals:* shape the canal so as to achieve the biological goals and to allow a root filling.
- *Structural goal:* retain the integrity of radicular dentine (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

## 3. Critical evaluation of the mechanical objectives

### Explanation

**Taper and flow.** Peters, Peters and Basrani: Schilder's uniform continuous taper "was aimed at facilitating obturation rather than targeting antimicrobial efficacy" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and Schäfer show that the shape needed depends on the obturation method: "a round diameter of the prepared root canal is essential as long as single-cone obturation techniques" are used, but with compaction techniques "the primary objectives should be to instrument and prepare the canal circumferentially" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]. They argue that "preparation should dictate the obturation technique rather than the obturation technique dictating a certain preparation technique" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC]; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, that shaping "should be tooth dependent and not depend on the obturation technique" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

**Incorporating the original canal.** The ideal mechanical objective is "complete and centered incorporation of the original canals into the prepared shape", but "this goal is unlikely to be met with current techniques" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC].

**Keeping the foramen in place.** Some transportation is unavoidable; "about 100 to 150 μm may be clinically acceptable" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Preparation errors "may not directly affect the probability of a favorable outcome", but they "leave parts of the root canal system inaccessible for disinfection and are undesirable for that reason alone" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. This is the key point of the critical evaluation: a mechanical objective matters because of its biological consequence.

**Keeping the foramen small.** This conflicts with the case for larger apical preparation in infected canals (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], and final apical size "remains a much-disputed entity" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; Peters and Arias conclude "no specific apical size leads to better clinical outcomes" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Critical evaluation of the mechanical objectives**

1. **Continuous taper and "flow".** Designed to ease obturation, not to improve disinfection (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Modern view: the shape should be dictated by the tooth and by irrigant delivery, and the obturation method should follow the preparation, not the reverse (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). A taper still matters biologically because it lets the irrigation needle reach the apical third.
2. **Narrowest apically, foramen as small as practical.** Sound for controlling the filling, but in infected canals it conflicts with the biological case for larger apical sizes (Siqueira et al, 2022). No apical size has been shown to give better outcomes (Peters and Arias, 2025), so the "small foramen" rule is a compromise rather than a principle.
3. **Preparation follows the original canal; foramen not moved.** Valid, but valid for a biological reason: transportation, ledges and zips leave parts of the canal that cannot be disinfected (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Small transportation (100-150 μm) is clinically acceptable.
4. **Complete incorporation of the original canal.** The ideal mechanical objective, but not achievable with current instruments in oval canals and isthmuses (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
5. **Omission in Schilder's list:** preservation of radicular dentine. More dentine removed means a weaker root (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021), and vertical fracture is now a leading cause of tooth loss (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

**Verdict:** the mechanical objectives remain useful as technical rules, but each is justified only by what it does for disinfection, filling or tooth survival.


**Example.** A narrow, severely curved mesiobuccal canal shaped to a perfect Schilder funnel with a large taper may meet every mechanical objective yet have thin walls at the danger zone and an untouched isthmus. A conservative shape of #25/.06 that admits a 30-gauge needle, combined with activated irrigation, may meet fewer of Schilder's criteria but serve both disinfection and fracture resistance better.

## 4. Critical evaluation of the biological objectives

### Explanation

**Remove all tissue and debris.** Not achievable: "chemomechanical procedures alone are unable to achieve thorough cleaning" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], and the practical aim is "to significantly reduce the irritants, not totally eliminate them" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues frame the realistic biological target: infection "eradicated or reduced to levels compatible with periradicular healing" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

**Confinement and no extrusion.** Remains valid, but must be balanced against cleaning to the terminus in infected canals (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC] and against patency, which Peters and Arias now associate with better outcomes (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

**Space for medicaments and one-appointment completion.** These two items look contradictory. Cohen's reports limited effectiveness of Ca(OH)2 (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; the ESE guideline recommends a single visit "without the use of inter-appointment calcium hydroxide" in asymptomatic mature teeth (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. Schilder's one-appointment objective, much criticised in its time, is closer to current guidance than the medicament objective is.

**Missing from the list.** Neutralisation of "microbial toxins and byproducts" (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019) [SRC], and the distinction between vital and infected cases: in vital pulps "asepsis is the decisive factor" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Critical evaluation of the biological objectives**

1. **Remove all tissue and debris.** Unattainable; no technique fully cleans isthmuses, fins, lateral canals and tubules (Siqueira et al, 2022). The modern objective is to reduce infection to a level compatible with healing (Siqueira et al, 2022), a significant reduction rather than elimination (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
2. **Confine instruments; do not extrude debris.** Still valid, but must be balanced against cleaning to the canal terminus in infected teeth (Siqueira et al, 2022) and against apical patency, now associated with better healing (Peters and Arias, 2025).
3. **Space for intracanal medicaments.** Weakened by evidence that Ca(OH)2 has limited antibacterial effectiveness (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
4. **Completion in one appointment.** Supported by the ESE guideline for asymptomatic mature teeth (Peters and Arias, 2025).
5. **Missing objectives:** neutralisation of toxins (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2019); different aims for vital teeth (asepsis) and infected teeth (disinfection) (Siqueira et al, 2022).

**Verdict:** the biological objectives remain the true goals of treatment but need restating in realistic and diagnosis-specific terms.

## 5. Interdependence and conflict between the two sets of objectives

### Explanation

The objectives are interdependent. "Preparation shape and antimicrobial efficacy are intimately related" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; persisting bacteria in less accessible areas "can be targeted only after root canal preparation" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; "shaping and cleaning" is the more exact order (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]; shaping and irrigation must act "in synergy" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Siqueira and colleagues add that irrigation has a mechanical effect irrespective of the irrigant (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], and that an antimicrobial irrigant "has been shown to be essential" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]: the mechanical and the chemical arms are inseparable.

The balance between the two has shifted historically. After the Washington study, failures were attributed to obturation, since "more than 60% of teeth with posttreatment disease were associated with inadequate root canal fillings"; Siqueira and colleagues argue that obturation "merely reflects the aptness of chemomechanical procedures" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], and Peters, Arias and Shabahang call the older view "fallacious" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Where they conflict, there is no settled answer, and "there is no consensus or clinical evidence on which technique or instrument design or type is clinically superior" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Interdependence**
- Shaping creates the space through which irrigants act; the shape and the antimicrobial effect are intimately related (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Bacteria in less accessible areas can be reached only after preparation, so "shaping and cleaning" is the more accurate order.
- Mechanical action and irrigation must work in synergy (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021): flushing alone removes bacteria regardless of the irrigant, and an antimicrobial irrigant is essential for infected canals (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Therefore the mechanical objectives are best seen as the means, and the biological objectives as the ends (Jain, 2016).

**Points of conflict**

| Issue | Mechanical / structural argument | Biological argument |
|---|---|---|
| Apical size | Small size: less transportation, less weakening, controlled filling | Larger size: more infected dentine removed, better irrigant penetration (Siqueira et al, 2022) |
| Apical length | Stay short to protect the constriction and avoid extrusion | Clean to the terminus in infected canals; each millimetre short of the terminus reduces the odds of success by 12% (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) |
| Patency | Risk of extrusion and widening the foramen | Keeps the apex clear; associated with better healing (Peters and Arias, 2025) |
| Coronal taper | Wide taper for spreaders and pluggers | Only enough taper for the needle; preserve pericervical dentine |
| Oval canals | Round preparation by rotary files | Recesses must be touched by files or irrigant |


**How the balance has shifted.** In the obturation-centred era, inspired by the Washington study in which over 60% of failures had inadequate fillings, preparation was judged by whether the canal could be filled densely, and mechanical objectives dominated. It is now accepted that a poor filling usually reflects poor cleaning and shaping (Siqueira et al, 2022; Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). Preparation is therefore judged first by its biological result, the reduction of infection, and second by whether it allows a seal. A third test, whether enough dentine remains for the tooth to survive, has been added only in the last decade. This sequence of priorities is the core of the modern answer to the question.

## 6. Biological concepts of debridement and the means to eliminate infection

### Explanation

Peters, Arias and Shabahang explain why diagnosis matters: success with a vital pulp "is higher than that of a tooth diagnosed with necrotic pulp and periradicular pathosis" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC], because of persistent microorganisms. Siqueira and colleagues call the apical canal "critical territory" (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC] and recommend cleaning infected canals to the terminus (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC], but vital teeth only to 1-2 mm short (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Even when bacteria are not eliminated, preparation may "disturb ... the environment enough to promote an imbalance in the bacterial community" that helps host defences (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC]. Obturation can "entomb any remaining organisms", although "some bacterial species have been shown to survive entombment" (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]; and outcome is "closely related to placement of an adequate coronal restoration" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Means of eliminating infection are given in full in Q15; irrigant exchange extends only 1-1.5 mm beyond the needle (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC], and about 70% more debris remains without irrigation (Siqueira et al, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Biological concepts of debridement**
1. **Microbial aetiology:** apical periodontitis is caused by microorganisms living as biofilm in the canal, its ramifications and dentinal tubules; the apical canal is "critical territory" for bacteria, host and clinician (Siqueira et al, 2022).
2. **Diagnosis decides the strategy:** in vital pulps the radicular pulp is inflamed but not infected, so asepsis is decisive and working length 1-2 mm short is acceptable; in necrotic and retreatment cases the canal is infected and must be cleaned to the terminus (Siqueira et al, 2022). Success is higher in vital than in necrotic teeth (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).
3. **Reduction, not sterilisation:** the aim is to reduce infection to levels compatible with healing (Siqueira et al, 2022); even incomplete reduction may disturb the microbial community enough for host defences to act.
4. **Entombment and seal:** obturation entombs survivors, though some survive (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021); the coronal restoration is closely related to outcome (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).

**Means to achieve elimination of infection**
- Asepsis: rubber dam, disinfected field, sterile instruments.
- Mechanical: access, coronal pre-enlargement, glide path, patency, NiTi or balanced force preparation to an adequate apical size and taper.
- Chemical: copious NaOCl (flushing reduces bacteria by itself; about 70% more debris remains without irrigant), EDTA for the smear layer (Siqueira et al, 2022).
- Physical: activation (sonic, ultrasonic, negative pressure, laser); irrigant exchange reaches only 1-1.5 mm beyond the needle (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Medicaments: Ca(OH)2 when treatment cannot be completed in one visit.
- Sealing: three-dimensional obturation and a timely coronal restoration.
- Surgery or extraction when orthograde treatment cannot eliminate infection.

## 7. What outcome evidence says, and a revised set of objectives

### Explanation

The Ng prospective study, as summarised by Peters and Arias, supports biological and length-related steps rather than shape alone: "achieving patency and avoiding overfills and other significant procedural errors, appeared to be associated with higher odds for success" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; patency at the terminus is "a prognostic factor" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; and "a restoration of good quality significantly increases the odds for success but is even more decisive" for retention (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]. Healing outcomes "remain substantially similar" despite technology (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC]; preparation and disinfection are still "the single most important factor" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

The addition of retention as a goal comes from Peters and Arias's twin aims (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC], Cohen's objective to "retain the integrity of radicular structures" (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC] and its definition of a well-shaped canal by absence of errors, disinfection and retained tooth structure (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) [SRC]. Peters, Arias and Shabahang call for "a more patient-centered outcome" that considers fracture susceptibility (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Evidence from outcome studies (as reported by Peters and Arias, 2025)**
- Patency and avoiding overfills and gross procedural errors are associated with higher success; patency at the terminus is a prognostic factor for healing.
- A good coronal restoration increases success and is even more decisive for tooth retention.
- Despite new technology, healing rates have changed little; preparation and disinfection remain the most important factors.

These findings favour the biological objectives (reach and clean the whole canal, avoid extrusion) over geometric ones, and they add a structural objective.

**A revised statement of objectives**
1. *Biological:* reduce intracanal infection and its products to a level compatible with healing, cleaning infected canals to the terminus with patency; maintain asepsis in vital cases; avoid extrusion.
2. *Technical (mechanical):* create a shape that admits irrigants and medicaments to the apical third, follows the original canal without transportation, and allows a dense filling to working length.
3. *Structural:* preserve pericervical and radicular dentine so the tooth can be restored and retained (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021; Peters and Arias, 2025).

## 8. Conclusion

### Explanation

Peters and Arias's practical rule sums up the evaluation: "consider adapting the tools to the anatomy of the specific canal system, rather than adapting the canal to the tool" (Peters and Arias, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** Schilder's division into mechanical and biological objectives remains a useful teaching framework, but it is not a partnership of equals. The mechanical objectives were largely designed to make obturation possible; they are justified today only to the extent that they allow disinfection, avoid errors that leave parts of the canal uncleaned, and preserve tooth structure. The biological objectives are the true goals, but the aim of removing all tissue must be restated as reducing infection to levels compatible with healing, with strategies tailored to vital and infected cases. To both should be added a structural objective, retention of radicular dentine, because vertical fracture and other non-endodontic causes, rather than persistent infection, are now the main reasons for loss (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021). Clinicians should adapt the instruments to the canal rather than the canal to the instruments (Peters and Arias, 2025).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

- Listing Schilder's objectives without evaluating them; the question asks for critical evaluation.
- Treating mechanical and biological objectives as separate rather than interdependent.
- Claiming that the canal can be sterilised or all tissue removed.
- Ignoring diagnosis: the biological aims differ for vital and infected teeth.
- Forgetting preservation of tooth structure and the coronal restoration.
- Not reaching a conclusion; a "critically evaluate" essay needs a defended position.
