## STUDY MODE

Dentin presents several intrinsic and technique-related challenges not shared by enamel bonding:

1. **Substrate heterogeneity, now quantified.** Superficial dentin has fewer, narrower tubules than deep dentin. Real figures (Sturdevant's, PDF p.426, citing Garberoglio & Brannström 1976): tubule density rises from **~20,000/mm² near the DEJ to ~45,000/mm² near the pulp**, with tubule diameter widening from **0.63µm to 2.37µm** over the same depth range. Pulpal fluid pressure (25-30 mmHg / 34-40 cmH₂O, Sturdevant's p.425) drives a real outward fluid flow — directly demonstrated in vivo (Sasazaki & Okuda 1996, cited in Nakabayashi & Pashley, PDF p.33: slow-setting impression material on vital dentin left bubble-like casts matching tubule number/diameter exactly, from fluid pushed outward during the setting window; a fast-setting material showed none). [SRC]
2. **Moisture control — the "moisture compromise," now with a real mechanistic account.** Nakabayashi & Pashley's monograph lays out at least three competing explanatory models for why desiccated collagen collapses (PDF p.63-64, 74): a passive "floating network" theory (fibrils simply come together as water evaporates, like "a heap of cooked spaghetti," fully reversible by rewetting); an active-shrinkage theory (individual fibrils lose inter-microfibrillar water and shorten, pulling the network down); and a surface-tension/capillarity theory (as an air-water interface forms between adjacent fibrils, surface tension actively pulls them together). On rewetting with 30% HEMA, collapsed dentin reexpanded 50% within 1 minute and ~100% within 10 minutes (Igarashi & Nakabayashi, 1996). [SRC]
3. **Smear layer variability, now quantified.** Thickness genuinely varies by cutting instrument: a medium-grit diamond bur leaves ~5µm (more compact plug), a tungsten-carbide bur leaves ~2µm (Summitt's, PDF p.476). In the root canal specifically, the endodontic smear layer is 0.5-2.0µm thick with smear plugs extending **up to 40µm into tubules** (Summitt's, PDF p.479-486). [SRC]
4. **Incomplete resin infiltration / the hybrid-layer "defective zone"** (Nakabayashi & Pashley's own term, not just generic "incomplete infiltration" — PDF p.86-88). Resin monomers must diffuse to the full depth of acid demineralization; mismatch leaves this named defective zone exposed to subsequent breakdown.
5. **Degradation over time — a DUAL enzymatic pathway, not just MMPs.** Summitt's gives the most detailed mechanistic account found in this audit (PDF p.492-493): alongside MMPs, **cysteine cathepsins** are a second collagenolytic pathway, activated in mildly acidic environments, and cathepsin activation can itself further activate matrix-bound MMPs. Real inhibitor concentrations: **0.2-2% aqueous chlorhexidine** inactivates exposed MMPs (Sturdevant's p.301-302; Summitt's p.448, 492-493 — TEM-confirmed reduced nanoleakage after 2-year storage, Breschi et al.); an alternative **5% glutaraldehyde/35% HEMA** solution crosslinks collagen and inactivates both MMPs and cathepsins (Sturdevant's p.301-302). [SRC]
6. **Polymerization shrinkage stress — quantified via the C-factor.** The configuration factor (C-factor = bonded : unbonded surface-area ratio) governs whether shrinkage stress disrupts the bond (Van Noort's, PDF p.138); composite shrinkage generates **~15 MPa** of stress during cure (Philips', PDF p.277) — directly comparable to, and capable of exceeding, early bond strength. **In a root canal specifically, C-factor may exceed 200** (vs. 1-5 for coronal restorations) — called "the worst scenario in achieving gap-free interfaces" (Bouillaguet et al. 2003/Breschi et al. 2009, cited in Perdigão's, p.146-147). [SRC]
7. **Postoperative sensitivity** — a real clinical consequence of several of the above factors.
8. **Technique sensitivity, with a real testing-methodology confirmation.** Multi-step systems demand precise timing; simplified systems reduce steps but show lower/more variable bond durability — directly confirmed by a specific dumbbell-specimen experiment (Nakabayashi & Pashley, PDF p.77, 86-88): bond strength fell from 24→14→12 MPa as etch time increased from 10→30→60s, detectable within just 24 hours using a sensitive specimen geometry, whereas conventional specimen geometries needed 3+ months of storage to reveal the same underlying defect — meaning some "no significant difference at 24 hours" literature may reflect an insensitive test, not true equivalence. [SRC]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Deep dentin: tubule density 45,000/mm² (2.37µm diameter) vs 20,000/mm² (0.63µm) near the DEJ — real quantified substrate heterogeneity.
- Degradation is a DUAL pathway: MMPs AND cathepsins (not MMPs alone) — real inhibitor concentrations are 0.2-2% chlorhexidine or 5% glutaraldehyde/35% HEMA.
- C-factor (bonded:unbonded surface ratio) governs shrinkage-stress risk — can exceed 200 in a root canal vs 1-5 coronally.
- Smear layer thickness is instrument-dependent (diamond ~5µm vs carbide ~2µm) — not a single fixed number.
- A dumbbell-specimen study showed bond-strength decline (24→12 MPa) detectable at 24h, while conventional test geometries needed 3+ months — some "no difference" literature may just be using an insensitive test.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
The "MMP-mediated degradation" framing taught generically in most textbooks is itself incomplete: Summitt's independently names **cysteine cathepsins** as a second, cooperating collagenolytic pathway (activated in acidic environments, capable of further activating matrix-bound MMPs) — a mechanistic nuance absent from simpler single-pathway accounts. **What this supports:** inhibitor strategies should, in principle, target both pathways, which is consistent with chlorhexidine's broad-spectrum protease-inhibiting action. **What it does not support:** treating MMP inhibition alone as a complete durability solution — cathepsin activity is a documented, separate contributor.
