## STUDY MODE

### Dentine bonding — core concept
Dentin bonding (as distinct from enamel bonding, which is purely micromechanical) requires engaging dentin's wet, tubular, collagen-containing structure. The breakthrough concept — **Nakabayashi's hybrid layer (1982)**: acid demineralization exposes a collagen fibril meshwork, which hydrophilic resin monomer then infiltrates and reinforces upon polymerization — had, by the mid-to-late 1990s, become the accepted mechanistic basis for essentially all modern dentin-bonding systems, replacing earlier (1st/2nd generation, 1970s) weak ionic/chelation-based mechanisms that gave unreliable, low bond strengths. ### Recent development (as of the mid/late-1990s sitting context)
1. **Multi-step etch-and-rinse systems (3rd/4th generation)** — separate phosphoric-acid etch, primer, and adhesive-resin steps, giving dramatically improved, more reliable bond strengths versus earlier generations, by reliably achieving hybrid-layer formation.
2. **The "wet bonding" concept** — a genuinely significant conceptual shift at this time: moving away from the older "etch and dry thoroughly" teaching (appropriate for enamel) toward recognizing dentin specifically needs to remain moist to preserve the demineralized collagen meshwork's porosity for resin infiltration.
3. **Simplification beginning** — the earliest one-bottle (5th-generation) systems, combining the formerly separate primer and adhesive-resin steps into a single bottle applied after a still-separate etch step, were a genuinely new development at this sitting's time, reducing clinical steps while (at this early stage) aiming to maintain comparable bond quality to the multi-step systems.
4. **Dentin substrate research** — growing recognition that dentin depth (superficial vs deep), sclerosis, and smear layer variability measurably affect bond quality — informing more nuanced clinical technique recommendations than a one-size-fits-all approach.
