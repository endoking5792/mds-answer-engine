## STUDY MODE

### S1. How to organise the uses [orientation]
Use follows type: Type I luts, Type II.1 restores aesthetically, Type II.2 restores where strength matters more than colour, Type III lines and bases; fissure sealing, orthodontic cementation and core build-up are extra uses in Sikri's wider listing (Mount, 1994; Sikri, 2020).

### S2. Uses by type [clinical decision-making]
- **Type I luting.** Crowns, bridges, inlays, orthodontic appliances. Low powder:liquid ratio (about 1.5:1), fast set, thin film, and low solubility when correctly proportioned (too little powder raises solubility) make it suitable; its adhesion improves marginal seal but does not add retention, so retention must come from preparation design (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). On vital teeth avoid conditioning with 10% polyacrylic acid because of hydraulic pressure; use a mineralising solution or tannic acid (Mount, 1994).
- **Type II.1 restorative aesthetic.** Class III, Class V, root caries, erosion lesions, and primary teeth; properties are adequate if the cement is supported and not overloaded (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- **Type II.2 reinforced restorative.** Fast set and higher strength where colour match is unimportant: posterior sites, tunnel restorations, repairs of margins, and limited cores (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Type III lining and base.** Lining (1.5:1) is a thin layer for a deficiency or thermal protection; a base (3:1 or more, 3.5:1 or more in Mount et al, 2016) replaces dentine; GIC gives the most effective long-term dentine seal (Mount, 1994). Under amalgam, minimal cavities need no lining; for deeper ones a small amount of low powder:liquid lining is used (Mount, 1994).
- **Other products by Sikri's classification.** By application: Type IV fissure sealant, Type V orthodontic cement, Type VI core build-up; Sikri's classification by manufacturer's characteristics instead lists Type IV as admixtures (Ketac Silver, miracle mix) (Sikri, 2020).

### S3. What a bi-layered restoration is [classification]
A bi-layered (sandwich, laminate) restoration uses GIC as the dentine replacement and composite as the enamel replacement, so each material is used where its properties suit (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount et al, 2016). It is **open** if the GIC forms part of the axial wall and is exposed to the mouth, and **closed** if composite covers the GIC completely (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### S4. Why do it [rationale]
Composite shrinks 1-5% by volume and the dentine bond is hard to keep over time; GIC bonds to dentine by ion exchange and bonds to composite, giving a near-monolithic reconstruction (Mount, 1994). Practical gains: less composite, fewer increments, less total shrinkage, time saved (Mount et al, 2016). As long as there is doubt about the efficiency and longevity of bonding composite to dentine, lamination is the preferred system in any cavity with at least one margin on dentine (Mount et al, 2016). A GIC base in larger restorations gives a much better chance of keeping oral microflora from reaching dentine and so reduces pulpal irritation (Mount et al, 2016).

### S5. Technique [treatment]
Mount's principles: use the strongest GIC in quantity as the dentine substitute, with no sub-base of calcium hydroxide; condition for 10 seconds (10-15 seconds in Mount, 1994); let it set fully; cut back about 2 mm to expose all enamel margins; leave GIC exposed at a subgingival margin without enamel; build the contact in composite; always use a radiopaque GIC (Mount et al, 2016). With auto-cure GIC, once it is set, etch cement and enamel together for 15 seconds only, apply thin low-viscosity enamel resin, then incremental composite (Mount et al, 2016); with resin-modified GIC no etching of cement is needed (Mount et al, 2016). The cement must cover all tubules, be at least 1 mm thick, and use the highest powder:liquid ratio (Mount, 1994). Timing of the etch differs between books: Mount starts the composite stage 6 minutes from the start of mix for Type II.2 or III, and after a 20-minute wait under a resin seal for auto-cure Type II.1 (Mount, 1994); Sikri advises about 20 minutes and 15-20 seconds of etching (Sikri, 2020 Ch.18); the conventional GIC liner or base etch should not exceed 20 seconds (Torres, 2020); Bonsor says etching within 5 minutes removes the matrix within 15 seconds and works properly only after 24 hours (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); McCabe says freshly placed lining GIC needs only washing and is etched only once matured (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### S6. Limits and cautions
Early sandwich studies showed more bulk fractures, though those used Black-style cavities and early bases (Mount et al, 2016). Pickard says that where a resin-modified GIC base is completely covered by composite (a closed restoration), there is clinical evidence of more long-term failure at the interfaces (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). Hilton et al (2013) state that conventional glass ionomers are less successful than resin-modified ones in the open sandwich, and that bases reduce the bulk of restorative material and raise fracture risk (Hilton et al, 2013). Bonsor and Pearson (2013) state that early etching harms the immature matrix and that composite shrinkage can pull glass particles from etched cement, whereas Mount et al (2016) etch the auto-cure cement for 15 seconds as soon as it is set. Lining-grade (about 1.5:1) cement is not designed to be exposed: Mount et al (2016) say it must not be left exposed to the oral environment, McCabe and Walls (2008) say purpose-designed lining materials should not be brought to the surface even at a dentine interface, whereas Mount (1994) and Mount et al (2016) leave a high powder:liquid base exposed at a subgingival margin without enamel. Sikri (2020) says Type III lining cement should not be relied on as a base for composite; Mount (1994) notes that Type III lining cements were developed with the technique in mind, used at the highest powder:liquid ratio. Type II.2 cement resists water uptake early but stays susceptible to dehydration for 2 weeks, and auto-cure Type II.1 needs immediate protection for at least 24 hours (Mount, 1994). McCabe and Walls (2008) call restorative GIC radiolucent, so a radiopaque composite over cut-back GIC can mimic recurrent decay; Garg and Garg (2011) say conventional GIC is radiolucent while metal-modified and resin-modified types are radiopaque; Mount et al (2016) say most restorative GICs are more radiopaque than dentine, though some auto-cure Type II.1 cements are entirely radiolucent.

Further author differences (second pass). Cores: Mount et al (2016) and Sikri (2020) list limited core use; Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says reinforced glass ionomers should not be used for core build-ups and may serve as bases and block-out (Ritter et al, 2019); Goldstein 3rd ed lists conventional glass ionomer only for blockout of undercuts and resin-modified glass ionomer for a partial core with adequate tooth structure (Goldstein et al, 2018); Perdigao (2016) reports all glass ionomer core crowns failed by 20,000 cycles against 80% of composite cores at 50,000 (Badr et al, in Perdigao, 2016). Tunnels: Mount (1994) teaches them; Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed does not support the technique and calls glass ionomer tunnel restorations inadequate as definitive restorations (Ritter et al, 2019); Goldstein 3rd ed calls tunnel preparations ideal for composite or glass ionomer (Goldstein et al, 2018). Sealants: Mount et al (2016) use GIC sealants; Kidd and Fejerskov 4th ed find high-viscosity GIC and resin sealants equally effective in trials, with GIC preferred in erupting teeth (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed calls GIC sealant efficacy questionable (Ritter et al, 2019). Calcium hydroxide: Mount et al (2016) use no calcium hydroxide sub-base; Garg and Garg (2011) and Singh 2nd ed (2020) place a calcium hydroxide liner where remaining dentine is very thin, and Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed places calcium hydroxide 0.5-1 mm then an RMGI base when remaining dentine is under 0.5 mm (Ritter et al, 2019). Etching a cured base: Goldstein 3rd ed says etching glass ionomer is not necessary but some clinicians use a 5 s final etch (Goldstein et al, 2018). Varnish must not be placed beneath glass ionomer (Garg and Garg, 2011; Singh, 2020). Garg and Garg (2011) list mouth breathers among contraindications. Cervical seal before non-vital bleaching: Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed gives a coronal sealing cement step (Ritter et al, 2019); Goldstein 3rd ed covers the root filling with 2 mm of cement including glass ionomer (Goldstein et al, 2018). Endodontic barrier: a 1 to 2 mm glass ionomer intracoronal barrier reduced coronal microleakage in laboratory studies (Barsness and Roach, in Perdigao, 2016). Cervical lesions: glass ionomer-based materials had a 2% annual failure rate in a 2014 review quoted by Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement is classified by Wilson and McLean into Type I (luting), Type II (restorative: II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced) and Type III (lining and base) (Mount, 1994). Each type has uses that follow from its powder:liquid ratio and strength.

### 2. Uses of each type
| Type | Powder:liquid | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| I luting | about 1.5:1 | crowns, bridges, inlays, onlays, posts, orthodontic bands and brackets (Mount, 1994; Sikri, 2020) |
| II.1 aesthetic restorative | 2.8:1 to 6.8:1 (Mount, 1994); 3.5:1 or more (Mount et al, 2016) | Class III and V, erosion, abrasion and abfraction lesions, root caries, primary teeth (Mount, 1994; Sikri, 2020) |
| II.2 reinforced restorative | 3:1 to 4:1 | posterior sites, tunnel restorations, limited cores, margin repairs (Mount, 1994) |
| III lining and base | lining 1.5:1; base 3:1 or more (Mount, 1994), 3.5:1 or more (Mount et al, 2016) | thermal protection, dentine substitute, dentine seal, undercut block-out (Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013) |
| IV (Sikri) | not stated | fissure sealant (Sikri, 2020) |
| V (Sikri) | not stated | orthodontic cement (Sikri, 2020) |
| VI (Sikri) | not stated | core build-up (Sikri, 2020); not for cores in Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed (Ritter et al, 2019) |


### 3. Advantages that justify these uses
Chemical adhesion, fluoride release, thermal expansion close to dentine and biocompatibility (Sikri, 2020); fast set with early water resistance in Type I and II.2 (Mount, 1994).

### 4. Special uses
- Transitional restoration and ART, with maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013; Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- Fissure sealing: GIC sealants bond to enamel by ion exchange; a weak low-powder mix is contraindicated because of wear, and even when the sealant is lost, fluoride uptake into enamel reduces caries activity (Mount et al, 2016); Kidd and Fejerskov (2016) find GIC and resin sealants equally effective.
- Cervical and root lesions (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Endodontic: repair of root resorption and perforation, retrograde filling (Sikri, 2020); resin-modified GIC is contraindicated for repairing a root perforation and for cores when over 50% of tooth is lost (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); cervical seal before non-vital bleaching (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011).

### 4A. Choosing the type by site (Mount and Hume)
- **Minor occlusal lesions (Site 1, Size 1):** GIC is the preferred material, often with a fissure seal on the remaining fissures; in Size 2 the remaining fissures can be sealed with GIC or composite (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Occlusal lesions needing more strength:** GIC is the best initial restoration; laminate with composite or amalgam if the occlusal load is heavy; use the strongest, preferably auto-cure, high powder:liquid cement (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Proximal tunnel:** Type II.2 high-strength auto-cure radiopaque cement, preferably capsulated, placed in two increments under a lightly wedged Mylar strip (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Cervical lesions (Site 3, Size 2):** resin-modified or auto-cure GIC depending on light access, always conditioned with 10% polyacrylic acid (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Large cavities:** a high-strength GIC base on the axial wall gives a thermal barrier and bioactivity (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Bi-layered (sandwich) restoration: definition
GIC as dentine replacement plus composite as enamel replacement, also called laminate restoration (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Types of sandwich
- **Open:** GIC forms part of the axial or gingival wall and is exposed in the mouth, used in Class II and Class V extending below the CEJ (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Hilton et al, 2013); conventional GIC is less successful here than resin-modified GIC (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Closed:** GIC completely covered by composite (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### 7. Indications
- Cavities with a margin on dentine or cementum, especially subgingival Class II, while bonding of composite to dentine remains in doubt (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- Erosive and cervical lesions, bonding composite to enamel margins only (Sikri, 2020).
- Deep Class II with amalgam: GIC in the gingival half, amalgam above (Mount, 1994).

### 8. Rationale
Composite shrinks 1-5% and loses dentine bond over time; GIC bonds to dentine and to composite; the combination uses less composite and fewer increments (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

### 9. Technique
1. Isolate; remove caries; condition dentine 10 seconds (Mount et al, 2016) or 10-15 seconds (Mount, 1994).
2. Place the strongest radiopaque GIC under a matrix, covering all tubules, at least 1 mm thick (Mount, 1994).
3. Let it set; cut back about 2 mm to expose all enamel margins (Mount et al, 2016).
4. Etch enamel and auto-cure GIC for 15 seconds only; wash; dry lightly (Mount et al, 2016). Timing: once set (Mount et al, 2016); about 20 minutes (Sikri, 2020); after 24 hours (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
5. Apply thin enamel bonding resin and light cure; for resin-modified GIC etch enamel only (Mount et al, 2016).
6. Place composite in increments; build the contact in composite (Mount et al, 2016).
7. Finish and polish; leave GIC exposed only at a subgingival margin without enamel (Mount et al, 2016). No varnish beneath GIC (Garg and Garg, 2011).

### 10. Advantages and limits
- **Advantages:** maximum GIC exposure for fluoride release, less composite, interproximal GIC tolerated by tissue and resists plaque (Mount, 1994).
- **Limits:** a closed restoration with a resin-modified GIC base is more prone to long-term interface failure (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); early studies reported more bulk fractures (Mount et al, 2016); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed calls the sandwich advantages controversial (Ritter et al, 2019). Lining-grade (about 1.5:1) GIC: Sikri (2020) says not to rely on it as a base for composite; Mount et al (2016) say not to leave it exposed; McCabe and Walls (2008) say not to bring lining materials to the surface even at dentine; Mount (1994) leaves a high-ratio base exposed at a subgingival margin without enamel.
- **Evidence:** retention at 3 years was better with the sandwich technique in non-carious cervical lesions (4 studies; not extended to Class I/II) (Q1, claim 3) (de Paula et al, 2019; abstract only).

### 10A. Sandwich restoration in other settings
- **Amalgam lamination:** the strongest GIC is used as a base, cut back generously to give mechanical interlocks; amalgam protects the cement from occlusal load (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Cermet or amalgam lamination in Class II:** the cement is tamped in, left to set for 6 minutes, then cut back below the contact area (Mount, 1994).
- **Anterior and trauma cases:** a strong GIC replaces dentine, and enamel margins are bevelled for composite (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Aesthetics:** wait up to a week for GIC to mature and reach its final colour before deciding on a composite laminate for appearance (Mount et al, 2016).

### 11. Conclusion
Each GIC type has a defined clinical niche set by strength and ratio; the sandwich restoration is the way to combine GIC's bond and fluoride with composite's wear resistance, and works best as a planned technique in subgingival or dentine-margin cavities.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Types: I luting, II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced, III lining and base; P/L from 1.5:1 to over 3:1 (Mount, 1994).
- Sandwich = GIC as dentine, composite as enamel; open vs closed (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- Etch GIC and enamel 15 s only (auto-cure) once set (Mount et al, 2016; Bonsor says after 24 h); RMGIC needs no etching (Mount et al, 2016).
- Cover all tubules, at least 1 mm (Mount, 1994).
- Closed sandwich with RM-GIC base: more interface failure (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
