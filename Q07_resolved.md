## STUDY MODE

### S1. How to organise the uses [orientation]
Use follows type: Type I luts, Type II.1 restores aesthetically, Type II.2 restores where strength matters more than colour, Type III lines and bases; fissure sealing, orthodontic cementation and core build-up are extra uses in Sikri's wider listing (Mount, 1994; Sikri, 2020).

### S2. Uses by type [clinical decision-making]
- **Type I luting.** Crowns, bridges, inlays, orthodontic appliances. Low powder:liquid ratio (about 1.5:1), fast set, thin film, and low solubility make it suitable; its adhesion improves marginal seal but does not add retention, so retention must come from preparation design (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). On vital teeth avoid conditioning with 10% polyacrylic acid because of hydraulic pressure; use a mineralising solution or tannic acid (Mount, 1994).
- **Type II.1 restorative aesthetic.** Class III, Class V, root caries, erosion lesions, and primary teeth; properties are adequate if the cement is supported and not overloaded (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- **Type II.2 reinforced restorative.** Fast set and higher strength where colour match is unimportant: posterior sites, tunnel restorations, transitional restorations, repairs of margins, and limited cores (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Type III lining and base.** Lining (1.5:1) is a thin layer for a deficiency or thermal protection; a base (3:1 or more) replaces dentine; GIC gives the most effective long-term dentine seal (Mount, 1994). Under amalgam, minimal cavities need no lining; for deeper ones a small amount of low powder:liquid lining is used (Mount, 1994).
- **Other products by Sikri's classification.** Type IV fissure sealant, Type V orthodontic cement, Type VI core build-up (Sikri, 2020).

### S3. What a bi-layered restoration is [classification]
A bi-layered (sandwich, laminate) restoration uses GIC as the dentine replacement and composite as the enamel replacement, so each material is used where its properties suit (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount et al, 2016). It is **open** if the GIC forms part of the axial wall and is exposed to the mouth, and **closed** if composite covers the GIC completely (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### S4. Why do it [rationale]
Composite shrinks 1-5% by volume and the dentine bond is hard to keep over time; GIC bonds to dentine by ion exchange and bonds to composite, giving a near-monolithic reconstruction (Mount, 1994). Practical gains: less composite, fewer increments, less total shrinkage, time saved (Mount et al, 2016). Lamination is preferred in any cavity with at least one margin on dentine (Mount et al, 2016). A GIC base in larger restorations gives a much better chance of keeping oral microflora from reaching dentine and so reduces pulpal irritation (Mount et al, 2016).

### S5. Technique [treatment]
Mount's principles: use the strongest GIC in quantity as the dentine substitute, with no sub-base of calcium hydroxide; condition for 10 seconds; let it set fully; cut back about 2 mm to expose all enamel margins; leave GIC exposed at a subgingival margin without enamel; build the contact in composite; always use a radiopaque GIC (Mount et al, 2016). With auto-cure GIC, etch cement and enamel together for 15 seconds, apply thin low-viscosity enamel resin, then incremental composite (Mount et al, 2016); with resin-modified GIC no etching of cement is needed (Mount et al, 2016). The cement must cover all tubules, be at least 1 mm thick, and use the highest powder:liquid ratio (Mount, 1994).

### S6. Limits and cautions
Early sandwich studies showed more bulk fractures, though those used Black-style cavities and early bases (Mount et al, 2016). Pickard says closed sandwich restorations are more prone to long-term failure at interfaces (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). Etching GIC too long or too early damages it: early etching harms the immature matrix, and composite shrinkage can pull glass particles from etched cement (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Lining-grade cement is not designed to be exposed (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Restorative GIC is radiolucent, so a radiopaque composite over cut-back GIC can mimic recurrent decay (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## EXAM MODE

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement is classified by Wilson and McLean into Type I (luting), Type II (restorative: II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced) and Type III (lining and base) (Mount, 1994). Each type has uses that follow from its powder:liquid ratio and strength.

### 2. Uses of each type
| Type | Powder:liquid | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| I luting | about 1.5:1 | crowns, bridges, inlays, onlays, posts, orthodontic bands and brackets (Mount, 1994; Sikri, 2020) |
| II.1 aesthetic restorative | 2.8:1 to 6.8:1 | Class III and V, erosion, abrasion and abfraction lesions, root caries, primary teeth (Mount, 1994; Sikri, 2020) |
| II.2 reinforced restorative | 3:1 to 4:1 | posterior sites, tunnel restorations, transitional restorations, limited cores, margin repairs (Mount, 1994) |
| III lining and base | lining 1.5:1; base 3:1 or more | thermal protection, dentine substitute, dentine seal, undercut block-out (Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013) |
| IV (Sikri) | high | fissure sealant (Sikri, 2020) |
| V (Sikri) | low | orthodontic cement (Sikri, 2020) |
| VI (Sikri) | high | core build-up (Sikri, 2020) |

### 3. Advantages that justify these uses
Chemical adhesion, fluoride release, thermal expansion close to dentine, biocompatibility, and fast set in Type I and II.2 (Sikri, 2020).

### 4. Special uses
- Transitional restoration and ART, with maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013; Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- Fissure sealing: GIC sealants bond to enamel by ion exchange; a weak low-powder mix is contraindicated because of wear, and even when the sealant is lost, fluoride uptake into enamel reduces caries activity (Mount et al, 2016).
- Cervical and root lesions (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Repair of perforation and resorption; endodontic sub-seal (Sikri, 2020; Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011).

### 4A. Choosing the type by site (Mount and Hume)
- **Minor occlusal lesions (Site 1, Size 1):** GIC is preferred, and remaining fissures are sealed with GIC or composite (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Occlusal lesions needing more strength:** GIC is the best initial restoration; laminate with composite or amalgam if the occlusal load is heavy; use the strongest, preferably auto-cure, high powder:liquid cement (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Proximal tunnel:** Type II.2 high-strength auto-cure radiopaque cement, preferably capsulated, placed in two increments under a lightly wedged Mylar strip (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Cervical lesions (Site 3, Size 2):** resin-modified or auto-cure GIC depending on light access, always conditioned with 10% polyacrylic acid (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Large cavities:** a high-strength GIC base on the axial wall gives a thermal barrier and bioactivity (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Bi-layered (sandwich) restoration: definition
GIC as dentine replacement plus composite as enamel replacement, also called laminate restoration (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Types of sandwich
- **Open:** GIC forms part of the axial or gingival wall and is exposed in the mouth, used in Class II and Class V extending below the CEJ (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Closed:** GIC completely covered by composite (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### 7. Indications
- Cavities with a margin on dentine or cementum, especially subgingival Class II (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- Erosive and cervical lesions, bonding composite to enamel margins only (Sikri, 2020).
- Deep Class II with amalgam: GIC in the gingival half, amalgam above (Mount, 1994).
- Where composite-dentine bonding is in doubt (Mount et al, 2016).

### 8. Rationale
Composite shrinks 1-5% and loses dentine bond over time; GIC bonds to dentine and to composite; the combination uses less composite and fewer increments (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

### 9. Technique
1. Isolate; remove caries; condition dentine 10 seconds (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Place the strongest radiopaque GIC under a matrix, covering all tubules, at least 1 mm thick (Mount, 1994).
3. Let it set; cut back about 2 mm to expose all enamel margins (Mount et al, 2016).
4. Etch enamel and auto-cure GIC for 15 seconds only; wash; dry lightly (Mount et al, 2016).
5. Apply thin enamel bonding resin and light cure; for resin-modified GIC etch enamel only (Mount et al, 2016).
6. Place composite in increments; build the contact in composite (Mount et al, 2016).
7. Finish and polish; leave GIC exposed only at a subgingival margin without enamel (Mount et al, 2016).

### 10. Advantages and limits
- **Advantages:** maximum GIC exposure for fluoride release, less composite, interproximal GIC tolerated by tissue and resists plaque (Mount, 1994).
- **Limits:** closed type more prone to interface failure (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); early studies reported more bulk fractures (Mount et al, 2016); lining-grade GIC is too weak to support composite and must not be exposed (Sikri, 2020; McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- **Evidence:** retention improved with the sandwich technique in cervical lesions in a meta-analysis of 4 studies at unclear risk of bias (Q1, claim 3) (de Paula et al, 2019).

### 10A. Sandwich restoration in other settings
- **Amalgam lamination:** the strongest GIC is used as a base, cut back generously to give mechanical interlocks; amalgam protects the cement from occlusal load (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Cermet or amalgam lamination in Class II:** the cement is tamped in, left to set for 6 minutes, then cut back below the contact area (Mount, 1994).
- **Anterior and trauma cases:** a strong GIC replaces dentine, and enamel margins are bevelled for composite (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Aesthetics:** wait up to a week for GIC to mature and reach its final colour before deciding on a composite laminate for appearance (Mount et al, 2016).

### 11. Conclusion
Each GIC type has a defined clinical niche set by strength and ratio; the sandwich restoration is the way to combine GIC's bond and fluoride with composite's wear resistance, and works best as a planned technique in subgingival or dentine-margin cavities.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Types: I luting, II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced, III lining and base; P/L from 1.5:1 to over 3:1 (Mount, 1994).
- Sandwich = GIC as dentine, composite as enamel; open vs closed (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- Etch GIC and enamel 15 s only (auto-cure); RMGIC needs no etching (Mount et al, 2016).
- Cover all tubules, at least 1 mm (Mount, 1994).
- Closed sandwich: more interface failure (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing uses without linking them to the type.
2. Acid-etching GIC for too long or etching a freshly set cement.
3. Writing that lining-grade cement can support composite.
4. Forgetting that restorative GIC is radiolucent.
