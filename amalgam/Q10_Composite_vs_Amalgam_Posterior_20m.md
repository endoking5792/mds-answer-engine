## STUDY MODE

### 1. Amalgam's merits
Craig's 14th edition gives a concise, balanced summary of amalgam's real advantages: it is reasonably easy to insert, is not overly technique-sensitive, maintains anatomical form well, has reasonably adequate fracture resistance, prevents marginal leakage after a period in the mouth via corrosion-product self-sealing, can be used confidently in stress-bearing areas, and has a relatively long service life (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Amalgam's long-term survival is also directly predictable from alloy composition: a composite of 14 independent controlled clinical studies rated survival by copper/zinc content, finding modern zinc-containing high-copper amalgams achieved nearly 90% survival at 12 years — the best of any alloy subtype (Anusavice et al, 2013, cited in Module 3 SubModule 6.1).

### 2. Amalgam's demerits
Real disadvantages, equally concisely stated: amalgam's silver colour does not match tooth structure; it is somewhat brittle; it is subject to corrosion and galvanic action; it may show a degree of marginal breakdown; it does not help retain weakened tooth structure the way an adhesively bonded material can; and there are real regulatory concerns about its mercury content and disposal (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Despite these shortcomings, Craig's own summary judgment is direct: amalgam "has a long history as a cost-effective and successful restorative material."

### 3. Composite's merits and the specific indications for posterior use
Philips' gives posterior composite selection its own explicitly titled section, and its content is more specific than a generic "esthetics vs. durability" framing. The primary indication for choosing composite over amalgam posteriorly is ESTHETICS — unless the patient's esthetic demand is genuinely high, there is minimal advantage to using resin restorations posteriorly, since the disadvantages, coupled with greater manipulation difficulty, often outweigh the benefit. A real secondary indication is conservation of tooth structure: because composite does not depend on mechanical undercuts for retention, the classical concepts of resistance form and retention form do not really apply to resin preparations the way they do to amalgam (Anusavice et al, 2013, cited in Module 3 SubModule 6.1). A further, lesser but genuine merit: composite's lower thermal conductivity relative to amalgam's metallic conductivity can itself be a reason to prefer composite, since amalgam's rapid heat conduction can produce tooth sensitivity and pain.

### 4. Composite's demerits and genuine contraindications
The contraindications are equally specific, not vague cautions. Because composite's physical properties are inferior to amalgam's and cast metal's, it should NOT be used for cuspal coverage or for restorations exceeding one-third of the buccolingual width of the tooth — occlusal load should always be borne by sound tooth structure, never resin. Because wear is a genuine concern, posterior composite should not be placed in patients with parafunctional habits (bruxism); Philips' states directly that a composite Class II restoration is "doomed to failure" in a bruxing patient, given its greater wear potential. Use of posterior composite in a caries-active mouth is also questionable, since few current restorative materials offer any anticariogenic effect or reliably resist leakage; a hydrophilic sealing resin along the margins may help if composite must be used regardless (Anusavice et al, 2013). Gingival-margin failure is particularly common with composite, since reliable long-term adhesion to dentin (as opposed to enamel) remains genuinely difficult to achieve; a glass-ionomer base (the sandwich/lamination technique) is strongly recommended specifically to reduce this risk (Mount, 2016, cited in Module 1 SubModule 6.3).

### 5. The evolving comparative evidence on longevity
Older comparative data consistently favoured amalgam: a late-1990s review found high-copper amalgam averaging a 1.1% annual failure rate (AFR) versus 2.4% for composite, though absolute 5-year survival differed little (94% vs. 93%); a large private-practice analysis of over 300,000 posterior restorations over 7 years found composite 16.4% more likely to fail than amalgam at any time point; a military cohort of 2,780 recruits found a 64% greater composite-failure risk over 3 years; and a controlled trial of 1,748 restorations found amalgam's AFR (0.8%) significantly lower than composite's (2.2%) at 7 years, with composite's secondary-caries risk 3.5 times greater (Hilton et al, 2013, cited in Module 1 SubModule 6.3). More RECENT practice-based trials have shifted this picture substantially: a 5-year trial of 1,262 restorations found NO significant difference (amalgam 3.2% vs. composite 4.4% AFR); a 10-year Class I/II study found no difference at all; and, most strikingly, a 12-year trial of 1,949 LARGE Class II restorations found composite performing SIGNIFICANTLY BETTER (1.7% AFR) than high-copper amalgam (2.4%) — though within that same study, HIGH-caries-risk patients specifically still showed an amalgam longevity advantage for 3-surface restorations, with no material difference for 4- and 5-surface restorations. A separate, more recent review (Manhart et al., 2004) found composite INLAYS and amalgam shared an identical AFR (1.9%), direct composite was somewhat worse (3.6%), and cast gold inlays were only marginally — not significantly — better than either (1.4%) (Anusavice et al, 2013, cited in Module 3 SubModule 6.1). Summitt's own direct conclusion, worth reproducing rather than overstating: it is not possible to say composite is a true amalgam-equivalent replacement in ALL clinical situations, but with appropriate case selection and meticulous technique, posterior composite can perform very acceptably — up to 10 years' comparable performance has been directly demonstrated.

### 6. A genuinely important longevity caveat applying to both materials
Amalgam's own longevity figures contain an easily overlooked caveat directly relevant to this comparison: reported survival depends heavily on whether restorations were placed in general practice or in controlled clinical studies. Median survival for posterior amalgam is 7-15 years in general practice (larger, more complex restorations at the lower end) but 55-70 years in controlled clinical studies under calibrated, unhurried conditions (Anusavice et al, 2013). This roughly eightfold gap illustrates how far general-practice conditions (time pressure, variable technique, variable compliance) fall short of a restoration's true achievable longevity — a point directly relevant to interpreting ANY published longevity figure for either material, not just amalgam's.

### 6b. A direct material-property comparison, and a third comparator (ceramic inlays)
A real three-way comparison table — Amalgam / Resin Composite / Ceramic Inlay — sets out placement method, ease of placement, proximal-contact reliability, bonding to tooth, and longevity side by side (Powers and Wataha, 2013). The ceramic-inlay arm is genuinely absent from the amalgam-vs-composite framing used so far in this topic: it is placed indirectly, achieves reliable proximal contacts, is the LEAST conservative of tooth structure of the three, bonds to the tooth via resin cementation, and had (at time of writing) a "good, but still long-term unknown" longevity rating — a real third option worth naming even in a question framed as a two-material comparison. A further, specific clinical rationale for amalgam's continued relevance is worth citing directly: amalgam may be successfully placed when ISOLATION of the tooth is genuinely impossible and bonding of a composite would therefore be compromised — amalgam's isolation-independence is a real, distinct practical advantage over composite, beyond the general merits/demerits list already given (Powers and Wataha, 2013). On pure material properties, amalgam's coefficient of thermal expansion is roughly 2.5 times that of tooth structure, against composite's considerably larger 3-6 times — meaning amalgam's thermal mismatch with tooth structure, while real, is still smaller than composite's (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020). Compressive-versus-tensile strength data explain a further real design implication: enamel's own compressive strength is roughly 400MPa, and amalgam's compressive strength (340-510MPa) is broadly comparable, but amalgam's tensile strength is only 48-70MPa — explaining both why amalgam is indicated for compression-dominant intracoronal preparations and why it remains vulnerable to fracture specifically under tension, as in an unprotected cusp (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020). A further conceptual distinction worth citing: amalgam is actively CONDENSED into a preparation (displacing material toward the walls, margins, and contact under pressure), while composite is merely ADAPTED — which is precisely why amalgam's proximal contour partly self-forms from condensation pressure, whereas composite's contour depends entirely on matrix and wedge technique (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020).

### 6c. Periodontal and retreatment comparisons beyond AFR
Two further real comparative findings, genuinely distinct from the AFR/longevity data already given, are worth citing. First, resin composite placed at or below the gingival crest has been shown to alter the subgingival microflora toward a more pathogenic mix, while a subgingival amalgam margin under the same conditions did NOT significantly alter the biofilm — a real periodontal-biocompatibility difference, not merely a mechanical one (Hilton et al, 2013, citing Paolantonio et al., 2004). Second, an insurance-claims-database review found that molars restored with amalgam required subsequent endodontic therapy considerably LESS often than molars restored with resin composite — though this finding should be stated with its own honest caveat, since it predates modern bonding systems and more recent data may tell a different story (Hilton et al, 2013, citing Bogacki et al., 2002). A further practical comparison worth citing: removing an amalgam restoration is generally possible WITHOUT enlarging the original preparation, since amalgam has no bond to the cavity walls and its colour contrasts clearly with tooth structure, aiding visualisation during removal — composite removal, by contrast, "consistently" results in a LARGER final preparation than the original (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 7. Current status — why amalgam persists despite composite's advances
Michigan Craig's (2002) gives a distinct public-health framing worth setting alongside Craig's 14th-edition (2019) advantage/disadvantage list, written 17 years apart: "many argue... that the use of amalgam must be strongly supported given its large public health benefit... and many other countries," alongside an explicit, dated market-share observation that cast gold and esthetic restorative materials were "now very competitive in terms of frequency of use" (Craig, 2002, cited in Module 3 SubModule 6.1). Read together, these capture a real, ongoing shift in amalgam's market position without either edition needing to state the trend explicitly — amalgam has not disappeared, but its share of restorations has genuinely declined, driven primarily by esthetic demand and regulatory/environmental pressure (covered in depth in Q1/Q2) rather than by any settled finding of material inferiority.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim — 20m)

**Claim — resin composite has become a true, universal replacement for amalgam as the posterior stress-bearing restorative material of choice.** EVIDENCE: a 12-year large-Class-II trial found composite performing SIGNIFICANTLY BETTER than amalgam overall (1.7% vs. 2.4% AFR), and several intermediate-duration trials (5- and 10-year) found no significant difference at all — a genuine, documented narrowing (and in one large trial, reversal) of amalgam's historical longevity advantage. INTERPRETATION: this evidence is real and should inform modern material selection away from a blanket "amalgam is always more durable" assumption. LIMITATION: the SAME 12-year trial's own high-caries-risk subgroup showed amalgam retaining a genuine longevity advantage for 3-surface restorations, and Philips' explicit contraindications (bruxism, large restorations, caries-active mouths) remain unchanged by this longevity data — meaning the evolving AFR evidence answers "which material lasts longer, on average, in a general population" but does NOT answer, and should not be used to answer, the separate question of which material is appropriate for a SPECIFIC patient's risk profile and restoration size. `[Certain]` that the average longevity gap has narrowed/reversed in some trials; `[Likely]` overreaching to conclude from this alone that composite should now universally replace amalgam regardless of case selection.

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~40 min)**

### Introduction
Amalgam and resin composite remain the two dominant posterior direct restorative materials, and a rigorous comparison must weigh their respective merits and demerits against both classical material-science criteria and the genuinely evolving comparative longevity evidence, rather than defaulting to either a fixed "amalgam is always more durable" or "composite has fully caught up" assumption. This answer sets out each material's real advantages and disadvantages, the specific clinical indications and contraindications that follow from them, the evolving annual-failure-rate (AFR) evidence comparing the two over several decades, an important caveat about how longevity figures themselves should be interpreted, and the current real-world status of each material in practice.

### Amalgam — merits
Easy, low-technique-sensitivity insertion; good anatomical form; adequate fracture resistance; self-sealing margins via corrosion products (reduces leakage over time); suitable for genuinely stress-bearing areas; long service life; best-performing alloy subtype (zinc-containing high-copper) reaches ~90% survival at 12 years.

### Amalgam — demerits
Non-tooth-coloured; somewhat brittle; subject to corrosion/galvanic action; some marginal breakdown; does NOT reinforce weakened tooth structure (unlike an adhesive material); mercury-related regulatory/disposal concerns (detailed in Q1/Q2).

### Composite — merits and specific indications
Primary indication: ESTHETICS — minimal advantage posteriorly unless esthetic demand is genuinely high. Secondary indication: conservative preparation, since resin does not depend on mechanical undercuts (classical resistance/retention form concepts don't fully apply). Lower thermal conductivity than amalgam — avoids amalgam's heat-transfer sensitivity.

### Composite — demerits and contraindications
NOT for cuspal coverage or restorations >1/3 buccolingual width — occlusal load must rest on sound tooth structure, never resin alone. Contraindicated in bruxism/parafunction (a Class II composite is "doomed to failure" in a bruxing patient). Questionable in caries-active mouths (no inherent anticariogenic effect); a hydrophilic sealing resin may help if unavoidable. Gingival-margin failure is common (dentin adhesion remains difficult) — a GIC sandwich base is recommended to mitigate this.

### The evolving comparative longevity evidence
OLDER data consistently favoured amalgam: a late-1990s review found amalgam averaging a 1.1% annual failure rate (AFR) against composite's 2.4%, despite similar 5-year survival (94% vs. 93%); a 300,000-restoration private-practice analysis found composite 16.4% more likely to fail; a military cohort found a 64% greater composite-failure risk over 3 years; and a 1,748-restoration trial found amalgam's AFR (0.8%) significantly lower than composite's (2.2%) at 7 years, with composite's secondary-caries risk 3.5× greater. More RECENT evidence narrows or reverses this: a 5-year trial found NO significant difference (3.2% vs. 4.4%); a 10-year Class I/II study found none either; and a 12-year trial of 1,949 LARGE Class II restorations found composite SIGNIFICANTLY BETTER (1.7% vs. 2.4%) — EXCEPT in high-caries-risk patients, where amalgam retained an advantage for 3-surface restorations. A separate review found composite inlays and amalgam sharing an identical AFR (1.9%); direct composite was worse (3.6%); gold inlays only marginally better (1.4%).

### The general-practice-vs-controlled-trial caveat
Reported longevity for EITHER material depends heavily on setting: amalgam's median survival is 7-15 years in general practice but 55-70 years in controlled clinical trials under calibrated, unhurried conditions — an ~8-fold gap illustrating how far ordinary practice conditions fall short of a material's true achievable longevity, a caution applicable to any comparable composite figure too.

### Current status
Amalgam's market share has genuinely declined, driven primarily by esthetic demand and mercury-related regulatory/environmental pressure (detailed in the mercury-controversy topics), not by a settled finding of material inferiority. It remains actively supported on public-health/cost-effectiveness grounds, particularly in high-caries-risk patients and low-/middle-income settings, where no mercury-free alternative yet matches its combined technique-insensitivity, longevity, and cost.

### Conclusion
Neither material is a universal "winner": amalgam offers superior technique-tolerance, self-sealing margins, and proven long-term survival, particularly in high-caries-risk and heavy-occlusal-load situations, while composite offers esthetics and conservative preparation, with longevity that has genuinely improved and in some modern large trials now matches or exceeds amalgam's — but only within its own real contraindications (bruxism, oversized restorations, uncontrolled caries activity). Material selection should therefore be case-specific — driven by esthetic demand, caries risk, and restoration size — rather than by either an outdated or an over-corrected blanket rule.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Quoting only one era of AFR data (old OR new) instead of showing the evidence has genuinely evolved.
2. Treating the 12-year trial's "composite significantly better" finding as universal, ignoring its own high-caries-risk subgroup exception.
3. Omitting composite's specific contraindications (bruxism, >1/3 width, caries-active mouth) — a common source of lost marks.
4. Forgetting the general-practice-vs-controlled-trial survival gap when citing any longevity figure.
5. Attributing amalgam's declining use to proven material inferiority, rather than esthetic demand and regulatory/environmental pressure.
