## STUDY MODE

### The parent reaction
Dental amalgam's setting reaction occurs between the alloy's Ag₃Sn intermetallic compound (the unreacted alloy particle itself, sometimes labelled the γ phase) and liquid mercury. Silver and tin both dissolve from the particle surface into the mercury, but because silver's solubility in mercury is much lower than tin's, the silver-mercury phase precipitates first, while the tin-mercury phase precipitates later (Anusavice et al, 2013). Mercury itself only reacts with the outer 3-5µm of each alloy particle — the particle's core remains unreacted, and in set high-copper amalgam these unreacted original particles make up more than 50% of the final volume (Powers and Wataha, 2013).

**Where the γ (Ag₃Sn) phase itself comes from**: the silver-tin system forms a disordered β solid solution that grows as dendrites below 724°C; at roughly 26.8% tin and 480°C, a peritectic transformation converts this β phase into the ordered γ phase, with α and δ phases forming at the compositional extremes — the manufacturing cooling rate controls the final β:γ ratio and, with it, the alloy's amalgamation reactivity (Bhat et al, 2019). A broader, alternative phase taxonomy used by some sources names SEVEN components as clinically significant to serviceability, not just three: the original γ, γ1, γ2, residual (unreacted) mercury, voids/pores, trace-element phases, and "interphases" — the boundary zones between γ/γ1/γ2 — whose continuity and thinness govern how well the whole structure actually bonds together (Marzouk, 1985). An exact phase-PERCENTAGE breakdown for conventional (low-copper) alloy is worth citing directly: AgHg (γ1) 66.25%, SnHg (γ2) 6.25%, Ag₃Sn (γ) 25%, and residual mercury 2.5% (Hilton et al, 2013).

### The named phases
- **γ (gamma) — Ag₃Sn**: the original, unreacted alloy particle itself. In low-copper amalgam, mercury is added at roughly a 1:1 ratio with the alloy — insufficient to consume every particle completely — so unconsumed γ particles remain embedded within the set mass, acting as a reinforcing filler within the reacted matrix (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **γ1 (gamma-1) — Ag₂Hg₃**: a body-centred cubic silver-mercury phase, the FIRST to precipitate (since silver is less soluble in mercury than tin). γ1 forms the continuous binding MATRIX that holds the set restoration together, and is the strongest and most corrosion-resistant of the mercury-containing phases (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **γ2 (gamma-2) — Sn₇₋₈Hg**: a hexagonal tin-mercury phase, precipitating LATER than γ1. γ2 is the clinically critical phase: it is simultaneously the WEAKEST mechanical phase and the LEAST corrosion-resistant phase in the entire system, especially vulnerable within restoration crevices and margins (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- **ε (epsilon) — Cu₃Sn**: a copper-tin intermetallic compound, present only when the alloy contains copper. It is the phase that reacts with γ2 in a secondary, copper-driven reaction.
- **η′ (eta-prime) — Cu₆Sn₅**: the product of the ε + γ2 secondary reaction; forming η′ consumes γ2 in the process. Once alloy copper content exceeds roughly 6% by weight, this pathway begins meaningfully reducing or eliminating γ2 altogether (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### How phase formation differs by alloy type — this is the real examinable mechanism
**Low-copper alloy** (≤5% Cu): too little copper for the ε/η′ reaction to go to completion, so the set structure is genuinely γ (unconsumed alloy) + γ1 (matrix) + γ2 (substantial, largely unreacted), making this the only alloy type in which γ2 persists in significant quantity.

**High-copper ADMIXED alloy** (≈11.8% Cu): γ1 and γ2 initially form exactly as in low-copper amalgam — but tin dissolved in the mercury simultaneously diffuses outward to the surface of the separate Ag-Cu spherical particles and reacts with their copper to form η′ there. This is a genuine SECONDARY, solid-state reaction: the γ2 that formed moments earlier is subsequently consumed by copper supplied from the Ag-Cu spheres, and with sufficient copper content, γ2 can be entirely eliminated by this secondary process (Anusavice et al, 2013).

**High-copper SINGLE-COMPOSITION alloy** (13-30% Cu): γ1 crystals form the binding matrix directly, and η′ crystals appear from the very start as meshes of fine rod-like crystals dispersed on and through that matrix — but because copper is already alloyed homogeneously throughout every particle (not existing as separate copper-rich spheres), little or NO γ2 forms in the first place, and no secondary elimination step is needed at all (Anusavice et al, 2013).

**A real, staged TIME-COURSE for the admixed high-copper reaction** is worth citing for a fuller answer: immediately after mixing, the Ag-Cu eutectic reacts with mercury to give γ1 together with Sn₇Hg (γ2), Cu₃Sn, and Cu₆Sn₅; after approximately one week, the γ2 phase has fully reacted with the remaining eutectic, leaving γ1 + Cu₃Sn/Cu₆Sn₅; and after approximately one year, the Ag-Sn (γ) phase itself has essentially vanished, as tin continues diffusing into the Ag-Cu eutectic (Hilton et al, 2013). The single-composition (unicompositional) alloy, by contrast, reacts directly and FASTER, with no γ2 intermediate ever forming (Ag₃Sn + Cu₃Sn + Hg → γ1 + Cu₆Sn₅ directly) (Hilton et al, 2013). The UNREACTED-phase composition itself differs genuinely by alloy type: low-copper alloy retains β+γ; admixed high-copper alloy retains β+γ+ε(Ag₃Cu₂); and single-composition high-copper alloy retains β+γ+Cu₃Sn (Bhat et al, 2019). **A genuine nomenclature discrepancy exists on which compound is actually "ε"**: Bhat et al. name Ag₃Cu₂ as the ε phase in admixed amalgam, while McCabe and Walls instead name Cu₃Sn as ε (both agree η′ is Cu₆Sn₅) — both labels are reported here rather than silently standardised (Bhat et al, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008). A further, related metallurgical point: amalgam cannot be strengthened by cold working, since mouth/room temperature already exceeds its recrystallisation temperature; such strengthening as dispersed phases (copper, the Ag-Cu eutectic, or the Ag-Cu-Pd eutectic) do provide instead arises from lattice distortion and precipitation interfering with dislocation slip (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### Corrosion-resistance ranking across every phase (most to least resistant)
γ1 (Ag₂Hg₃) > γ (Ag₃Sn) > Ag₃Cu₂ > ε (Cu₃Sn) > η′ (Cu₆Sn₅) > γ2 (Sn₇₋₈Hg) — confirming γ2 as the single weakest link in the whole system (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### The internal galvanic consequence
A real internal galvanic cell exists between γ1 and γ2 wherever both are present (low-copper amalgam), since the two phases have different electrochemical potentials; γ2 is anodic and corrodes significantly faster purely because it is in electrical contact with γ1 (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). High-copper amalgam, by eliminating γ2 through either route above, removes this internal galvanic couple entirely — the direct mechanistic reason high-copper amalgam resists corrosion so much better than low-copper amalgam.

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes)**

### The parent reaction
Alloy's Ag₃Sn (γ) + liquid Hg. Ag less soluble in Hg than Sn → γ1 (Ag-Hg) precipitates FIRST; γ2 (Sn-Hg) precipitates LATER.

### Phase table
| Phase | Formula | Structure/Role | Property |
|---|---|---|---|
| γ | Ag₃Sn | Unreacted alloy particle (filler) | Reinforcing |
| γ1 | Ag₂Hg₃ | BCC; binding MATRIX | Strongest, most corrosion-resistant of Hg phases |
| γ2 | Sn₇₋₈Hg | Hexagonal | WEAKEST, LEAST corrosion-resistant — the clinically critical phase |
| ε | Cu₃Sn | Cu-Sn intermetallic (Cu-containing alloys only) | Reacts with γ2 |
| η′ | Cu₆Sn₅ | Product of ε + γ2 reaction | Formation consumes/eliminates γ2 |

### Phase formation by alloy type
- **Low-Cu (≤5%)**: γ + γ1 + γ2 (substantial, unreacted) — insufficient Cu for ε/η′ reaction to complete.
- **High-Cu admixed (~11.8%)**: γ1+γ2 form first, THEN secondary reaction (Sn diffuses to Ag-Cu spheres → η′) consumes γ2; complete elimination possible.
- **High-Cu single-composition (13-30%)**: γ1 matrix + η′ rods form together from the start; little/no γ2 ever forms — no secondary step needed.

### Corrosion ranking
γ1 > γ(Ag₃Sn) > Ag₃Cu₂ > ε > η′ > γ2 (weakest).

### Clinical consequence — internal galvanism
γ1-γ2 form an internal galvanic couple (different electrochemical potentials); γ2 (anodic) corrodes faster when in contact with γ1. High-Cu amalgam eliminates γ2 → eliminates this couple → far superior corrosion resistance. This is the direct mechanistic reason high-copper alloys have replaced low-copper alloys in modern practice.

### Conclusion
Amalgam's phase structure is not incidental chemistry — the presence or elimination of γ2 specifically (via copper content and manufacturing route) directly determines corrosion resistance, creep behaviour, and ultimately clinical longevity. A complete answer should therefore name not just which phases exist, but explain the mechanistic REASON copper content changes which phases form — this causal link, rather than bare nomenclature, is what distinguishes a full-marks answer from a partial one.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- γ (Ag₃Sn, unreacted alloy) → γ1 (Ag₂Hg₃, matrix, precipitates first) → γ2 (Sn₇₋₈Hg, weakest/least resistant, precipitates later).
- ε (Cu₃Sn) + γ2 → η′ (Cu₆Sn₅) — the copper-driven reaction that eliminates γ2.
- Low-Cu: γ2 persists substantially. Admixed high-Cu: γ2 eliminated by SECONDARY reaction. Single-composition high-Cu: γ2 barely forms at all.
- Corrosion ranking: γ1 > γ > Ag₃Cu₂ > ε > η′ > γ2.
- Internal γ1-γ2 galvanic cell = why γ2's presence accelerates corrosion specifically.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing only γ1/γ2 and omitting ε/η′, missing the actual mechanism of γ2 elimination in high-copper alloys.
2. Treating "high-copper alloy" as one uniform mechanism — admixed and single-composition alloys eliminate γ2 by genuinely different routes (secondary reaction vs. none forming at all).
3. Forgetting which phase precipitates first (γ1, not γ2) and why (silver's lower solubility in mercury).
4. Omitting the corrosion-resistance ranking when asked to "discuss" phases — examiners expect the clinical link, not just nomenclature.
5. Not connecting γ2 to the internal galvanic-cell mechanism that explains WHY it corrodes fastest.

## MNEMONIC
"**1 before 2, Cu takes 2 away**" — γ1 forms before γ2 (silver precipitates first); copper (via ε→η′) is what removes γ2 in high-copper alloys.
