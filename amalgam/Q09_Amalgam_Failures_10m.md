## STUDY MODE

### The two leading real-world failure modes
Clinical diagnosis of secondary (recurrent) caries is the single most common reason amalgam restorations are replaced; fracture is the second most common reason (Anusavice et al, 2013). A direct, quantified failure-cause breakdown corroborates and refines this ranking: 56% of amalgam failures have been attributed to incorrect tooth preparation, 40% to improper handling/manipulation of the material, and only the remaining 4% to periodontal disease, pulpal problems, and other causes (Hilton et al, 2013, citing Healey and Phillips) — a genuinely useful reframing, since it locates most failure in OPERATOR technique (preparation + manipulation = 96%) rather than in the material itself. A further, specific diagnostic critique is worth stating honestly: 50-60% of all replaced amalgam restorations are diagnosed as "secondary caries," but the term is poorly defined in everyday clinical use — many dentists equate any probe-catch or visually-inaccessible margin with caries even without confirmed disease, when this often really reflects inadequate condensation rather than true recurrent decay (Hilton et al, 2013). Replacement itself shows a real behavioural pattern: amalgam restorations are replaced roughly SEVEN TIMES more often when a patient switches to a different dentist than when the same dentist continues their care — evidence that at least some replacement is driven by clinician judgment/preference rather than objective failure (Hilton et al, 2013, citing Bogacki et al.). A genuinely important iatrogenic-damage statistic is also worth citing: 69% of adjacent teeth are damaged by the bur during ordinary Class II preparation, and the risk of proximal caries on those damaged teeth is almost TRIPLED as a direct result (Roberson et al, 2006, citing Qvist et al., 1992) — a real, quantified failure risk the operator themselves creates. Any complete answer on amalgam failure should lead with this real-world ranking rather than an exhaustive mechanism list, since examiners reward recognising which failure modes actually drive clinical decision-making.

### Marginal failure
Amalgam has relatively poor edge strength, and marginal DITCHING is genuinely common — but because amalgam corrodes and self-seals its own margins over time, this rarely progresses to further caries, and repair of ditched margins purely to eliminate the ditching is NOT normally indicated; ditching of a low-copper amalgam should be regarded as a NORMAL finding, not a defect (Mount, 2016, cited in Module 1 SubModule 6.3). This stands in direct contrast to composite resin and gold, neither of which has a comparable self-sealing ability — a failed margin in either of those materials demands prompt attention, since it offers no equivalent protective mechanism.

### Bulk failure of the restorative material itself
Each section of a complex amalgam restoration (proximal box, occlusal extension) must be INDEPENDENTLY retentive — one section cannot be expected to support another. Real, common causes of bulk failure include inadequate retention in one section, an unlocked isthmus lacking retentive grooves or ditches, and excessive or hydrolysing lining material reducing the amalgam's own physical bulk. A genuinely important caution: bonding an amalgam with a composite-resin bonding agent is INSUFFICIENT on its own to retain it — mechanical interlocks (pins, slots, grooves, or occlusal convergence) remain essential regardless of whether a bonding agent is also used (Mount, 2016, cited in Module 1 SubModule 6.3).

### Creep and marginal breakdown — a real, threshold-dependent correlation
There is little correlation between creep and marginal breakdown for alloys with creep values BELOW 1%; above this threshold, restorations made from higher-creep alloys generally show greater marginal breakdown than those made from lower-creep alloys (Anusavice et al, 2013). A genuinely important nuance worth stating honestly: the absence of the corrosion-susceptible γ2 phase is ASSUMED to be the principal reason high-copper amalgams resist marginal breakdown so much better than low-copper amalgams — and if this assumption is correct, creep itself may not actually be the operative predictive property for marginal breakdown specifically within high-copper amalgams, even though creep remains a real, independently measured property. Separately, moisture-contamination-driven expansion in a zinc-containing alloy (delayed expansion) can ALSO cause this same type of marginal-breakdown failure. Several mechanisms, Philips' states directly, may therefore act separately or work synergistically to produce marginal breakdown — a single-cause explanation should be avoided.

### The "5 Rs" framework — a named, structured decision tool
A genuinely useful, structured organising framework exists specifically for managing a failing or questionable amalgam restoration, worth citing as a named system rather than an ad hoc list: **Review, Refurbishment, Resealing, Repair, and Replacement** (Banerjee and Watson, eds., 2015, the "5 Rs," applied explicitly to dental amalgam). REVIEW means simply monitoring a borderline finding over time rather than intervening immediately. REFURBISHMENT means re-contouring/re-polishing an existing restoration without adding or removing bulk material. RESEALING is a genuinely distinct, non-operative repair modality: applying a flowable resin sealant into a non-carious marginal gap or defect of an old amalgam (after air abrasion, orthophosphoric-acid etching, and dentine-bonding-agent application), which has been shown to increase the restoration's clinical longevity — distinct from both the GIC-ditching repair and the roughened-fresh-amalgam repair already covered below (Banerjee and Watson, eds., 2015). REPAIR and REPLACEMENT are the two options already covered in depth. Real average age-at-replacement figures corroborate why this graduated framework matters: amalgam restorations average 10-15 years before replacement, resin composite restorations 5-8 years, and GIC restorations only 3-5 years (Banerjee and Watson, eds., 2015) — amalgam's own longevity is itself a reason not to default to replacement prematurely.

A genuinely important, easily overlooked clinical insight extends the self-sealing theme already covered: operative intervention on an old, well-established amalgam can itself cause SIGNIFICANT tooth-tissue destruction, because the corroded alloy has, over time, come to offer real structural "support" to an already-weakened tooth — removing that corroded alloy can therefore accelerate cusp or wall failure and materially complicate the replacement restoration (Banerjee and Watson, eds., 2015). A related, documented CLINICIAN decision-making bias is worth citing honestly: dentists have been shown to be more likely to intervene operatively on a resin composite restoration than on an amalgam restoration showing comparable wear, likely reflecting both a perception that amalgam is inherently longer-lasting and the lack of correlation between amalgam margin breakdown and actual caries — a black, tarnished, ditched-margin amalgam on a premolar may reasonably be left as "still functional," whereas a mildly stained composite restoration is often considered for repair or replacement far sooner (Banerjee and Watson, eds., 2015). A specific repair instrument worth naming: a tungsten carbide (TC) Beaver bur is used to cut through old amalgam from its centre toward the periphery during a repair procedure (Banerjee and Watson, eds., 2015; Ricketts, ed., 2011).

### Repair as a genuine alternative to replacement
When part of an amalgam restoration fails — most often from marginal fracture — repair is now considered a viable, cost-effective alternative to complete replacement, rather than a compromise measure. The critical factor governing repair quality is the interfacial bond between the new and the existing amalgam; surface treatment of the AGED amalgam is the major factor in achieving a high-quality bond, and the old surface should be roughened to remove corrosion and saliva contaminants and freed of loose debris. Freshly triturated amalgam condensed directly onto a properly roughened existing-amalgam surface can achieve a flexural strength of up to 50% of unrepaired amalgam — real and clinically useful, but genuinely limited, so repair should be attempted only where the area will not bear high stress, or where both restoration parts are already adequately supported and retained (Anusavice et al, 2013). **A genuine contradiction on the achievable repair-strength figure is worth flagging explicitly rather than quietly reconciling**: a separate source instead gives the achievable repair strength as a RANGE of 30% to 60% of unrepaired amalgam, rather than the single 50% figure already given — both figures are reported here, since they may reflect different surface-treatment techniques or testing conditions across studies (Hilton et al, 2013). For conservative marginal ditching specifically, the defect can often be opened with a fine tapered diamond bur and restored directly with glass-ionomer cement — though if the defect is long-standing, the underlying dentin must first be explored to rule out active caries before proceeding (Mount, 2016, cited in Module 1 SubModule 6.3). When secondary caries is suspected, removing part of the restoration to full depth at the site of the defect — rather than replacing the entire restoration outright — is itself a recognised procedure for making a definitive diagnosis of the caries' actual extent; Sturdevant's gives a specific numeric threshold for this decision directly: an amalgam restoration that is more than minimally overcarved — specifically, a submarginal defect greater than 0.2mm — should be replaced rather than repaired (Roberson et al, 2006).

### Longevity in context, and two findings that correct common assumptions
Two further real findings are worth citing to correct assumptions that otherwise go unchallenged. First, POLISHING an amalgam restoration does NOT improve its clinical performance or marginal integrity, even for high-copper amalgam specifically — polishing is a cosmetic/comfort intervention, not a performance one, and should not be presented as a failure-prevention measure (Hilton et al, 2013, citing Mayhew et al., 1986). Second, and directly relevant to the amalgam-vs-composite debate elsewhere in this topic: amalgam-restored teeth do NOT fracture more frequently than composite-restored teeth — the likelier real cause of cuspal fracture is weakening from an overly large tooth PREPARATION, not the choice of restorative material itself (Hilton et al, 2013, citing Wahl et al., 2004). Clinical survival is also directly, inversely related to the degree of unsupported enamel left at the margin — a real, quantified design-quality predictor of longevity in its own right (Hilton et al, 2013, citing De C Luz et al., 2001). Finally, real-world longevity examples are worth citing as illustrative evidence beyond bare survival percentages: clinical photographs exist in the literature of amalgam restorations still functioning at 44, 58, and even 65 years of age (Roberson et al, 2006, citing Osborne and Summitt) — and a large review of almost 3,500 four-surface and five-surface amalgams found 72% and 65% successful outcomes at 5 years respectively, figures that compare favourably with the 5-year success rates reported for gold and porcelain crowns over the same period (84% and 85%) (Roberson et al, 2006, citing Martin and Bader, 1997).

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes)**

### Leading real-world failure modes
1. Secondary (recurrent) caries — the single most common reason for replacement.
2. Fracture — the second most common reason.

### Marginal failure
Poor edge strength; marginal ditching is common but SELF-SEALING (via corrosion) — a NORMAL finding, not a defect requiring repair/repolishing. Contrast: composite/gold have no self-sealing ability and need prompt attention on margin failure.

### Bulk failure of the restoration
Each section (box/occlusal extension) must be INDEPENDENTLY retentive. Causes: inadequate retention per section; unlocked isthmus (no grooves/ditches); excessive/hydrolysing liner reducing bulk. Bonding agent ALONE is insufficient — mechanical interlock remains essential.

### Creep and marginal breakdown
Little correlation below 1% creep; above 1%, higher creep → more marginal breakdown. High-copper's resistance is mainly attributed to γ2 ABSENCE (not creep itself) — creep may not be the predictive property for high-copper alloys specifically. Zinc-driven moisture expansion (delayed expansion) can also cause marginal breakdown. Mechanisms act separately OR synergistically — avoid a single-cause explanation.

### Repair vs. replacement
Repair is now a viable, cost-effective alternative, not a compromise. Key factor: interfacial bond quality — roughen aged amalgam surface (remove corrosion/saliva contaminants/debris) before condensing fresh amalgam. Achievable flexural strength: up to 50% of unrepaired amalgam — use only in low-stress or well-supported areas. Minor ditching: open with fine tapered diamond, restore with GIC (rule out underlying caries first if long-standing). Partial-depth removal is a recognised technique to diagnose true caries extent before committing to full replacement.

### Conclusion
Amalgam failure is best organised by real clinical frequency (caries, then fracture) rather than an exhaustive mechanism list; marginal ditching specifically should be recognised as a normal, self-sealing finding rather than reflexively treated, and repair — correctly executed with surface roughening — is a genuine, evidence-supported alternative to full replacement in appropriate cases. A candidate should also be able to state WHY high-copper alloys fail less: eliminating the γ2 phase removes amalgam's single weakest, least corrosion-resistant link, directly reducing both the creep-driven and corrosion-driven routes to marginal breakdown simultaneously, which is the material-science reason high-copper amalgam has replaced low-copper amalgam in virtually all modern practice.

- Creep correlates with marginal breakdown only ABOVE 1% creep value.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Treating marginal ditching as a true failure requiring repair, rather than a normal self-sealing finding.
2. Omitting the real clinical ranking (caries #1, fracture #2) and jumping straight to mechanism-level detail.
3. Claiming a composite-resin bonding agent alone is sufficient to retain an amalgam restoration.
4. Stating creep correlates with marginal breakdown at ALL creep values, rather than specifically above the 1% threshold.
5. Forgetting that repair is a legitimate, evidence-supported alternative to replacement, not merely a stopgap.

## MNEMONIC
"**C-F-D-B**" for amalgam failure, in real clinical frequency order: **C**aries (secondary) → **F**racture → **D**itching (usually benign) → **B**ulk-section failure.
