## STUDY MODE

### Why additional/secondary retention is needed
When a cavity preparation has lost enough of its own conventional retention form (undercuts, box walls) through caries or previous restoration — most often in large, complex, or multi-surface restorations, or where cusps have been reduced/capped — an amalgam restoration requires AUXILIARY (additional/secondary) retention features to prevent displacement. The three such features, precisely distinguished by geometry, are **slots** (a retention groove whose length runs in a HORIZONTAL plane), **retention locks** (a groove whose length runs in a VERTICAL plane), and **coves** (a small, rounded supplementary feature prepared with a No. ¼ bur, used alongside pins or slots); plus **pins**, placed directly into dentin (Roberson et al, 2006, cited in Module 1 SubModule 6.1).

### Pin types, by mechanism and retentiveness
Three pin types exist, differing in how they engage the pinhole. The **self-threading pin** is the most widely used and most retentive type — three to six times more retentive than a cemented pin — because its threads actively engage dentin on insertion, exploiting dentin's elasticity to seat into a hole smaller than its own diameter; it was first described by Going in 1966. **Friction-locked (friction-grip)** pins (described by Goldstein in 1966) are tapped into a hole 0.025mm SMALLER than the pin itself, giving two to three times the retention of a cemented pin. **Cemented** pins (described by Markley in 1958) are luted into a hole 0.025-0.05mm LARGER than the pin and are the least retentive of the three, now rarely used in modern practice (Roberson et al, 2006). A useful teaching equivalency ties the three together directly: to match the retention provided by just 1mm of self-threading-pin depth requires roughly 2-3mm of friction-locked pin depth, or 5-6mm of cemented pin depth — exact channel-size differentials are, respectively, roughly 0.038-0.1mm smaller than the pin and 1.5-2mm deep (self-threading), roughly 0.025mm undersized and 2-4mm deep (friction-locked), and roughly 0.025mm oversized and 3-4mm deep (cemented) (Ricketts, ed., 2011). Pins themselves may be made of stainless steel, silver, platinum-iridium, titanium, or gold; stainless steel is the most commonly used but corrodes on direct contact with amalgam, which is why some stainless-steel pins are coated with titanium oxide or gold specifically to prevent this (Ricketts, ed., 2011).

### Self-threading (TMS) pin sizes and selection
Four colour-coded TMS pin sizes exist: **Regular** (0.78mm), **Minim** (0.61mm), **Minikin** (0.48mm), and **Minuta** (0.38mm). MINIKIN is the pin of choice for severely involved posterior teeth, specifically because it minimises dentin crazing, pulpal-penetration risk, and perforation risk. MINIM serves as a useful backup when a Minikin pinhole has been overprepared or its threads have stripped the dentin. MINUTA is usually too small to give adequate retention in posterior teeth. REGULAR, the largest, is rarely used, since laboratory testing found it caused the HIGHEST incidence of dentinal cracking communicating with the pulp chamber of all four sizes (Roberson et al, 2006).

### Precise placement rules
Pinhole depth should be a general 2mm — retention does NOT increase significantly beyond this depth, and deeper placement only risks weakening dentin or amalgam without benefit; laboratory testing found that removing a 0.61mm pin embedded deeper than 2mm fractures the PIN, while removing a 0.78mm pin embedded that deep instead fractures the DENTIN. The governing rule for NUMBER of pins is **ONE PIN PER MISSING AXIAL LINE ANGLE** — using the fewest pins that achieve adequate retention, since excessive pins can themselves fracture the tooth and weaken the restoration. Pinholes should be positioned near the tooth's line angles, no closer than 0.5-1mm to the dentinoenamel junction (DEJ) OR 1-1.5mm to the external tooth surface — whichever distance is GREATER — and oriented parallel to the adjacent external surface; sufficient occlusal clearance must remain to provide 2mm of amalgam bulk directly over the pin. Whenever three or more pinholes are placed, they should sit at different vertical levels where possible, to reduce cumulative stress concentration (Roberson et al, 2006).

### A genuine, separate sub-topic: horizontal pins
All of the placement rules above describe VERTICAL pins. HORIZONTAL pins are a genuinely distinct technique, well-evidenced but rarely taught alongside vertical placement: two horizontal self-threading pins (Minim or Minikin size) placed into a free-standing facial cusp of a maxillary premolar have been shown to effectively reinforce it (Burgess, 1985), and horizontal pins used to cross-splint the cusps of maxillary premolars to one another similarly reinforce/strengthen them (Lambert et al., 1985) (Hilton et al, 2013). Specific horizontal placement rules differ from vertical ones: the channel is initiated in dentin 0.5-1.0mm from the DEJ, directed parallel to the adjacent DEJ/external surface, and kept shallow — only 1.5-2.0mm deep, often reaching enamel, at which point deepening should STOP, since even 1.0mm of horizontal pin gives a useful retentive effect; the channel is positioned 0.5-1.0mm gingival to the occlusal DEJ for the best mechanical advantage (Hilton et al, 2013). A related solution for a tooth that has lost BOTH adjacent cusps: single vertical pins per cusp fail to resist the oblique-load rotation this creates, so the real fix is either horizontal pins through the remaining cusps, or, if those cusps are themselves too fragile, additional vertical pins placed at the gingival wall near the marginal-ridge areas instead (Hilton et al, 2013).

### Named non-pin retention techniques: amalgapins, dentin pins, and circumferential slots
Beyond the slots/locks/coves already described, several further NAMED techniques exist and are genuinely distinct from them. DENTIN PINS are a non-metallic natural-retention feature: where enamel at a cusp tip has fractured leaving shallow dentin caries, the dentist cuts AROUND the remaining dentin at the cusp tip to leave a pin-shaped dentin projection (needing at least 2mm clearance to the opposing tooth), with additional coves cut at its base if needed (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020). AMALGAPINS are a distinct, named technique first described as "amalgam inserts" by Seng et al. (1980) — small retentive channels roughly ¼mm in diameter/depth cut with a No. 35 inverted-cone bur, giving resistance similar to a self-threading pin; Shavell (1980) named the specific variant "amalgapin," prepared instead with a No. 1157 or 1156 bur to 3.0mm depth, with later studies finding 1.5-2.0mm adequate and showing that 0.8mm-diameter amalgapins give resistance similar to 1.0mm ones (Hilton et al, 2013). The Shavell amalgapin-CHANNEL technique specifically (1980) uses a channel 1.5-2mm deep and 0.8-1mm in diameter, cut with a No.329/330 bur or a No.1031 diamond, kept at least 1mm from the DEJ, with one channel per lost cusp positioned near the axial angles to avoid furcation involvement; a practical bur-alignment trick is to feel the gingival sulcus with a finger to set the bur parallel to the external tooth surface before cutting, and a bevel is placed at the channel entrance specifically to reduce stress concentration there (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020). CIRCUMFERENTIAL SLOTS are a further named technique (Outhwaite et al., 1979, cut with a No. 33½ inverted-cone bur): in vitro testing found NO significant resistance difference between a circumferential slot and 4 TMS Minim pins — but pin-retained restorations showed a greater tendency to slip on their base before outright failure, a tendency slot-retained restorations did not share, even though slot-retained restorations are themselves MORE sensitive to displacement during matrix-band removal than pin-retained ones (Hilton et al, 2013). Short segments of circumferential slots are informally called "segmental slots" or "cleats" by some clinicians (Hilton et al, 2013). Two further slot-specific findings are worth citing directly, since they are genuinely counterintuitive: shorter slots provide AS MUCH resistance to horizontal displacing force as longer slots do, and preparations with shorter slots actually fail LESS frequently than those with longer slots; increasing slot width and depth does NOT increase retentive strength either (Hilton et al, 2013, citing McMaster et al. and Chan and Chan). Loading angle also matters: standard 45°-loading resistance testing shows amalgapins performing roughly equal to pins, but at a 90° loading angle, stainless-steel pins give significantly MORE resistance than amalgapins — though genuinely few intraoral forces are applied at a full 90° angle in practice (Hilton et al, 2013). A further real finding on distribution, not just quantity, of retentive features: loading specimens from both sides (not only one) found that more load was needed to cause failure specifically when retention/resistance features were increased on the side OPPOSING the load direction — the directional placement of features matters, not simply how many are present (Hilton et al, 2013, citing Plasmans et al., 1987).

### Pulpal chamber retention — the Nayyar/chamber-retention mechanics in full
For an endodontically treated tooth, the pulp chamber itself can serve as a retentive feature, extending 2-4mm into the root canal space when the pulp-chamber height is 2mm or less; when chamber height is 4-6mm, NO additional advantage is gained from extending further into the canal space at all, and an endodontic post should be considered instead once chamber height falls below 2mm (Roberson et al, 2006, citing Kane et al.). A genuinely useful variant fills the chamber first with glass-ionomer cement as a core, into which slots or an amalgapin channel are then cut — this preserves future endodontic-retreatment access that direct canal-space extension would otherwise compromise (Roberson et al, 2006). This 2-4mm figure is independently corroborated by both Sikri's (the pulp chamber plus 2.0-4.0mm of each canal filled with amalgam, explicitly naming the amalgapin as a secondary retentive device in this context) and Pickard's (packing the coronally flared 2-3mm of endodontically treated root canals) (Ricketts, ed., 2011; Banerjee and Watson, eds., 2015). Amalgam used as a core material in this context has its own real advantage/disadvantage profile, corroborated independently by two sources: advantages include reduced marginal leakage, good dimensional stability, good compressive strength and modulus of elasticity, and (per Advanced Operative Dentistry specifically) low technique sensitivity, strength when placed in sufficient bulk, compatibility with the bonded-amalgam technique, easy visual distinction from tooth structure, and good packability with few voids when condensed well; disadvantages include low tensile strength, a real risk of galvanic corrosion against other metals already present (e.g. a crown), long setting time with low initial strength, being weak in thin section, mercury content being a genuine concern to some patients, and amalgam's lack of inherent adhesiveness (it still needs mechanical retention) (Ricketts, ed., 2011, citing Sikri's Post & Core chapter and its own "Cores" chapter). A genuinely practical handling disadvantage specific to amalgam cores: the core is more easily displaced during matrix-band removal than a composite core is, and if amalgam is used, placing an orthodontic band or a copper band left in place while the amalgam sets is worth considering as a direct fix (Ricketts, ed., 2011). Pin-generated stress specifically within a core can also be reduced by coating the pin with an adhesive such as Panavia or 4-META before insertion, and a practical pin-NUMBER ceiling is worth citing: use one pin per missing cusp or marginal ridge, up to a maximum of four (Ricketts, ed., 2011) — tighter than the generic "one pin per missing axial line angle" rule already given above.

### Clinical safety: perforation risk, insertion protocol, and correct technique
A real, named anatomic danger map for pin placement exists and is worth citing directly rather than relying only on the generic DEJ/surface-clearance rule: external-perforation risk is concentrated at the prominent mesial concavity of the maxillary first premolar and at the midlingual/midfacial bifurcations of the mandibular first and second molars and the midfacial/midmesial/middistal furcations of the maxillary first and second molars; pulpal-PENETRATION risk is concentrated at the mesiofacial corner of both the maxillary and mandibular first molars; and angulation hazards include distal root flaring of mandibular molars and palatal root flaring of maxillary molars (Roberson et al, 2006). A real insertion-safety protocol follows: rubber dam isolation is used specifically to prevent a dislodged pin being swallowed or aspirated; where rubber dam is not feasible, gauze placed over the throat plus tying the pin wrench to a 30-35cm length of dental floss is the alternative safety measure (Roberson et al, 2006). Self-shearing pins have a built-in weak point that breaks off cleanly once the pin is fully seated; standard (non-self-shearing) pins instead need a wrench, and can be backed off a quarter turn after full seating specifically to relieve apical dentinal stress (Roberson et al, 2006). If PERFORATION is suspected — signalled by bleeding through the pin channel or a sudden loss of resistance to hand pressure (note that a radiograph can give a FALSE positive here, from simple image overlap) — management follows the same principle as any accidental pulp exposure: remove the pin, irrigate with saline or calcium hydroxide, and place a calcium hydroxide dressing (Roberson et al, 2006). A specific mechanical hazard during PIN-SHORTENING is worth naming: if a bur approaches the pin obliquely rather than perpendicularly while cutting it down, the bur's own clockwise rotation can cause the pin to unscrew counterclockwise out of its channel — the operator should stabilise the pin with a wrench, forceps, or hemostat unless cutting perpendicular; a long, narrow diamond vibrates less and is less likely to trigger this unscrewing than a standard bur (Roberson et al, 2006). Pin BENDING, where needed, must use a purpose-made fork-shaped bending tool, which places the rotation fulcrum away from the channel entrance — condensers or dentin spoons must NEVER be used to bend a pin, since their fulcrum sits directly at the channel entrance and risks cracking the dentin there (Roberson et al, 2006). Pin-channel drills must not be steam-autoclaved, since this measurably reduces their cutting efficiency; dry heat, chemical vapour, or glutaraldehyde immersion are used instead, and a drill should be withdrawn at least once during any deep (≥2mm) channel preparation to clear dentinal debris from its flutes and reduce frictional heat — though repeated withdrawal/re-entry itself risks over-enlarging the channel (Roberson et al, 2006). A drill becomes too dull for further safe use after cutting roughly 20 pinholes or fewer, signalled by the need for increasing insertion pressure (Roberson et al, 2006, citing Standlee et al.). Insertion method itself affects outcome: hand insertion with a small pin wrench lets the operator feel contact at the channel's end and back off a quarter turn (reducing dentinal-thread stripping) but is slower, while a low-speed handpiece is faster and often gives better access; wide-thread pin designs strip less often regardless of which method is used (Roberson et al, 2006).

A further, genuinely important biologic distinction between vertical pin diameters is worth citing: insertion of a larger (0.031-inch) self-threading pin produces measurably MORE dentinal craze lines than either a smaller (0.021-inch) self-threading pin or a comparably-sized (0.022-inch) friction-lock pin (Roberson et al, 2006) — and pulpal stress itself is MAXIMAL when a self-threading pin is inserted perpendicular to the pulp, a specific orientation-dependent finding (Roberson et al, 2006, citing Trabert et al.). A minimum horizontal interpin distance is also specified: 3mm for the Minikin pin and 5mm for the Minim pin (Roberson et al, 2006, citing Khera et al.) — a genuinely different axis of spacing from the vertical-staggering rule already given for 3-or-more pins. Pin-size-based retention ratios exist on a separate axis from the type-based ratios already given: the Minuta pin is approximately half as retentive as the Minim pin, and roughly a third as retentive as the Regular pin (Roberson et al, 2006). Finally, failure of a pin-retained restoration can occur at any of five distinct, classified locations — within the restoration itself, at the pin-restoration interface, within the pin, at the pin-dentin interface, or within the dentin — and failure is genuinely more likely to occur at the pin-DENTIN interface than at the pin-restoration interface (Roberson et al, 2006).

### A real, unresolved controversy on proximal retention locks
Several independent findings genuinely conflict on the value of proximal retention locks specifically, and are reported here rather than resolved into one rule: locks placed at the axiofacial/axiolingual line angles have been reported significantly superior to axiogingival grooves for strengthening the isthmus; separately, locks placed OCCLUSAL to the axiopulpal line angle have been found to give MORE resistance than conventional grooves; yet with modern high-copper amalgam specifically, locks have been reported UNNECESSARY in preparations that already include a dovetail; and separately again, locks "may not be needed" in conservative, narrow proximal boxes at all (Roberson et al, 2006, citing four separate studies). A related, creep-specific finding ties directly back to the material-science content of Q3: once an amalgam's creep value falls below 1.0% — true of modern high-copper alloys — locks have been established to be of no practical use at all (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020).

### Cusp reduction and complex-restoration definitions
A real DEFINITIONAL point worth stating: "complex amalgam restoration" historically meant any restoration involving three or more surfaces, but more recent literature redefines it instead as any restoration that replaces one or more cusps — a genuinely different, and now more commonly used, definition (Hilton et al, 2013). Cusp-reduction depth cuts are specifically quantified at 2.5mm (using half the length of a No.169L bur head, which is 5.5mm total) to ensure even, consistent amalgam thickness once a cusp is reduced for coverage — bur-head length itself must be measured before use with a Boley gauge or periodontal probe, since it varies by manufacturer (Hilton et al, 2013). The protective thickness guideline differs by cusp type: 2.5mm of amalgam over a functional (stamp) cusp of a molar or premolar is standard, while a non-functioning facial cusp of a maxillary premolar may be reduced to as little as 1.5mm specifically to preserve facial enamel for esthetics (Hilton et al, 2013). Before reducing any cusp for a complex restoration, the original cusp height/position should be registered — noted, photographed, or measured with a periodontal probe against an adjacent cusp tip or the cervical line — so the new amalgam cusp can be carved back to that same original height before rubber-dam removal (Hilton et al, 2013).

### The mechanical stress account — why pin TYPE matters beyond retention alone
Pins are explicitly NOT reinforcing agents for the amalgam's own mechanical properties — they are retentive devices only, and may actually REDUCE the bulk restorative material's properties by introducing an internal discontinuity (El-Mowafy, cited in Module 1 SubModule 6.1, citing Marzouk's). Comparing insertion stress by pin-channel-to-pin-diameter relationship: CEMENTED pins (channel larger than the pin) generate little to no insertion stress, since there is no interference fit. FRICTION-GRIP pins (channel only slightly narrower) generate the MAXIMUM stress of the three types, since both the interference fit and the impact force used to seat the pin add to total stress. THREADED pins sit at an INTERMEDIATE stress level: threading dissipates some insertion energy by actively cutting into dentin rather than displacing it by force, though stress still rises with greater pin-to-channel diameter difference, bluntER thread profiles, and more closely spaced threads. Greater remaining dentin bulk lowers stress per unit volume — directly reinforcing why the DEJ/external-surface clearance rules matter mechanically, not just as an arbitrary margin. Younger, primary dentin tolerates pin-insertion stress better than older, more mineralised, more brittle dentin, owing to its higher elastic/plastic limit.

### Slots versus pins — a risk/anatomy-driven choice, not a retention-driven one
Slots are prepared with a No. 33½ bur to a depth of approximately 0.6mm, positioned 0.5-1mm inside the DEJ. Slots are specifically indicated in short clinical crowns and in cusps reduced 2-3mm for amalgam, and are chosen preferentially where vertical walls remain (allowing opposing retention locks); pins are used more often where few or no vertical walls remain. Slots remove MORE tooth structure than pins but are LESS likely to cause dentinal microfractures or perforate the tooth — a medium-sized self-threading pin placed within 0.5mm of the pulp can provoke an inflammatory response, a risk slots do not carry. Overall retention potential between pins and slots is not significantly different, making this genuinely a risk/anatomy-driven decision rather than a retention-driven one (Roberson et al, 2006).

### A genuinely important, honest longevity finding
Complex amalgam restorations with one or more capped cusps show 72% survival at 15 years, and there is NO significant difference in 15-year survival between restorations placed WITH pins and those placed WITHOUT — though cusp-covered restorations do significantly increase fracture resistance compared with non-cusp-covered ones. Not every reduced cusp needs a pin: when only 2-3mm of occlusogingival cusp height has been removed, no pin is required at all, since sufficient tooth structure remains for conventional retention alone (Roberson et al, 2006).

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes)**

### Indications
Large/complex multi-surface amalgam restorations lacking sufficient conventional (undercut) retention form — typically after capping a reduced cusp, or when few/no vertical walls remain for conventional retention.

### Three pin types (by retentiveness and mechanism)
- **Self-threading**: most retentive (3-6× cemented); threads actively engage dentin, seats into an undersized hole.
- **Friction-locked**: tapped into hole 0.025mm smaller than pin; 2-3× cemented retention; generates MAXIMUM insertion stress.
- **Cemented**: luted into oversized hole (0.025-0.05mm larger); least retentive; least stress; rarely used now.

### TMS self-threading pin sizes
Regular (0.78mm, rarely used, highest dentinal-crack incidence) > Minim (0.61mm, backup) > Minikin (0.48mm, pin of choice, posterior) > Minuta (0.38mm, usually inadequate posteriorly).

### Placement rules
- Depth: 2mm (no benefit beyond; risk of fracturing pin/dentin if deeper).
- Number: ONE PIN per missing axial line angle (minimum effective number).
- Position: ≥0.5-1mm from DEJ OR ≥1-1.5mm from external surface (whichever greater); parallel to external surface.
- Need 2mm amalgam bulk over pin.
- ≥3 pins: stagger at different vertical levels.

### Mechanical stress principle
Pins are RETENTIVE devices only — NOT reinforcing agents; can even weaken bulk amalgam by introducing a discontinuity. Stress ranking: friction-grip (max) > threaded (intermediate, cutting dissipates energy) > cemented (min, no interference fit). More remaining dentin bulk and younger (more resilient) dentin both lower induced stress.

### Alternatives — slots, locks, coves
Slot = horizontal groove (0.6mm deep, 0.5-1mm inside DEJ, No. 33½ bur); lock = vertical groove; cove = small rounded supplementary feature (No. ¼ bur). Slots: more tooth removed but LOWER microfracture/pulpal-inflammation risk than pins; retention potential similar — choice is anatomy/risk-driven, not retention-driven.

### Longevity
Cusp-covered complex amalgams: 72% survival at 15 years; NO significant survival difference with vs. without pins — pins aid RETENTION during placement, not long-term survival per se.

### Conclusion
Pin (and slot/lock/cove) retention is selected by remaining tooth anatomy and perforation/fracture risk, not by retention potential alone — the smallest effective pin (Minikin, posteriorly), fewest pins (one per missing axial line angle), and correct 2mm depth/DEJ-clearance placement together maximise retention while minimising dentinal damage.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Retentiveness: self-threading (3-6× cemented) > friction-locked (2-3× cemented) > cemented (least).
- Stress generated: friction-grip (MAX) > threaded (intermediate) > cemented (least) — inverse of a simple "more retentive = more stress" assumption for threaded vs. friction-grip.
- TMS sizes: Regular 0.78mm (rarely used) / Minim 0.61mm (backup) / Minikin 0.48mm (posterior pin of choice) / Minuta 0.38mm (often inadequate).
- Placement: 2mm depth; one pin per missing axial line angle; ≥0.5-1mm from DEJ or ≥1-1.5mm from surface (greater); 2mm amalgam bulk over pin.
- Pins are retentive devices ONLY — not reinforcing agents; may weaken bulk amalgam.
- Slots remove more tooth structure but carry LOWER microfracture/pulpal risk than pins; similar overall retention.
- 72% survival at 15 years for cusp-covered complex amalgams; NO significant pin-vs-no-pin survival difference.
- Horizontal pins (0.5-1mm from DEJ, 1.5-2mm deep) reinforce a free-standing cusp or cross-splint two cusps — a distinct technique from vertical placement.
- Amalgapins (Shavell, 1980) and dentin pins are named non-metallic/non-standard-pin retention features, distinct from slots/locks/coves.
- Pulpal-chamber extension of 2-4mm is only beneficial when chamber height ≤2mm; no benefit gained extending further when chamber height is already 4-6mm.
- Pin-number ceiling (per Advanced Operative Dentistry): one pin per missing cusp/marginal ridge, up to a MAXIMUM of four.
- Perforation signs: bleeding through the channel, sudden loss of resistance to hand pressure — radiographs can give a false positive.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Stating pins "reinforce" the amalgam — they are retentive only, and can weaken it.
2. Reversing the stress ranking (assuming threaded pins, being most retentive, also generate the most stress — friction-grip actually generates more).
3. Recommending Regular (largest) TMS pins for posterior teeth — Minikin is preferred specifically to reduce crack/perforation risk.
4. Omitting the DEJ/external-surface clearance rule (whichever distance is GREATER) — a frequently tested numeric detail.
5. Treating slots and pins as interchangeable on retention grounds alone, rather than as an anatomy/risk-driven choice.

## MNEMONIC
"**Mini wins** posteriorly" — Minikin is the pin of choice in severely involved posterior teeth, not the largest (Regular) size.
