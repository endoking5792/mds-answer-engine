## STUDY MODE

### Creep
**DEFINITION — Creep**: time-dependent plastic (permanent) deformation of a material under a constant, sustained static stress, below its yield strength. ANSI/ADA Specification No. 1 quantifies this directly: a 7-day-old cylindrical amalgam specimen is stressed at 36MPa in a 37°C environment, and creep is measured between the 1st and 4th hour of stressing; the maximum permissible value is 3% (Craig, 2002, cited in Module 3 SubModule 8.2).

Low-copper amalgam shows measurably GREATER creep than high-copper amalgam under identical load/time conditions (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) — typical clinical ranges are roughly 1-8% for low-copper alloy against only 0.1-1.0% for high-copper alloy, materially wider bands than the single 3% ADA ceiling (Hilton et al, 2013). Creep becomes clinically significant specifically because amalgam's EQUICOHESIVE TEMPERATURE (the point above which grain boundaries switch from barriers to dislocation pathways) lies below mouth temperature; a concrete ratio worth citing is that a 53%-mercury mix creeps roughly 1.5 times as much as a 48%-mercury mix (Marzouk, 1985). On pure mechanical weakness (distinct from corrosion resistance), mercury itself is the single weakest constituent phase and γ2 is the second weakest — and a porosity level of only 1% has been found to be as damaging to strength as 10% excess mercury (Marzouk, 1985). This is clinically significant, not merely a laboratory curiosity: greater creep allows the restoration's margins to flow slowly outward over time, producing thin, unsupported marginal ledges that readily fracture under occlusal load — fracture that in turn opens a pathway for secondary (recurrent) caries. Creep is therefore a real, independent, material-science contributor to low-copper amalgam's clinical decline, separate from (though compounding) the corrosion susceptibility discussed below. **A unit-level inconsistency is worth flagging rather than silently resolving**: the ANSI/ADA creep test is specified as a 36MPa STRESS by most sources, but one source instead describes the identical test as a static 36-NEWTON load — stress and force are not interchangeable units, and this is reported here as a textual inconsistency between sources rather than corrected silently (Bhat et al, 2019).

### Corrosion
**DEFINITION — Corrosion vs. Tarnish**: tarnish is a SURFACE-limited chemical reaction between the metal and the oral environment, producing discoloration without penetrating the body of the restoration; corrosion is a deeper chemical reaction that penetrates INTO the metal itself and, unlike tarnish, can eventually cause outright restoration failure (Powers and Wataha, 2013). Both occur more readily on a rough surface, making thorough finishing and polishing a genuine corrosion-prevention measure, not just a cosmetic one.

**Mechanisms of corrosion in amalgam**: (i) a real internal GALVANIC cell exists within set low-copper amalgam between the γ1 and γ2 phases themselves, since the two phases have different electrochemical potentials — γ2 is anodic and corrodes significantly faster than γ1 purely because the two are in electrical contact with each other (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). High-copper amalgam, which eliminates γ2, removes this internal galvanic couple entirely — a direct mechanistic explanation for its superior corrosion resistance. A genuinely distinct, EXTERNAL galvanic mechanism also exists: direct contact between an amalgam restoration and a gold restoration generates a large potential difference that measurably accelerates the amalgam's corrosion; this same external-coupling principle generalises to any dissimilar-metal contact, including between high- and low-copper amalgams themselves (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Bhat et al, 2019). (ii) CREVICE corrosion occurs at any oxygen-depleted space (an open margin or incompletely sealed interface): the crevice base becomes anodic and progressively loses material, while the exposed surface acts as the cathode; corrosion products accumulating within the crevice further restrict oxygen supply, making this a self-sustaining, progressive process once started (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). (iii) BIOCORROSION is a modern, biofilm-specific mechanism: bacterial metabolic activity creates a local acidic, oxygen-depleted concentration cell, accelerating attack specifically at surface pits and cracks (Ionescu and Hahnel, 2021). (iv) CORROSION FATIGUE is a distinct, two-stage mechanism in which chemical attack first initiates surface cracks, and cyclic mechanical (masticatory) loading then drives crack propagation to failure (Bhat et al, 2019).

**Corrosion products differ chemically by alloy type**, a genuinely testable distinction: low-copper amalgam's γ2-driven corrosion forms tin oxychloride, whose subsequent dissolution produces porosity; high-copper amalgam's corrosion instead forms a basic copper chloride, CuCl₂·3Cu(OH)₂, with η (Cu₆Sn₅) identified as the least corrosion-resistant phase specifically within high-copper amalgam (Bhat et al, 2019). Corrosion itself measurably reduces amalgam's strength, by up to 5% or more (Bhat et al, 2019), and corrosion products migrating into the permeable dentinal tubules can cause a visible intrinsic tooth discoloration, particularly troublesome in anterior teeth and upper premolars (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

A complete corrosion-resistance ranking across every phase present, most to least resistant: γ1 (Ag₂Hg₃) > γ (Ag₃Sn) > Ag₃Cu₂ > ε (Cu₃Sn) > η′ (Cu₆Sn₅) > γ2 (Sn₇₋₈Hg) — confirming γ2 as the single weakest link (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Average corrosion depth is 100-500µm from the margin, though the salivary protein pellicle and phosphate buffering genuinely retard this process; amalgams retrieved after 2-25 years in service showed bulk composition close to freshly prepared material, aside from minor chloride contamination and internal amalgamation of unconsumed γ particles (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). A directly quantified compressive-strength triad by phase (using the alternative "λ" phase-labelling convention some sources use for γ) is worth citing: λ (Ag-Sn) = 4923kg/cm², λ1 (Ag-Hg, i.e. γ1) = 1758kg/cm², and λ2 (Sn-Hg, i.e. γ2) = only 703kg/cm² — confirming γ2 as "the most fragile phase," forming a continuous web through which corrosion spreads (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020).

### A genuinely nuanced point: corrosion is not simply "bad" for amalgam
Real clinically-retrieved-restoration evidence (Marshall et al., 1987) found zinc-rich and tin-rich corrosion-product layers forming at the tooth-amalgam interface over years in service — and, counterintuitively, zinc corrosion products forming within a marginal gap have been reported to REDUCE marginal fracture, by partially sealing the very gap they form in (Anusavice et al, 2013). A separate controlled trial (Letzel et al., 1989) found that bulk fracture or fracture at the marginal ridge — not corrosion itself — was the leading mode of amalgam failure (Anusavice et al, 2013); Sturdevant's refines this further specifically for HIGH-copper amalgam, stating directly that its fracture is usually a BULK fracture rather than a marginal one — a more granular distinction than the general low-copper-era finding (Roberson et al, 2006). Corrosion in amalgam is therefore genuinely double-edged: it is simultaneously the basis of amalgam's clinically valuable marginal self-sealing, and, via the weak γ2 phase, a real long-term contributor to marginal breakdown. A direct engineering consequence of this trade-off is worth naming: because modern high-copper alloys corrode LESS than older low-copper alloys, they also self-seal the tooth-amalgam interface less effectively — the "sealed amalgam" technique, coating the interface with a flowable resin or dentine-bonding agent, is a real clinical response specifically developed to compensate for this reduced natural sealing in high-copper restorations (Banerjee and Watson, eds., 2015).

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes)**

### Creep
Time-dependent plastic deformation under sustained static stress. ANSI/ADA Spec. No. 1: 7-day specimen, 36MPa, 37°C, measured 1-4h, MAX 3%. Low-Cu > high-Cu creep. Clinical link: creep → thin marginal ledges → fracture → secondary caries. An independent material-science contributor to low-Cu amalgam's clinical decline.

### Corrosion vs. tarnish
Tarnish = surface-limited discoloration, does not usually cause failure. Corrosion = penetrates into the metal body, CAN cause failure. Both worsened by a rough surface — polishing genuinely helps both.

### Mechanisms
- **Internal galvanic cell** (γ1-γ2, low-Cu amalgam only): γ2 anodic, corrodes faster; eliminated in high-Cu amalgam (no γ2) — explains high-Cu's superior resistance.
- **Crevice corrosion**: O₂-depleted gap/margin → crevice base anodic, self-sustaining once started.
- **Biocorrosion**: biofilm-driven local acidic/anoxic cell, accelerated at pits/cracks.
- **Corrosion fatigue**: chemical attack initiates cracks + cyclic load propagates them (two-stage).

### Corrosion-resistance ranking (most→least resistant)
γ1 (Ag₂Hg₃) > γ (Ag₃Sn) > Ag₃Cu₂ > ε (Cu₃Sn) > η′ (Cu₆Sn₅) > γ2 (Sn₇₋₈Hg). γ2 = weakest link. Average corrosion depth 100-500µm; saliva/pellicle retard it; 2-25-year retrieved amalgams show stable bulk composition.

### The double-edged nature of corrosion
Zinc corrosion products at margins can REDUCE marginal fracture (self-sealing) — yet bulk/marginal-ridge fracture (not corrosion per se) is the leading real-world failure mode (Letzel et al., 1989). Corrosion is therefore both beneficial (sealing) and detrimental (via γ2), depending on which phase and location is involved.

### Conclusion
Creep and corrosion are mechanistically linked through the same weak γ2 phase — both are minimised by eliminating γ2 via high-copper alloy formulation, which is the single most important material-science reason high-copper amalgam has replaced low-copper amalgam in modern clinical practice. A candidate should remember that neither property is purely destructive: corrosion in particular is genuinely double-edged, contributing both to amalgam's valued self-sealing margin behaviour and, via γ2, to its long-term marginal breakdown — a nuance worth stating explicitly for full marks.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Treating creep and corrosion as unrelated — both trace back to the same weak γ2 phase.
2. Describing corrosion as purely harmful, missing the genuinely beneficial self-sealing zinc-corrosion-product finding.
3. Confusing tarnish (surface, usually harmless) with corrosion (penetrating, can cause failure).
4. Omitting the exact ANSI/ADA creep test parameters (36MPa/37°C/1-4h/max 3%).
5. Forgetting that fracture, not corrosion itself, is the leading documented clinical failure mode.

## MNEMONIC
"**Creep Crawls, Corrosion Cores**" — creep slowly deforms the surface margin; corrosion eats into the core (body) of the metal.
