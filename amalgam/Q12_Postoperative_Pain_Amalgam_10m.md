## STUDY MODE

### Why this deserves its own answer, not just a line under "failures"
Postoperative pain following amalgam placement is mechanistically distinct from the LONG-TERM failure modes already covered under Q9 (caries, fracture, marginal breakdown) — it presents in the IMMEDIATE postoperative period (hours to a few days) and, critically, several of its causes are entirely preventable with correct technique rather than being inherent material limitations. A structured, named four-category framework is worth citing directly: postoperative pain following amalgam restoration is attributable to (I) hyperocclusion, (II) a cracked tooth, (III) galvanism, and (IV) inadequate pulp protection or delayed expansion — a genuinely useful organising scheme distinct from the separate "faulty preparation / poor matrix adaptation / faulty manipulation" causes of long-term FAILURE already covered in Q9 (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020).

### 1. Hyperocclusion
A restoration left even slightly "high" concentrates excessive occlusal force on that tooth with every closure, producing inflammation of the apical periodontal ligament — the classic presentation is pain specifically on BITING, often appearing within hours of placement. This is directly preventable and correctable: checking and adjusting occlusion with articulating paper immediately after placement, verified with thin (12µm) shimstock to confirm simultaneous contact with adjacent teeth, resolves it (cross-referenced to the occlusal-adjustment protocol already covered in Q8).

### 2. Galvanism
Galvanism results from a difference in electrical potential between dissimilar metallic restorations in opposing or adjacent teeth. Each restoration, together with saliva acting as an electrolyte, forms an electric cell; when two opposing dissimilar metal restorations physically contact each other, the cell short-circuits, and if the resulting current passes through the pulp, the patient feels a sharp, transient pain — the more anodic (less noble) of the two restorations may also corrode as a result (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, cited in Module 3 SubModule 2.1). Even a SINGLE restoration, together with saliva alone, can form a liquid-junction-type cell, since ions migrate readily through dentin and around a restoration's margins. A real, quantified explanation for why some patients feel this and others do not: the MAGNITUDE of the current is not the primary determinant of pain — individual patient sensitivity is. Most patients feel pain somewhere between 20-50 microamps, but the documented range spans as low as 10 microamps for the most sensitive patients to as high as 110 microamps for the least sensitive, an 11-fold spread that directly explains why two patients with structurally similar dissimilar-metal restorations can have completely different galvanic experiences (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). A measured galvanic series of dental alloys in artificial saliva at 35°C ranks materials by voltage — conventional spherical amalgam (-0.023V) and dispersed high-copper amalgam (-0.108V), through nickel-chromium (-0.126 to -0.240V) and cobalt-chromium (-0.292V), to gold alloys (-0.345 to -0.455V) — and the further apart two materials sit on this series, the greater the potential difference driving a galvanic cell between them, which is precisely why the clinical prevention strategy is to avoid placing widely-separated materials from this series into direct occlusal contact (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). A genuinely distinct, SECOND form of galvanism is also worth citing: galvanic pain can arise even WITHIN a single restoration, from an uneven distribution of silver/mercury concentration caused by poor condensation technique, rather than requiring two separate restorations at all (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020).

### 3. Cracked tooth / differential thermal response
A real, distinct mechanism: amalgam's own coefficient of thermal expansion differs genuinely from tooth structure's, so differential expansion and contraction under a temperature change (hot or cold food/drink) can itself generate pain, particularly where an underlying, possibly undetected crack in the tooth is present — biting load transmitted unevenly across a cracked tooth produces a characteristic SHARP pain specifically on RELEASE of biting pressure, distinct from the more constant ache of hyperocclusion (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020).

### 4. Delayed expansion
Covered in full mechanistic detail in Q1 §4 and Q11 — in zinc-containing amalgam, trapped moisture is electrolysed, producing gas that builds internal pressure and causes a dramatic, delayed (typically days-later) expansion; this is "peculiar to zinc-containing alloys" specifically (Torres and Zanatta, eds., 2020) and is cross-referenced here rather than re-derived, since the mechanism, magnitude, and prevention are identical to the content already built for those two topics.

### 5. Inadequate pulp protection
Amalgam's high thermal conductivity transmits temperature change directly through the restoration to the pulp if no intervening liner or base is placed in a deep preparation, producing real thermal pain — this is why the effective-depth classification and base/liner protocol already covered in Q1 §5b (≥2mm: varnish alone; 1-2mm: modified ZOE base; ≤1mm: calcium hydroxide or ZOE subbase depending on pulp history) exists in the first place (Marzouk, 1985). A further, related irritant mechanism from the same source: condensation-energy trauma to odontoblasts during placement, and heat generated during finishing/polishing (risking local pulpal burns and, via anachoresis, bacterial seeding of the pulp) can both independently contribute to postoperative pain, even where the preparation depth itself was adequately protected (Marzouk, 1985).

### Diagnostic approach — distinguishing the causes
A rigorous diagnostic approach distinguishes these causes by their PRESENTATION rather than assuming a single default cause: pain specifically on biting, appearing within hours, suggests hyperocclusion (check with articulating paper/shimstock first); a sharp, transient pain specifically on dissimilar-metal CONTACT suggests galvanism; pain on hot/cold stimuli in a deep restoration suggests inadequate pulp protection; pain appearing progressively over several DAYS suggests delayed expansion (radiograph may show restoration bulging); and pain specifically on RELEASE of biting pressure, especially with a history of heavy mastication, suggests an underlying crack rather than the restoration itself.

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes)**

### A named four-category framework
Postoperative pain after amalgam placement: (I) hyperocclusion, (II) cracked tooth, (III) galvanism, (IV) inadequate pulp protection/delayed expansion — distinct from Q9's long-term FAILURE causes (preparation/matrix/manipulation errors).

### Hyperocclusion
High restoration → apical periodontal inflammation → pain on BITING, within hours. Diagnose/correct with articulating paper + 12µm shimstock.

### Galvanism
Dissimilar metal restorations (opposing/adjacent) + saliva electrolyte → electric cell → contact short-circuits the cell → current through pulp → sharp transient pain; more anodic restoration may also corrode. Pain threshold is patient-specific, NOT current-magnitude-specific: most feel pain at 20-50 microamps, range 10-110 microamps (11-fold spread). Galvanic series (most to least noble): gold alloys > cobalt-chromium > nickel-chromium > high-copper amalgam > conventional amalgam — avoid placing widely-separated materials in direct contact. A SECOND form: galvanism WITHIN a single restoration from uneven silver/mercury distribution (poor condensation), not requiring a second restoration at all.

### Cracked tooth / differential thermal response
Amalgam's CTE differs from tooth structure → differential expansion/contraction under thermal change → pain, especially with an underlying crack. Characteristic sign: sharp pain on RELEASE of biting pressure (distinct from hyperocclusion's constant-bite pain).

### Delayed expansion
Zinc-containing alloy + trapped moisture → electrolysis → gas → pressure → pain appearing days later (see Q1/Q11 for full mechanism). No fix once occurred — remove and replace.

### Inadequate pulp protection
High thermal conductivity + no liner/base in deep preparation → thermal pain. Prevention: effective-depth-based liner/base protocol (≥2mm varnish; 1-2mm modified ZOE base; ≤1mm Ca(OH)₂ or ZOE subbase per pulp history). Condensation-energy trauma and finishing/polishing heat are further, independent contributors.

### Diagnostic distinction
Pain on biting (hours) → hyperocclusion. Pain on metal contact → galvanism. Pain on hot/cold in deep prep → pulp protection. Pain progressive over days → delayed expansion. Pain on release of pressure → cracked tooth.

### Conclusion
Postoperative pain after amalgam placement has several genuinely distinct causes, most of them preventable with correct technique (occlusal check, avoiding dissimilar-metal contact, depth-appropriate liner/base, dry field for zinc-containing alloy) — a candidate should diagnose by PRESENTATION pattern rather than assuming a single default mechanism, and should distinguish this topic clearly from Q9's separate long-term failure-mode content.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Four-category framework: hyperocclusion, cracked tooth, galvanism, inadequate pulp protection/delayed expansion.
- Galvanism pain threshold is patient-specific (10-110 microamps, most 20-50) — NOT simply proportional to current magnitude.
- Galvanic series: gold alloys (most noble) > Co-Cr > Ni-Cr > high-Cu amalgam > conventional amalgam (least noble) — avoid wide separations in direct contact.
- A SECOND galvanism mechanism exists WITHIN a single restoration (uneven Ag/Hg distribution from poor condensation) — doesn't need two restorations.
- Cracked-tooth pain is characteristically on RELEASE of biting pressure, distinct from hyperocclusion's pain ON biting.
- Delayed expansion is "peculiar to zinc-containing alloys" specifically — cross-reference Q1/Q11 for full mechanism.
- Effective-depth classification governs liner/base need: ≥2mm varnish only; 1-2mm modified ZOE base; ≤1mm Ca(OH)₂/ZOE subbase.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Conflating this topic with Q9's long-term FAILURE causes (preparation/matrix/manipulation errors) — postoperative pain is an immediate, often preventable, distinct phenomenon.
2. Assuming galvanic pain intensity is proportional to the voltage/current generated — patient sensitivity, not current magnitude, is the real determinant.
3. Forgetting that galvanism can occur WITHIN a single restoration (uneven condensation), not only between two separate restorations.
4. Omitting the diagnostic distinction between pain ON biting (hyperocclusion) versus pain on RELEASE of biting pressure (cracked tooth).
5. Treating delayed expansion as if it applies to all amalgam — it is specifically a zinc-containing-alloy phenomenon.
