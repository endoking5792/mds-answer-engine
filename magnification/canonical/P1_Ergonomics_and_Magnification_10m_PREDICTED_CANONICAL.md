## Explanation

Ergonomics comes from Greek *ergon* (work) and *nomoi* (natural laws). Working with the head bent forward causes Tension Neck syndrome; neck load doubles with every 2-3 cm of forward head shift; loupes allow less than 25° of head tilt, while fixed loupes still need 20-40° and the microscope near neutral (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Upright neutral posture with the eyes at infinity (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; parallel Galilean beams versus convergence strain on the rectus muscles (Rubinstein, 2017; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]. Double S-curve; bending collapses the diaphragm (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Seating and positions: thighs parallel to the floor; 12 o'clock, or 7-12 o'clock zones for right-handers (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; 12 o'clock maxilla and 8-9 o'clock mandible, more than 50 cm away, arm-supported chair, procedures over 2 hours, four- and six-handed work (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Inclinable binoculars (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; shallow viewing angles make the neck tilt (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]; six positioning factors and two assistants (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]; positioning difficulty is the commonest reason for abandoning the microscope (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Ergonomics (Greek *ergon*, work, and *nomoi*, natural laws) adapts work to the human body. Working without magnification forces the head forward. Neck and shoulder load doubles with every 2-3 cm the head moves forward, and the result is Tension Neck syndrome: headache, interscapular pain and, later, cervical spondylosis (Sikri, 2019).

**Effect of each aid on posture (Sikri, 2019):**

| Aid | Head position |
|---|---|
| None | Marked forward flexion |
| Loupes (fixed, TTL) | 20-40° forward in Sikri's table; Sikri's text says loupes allow under 25° |
| Procedure scope | Near neutral |
| Microscope | Near neutral |

**Why the microscope is ergonomic:**
1. **Upright, neutral, balanced posture** with minimal neck, shoulder and back fatigue (Sikri, 2019). The spine keeps its double S-curve, and avoiding constant bending keeps the diaphragm free (Rubinstein, 2017).
2. **Eyes at rest:** Galilean optics send parallel beams to each eye. Loupes need convergence, which strains the rectus muscles (Rubinstein, 2017; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020).
3. **Inclinable binoculars** adapt to the operator; shallow viewing angles force the neck to tilt (Gopikrishna, 2021; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020).

**Correct set-up:**
- Seat the operator first: thighs parallel to the floor, feet flat, shoulders straight, elbows close. Then adjust the microscope to the eyes, and finally the patient to the hands (Sikri, 2019).
- **Positions:** Sikri (2019) uses 12 o'clock for almost all teeth (7-12 o'clock zones for right-handers). Baumann and Beer (2010) use 12 o'clock for maxillary and 8-9 o'clock for mandibular teeth, more than 50 cm from the patient.
- A chair with arm supports gives vibration-free work, and procedures over 2 hours become comfortable (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Team ergonomics.** Four-handed work in the mandible and six-handed work in the maxilla (Baumann and Beer, 2010). In surgery, two assistants are used and position is set by six factors (Rubinstein, 2017).

**Conclusion.** Difficulty in positioning is the commonest reason clinicians abandon the microscope (Sikri, 2019). Correct set-up is therefore as important as the optics.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Load doubles per 2-3 cm of forward head shift; Tension Neck syndrome.
- Loupes 20-40° head tilt; microscope neutral.
- Seat yourself, then the microscope, then the patient.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Saying loupes give a neutral posture (they reduce, but do not remove, head tilt).
- Adjusting the operator to the patient instead of the patient to the operator.
