## 1. Introduction and rationale

### Explanation

The unaided eye resolves about 0.2 mm; two lines closer than this are seen as one (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri tabulates resolution falling to 80 µm with 2.5× loupes, 50 µm with 6× loupes and 10 µm with a microscope at 20×, and adds that a microscope at 10× gives 100 times the visual information of the naked eye, because information scales with area, not linear size (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's describes root canal work as work "inside a blind space" done by touch and radiographs (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer cite Zaugg (2004) that the dental microscope provides for optimal clinical diagnosis (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Dentistry is a discipline of precision, but the unaided human eye resolves only about 0.2 mm (200 µm): two lines closer than this are seen as one (Sikri, 2019). Much of what decides the outcome of treatment lies below this limit: incipient caries, enamel cracks, a calcified orifice, an isthmus on a resected root, an open margin. Root canal treatment in particular has long been described as work "inside a blind space", carried out by tactile sense and radiographs (Gopikrishna, 2021).

The dental operating microscope (DOM), also called the surgical operating microscope (SOM), combines three things: variable magnification, coaxial shadow-free illumination and an ergonomic upright working position. It turns a blind procedure into a visual one.

**Resolution with different aids (Sikri, 2019):**

| Aid | Magnification | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Unaided eye | none | 200 µm |
| Low-power loupes | 2.5× | 80 µm |
| High-power loupes | 6× | 50 µm |
| Microscope, high | 20× | 10 µm |

Sikri adds that a microscope at 10× gives about 100 times the visual information of the naked eye, because information grows with area, not just with linear size. Baumann and Beer (2010), citing Zaugg (2004), state that the dental microscope provides for an optimal clinical diagnosis.

## 2. History

### Explanation

The books disagree on several dates, so each is shown with its source. Rubinstein and Baumann and Beer both describe Nylen's 1921 ear-surgery microscope as monocular; Bharti and Bhasin call it binocular (Rubinstein, 2017; Baumann and Beer, 2010; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]. Rubinstein records the Zeiss-Holmgren binocular microscope of 1922, Saemisch's binocular loupes of 1876, Jako's use of the SOM in oral surgery in 1962, and Galilean (parallel) optics versus Greenough (convergent) optics that cause eye fatigue (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

For the first dental use: Baumann and Beer say "In 1975, Baumann described the" first use in dental practice; Rubinstein and Lins et al give 1977 (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Rubinstein, 2017; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Rubinstein and Bharti and Bhasin give 1978 for Apotheker and Jako's dental microscope (Rubinstein says development began then; Bharti and Bhasin say it was introduced then), while Sikri and Grossman's date its introduction to 1981 (Rubinstein, 2017; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020; Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Rubinstein's own reference list includes two Apotheker and Jako papers of 1981, which suggests 1978 is the start of development and 1981 the publication [Likely]. Apotheker coined "microdentistry"; the DentiScope had a single ×8 magnification; Sikri credits Chivian with an 8× microscope in endodontics; Reuben and Apotheker first reported apical surgery with it in 1984 (Rubinstein, 2017; Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Endodontics: Pecora gave the first AAE presentation on the SOM in surgery (1990); Rubinstein and Carr began using medical-grade microscopes for apical surgery in 1990; Grossman's dates Carr's ergonomic DOM with Galilean optics to 1991; Lins et al date Carr's first publication to 1992; Sikri calls Carr the "Father of Microscopic Endodontics" and quotes "You cannot treat what you cannot see" (Rubinstein, 2017; Gopikrishna, 2021; Lins et al, 2016; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The first symposium followed in March 1993; Shanelec and Tibbetts presented "Microscope-Assisted Precision Dentistry" in 1994 (Rubinstein, 2017; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]. On mandatory teaching, Rubinstein says the Commission met in January 1996 and the standards took effect in January 1997, the term later amended to "magnification"; Lins et al place the ADA mandate in early 1998 (Rubinstein, 2017; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Use rose from 52% (Mines, 1999) to 90% (Kersten, 2008), as reported by Rubinstein; Grossman's states every endodontist is now expected to learn and use a DOM (Rubinstein, 2017; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Medicine.** Simple binocular loupes were introduced to surgery by Saemisch in 1876 (Rubinstein, 2017). Nylen (1921) used a microscope for ear surgery; Rubinstein (2017) and Baumann and Beer (2010) describe it as monocular, whereas Bharti and Bhasin (2020) call it binocular. In 1922 Zeiss, with Holmgren, produced a binocular operating microscope. The key optical step was Galilean optics, which focus at infinity and send parallel beams to each eye, unlike convergent (Greenough) laboratory microscopes, which cause eye fatigue (Rubinstein, 2017).

**Dentistry (dates differ between books):**

| Year | Event | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1962 | Jako used the SOM in oral surgery | Rubinstein, 2017 |
| 1975 | Baumann described the microscope in dental practice | Baumann and Beer, 2010 |
| 1977 | Baumann's report on the otologic microscope in dentistry | Rubinstein, 2017; Lins et al, 2016 |
| 1978 | Apotheker and Jako: dental microscope (development began / introduced) | Rubinstein, 2017; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020 |
| 1981 | Apotheker: introduction of the DOM | Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021 |
| 1980s | DentiScope, single ×8 magnification; Apotheker coined "microdentistry" | Rubinstein, 2017 |
| 1984 | Reuben and Apotheker: first use in apical surgery | Rubinstein, 2017 |

The 1978 and 1981 dates probably mark the start of development and the published reports respectively.

**Endodontics.** Pecora gave the first AAE presentation on the SOM in surgery (1990), and Rubinstein and Carr began using medical-grade microscopes for apical surgery in 1990 (Rubinstein, 2017). Carr introduced the ergonomic DOM with Galilean optics in 1991 (Gopikrishna, 2021); his first publication is dated 1992 (Lins et al, 2016). Sikri (2019) calls Carr the "Father of Microscopic Endodontics": "You cannot treat what you cannot see". The first symposium on microscopic endodontic surgery was held in March 1993, and in 1994 Shanelec and Tibbetts presented "Microscope-Assisted Precision Dentistry" (Rubinstein, 2017; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020).

**Accreditation.** Rubinstein (2017): the Commission on Dental Accreditation adopted mandatory microscopy teaching in January 1996, effective January 1997, later broadened to "magnification". Lins et al (2016) place the ADA mandate in early 1998. Microscope use among endodontists rose from 52% (1999) to 90% (2008), and every endodontist is now expected to use a DOM (Rubinstein, 2017; Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 3. Optical principles and terms

### Explanation

Sikri and Rubinstein explain that the DOM's Galilean optics form the image at infinity, so the eyes do not converge, while loupes rely on convergent vision (Sikri, 2019; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Bharti and Bhasin add that convergence strains the internal and external rectus muscles (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]. As magnification increases, field of view and depth of field fall (Gopikrishna, 2021; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. "Usable power" is the highest magnification usable for a given depth and field (Rubinstein, 2017; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; with variable magnification this is not limiting, since the operator simply steps down (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri defines declination angle; Bharti and Bhasin say the shallower the viewing angle, the more the neck tilts, and that interpupillary distance varies with each person (Sikri, 2019; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Galilean (parallel) optics.** The DOM forms its image at infinity, so the eyes look straight ahead as if at a distant object; there is no convergence and eye strain is minimal (Sikri, 2019; Rubinstein, 2017). Loupes rely on convergent vision; convergence loads the internal and external rectus muscles and is a source of eye fatigue (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020).

**Key terms:**
- **Magnification:** the increase in image size.
- **Resolution:** the smallest separation seen as two points.
- **Field of view:** the area seen; it falls as magnification rises.
- **Depth of field:** the range in focus from near to far; it also falls as magnification rises (Gopikrishna, 2021; Lins et al, 2016).
- **Working distance:** from the lens (or the eye, for loupes) to the field.
- **Usable power:** the highest magnification usable in a given situation relative to depth of field and field of view (Rubinstein, 2017; Sikri, 2019).
- **Convergence angle:** the angle at which the two oculars meet on the object.
- **Declination angle:** the downward inclination of the operator's eyes towards the work.
- **Viewing angle:** the shallower it is, the more the neck must tilt (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020).
- **Interpupillary distance (IPD):** varies with each person and must be set first.

**The trade-off.** Magnification up means a smaller field and less depth; magnification down means a larger field and more depth. With loupes this is fixed. With the microscope it is not limiting, because the operator can step down whenever the field becomes too narrow (Rubinstein, 2017). Hence the clinical rule: orient at low power, work at mid power, inspect at high power.

## 4. Parts of the dental operating microscope

### Explanation

Lins et al list the base, optical head, light source, engine (motorised zoom and focus) and light splitter; Grossman's lists four body parts, namely eyepiece, binoculars, magnification changer and objective lens (Lins et al, 2016; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Mounting: floor, wall or ceiling (or bench) on a counterbalanced arm; a vibration-free site is an absolute requirement, and cheap supports vibrate (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Eyepieces: Grossman's 10×, 12.5×, 16× and 20× (10× and 12.5× commonest); Sikri starts at 6.3×; diopters −5 to +5 (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Inclinable binoculars are recommended, rotating 0-220 degrees and beyond; longer binocular focal length means more magnification and a narrower field (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Changers come as 3-, 5- or 6-step or power zoom; zoom avoids the visual jump of step changers (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Objective: 100-400 mm; 200 mm ≈ 8 inches recommended by Sikri and used by many surgeons (Rubinstein); Grossman's lists 200, 250 and 300 mm as commonest and describes varifocal 200-300 mm lenses; Lins et al advise the longest focal length the operator's height permits; a 250 mm lens gives lower magnification than a 200 mm one (Sikri, 2019; Rubinstein, 2017; Gopikrishna, 2021; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Rubinstein's worked example: 160 mm binoculars, ×10 eyepieces, 200 mm objective, changer factors 0.5-2.0 give ×4 to ×16 (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri's endodontic set-up is a 12.5× eyepiece, 125 mm inclined binocular, 200 mm objective and a 5-step changer of 4×-28× (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Light: Sikri lists xenon (brighter, daylight-like), metal halide and quartz halogen (yellow); Grossman's says powerful LEDs are now used; Lins et al list halogen, LED, xenon or metal halide, ideally two lamps; Baumann and Beer prefer xenon at about 5800 K and 420 klx against halogen's 100 klx (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021; Lins et al, 2016; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Illumination is coaxial, with the light path and line of sight similar, and the split return beams give stereopsis; the SOM's light is two to three times a headlamp's, but the field darkens at high magnification (Gopikrishna, 2021; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. A 50:50 beam splitter feeds cameras or an assistant's binocular, and the assistant then sees exactly what the operator sees (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Lins et al (2016) describe the DOM as the base, the optical head, the light source, the engine (motorised zoom and focus) and the light splitter. Grossman's lists four optical parts of the body: eyepiece, binoculars, magnification changer and objective lens (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**A. Support (mounting).** Floor stand, wall, ceiling or bench mount on a counterbalanced arm (Gopikrishna, 2021). A vibration-free site is an absolute requirement at high magnification, and cheap supports vibrate; mechanical or electromagnetic brakes hold the head in position (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**B. Optical head:**
1. **Eyepieces:** 10×, 12.5×, 16× and 20×, of which 10× and 12.5× are most used (Gopikrishna, 2021); Sikri (2019) also lists 6.3×. Diopter settings of −5 to +5 correct for accommodation and refractive error; the rubber cups are folded down for spectacle wearers.
2. **Binoculars:** hold the eyepieces and set the IPD. They come straight, inclined (45°) or inclinable; inclinable binoculars are recommended for endodontics and surgery (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) and rotate from 0 to 220 degrees and beyond (Rubinstein, 2017). A longer binocular focal length gives more magnification and a narrower field.
3. **Magnification changer:** 3-, 5- or 6-step manual turrets, or manual or power zoom. Zoom avoids the momentary visual jump of a step changer (Sikri, 2019).
4. **Objective lens:** its focal length sets the working distance (100 mm ≈ 4 in, 175 mm ≈ 7 in, 200 mm ≈ 8 in, 400 mm ≈ 16 in). The books differ on the best choice:
   - Sikri (2019) recommends 200 mm, and Rubinstein (2017) says many surgeons use it.
   - Grossman's gives 200, 250 and 300 mm as commonest and describes variable-focus 200-300 mm objectives (Gopikrishna, 2021).
   - Lins et al (2016) advise the longest focal length the operator's height allows, for room to pass instruments.
   - A 250 mm lens gives lower magnification than a 200 mm one at every setting.

**Total magnification (Rubinstein, 2017):** M = (focal length of binoculars ÷ focal length of objective) × eyepiece power × changer factor. For example, 160 mm binoculars, ×10 eyepieces and a 200 mm objective with changer factors of 0.5, 0.66, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 give ×4, ×5.3, ×8, ×12 and ×16. Suggested endodontic set-up: 12.5× eyepieces, 125 mm inclined binocular, 200 mm objective, 5-step changer of 4×-28× (Sikri, 2019).

**C. Illumination:**
| Source | Point made | Source book |
|---|---|---|
| Xenon | Brighter, near daylight; about 5800 K, 420 klx vs halogen 100 klx | Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010 |
| Metal halide | Compact, powerful, efficient | Sikri, 2019 |
| Quartz halogen | Yellowish light | Sikri, 2019 |
| LED | Powerful LEDs are used in current microscopes | Gopikrishna, 2021 |

Lins et al (2016) add that the source should ideally hold two lamps (one in use, one reserve). The light is **coaxial**: the light path and the line of sight are the same, so the field has no shadows (Gopikrishna, 2021). The returning image travels as two separate beams, which gives stereoscopic depth (Rubinstein, 2017). Microscope light is two to three times that of a surgical headlamp, but the field darkens as magnification increases (Rubinstein, 2017).

**D. Accessories.** A beam splitter (typically 50:50) sends part of the light to a camera or an assistant's observer tube (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021). The assistant then sees stereoscopically what the operator sees (Rubinstein, 2017). Other accessories are pistol or bicycle handgrips, camera and video adapters, LCD monitors, fluorescence filters and sterilisable knob covers.

## 5. Setting up, posture and team work

### Explanation

Parfocalling: focus at the highest magnification on a flat object, then set each eye's diopter at the lowest; the setting is operator-specific (Sikri, 2019; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri: upright neutral posture; neck load doubles with every 2-3 cm the head moves forward; working with the head forward causes Tension Neck syndrome; the 12 o'clock position suits almost all teeth, with 7-12 o'clock zones for right-handers; thighs parallel to the floor; difficulty in positioning is the commonest reason for not using a microscope (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer: 12 o'clock for maxillary and 8-9 o'clock for mandibular teeth, more than 50 cm from the patient, chair with arm supports, four-handed work for the mandible and six-handed for the maxilla (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Rubinstein: upright posture with a double S-curve of the spine (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Parfocalling.** Before use the microscope is made parfocal, so it stays in focus over the whole magnification range (Rubinstein, 2017; Sikri, 2019):
1. Set the IPD.
2. Set both diopters to zero.
3. Focus on a flat object at the highest magnification.
4. Change to the lowest magnification.
5. Correct the right eye's diopter, then the left eye's.

The setting is personal and is repeated for each new operator; cameras and auxiliary binoculars are then also in focus.

**Posture (ergonomics).** The DOM lets the operator sit upright, neutral and balanced (Sikri, 2019). The spine keeps its double S-curve (Rubinstein, 2017). Neck muscle load doubles with every 2-3 cm the head shifts forward, and working with the head bent forward causes Tension Neck syndrome (Sikri, 2019). Sit with thighs parallel to the floor, feet flat, shoulders straight and elbows close to the body. Adjust the microscope to the eyes, then the patient to the hands, never the other way round.

**Operator positions (books differ in detail):**
- Sikri (2019): 12 o'clock is the most comfortable position for almost all teeth; the ideal zones are 7-12 o'clock for right-handed operators.
- Baumann and Beer (2010): 12 o'clock for maxillary teeth and 8-9 o'clock for mandibular teeth, more than 50 cm from the patient, on a chair with arm supports.

**Team work.** Four- or six-handed work is required, because the operator should not take the eyes from the eyepieces. Baumann and Beer (2010) advise four-handed work for the mandible and six-handed work for the maxilla, where one assistant handles suction, irrigation and documentation and the other passes instruments. Sikri (2019) notes that difficulty in positioning and adjusting the eyes is the commonest reason clinicians give up the microscope, which is why training matters.

## 6. Levels of magnification and their uses

### Explanation

Grossman's: low 2.5-5× for examination and initial access, medium 5-12× for most nonsurgical and surgical work, high 12-20× sparingly, e.g. instrument retrieval (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Rubinstein (surgery): about 2.5-8× orientation, 10-16× operating, 20-30× fine detail; more than ×30 is of little value in apical surgery (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Lins et al: 8× and 12.5× are most useful for finding canals (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer: usually up to ×20-25, but endodontics needs up to ×30 (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri calls mid-range the "working magnification" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

The ranges given by the books overlap but do not match:

| Range | Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021) | Surgical (Rubinstein, 2017) | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 2.5-5× | 2.5-8× | Examination, orientation, initial access, flaps |
| Medium | 5-12× | 10-16× | Most nonsurgical and surgical work ("working magnification", Sikri, 2019) |
| High | 12-20× | 20-30× | Inspection: instrument retrieval, cracks, resected root surface, documentation |

Lins et al (2016) find 8× and 12.5× most useful for locating canals, because the pulp floor distorts and depth of field is lost at higher power. Baumann and Beer (2010) say ×20-25 is the usual maximum, yet endodontics needs a capacity of up to ×30 for work beyond the middle third. Rubinstein (2017) finds more than ×30 of little practical value in apical surgery, because every small movement of the patient throws the field out of focus. At 2-2.5× several quadrants are in view, at 3.5× a single quadrant, and above this the view narrows to a single tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021, describing the microscope changer). Ritter et al (2019) make the same point for loupes.

## 7. Microscope compared with other magnification aids

### Explanation

Sikri: loupes allow less than 25° forward head movement, but have one fixed magnification, grow heavier with power, use a headgear light that casts shadows, and reach at most 6×; Rubinstein puts the practical limit at 3.5-4.5× (Sikri, 2019; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Coaxial microscope light gives shadow-free illumination not possible with loupes (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The procedure scope projects up to 20× onto an LCD screen; the endoscope's 0.9 mm optic gives up to 20×; endoscopic images are two-dimensional (Sikri, 2019; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Feature | Loupes | Procedure scope / endoscope | Operating microscope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnification | Fixed; practical limit 3.5-4.5× (Rubinstein, 2017), maximum 6× (Sikri, 2019) | Up to 20× | Variable, about 2-20× and more |
| Light | None built in (headlamp added; shadows) | Standard light | Coaxial; shadow-free (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| Image | 3D, convergent | 2D on a screen (Rubinstein, 2017) | 3D, parallel (stereoscopic) |
| Posture | Head 20-40° forward (fixed loupes) | Neutral | Neutral, upright |
| Documentation | Not possible | Yes | Beam splitter, camera |
| Cost and training | Low, easy | Procedure scope less expensive (Sikri, 2019) | High; learning curve |

Loupes remain the entry point, but in the books' comparison only the microscope combines high variable magnification, coaxial light and neutral posture (Sikri, 2019).

**Endoscope and orascope as complements.** Sikri (2019) lists the endoscope and orascope as further magnification aids. The orascope is a flexible fibre-optic endoscope with a 0.8 mm tip, a 0-degree lens and a 15 mm working portion, used for intracanal viewing (Lins et al, 2016). Its focus runs from 0 mm to infinity, so it can image the apical canal without reaching it (Sikri, 2019). Both give a two-dimensional image on a monitor, and neither gives a discernible image in blood, so haemostasis is essential (Rubinstein, 2017). Rubinstein (2017) states that orascopy was never intended to replace loupes or the microscope, only to complement them where a particular view is needed.

## 8. Applications in endodontics

### Explanation

Sikri: diagnosis of incipient caries, inconspicuous sinus tracts and cracks; better removal of pulp stones and identification of landmarks; MB2 detection rose from 73% before magnification to 93% with the microscope; middle mesial and middle distal canals; retreatment (ledge bypass, separated instrument removal, perforation repair), residual post cement; resected root showing isthmus, fins and lateral canals; microsurgery uses smaller osteotomies (about 0.5 cm against over 1.0 cm) and shallower bevels (10° against 45°) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Lins et al: vertical root fracture can be seen on the canal wall, avoiding exploratory surgery; Stropko's red or white line between MB1 and MB2; remaining filling material during retreatment; traditional surgery below 60% success against above 90% with microscope and ultrasonics (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Rubinstein: craze lines and cracks on the bevelled surface (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Diagnosis.** The microscope helps to see:
- incipient caries and hidden decay (Sikri, 2019);
- inconspicuous sinus tracts;
- enamel cracks, and cracks on the pulp floor and canal walls.

A vertical root fracture can be confirmed on the inner canal wall, avoiding exploratory surgery (Lins et al, 2016).

**B. Nonsurgical treatment:**
- **Access:** conservative refinement; removal of overhanging roof and pulp stones; protection of the pulp floor.
- **Canal location:** dentine covering an orifice is seen and removed precisely with ultrasonics. MB2 detection in maxillary first molars rose from 73% before magnification to 93% with the microscope (Sikri, 2019). Middle mesial and middle distal canals in mandibular molars are found more often. Stropko's red or white line between MB1 and MB2 is a guide (Lins et al, 2016).
- **Calcified canals:** ultrasonic troughing under vision, with micro-openers (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Cleaning, shaping and obturation:** removal of pulp remnants, assessment of dryness and sealer distribution, and confirmation of the apical seal.

**C. Retreatment and mishaps.** Bypassing ledges, removing separated instruments and repairing perforations have become more predictable (Sikri, 2019). Residual post cement and remaining dentine are seen during post removal, and remaining filling material can be seen and removed (Lins et al, 2016).

**D. Surgical endodontics:**

| Step | Conventional | Microsurgery (Sikri, 2019) |
|---|---|---|
| Finding the apex | Difficult | Easier |
| Osteotomy | Large, more than 1.0 cm | Small, 0.5 cm is enough |
| Bevel | 45° | About 10° |
| Isthmus | Very difficult to see | Easy to see |
| Inspection and retrofill | Approximate | Precise |

The resected surface shows isthmuses, fins, lateral canals, and craze lines and cracks (Rubinstein, 2017). Lins et al (2016) report success below 60% for the traditional technique against above 90% with microscope and ultrasonics.

## 9. Applications in restorative dentistry and other disciplines

### Explanation

Ritter et al: microscopes are used for finishing porcelain margins, identifying minute caries lesions and minimising removal of sound tooth; they typically have five or six levels of 2.5-20×; cost, size and perceived lack of value have limited their use (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Hilton et al: visual caries detection can improve by 50% with a prism loupe or microscope, and some form of magnification is recommended for every restorative dentist (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Rubinstein: flaps can be raised with little magnification, but papilla-base incisions need 7-0 sutures and at least ×4.3 (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Restorative and operative dentistry.** Microscopes are used in detail-oriented work: finishing porcelain restoration margins, identifying minute caries lesions and minimising the removal of sound tooth structure (Ritter et al, 2019). They typically have five or six steps of 2.5-20×. The accuracy of visual caries detection can improve by 50% with a prism loupe or surgical microscope (Hilton et al, 2013). Other uses, not stated in the books read, are [RECALL]:
- checking cavity walls and margins for residual caries and enamel cracks;
- precise bonding, with control of the adhesive layer and of excess cement.

Cost, equipment size and perceived lack of value have limited microscope use in operative practice (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Periodontal and surgical work.** Flaps can be raised with little magnification, but papilla-base incisions, which need 7-0 sutures, call for about ×4.3 or more (Rubinstein, 2017). Microsurgical root coverage and suturing follow the same principle. [RECALL]

**Fixed prosthodontics.** Ritter et al (2019) name the finishing of porcelain margins. Impression checks and cementation under magnification follow the same "see more, remove less" principle. [RECALL]

## 10. Instruments for working under the microscope

### Explanation

The micro-mirror is the key instrument, front-surface to avoid double images, round 2-5 mm (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Baumann and Beer use rhodium-plated mirrors and note that a handpiece head can obstruct direct vision; micro-openers negotiate calcified orifices (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ultrasonic tips do not block the view the way a handpiece head does and suit the lateral motion needed for MB2; they were first designed for retrograde work (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Conventional instruments block the field, so microdentistry needs its own armamentarium:
- **Micro-mirrors:** front-surface (no double image), round 2-5 mm, oval or rectangular (Sikri, 2019); rhodium-plated mirrors lose little light (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- **Ultrasonic tips:** they do not obstruct the view like a handpiece head, de-roof and trough conservatively, and suit the lateral motion needed to find MB2 (Sikri, 2019). They were first designed for retrograde preparation (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- **Micro-openers and handpiece-driven fine files** for calcified orifices (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Others:** mini-head handpieces (Sikri, 2019); fine irrigating needles, pluggers with hand grips and MTA carriers (Baumann and Beer, 2010); microsurgical blades and fine sutures [RECALL].

2. Communication with the referring dentist.
3. Legal documentation of diagnosis and treatment.

Recordings support the clinician legally and help patients understand visually (Lins et al, 2016). They also act as an internal marketing tool for the practice (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020).

## 12. Advantages, limitations, care and selection

### Explanation

Advantages: higher variable magnification, shadow-free light, an apparent gain in fine motor skill, relaxed eyes and documentation (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; no prescription spectacles needed, and collateral vision is reduced (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]. Disadvantages: indirect mirror vision, four- or six-handed work, slower procedures, smaller instruments, cost and a learning curve (Sikri, 2019; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; floor stands take space (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; maintenance costs (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; the SOM does not improve physical access (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Care: dust is the main enemy, so the microscope is kept covered; abrasive disinfectants remove lens coatings; sterilisable rubber knobs are available; there is no practical method of disinfecting handgrips and switches (Rubinstein, 2017; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Learning depth perception and orientation take time and patience, and practice on extracted teeth is advised (Rubinstein, 2017; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages:**
1. Variable high magnification, from about 2× to 20× and more.
2. Coaxial, shadow-free light.
3. Stereoscopic 3D view.
4. Upright, neutral posture, so less neck and back strain.
5. Eyes at rest (parallel optics), so long sessions are possible.
6. An apparent increase in fine motor skills (Sikri, 2019).
7. Prescription spectacles are not needed, and reduced collateral vision sharpens attention (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020).
8. Documentation and teaching.
9. More conservative, predictable treatment.

**Limitations:**
1. High cost of purchase and maintenance (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
2. A steep learning curve (Lins et al, 2016).
3. Indirect vision through mirrors, with reversed hand-eye coordination at first.
4. Four- or six-handed dentistry is mandatory.
5. Procedures are slower at first (Sikri, 2019).
6. Smaller instruments are needed.
7. A small field and shallow depth at high power, and a darker field.
8. Floor stands take space (Lins et al, 2016).
9. The SOM does not improve physical access (Rubinstein, 2017).

**Care and infection control.** Dust is the main enemy, so the microscope is kept covered and lenses are cleaned from the centre outwards. Abrasive disinfectants remove the antireflective coating over time; only the body is disinfected, not the lenses. Sterilisable rubber knobs and adhesive barriers cover the controls (Rubinstein, 2017). Baumann and Beer (2010) note there is still no practical method of disinfecting handgrips and switches.

**Learning.** Depth perception and orientation take time and patience (Rubinstein, 2017); practice on extracted teeth is recommended (Lins et al, 2016).

**Selecting a microscope.** Baumann and Beer (2010) advise testing the planned use before buying. The purchaser should consider:
- a stable, vibration-free mount, since cheap supports vibrate;
- variable working distance, continuous zoom, magnetic brakes and controls on the handgrips;
- for endodontics, a capacity of up to ×30 and a strong light source (Baumann and Beer specify a 180 W xenon lamp);
- the running costs of maintenance and repair, not just the purchase price, which makes a maintenance contract worthwhile.

## 13. Recent advances

### Explanation

LED light (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; variable-focus 200-300 mm objectives (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; sodium fluorescein with a cobalt-blue filter makes orifices fluoresce green (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; 3D vision systems designed from the ground up for dentistry, and the MoraVision camera, where a 42-inch monitor gives about ×10-×13 (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- LED illumination (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Variable-focus 200-300 mm objectives, motorised zoom and focus on the handgrips, and magnetic brakes.
- Fluorescence: a cobalt-blue filter with 1% sodium fluorescein makes canal orifices glow green against blue dentine (Lins et al, 2016).
- Integrated high-definition video.
- Heads-up 3D vision systems, with stereo cameras and a 3D monitor, designed from the ground up for dentistry; with the MoraVision system a 42-inch monitor gives about ×10-×13 (Rubinstein, 2017). Whether they can replace the SOM is not yet known.

## 14. Conclusion

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** The operating microscope has moved dentistry from tactile guesswork to visual precision. Its value rests on the triad of magnification, illumination and instrumentation (Baumann and Beer, 2010). It improves diagnosis, conservation of tooth structure, management of complex anatomy and mishaps, and the outcome of apical surgery, while protecting the clinician's posture. Its cost and learning curve are real, but now that its use is part of specialty training, the microscope has become a standard of care rather than a luxury.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

1. **"The microscope improves outcomes."** Lins et al (2016) report that microsurgery with microscope and ultrasonics succeeds in over 90% of cases against under 60% for traditional surgery, and Rubinstein (2017) cites amalgam-sealed surgery falling to 57.7% at 10 years. The comparison mixes magnification with new instruments, root-end materials and bevel design, so the gain cannot be attributed to the microscope alone. [Likely]
2. **"The microscope finds more canals."** Sikri (2019) reports MB2 detection rising from 73% to 93%. This is a detection figure, not an outcome figure; better detection plausibly improves outcome, but the books read give no outcome data for nonsurgical treatment under the microscope. [Likely]
3. **History.** The books disagree on the first dental use (1975, 1977, 1978 or 1981) and on the accreditation date (1996/1997 or 1998). The differences probably reflect first report versus first publication, and committee decision versus implementation. They are best shown side by side, not resolved. [Certain that they disagree; Guessing on the reason]
4. **Restorative dentistry.** Ritter et al (2019) note limited uptake; Hilton et al (2013) report a 50% gain in caries-detection accuracy. Evidence is strongest for endodontics and weakest for routine operative work. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Eye resolves 0.2 mm; microscope at 20× about 10 µm (Sikri).
- Galilean optics: parallel beams, so no eye fatigue; loupes are convergent.
- Magnification up → field of view and depth of field down.
- M = (binocular f ÷ objective f) × eyepiece × changer factor.
- 200 mm objective ≈ 8 inches working distance.
- Coaxial light means no shadows; beam splitter 50:50.
- MB2: 73% → 93%.
- Microsurgery: 0.5 cm osteotomy, about 10° bevel; >90% vs <60%.
- Carr, "Father of Microscopic Endodontics"; Apotheker coined "microdentistry".
