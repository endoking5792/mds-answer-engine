## 1. Introduction

### Explanation

The unaided eye resolves only 0.2 mm (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's describes endodontic therapy as work "inside a blind space" relying on touch and radiographs (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Working with the head bent forward causes Tension Neck syndrome (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri names three basic types of magnification (loupes, procedure scope and operating microscope) and adds the endoscope and orascope (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Endodontic procedures inside the pulp chamber and root canal demand great precision, yet they have traditionally been done "inside a blind space" by touch and radiographs (Gopikrishna, 2021). The unaided eye resolves only about 0.2 mm (Sikri, 2019). Orifices, isthmuses, cracks, fragments and perforations are often smaller than this. Magnification with good illumination makes them visible.

There is also an ergonomic reason: working with the head bent forward causes muscle fatigue and Tension Neck syndrome, with headache, interscapular pain and, later, cervical spondylosis (Sikri, 2019).

**Magnification** is the visual enlargement of an object. In endodontics it is used with illumination, so that what is enlarged is also clearly lit.

## 2. Historical milestones

### Explanation

Saemisch's loupes 1876; Nylen's monocular microscope 1921 (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Apotheker's introduction of the DOM is dated 1981 by Sikri and Grossman's but 1978 by Bharti and Bhasin (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]. Carr's ergonomic Galilean DOM is dated 1991 (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and Sikri calls him the "Father of Microscopic Endodontics" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The accreditation date is given as January 1997 by Rubinstein and early 1998 by Lins et al; endodontists' use of the microscope reached 90% by 2008 (Rubinstein, 2017; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- 1876: Saemisch introduced binocular loupes to surgery (Rubinstein, 2017).
- 1921: Nylen used a monocular microscope for ear surgery.
- 1978 / 1981: Apotheker and Jako introduced the dental operating microscope. Bharti and Bhasin (2020) give 1978; Sikri (2019) and Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021) give 1981.
- 1991: Carr introduced the ergonomic DOM with Galilean optics (Gopikrishna, 2021). Sikri (2019) calls him the "Father of Microscopic Endodontics": "You cannot treat what you cannot see".
- 1984: Reuben and Apotheker first reported the microscope in apical surgery. In 1990 Pecora gave the first AAE presentation on it, and Rubinstein and Carr began using medical-grade microscopes for apical surgery (Rubinstein, 2017).
- Training: proficiency in microscopy became mandatory in US endodontic programmes. Rubinstein (2017) gives January 1997 as the date the standards took effect; Lins et al (2016) give early 1998. Microscope use among endodontists rose from 52% in 1999 to 90% in 2008 (Rubinstein, 2017).

## 3. Optical principles

### Explanation

Magnification up means field of view and depth of field down (Gopikrishna, 2021; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Sikri defines usable power and declination angle (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The microscope's Galilean optics form the image at infinity, while loupes rely on convergent vision; convergence strains the rectus muscles (Sikri, 2019; Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]; Greenough-type convergent microscopes cause accommodation and eye fatigue (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri's resolution table is shown in the exam core (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Field of view** (the area seen) and **depth of field** (the near-to-far range in focus) both fall as magnification rises (Gopikrishna, 2021; Lins et al, 2016).
- **Usable power:** the highest magnification usable in a given situation (Sikri, 2019).
- **Working distance:** from the lens or eye to the field.
- **Convergence angle:** the angle at which the two oculars meet on the object.
- **Declination angle:** the downward angle of the eyes towards the work.
- **Convergent vs parallel optics:** loupes use convergent vision, which tires the rectus muscles (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020). The microscope uses Galilean optics, focused at infinity, so the eyes rest (Sikri, 2019). Convergent (Greenough-type) laboratory microscopes force accommodation and cause eye fatigue, which is why they are unsuitable for long clinical work (Rubinstein, 2017).
- **The field-depth trade-off:** with fixed loupes it cannot be escaped. With the microscope the operator simply steps down in power when the field becomes too narrow (Rubinstein, 2017).

**Resolution (Sikri, 2019):**

| Aid | Magnification | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Unaided eye | none | 200 µm |
| Loupes, low | 2.5× | 80 µm |
| Loupes, high | 6× | 50 µm |
| Microscope, high | 20× | 10 µm |

## 4. Classification of magnification aids

#### ✎ Exam core

1. **Loupes (surgical telescopes):** single-lens (diopter, flat-plane); Galilean (two or three lenses); prism (Keplerian). By mounting they are flip-up or through-the-lens (TTL).
2. **Procedure scope:** camera above the mouth with the image on a screen.
3. **Dental operating microscope.**
4. **Endoscope:** rigid rod-lens or flexible.
5. **Orascope:** flexible fibre-optic endoscope.

These are adapted from Sikri (2019), Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021) and Bharti and Bhasin (2020).

## 5. Loupes

### Explanation

Sikri: loupes allow less than 25° forward head movement; Galilean loupes give up to 4.5× and prism loupes up to 6×; loupes have one fixed magnification, become heavier with power, use a headgear light that casts shadows, and reach at most 6× (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's: single-lens loupes cause compromised posture and musculoskeletal stress; Galilean loupes are most recommended at 2-4.5×; Keplerian prism loupes reach 6× (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; flip-up loupes allow adjustment of IPD and declination, whereas TTL loupes are fixed and custom-made (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Rubinstein: prism loupes are the most optically advanced loupes, being low-power telescopes with prisms; the practical maximum is ×3.5-×4.5; headlamps raise light up to four times operatory lights; convergence causes eye strain (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Bharti and Bhasin list simple, compound, Galilean and prism loupes; simple loupes suffer spherical and chromatic aberration; Galilean loupes are limited to 2.5- or 3.5-fold with a blurred periphery; prism loupes contain Pechan or Schmidt prisms; it is ideal to start with loupes before moving to the microscope (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]. Ritter et al: most dentists use 2-4×; 2-2.5× shows several quadrants; higher power is heavier, narrower, dearer and needs more light; working distance about 13-14 in (33-35 cm) for average height; LED headlamps (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Loupes are two monocular magnifiers mounted side by side and angled inwards (convergent optics).

**Types by optics (the books' figures differ):**

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| Single-lens (diopter, flat-plane) | Cheapest; spherical and chromatic aberration (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020); poor posture (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Galilean (two to three lenses) | Light, compact, most recommended; 2-4.5× (Gopikrishna, 2021), up to 4.5× (Sikri, 2019); limited to 2.5-3.5× with a blurred periphery (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) |
| Prism (Keplerian) | Most advanced loupe: low-power telescopes with prisms (Pechan or Schmidt); larger field, more depth, longer working distance; up to 6×; heavier (Rubinstein, 2017; Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) |

**Types by mounting (Gopikrishna, 2021):** flip-up loupes have adjustable IPD and declination, can be shared, are cheaper and heavier. TTL loupes are custom-made for one operator, fixed, lighter and dearer.

**Selection (Ritter et al, 2019):**
- Most dentists use 2-4×; 2-2.5× shows several quadrants, and 3-4× a few teeth or one tooth.
- Higher power is heavier and narrower, and needs more light.
- Working distance is about 13-14 in (33-35 cm) for a dentist of average height.

**Advantages:** cheap, easy to learn, posture better than with the naked eye (less than 25° forward head tilt; Sikri, 2019). Bharti and Bhasin (2020) advise learning on loupes before moving to the microscope.

**Limitations:**
- One fixed magnification, with a practical limit of ×3.5-×4.5 (Rubinstein, 2017) and a maximum of 6× (Sikri, 2019).
- Heavier at higher power.
- Headlamp light casts shadows.
- Convergence causes eye strain (Rubinstein, 2017).
- No documentation.

A fibre-optic headlamp can raise light up to four times that of the operatory light (Rubinstein, 2017).

## 6. Procedure scope, endoscope and orascope

### Explanation

Procedure scope: camera above the mouth projecting up to 20× onto an LCD screen (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Endoscope: a 0.9 mm optic gives up to 20× (Sikri, 2019; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; first used in endodontics in 1979 for root fractures (Lins et al, 2016; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Rod-lens choice differs: Sikri recommends the 2.7 mm, 3 cm rod lens for nonsurgical work, while Lins et al and Rubinstein give 4 mm, 30°, 4 cm for nonsurgical and 2.7 mm, 70°, 3 cm for surgical work (Sikri, 2019; Lins et al, 2016; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Orascope: fibre-optic, 0.8 mm tip, 0° lens, 15 mm working portion; focus from 0 to infinity; Sikri says the coronal half is prepared to size 80-90, Lins et al say 15 mm of the cervical third to #90; difficult in curved canals (Sikri, 2019; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. No image in blood; images are 2D; close approach gives ×30-×40; orascopy complements rather than replaces loupes and the microscope (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Procedure scope.** A camera above the mouth projects up to 20× onto a flat screen, which allows a neutral head posture (Sikri, 2019).

**Endoscope.** A rod-lens telescope with a camera, light source and monitor. It was first used in endodontics in 1979 to diagnose root fractures (Lins et al, 2016; Rubinstein, 2017). A 0.9 mm optic gives up to 20× (Sikri, 2019). The recommended lenses differ between books:

| Use | Lins et al (2016); Rubinstein (2017) | Sikri (2019) |
|---|---|---|
| Nonsurgical | 4 mm diameter, 30°, 4 cm rod lens | 2.7 mm diameter, 3 cm rod lens |
| Surgical | 2.7 mm diameter, 70°, 3 cm rod lens | not specified |

**Orascope.** A flexible fibre-optic endoscope for intracanal viewing, with a 0.8 mm tip, 0° lens and 15 mm working portion (Lins et al, 2016). Its focus runs from 0 to infinity, so it can image the apical canal without reaching it (Sikri, 2019). The canal must be enlarged first: Sikri (2019) says the coronal half to size 80-90; Lins et al (2016) say 15 mm of the cervical third to #90. The canal is then dried. Use is difficult in curved canals.

**Limits.** No discernible image in blood, so haemostasis is essential, and the image is two-dimensional. Because the lens lies close to the object, magnification can reach ×30-×40 (Rubinstein, 2017). Orascopy complements, but does not replace, loupes and the microscope.

**Comparison of the main aids (adapted from Sikri, 2019):**

| Feature | Loupes | Procedure scope | Microscope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnification | Usually fixed, 2-6× | Variable, 2-20× | Variable, 2-20× |
| Focusing | By moving the neck | By the camera | By the microscope |
| Light | Separate headlamp | Standard | Coaxial |
| Head posture | 20-40° forward | Near neutral | Near neutral |
| Image | 3D, convergent | 2D on a screen | 3D, binocular |
| Cost | Low | Low | High |

## 7. The dental operating microscope

### Explanation

Body parts: eyepiece, binoculars, changer, objective (Gopikrishna, 2021); base, optical head, light, engine and splitter (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Eyepieces 10× and 12.5× commonest; diopters −5 to +5; inclinable tubes recommended; 3-, 5- or 6-step changers, or zoom without visual jump (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Objective: Sikri recommends 200 mm (8 inches); Grossman's lists 200, 250 and 300 mm, a 250 mm lens giving less magnification than a 200 mm one; Lins et al advise the longest focal length the operator's height allows (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Sikri's set-up: 12.5× eyepiece, 125 mm binocular, 200 mm objective, 4-28× changer (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Light: Sikri describes xenon versus yellow halogen; Grossman's says LEDs are now used; Baumann and Beer give xenon 420 klx versus halogen 100 klx (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Coaxial illumination; 50:50 beam splitter; observer tubes (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Parfocalling (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Parts** (Gopikrishna, 2021; Lins et al, 2016):
1. **Support:** floor, wall or ceiling mount on a counterbalanced arm.
2. **Eyepieces:** 10× or 12.5× are most used; diopter settings of −5 to +5 (Sikri, 2019).
3. **Binoculars:** straight, inclined or inclinable; inclinable binoculars are recommended in endodontics; they set the IPD.
4. **Magnification changer:** 3-, 5- or 6-step manual, or zoom, which avoids the visual jump of a step changer (Sikri, 2019).
5. **Objective lens:** sets the working distance. Sikri (2019) recommends 200 mm (about 8 in). Grossman's lists 200, 250 and 300 mm, noting a 250 mm lens magnifies less than a 200 mm one. Lins et al (2016) advise the longest focal length the operator's height allows.
6. **Light:** xenon (daylight colour; 420 klx against halogen's 100 klx, Baumann and Beer, 2010), halogen (yellow), metal halide, or LED in current models (Gopikrishna, 2021).
7. **Accessories:** 50:50 beam splitter, camera, observer tube for the assistant, and handgrips.

**Total magnification** = (binocular focal length ÷ objective focal length) × eyepiece power × changer factor. Suggested endodontic set-up (Sikri, 2019): 12.5× eyepieces, 125 mm inclined binocular, 200 mm objective, 5-step changer of 4×-28×.

**Coaxial illumination.** The light path and the line of sight are the same, so the field has no shadows (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Worked example (Rubinstein, 2017):** 160 mm binoculars, ×10 eyepieces and a 200 mm objective with changer factors of 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 give ×4, ×8 and ×16.

**Illumination in more detail:**
- The light passes through a condensing lens and prisms, then the objective, to the field. It returns through the changer and binoculars as two separate beams, which produces stereoscopic depth (Rubinstein, 2017).
- It is two to three times brighter than a surgical headlamp.
- The effective aperture narrows as magnification rises, so the field darkens. A beam splitter takes a further share of the light from the operator.
- Lins et al (2016) advise a source holding two lamps, one in use and one in reserve.

**Stability.** The site must be vibration-free; cheap supports vibrate and delay treatment (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Parfocalling.** Focus at the highest power, then set each eye's diopter at the lowest power, so the image stays sharp at every step (Rubinstein, 2017).

## 8. Ergonomics and set-up

### Explanation

Upright neutral posture; eyes focused at infinity; neck load doubles with every 2-3 cm the head moves forward (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri: 12 o'clock for almost all teeth; Baumann and Beer: 12 o'clock for maxillary and 8-9 o'clock for mandibular teeth; four-handed work in the mandible and six-handed in the maxilla (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Difficulty in positioning is the commonest reason for abandoning the microscope (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

With the microscope the operator sits upright and neutral, eyes focused at infinity, with minimal neck, shoulder and back fatigue (Sikri, 2019). Neck load doubles with every 2-3 cm the head moves forward.

**Sequence:** seat the operator first, thighs parallel to the floor; then adjust the microscope to the eyes; then position the patient between the hands.

**Positions:** Sikri (2019) finds 12 o'clock the most comfortable for almost all teeth. Baumann and Beer (2010) use 12 o'clock for maxillary teeth and 8-9 o'clock for mandibular teeth.

**Team work:** four-handed work in the mandible and six-handed work in the maxilla (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Indirect vision through a front-surface mirror is the rule in the maxilla.

Upright sitting keeps the double S-curve of the spine. Constant bending over the patient collapses the diaphragm and may reduce oxygen exchange, adding to fatigue (Rubinstein, 2017). A chair with arm supports is essential for vibration-free work at high power, and the operator sits more than 50 cm from the patient. With this set-up, procedures longer than 2 hours can be done without loss of concentration (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

Difficulty in positioning and adjusting the eyes is the commonest reason clinicians stop using the microscope (Sikri, 2019).

## 9. Levels of magnification

### Explanation

Grossman's: low 2.5-5× (examination, access), medium 5-12× (most procedures), high 12-20× (sparingly, e.g. instrument retrieval) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Lins et al: 8× and 12.5× for locating canals (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer: endodontics needs capacity up to ×30 (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri calls mid-range the "working magnification" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Level | Range (Gopikrishna, 2021) | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 2.5-5× | Examination, orientation, initial access |
| Medium | 5-12× | Most nonsurgical and surgical work (the "working magnification", Sikri, 2019) |
| High | 12-20× | Used sparingly: instrument retrieval, inspection, documentation |

Lins et al (2016) find 8× and 12.5× most useful for finding canals; higher power distorts the pulp floor and loses depth of field. Baumann and Beer (2010) want capacity up to ×30 for removing fragments and managing ledges beyond the middle third. In apical surgery Rubinstein (2017) uses about ×2.5-×8 for orientation, ×10-×16 for operating and ×20-×30 for observing fine detail.

## 10. Clinical applications in endodontics

### Explanation

Diagnosis: incipient caries, sinus tracts and cracks (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; vertical root fracture seen on the canal wall, avoiding exploratory surgery; dentine moisture must be controlled (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Canal location: MB2 detection rose from 73% to 93%; middle mesial canals (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Stropko's line, NaOCl bubbles and fluorescein (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; troughing and micro-openers (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Obturation: dryness and sealer can be assessed (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Retreatment: ledges, fragments and perforations are more predictable; post cement is seen (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; ultrasonic tips touch only the fragment; remaining filling material is seen (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Surgery: isthmus, fins and lateral canals; osteotomy 0.5 cm against over 1 cm; bevel about 10° against 45° (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; success above 90% against below 60% (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; the SOM is used at its best for osteotomy, apicoectomy, root-end preparation, retrofilling and documentation, and for seeing cracks on the bevel (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Diagnosis:**
- Incipient and hidden caries, inconspicuous sinus tracts, and enamel and pulp-floor cracks (Sikri, 2019).
- A vertical root fracture can be seen on the inner canal wall, avoiding exploratory surgery. The dentine must be neither too dry (it looks chalky and hides the line) nor too wet (Lins et al, 2016).

**B. Access and canal location:**
- Conservative access, removal of the overhanging roof and pulp stones, and protection of the floor.
- MB2 detection in maxillary first molars rose from 73% before magnification to 93% with the microscope; middle mesial and middle distal canals in mandibular molars are found more often (Sikri, 2019).
- Aids under the microscope (Lins et al, 2016): Stropko's red or white line joining MB1 to MB2; NaOCl bubbling over an orifice; 1% methylene blue; 1% sodium fluorescein with a cobalt-blue filter (green orifices).
- Calcified canals: ultrasonic troughing of the floor and micro-openers (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**C. Cleaning, shaping and obturation:** removal of pulp remnants; assessment of canal dryness and sealer distribution (Sikri, 2019); control of the apical seal and down-pack.

**D. Retreatment and mishaps:**
- Bypassing ledges, removing separated instruments and repairing perforations are now more predictable (Sikri, 2019).
- Fragments are loosened with thin ultrasonic tips that touch only the fragment (Lins et al, 2016).
- Residual post cement is seen during post removal, and remaining gutta-percha and sealer are seen and removed.

**E. Surgical endodontics (microsurgery):**

| Step | Conventional | Microsurgery (Sikri, 2019) |
|---|---|---|
| Osteotomy | More than 1.0 cm | 0.5 cm |
| Bevel | 45° | About 10° |
| Isthmus | Very difficult | Easy |
| Retrofill | Approximate | Precise |

The resected surface shows isthmuses, fins and lateral canals (Sikri, 2019), and cracks on the bevel (Rubinstein, 2017). Lins et al (2016) report success above 90% with microscope and ultrasonics, against below 60% for traditional surgery.

**Magnification at each surgical stage (Rubinstein, 2017):**
- Incising and reflecting flaps are not high-magnification steps, but papilla-base incisions with 7-0 sutures need about ×4.3 or more.
- The microscope is used to best advantage for osteotomy, apicoectomy, root-end preparation, retrofilling and documentation.
- Magnification above ×30 is of little value, because slight patient movement keeps throwing the field out of focus.
- Two assistants are advised. One suctions while watching through an assistant binocular, and so sees exactly what the operator sees; the other passes instruments.

## 10A. Learning and adoption

### Explanation

Bharti and Bhasin advise adapting to magnification on loupes first and call loupe training standard practice for students (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]; Lins et al say the learning curve is long and recommend training on extracted teeth (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; Grossman's expects every endodontist to use a DOM (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- Start with loupes, which teach focusing under magnification and hand-eye coordination, then move to the microscope; loupe training is now standard in preclinical courses (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020).
- Early on the microscope can slow the work because the learning curve is long, so hands-on courses and practice on extracted teeth are recommended (Lins et al, 2016).
- Once proficiency is gained, the clinician works in a more comfortable position with less fatigue and stress, and greater efficiency.
- Every endodontist is now expected to learn and use a DOM (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 11. Micro-instruments and ultrasonics

### Explanation

The micro-mirror is the key instrument, front-surface and 2-5 mm (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; rhodium-plated mirrors (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; a handpiece head obstructs the view, which ultrasonic tips avoid, and they suit lateral motion (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Front-surface micro-mirrors** (no double image), 2-5 mm, round or oval (Sikri, 2019); rhodium-plated mirrors (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- **Ultrasonic tips:** a handpiece head often blocks direct vision, which ultrasonic tips avoid. They de-roof the chamber and trough conservatively, and their lateral motion suits the search for MB2 (Sikri, 2019).
- **Others:** micro-openers, fine irrigating needles, micro-pluggers and MTA carriers.

## 12. Documentation

### Explanation

Documentation serves education, referral communication and legal records (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; recordings educate patients and help legally (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Photographs and video taken through the beam splitter serve three purposes (Sikri, 2019):
1. Education of patients and students.
2. Communication with the referring dentist.
3. Legal records.

They also help patients understand their treatment (Lins et al, 2016).

## 13. Advantages and limitations

### Explanation

Advantages include an apparent increase in fine motor skill (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Disadvantages: indirect mirror vision, four- or six-handed work, slower procedures (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; steep learning curve (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; purchase and maintenance cost (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; no improvement in physical access, and a darker field at high power (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]; practice on extracted teeth is advised (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Care.**
- Dust is the main enemy, so the microscope is kept covered.
- Lenses are cleaned with lens solution, and abrasive disinfectants are avoided because they strip the antireflective coating.
- Only the body is disinfected; sterilisable knobs and adhesive barriers cover the controls (Rubinstein, 2017).

## 14. Conclusion

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** Magnification has changed endodontics from a tactile, radiograph-guided discipline into a visual one. Loupes are the entry level. The operating microscope, with variable power, coaxial light and neutral posture, is the standard for locating canals, managing calcification and mishaps, retreatment and microsurgery. Endoscopes and orascopes complement it. As Carr put it, "You cannot treat what you cannot see" (Sikri, 2019).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

1. **Detection versus outcome.** Sikri (2019) reports MB2 detection rising from 73% to 93%, and Lins et al (2016) report microsurgery success above 90% against below 60%. The first is a detection figure. The second compares whole techniques (microscope, ultrasonics, new root-end materials and bevel), not magnification alone. [Likely]
2. **Loupes versus microscope.** All the books put the microscope ahead of loupes for endodontics. The difference is clearest at the magnifications loupes cannot reach (above about 4.5-6×). For examination and access at 2.5-5×, loupes and microscope overlap (Gopikrishna, 2021; Rubinstein, 2017). [Likely]
3. **Disagreements kept side by side:** the endoscope lens for nonsurgical use (Sikri vs Lins et al and Rubinstein); orascope canal preparation (coronal half to 80-90, Sikri; cervical 15 mm to #90, Lins et al); the date the DOM was introduced (1978 vs 1981); the accreditation year (1997 vs 1998). [Certain that they disagree]
