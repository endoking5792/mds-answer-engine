## 1. Introduction

### Explanation

The eye resolves only 0.2 mm (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Bharti and Bhasin: only good magnification with good illumination reduces eye strain and back pain (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]. Lam: intraoral digital radiography should be the primary radiographic technique in endodontics, with small-field CBCT as a supplement (Lam, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Seeing is the basis of diagnosis and treatment, but the unaided eye resolves only about 0.2 mm (Sikri, 2019). Three technologies extend sight:
1. **Illumination:** light the field well.
2. **Magnification:** enlarge it.
3. **Digital imaging:** see inside the tooth and bone, and record what is seen.

Magnification alone is not enough: only good magnification with good illumination reduces the operator's eye strain and back pain (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020). In imaging the trend is from film to digital sensors: intraoral digital radiography is now the primary technique in endodontics, supplemented by small-field cone beam CT (CBCT) (Lam, 2012).

## 2. Trends in illumination

### Explanation

The loupe headgear light casts shadows (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; a fibre-optic headlamp gives up to four times operatory light, and the microscope two to three times a headlamp (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]; LED headlamps attach to the frame or headband (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sources: xenon is daylight-like and halogen yellow; metal halide is compact and efficient (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; xenon at about 5800 K and 420 klx against halogen's 100 klx (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; LEDs are now standard (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; halogen, LED, xenon or metal halide, ideally two lamps (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Coaxial light gives shadow-free illumination that loupes cannot (Gopikrishna, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The field darkens at high power (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Fluorescein with a cobalt-blue filter and methylene blue (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. From operatory lamp to headlamp to coaxial light:**
1. **Operatory light:** a fixed angle and shadows.
2. **Loupe headlamp:** the headgear light can still cast shadows over the field (Sikri, 2019). A fibre-optic headlamp mounted at the centre of the forehead raises light up to four times that of the operatory light and keeps the beam in the centre of the visual field (Rubinstein, 2017). The current trend is small, light LED headlamps fixed to the loupe frame or headband (Ritter et al, 2019).
3. **Microscope light:** two to three times brighter than a surgical headlamp (Rubinstein, 2017). It is coaxial: the light path and line of sight are the same, so the field is shadow-free (Gopikrishna, 2021). Baumann and Beer (2010) note this is not possible even with a loupe light placed between the eyes.

**B. Light sources (the books show the trend):**

| Source | Features | Source book |
|---|---|---|
| Quartz halogen | Older; yellowish light; fibre-optic cable loses light | Sikri, 2019 |
| Xenon | Daylight-like, brighter; about 5800 K, 420 klx (halogen about 100 klx) | Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010 |
| Metal halide | Compact, powerful, efficient; cool site | Sikri, 2019 |
| LED | Powerful LEDs in current microscopes | Gopikrishna, 2021 |

Lins et al (2016) advise a unit holding two lamps, one in use and one in reserve. Light falls as magnification rises, because the effective aperture narrows, and a beam splitter takes a further share (Rubinstein, 2017). This is why brighter sources are being sought.

**C. Light as a diagnostic tool:**
- Fibre-optic transillumination for cracks.
- Fluorescence: 1% sodium fluorescein under a cobalt-blue filter on the microscope makes canal orifices glow green against blue dentine (Lins et al, 2016).
- Dyes such as 1% methylene blue.
- Orange filters, which slow the setting of light-cured materials under the microscope light. [RECALL]

## 3. Trends in magnification: loupes

### Explanation

Saemisch introduced loupes in 1876 (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Bharti and Bhasin list simple, compound, Galilean and prism loupes; simple loupes suffer spherical and chromatic aberration; achromatic compound lenses give colour-perfect images; Galilean loupes are limited to 2.5-3.5× with a blurred periphery; prism loupes use Pechan or Schmidt prisms; loupes range 1.5-10× (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]. Prism loupes are the most advanced, and ×3.5-×4.5 is the practical limit (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Galilean 2-4.5×; Keplerian prism up to 6×; flip-up loupes are adjustable, while TTL loupes are custom-made and fixed (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Most dentists use 2-4×; higher power is heavier, narrower, costlier and needs more light; working distance about 13-14 in (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Loupes allow less than 25° forward head movement (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Loupes date from Saemisch (1876) (Rubinstein, 2017), but their design has advanced:
- **Optics:** from simple single lenses with spherical and chromatic aberration, to compound achromatic lenses giving colour-perfect images, to Galilean loupes (light, 2-4.5×), to prism (Keplerian) loupes with Pechan or Schmidt prisms. Prism loupes give better magnification, a larger field, more depth and a longer working distance, up to about 6× (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020; Gopikrishna, 2021; Rubinstein, 2017).
- **Mounting:** through-the-lens (TTL) loupes are custom-made for one operator, light, and fixed in IPD and declination. Flip-up loupes are adjustable and shareable, but heavier (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Selection:** magnification 2-4× for most dentists. Working distance is matched to the operator's height (about 13-14 in, 33-35 cm, for average height), so that the head stays within about 25° of upright, as Sikri (2019) states loupes allow (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2019).
- **Integrated LED lights** and prescription lenses (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Limits that remain:** practical power of about ×3.5-×4.5 (Rubinstein, 2017); heavier at higher power; fixed magnification; no documentation.
- **Training trend:** learning with loupes is now standard practice in preclinical conservative dentistry. Students adapt to magnified vision on loupes before moving to the microscope (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020).

## 4. Trends in magnification: microscope and other systems

### Explanation

Bharti and Bhasin: the DOM gives 2× up to 40×, and some have 2-32× (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020) [SRC]; Lins et al: 2.5-24× (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Galilean optics (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Step changers versus zoom without visual jump (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Variable-focus 200-300 mm objectives (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; the longest focal length the height allows (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; binoculars rotating to 220° (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]; inclinable binoculars (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; capacity up to ×30 (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Procedure scope up to 20× on an LCD (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Endoscope and orascope dimensions (Sikri, 2019; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; no image in blood, ×30-×40 close up, complementary role (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. 3D vision systems; MoraVision ×10-×13 on a 42-inch monitor (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Dental operating microscope.** The microscope offers from 2× up to 40× (Bharti and Bhasin, 2020). Lins et al (2016) give a lens range of 2.5-24×; some models have 2-32× steps. Trends:
1. **Zoom:** manual step changers (3-, 5- or 6-step) giving way to manual or power zoom, which avoids the visual jump of turret changers (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019).
2. **Variable-focus objectives (200-300 mm)** set by a knob. Lins et al (2016) advise the longest focal length the operator's height permits.
3. **Inclinable binoculars** rotating to 220° and beyond; motorised focus and zoom on the handgrips or a foot pedal; magnetic brakes (Rubinstein, 2017).
4. Built-in high-definition cameras and LED light.
5. Capacity of up to ×30 for endodontics (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**B. Procedure scope.** A camera above the mouth projects up to 20× onto a flat screen, so the head stays neutral (Sikri, 2019).

**C. Endoscope and orascope:**
- **Endoscope:** rigid rod lens; the flexible type has a Nitinol coating and a 0.9 mm optic giving 20× (Lins et al, 2016). Lins et al recommend 2.7 mm, 70°, 3 cm for surgery and 4 mm, 30°, 4 cm for nonsurgical work.
- **Orascope:** fibre-optic, 0.8 mm tip, 0° lens, 15 mm working portion, focus from 0 to infinity (Lins et al, 2016; Sikri, 2019).
- Close placement gives ×30-×40, but no image in blood and a 2D image; they complement, rather than replace, the microscope (Rubinstein, 2017).

**D. Heads-up 3D visualisation.** Stereo cameras send the image to a 3D monitor, and the operator works looking straight ahead. 3D vision systems are designed from the ground up for dentistry. With the MoraVision camera a 42-inch monitor gives about ×10-×13 (Rubinstein, 2017). Whether they can replace the microscope is not yet known.

**E. Principles behind the trends.** Field of view and depth of field fall as power rises (Gopikrishna, 2021). Variable systems let the operator use the lowest power that shows the detail needed (Rubinstein, 2017).

## 4A. Magnification spreading beyond endodontics, and the ergonomic trend

### Explanation

Ritter et al: microscopes, once mainly in endodontic practice, are now used in some restorative practices for porcelain margins, minute caries and conserving tooth; 2.5-20×; cost, size and perceived lack of value limit use (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Hilton et al: caries detection improves 50% with prism loupes or a microscope (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri: neck load doubles with every 2-3 cm of forward head shift, and the microscope allows an upright neutral posture (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Restorative dentistry.** Microscopes, once largely confined to endodontic practice, are now used in some restorative practices, at typically 2.5-20×. Uses include finishing porcelain margins, finding minute caries and minimising removal of sound tooth structure (Ritter et al, 2019). The accuracy of visual caries detection improves by about 50% with prism loupes or a microscope (Hilton et al, 2013). Cost, equipment size and perceived lack of value still limit uptake.

**Ergonomics as a driver.** Neck muscle load doubles with every 2-3 cm the head moves forward (Sikri, 2019). Each newer system moves the operator towards a neutral head:
- loupes allow less than 25° of forward head tilt;
- the microscope gives an upright posture;
- the procedure scope and 3D heads-up displays let the operator look straight ahead at a screen.

## 5. Integration: documentation and the visual team

### Explanation

A 50:50 beam splitter feeds cameras or an assistant's binocular (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and the assistant sees what the operator sees (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Recordings help legally and educate patients (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; documentation serves three purposes (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; clinical stills are reviewed with digital radiographs after surgery (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Magnification and digital imaging now meet in one workflow:
- A beam splitter (typically 50:50) sends the image to a high-definition camera, a monitor and an assistant's binocular. The assistant sees exactly what the operator sees (Sikri, 2019; Rubinstein, 2017).
- Clinical stills and video are stored with the digital radiographs and reviewed with the patient (Rubinstein, 2017).
- The records serve education, communication with the referring dentist, and medicolegal records (Sikri, 2019; Lins et al, 2016).

## 6. Trends in digital imaging: intraoral digital radiography

### Explanation

Lam: digital intraoral radiography should be primary in endodontics, with occlusal radiography and small-field CBCT as supplements; there are three receptor types (CCD, CMOS and PSP); CCD and CMOS use a scintillator; PSP plates are barium fluorohalide phosphors; sensors display 16,384 grey shades, monitors only 256, and the eye 64; CCD/CMOS resolve about 11 lp/mm and PSP about 8 lp/mm, the newest more than 20 and 13 lp/mm, against film above 22 lp/mm and the eye's 8 lp/mm; less than 80% of a CCD/CMOS surface is active; mean dose reduction about 55% against D-speed film; rectangular collimation cuts skin dose by up to two-thirds; time saving 36 min/day for CCD/CMOS users; little evidence that software enhancement has a biological correlate (Lam, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Lam (2012) recommends intraoral digital radiography as the primary radiographic technique in endodontics.

**Sensor types:**

| Feature | CCD / CMOS (solid state) | PSP (phosphor plate) |
|---|---|---|
| Principle | Scintillator turns X-rays into light, then electrons captured by the chip | Barium fluorohalide phosphor stores the energy, released by a laser scanner |
| Image | Immediate | After scanning |
| Active area | Less than 80% of the sensor | Whole plate (sizes 0, 1, 2, 4) |
| Resolution | About 11 lp/mm (newest more than 20) | About 8 lp/mm (newest about 13) |
| Handling | Thick (4-7 mm), rigid, usually wired; hard to position in small mouths | Film sizes; surface damaged by scratches, so plates need replacing |
| Cost | High | Low per plate |

Data from Lam (2012).

**Advantages:**
- Dose reduction, mean about 55% against D-speed film; rectangular collimation cuts skin dose by up to two-thirds.
- Speed: CCD/CMOS users save about 36 minutes a day.
- Easier image management after exposure (Lam, 2012); no chemical processing [RECALL].
- Software enhancement of contrast and density.

**Limits:** sensors capture 16,384 grey shades, monitors show only 256, and the eye distinguishes about 64. Film still resolves more than 22 lp/mm, against the eye's 8 lp/mm. There is little evidence that software enhancement of an image has a biological correlate (Lam, 2012).

## 7. Trends in digital imaging: cone beam CT

### Explanation

Lam: CBCT was first described for dentistry in 1998; it uses a cone beam with a flat sensor rotating round the patient; small fields of view are under 8 cm and large fields above 8 cm; it builds voxels and is viewed in axial, coronal and sagittal planes; small-field, high-resolution units suit endodontics; effective doses are about 5.3-38.3 µSv for small fields, against 388 µSv for a D-speed full-mouth series, and 74-1073 µSv for large fields (Lam, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** CBCT was first described for dentistry in 1998. A cone-shaped beam and a flat digital sensor rotate once around the stationary patient. The data form cube-shaped voxels, viewed in axial, coronal and sagittal planes, as oblique slices or as 3D renderings (Lam, 2012).

**Reading the volume.** An abnormality seen in one plane is colocalised in the other two planes using bony landmarks and adjacent teeth. 3D renderings can be rotated to show the spatial relationship of teeth and bone. Lam (2012) stresses that how a volume is manipulated and viewed can change what is seen, and so the diagnosis; CBCT interpretation therefore needs training.

**Small vs large field:** small fields of view (under 8 cm) with pixel dimensions as low as 0.076 mm suit endodontics; large fields are above 8 cm (Lam, 2012).

**Dose:**
- small-field CBCT: about 5.3-38.3 µSv;
- full-mouth series with D-speed film and round collimation: about 388 µSv;
- large-field CBCT: 74-1073 µSv.

**Endodontic uses:**
- periapical lesions hidden on periapical films;
- root and canal morphology (e.g. MB2);
- resorption;
- root fractures;
- surgical planning (distance to the sinus or canal);
- outcome assessment.

Small-field, higher-resolution machines are of greater value for these tasks (Lam, 2012). The list of uses beyond lesions and root fractures is [RECALL].

**Integration with magnification.** CBCT shows where to look, for example a suspected second canal; the microscope at about 8-12.5× shows it clinically (Lins et al, 2016). Sikri (2019) reports MB2 detection rising from 73% to 93% with the microscope. A vertical root fracture can be confirmed visually inside the canal (Lins et al, 2016).

## 8. Challenges

### Explanation

The learning curve is long early in training (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; purchase, maintenance and repair costs matter (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Cost:** purchase, maintenance and repair, for microscopes and sensors alike (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- **Learning curve:** early on the microscope can slow the work (Lins et al, 2016).
- **Dose:** CBCT must be justified, using the smallest field that answers the question [RECALL]; Lam (2012) notes dose varies widely with field size, kVp, mA and mode.
- **Image quality:** monitor and software limits; data management and storage. [RECALL]

## 9. Conclusion

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** The trends run in one direction: brighter, cooler, coaxial light (xenon, then LED); variable, ergonomic magnification (zoom microscopes, variable-focus optics, heads-up 3D systems); and digital images, both two-dimensional (CCD, CMOS and PSP sensors) and three-dimensional (small-field CBCT). Together they let the clinician see and record more, at lower dose, so treatment can be more precise and more conservative.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

1. **"Digital is better than film."** Lam (2012) reports lower dose and faster workflow, but film still resolves more than 22 lp/mm against about 11 lp/mm for typical CCD sensors, and monitors cannot show all the grey shades captured. Digital wins on dose and workflow rather than on raw resolution. [Certain]
2. **"Brighter light helps."** Light falls as magnification rises (Rubinstein, 2017), which is why stronger sources (xenon, then LED) are sought (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Gopikrishna, 2021). The books give no outcome data linking light source to treatment results. [Likely]
3. **"3D displays will replace the microscope."** Rubinstein (2017) says this is unknown. It is a trend, not established practice. [Certain]
4. **Currency.** No live literature search was run, so the newest developments (for example, very low-dose CBCT protocols) are not covered. [Certain]
