## 1. Introduction and aids

### Explanation

The eye resolves 0.2 mm (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; endodontics is work "inside a blind space" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Aids: loupes, procedure scope and microscope, plus endoscope and orascope (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Galilean loupes 2-4.5× are most recommended (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; ×3.5-×4.5 is the practical loupe limit; headlamps raise light up to four times (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** The unaided eye resolves only about 0.2 mm (Sikri, 2019), and endodontic work has long been done "inside a blind space" by touch and radiographs (Gopikrishna, 2021). Magnification with illumination makes the pulp chamber, canals and resected root end visible.

**Magnification aids:**
1. **Loupes.** Galilean loupes (2-4.5×) are the most recommended (Gopikrishna, 2021); prism loupes reach about 6×. The practical loupe limit is ×3.5-×4.5 (Rubinstein, 2017). A fibre-optic headlamp raises light up to four times that of the operatory light.
2. **Procedure scope:** camera and screen.
3. **Dental operating microscope (DOM / SOM):** variable 2-20× and more.
4. **Endoscope and orascope** (Sikri, 2019).

**Loupes vs procedure scope vs microscope (adapted from Sikri, 2019):**

| Feature | Loupes | Procedure scope | Microscope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head posture | 20-40° forward | Near neutral | Near neutral |
| Image | 3D, convergent | 2D on a screen | 3D, binocular |
| Light | Separate headlamp | Standard | Coaxial |
| Magnification | Fixed, 2-6× | Variable, 2-20× | Variable, 2-20× |

## 2. Principles that make the microscope useful

### Explanation

Coaxial illumination: the light path and line of sight are the same, so there are no shadows (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; two return beams give stereopsis (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Higher magnification narrows the field and depth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], which is not limiting with variable power (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Grossman's gives the ranges low 2.5-5×, medium 5-12× and high 12-20× (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Coaxial light:** the light travels along the line of sight, so there are no shadows (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Stereopsis:** the image returns as two beams, so depth is seen (Rubinstein, 2017).
- **Parallel (Galilean) optics:** the eyes are at rest, so long procedures are possible.
- **Variable power:** higher power narrows the field and depth, but the operator can step down whenever needed (Rubinstein, 2017).
- **Ranges (Gopikrishna, 2021):** low 2.5-5× for examination and access; medium 5-12× for most nonsurgical and surgical work; high 12-20× used sparingly, e.g. for instrument retrieval.

**The microscope in brief.** The body has four parts: eyepieces (10× or 12.5× most used), binoculars, magnification changer and objective lens (Gopikrishna, 2021). A 200 mm objective, giving about 8 inches of working distance, is recommended (Sikri, 2019). Light sources are xenon (daylight-like; halogen is yellow) and, in current models, powerful LEDs (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021). A 50:50 beam splitter feeds a camera or the assistant's binocular. The microscope is parfocalled before use, so the image stays sharp across the whole range (Rubinstein, 2017).

**Ergonomics in nonsurgical work.** The operator sits upright and neutral (Sikri, 2019). Sikri (2019) finds 12 o'clock comfortable for almost all teeth; Baumann and Beer (2010) use 12 o'clock for maxillary teeth and 8-9 o'clock for mandibular teeth. They advise four-handed work in the mandible and six-handed work in the maxilla.

## 3. Role in nonsurgical endodontics

### Explanation

Diagnosis of incipient caries and cracks (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; vertical root fracture seen on the canal wall (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. MB2 detection 73% to 93% (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Stropko's line (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]; troughing and micro-openers (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; dryness and sealer assessment (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Ledges, fragments and perforations are more predictable; post cement is seen (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; ultrasonic tips touch only the fragment; remaining filling material is seen (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Diagnosis:**
- Incipient caries and enamel and pulp-floor cracks (Sikri, 2019).
- An inconspicuous sinus tract can be traced at higher magnification (Sikri, 2019).
- A vertical root fracture can be seen on the inner canal wall, avoiding exploratory surgery. The dentine must not be over-dried, which makes it chalky and hides the line (Lins et al, 2016).

**B. Access and canal location:**
- Conservative access and removal of pulp stones.
- MB2 detection in maxillary first molars rose from 73% before magnification to 93% with the microscope (Sikri, 2019).
- Stropko's red or white line joins MB1 to MB2 (Lins et al, 2016).
- Calcified orifices are found by ultrasonic troughing and micro-openers (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Middle mesial and middle distal canals in mandibular molars are found more often (Sikri, 2019).
- Lins et al (2016) find 8× and 12.5× most useful for locating canals. Further aids are NaOCl bubbling over an orifice, 1% methylene blue, and 1% sodium fluorescein under a cobalt-blue filter, which makes orifices fluoresce green.

**C. Cleaning, shaping and obturation:** pulp remnants are removed; canal dryness and sealer distribution are checked (Sikri, 2019).

**D. Retreatment and mishaps:**
- Ledge bypass, fragment removal and perforation repair have become more predictable (Sikri, 2019).
- Thin ultrasonic tips touch only the fragment (Lins et al, 2016).
- Residual cement after post removal, and remaining gutta-percha and sealer, can be seen and removed. Once the canal walls are clean, causes of failure such as a vertical fracture, a missed canal or a perforation can be identified (Lins et al, 2016).
- Perforations in the floor or canal are sealed under direct vision, which favours a better seal (Lins et al, 2016).

## 4. Why apical surgery needed magnification

### Explanation

Hess's studies of the 1920s first showed canal complexity; Weller et al found a partial or complete isthmus 100% of the time at the 4 mm resection level in maxillary first molar mesiobuccal roots; 93% of ramifications lie in the apical 3 mm; West found every failed specimen had at least one unfilled portal of exit; failure to address this leaves the cause of failure and reinfection may recur; amalgam-sealed surgery fell to 57.7% success at 10 years (Frank et al) (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Reuben and Apotheker first reported microscope-aided apical surgery in 1984; Pecora presented on it at the AAE in 1990; Rubinstein and Carr began using it in 1990; 90% of endodontists used microscopes by 2008 (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Apical surgery aims to remove the part of the root that harbours uncleaned anatomy and to seal the canal from the root end. That anatomy is small and complex (Rubinstein, 2017):
- Hess's studies in the early 1920s first showed this complexity.
- Weller et al found a partial or complete isthmus in 100% of maxillary first molar mesiobuccal roots at the 4 mm level of resection.
- 93% of canal ramifications occur in the apical 3 mm.
- West found that 100% of failed specimens had at least one unfilled or underfilled portal of exit.

Leaving these untreated leaves the cause of failure in place, so reinfection can recur even after the lesion is removed. Traditional surgery done without magnification and with amalgam retrofills fell to 57.7% success at 10 years (Frank et al, cited by Rubinstein, 2017).

**Milestones (Rubinstein, 2017):** Reuben and Apotheker first reported microscope-aided apical surgery in 1984. Pecora presented on it at the AAE in 1990, and Rubinstein and Carr began using medical-grade microscopes for apical surgery in the same year. The first symposium on microscopic endodontic surgery followed in March 1993, and training in microscopy became mandatory for US specialty programmes from January 1997. By 2008, 90% of endodontists used the microscope.

## 5. Role of magnification at each step of surgery

### Explanation

Rubinstein's surgical ranges: about 2.5-8× orientation, 10-16× operating, 20-30× fine detail; more than ×30 is of little value; flaps need little magnification, but papilla-base incisions with 7-0 sutures need about ×4.3 or more; the SOM is used at its best for osteotomy, apicoectomy, root-end preparation, retrofilling and documentation; cracks on the bevelled surface are diagnosed (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri: magnification, illumination and micro-instruments form the microsurgical triad; isthmuses, fins and lateral canals are seen; osteotomy 0.5 cm against over 1.0 cm; bevel 10° against 45° (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Lins et al: the resection angle is perpendicular to the long axis; success above 90% against below 60% (Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. Ultrasonic tips were first designed for retrograde work (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; micro-mirrors are front-surface (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's lists the microsurgical uses (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Sikri (2019) calls magnification, illumination and micro-instruments the microsurgical triad. Rubinstein (2017) uses about ×2.5-×8 to orient, ×10-×16 to operate and ×20-×30 to inspect fine detail.

| Stage | Magnification and its role |
|---|---|
| Flap design and reflection | Low power is enough, but papilla-base incisions with 7-0 sutures need about ×4.3 or more (Rubinstein, 2017) |
| Osteotomy | Bone is told apart from root by colour; small osteotomy of about 0.5 cm against more than 1.0 cm conventionally (Sikri, 2019) |
| Root-end resection | Shallow bevel (about 10° against 45°); Lins et al (2016) describe a cut perpendicular to the long axis |
| Inspection of the resected surface | High power with a micro-mirror shows the isthmus, fins, lateral canals, missed canals, and craze lines or cracks (Rubinstein, 2017; Sikri, 2019) |
| Root-end preparation | Ultrasonic tips, first designed for retrograde work (Baumann and Beer, 2010), prepare the isthmus along the long axis; checked with a mirror |
| Retrofilling | MTA or bioceramic placed and condensed precisely and checked for voids |
| Suturing | Microsuturing (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Documentation | Every step recorded |

**Comparison (Sikri, 2019):**

| Feature | Conventional | Microsurgery |
|---|---|---|
| Finding the apex | Difficult | Easier |
| Osteotomy | More than 1.0 cm | 0.5 cm |
| Bevel | 45° | About 10° |
| Isthmus | Very difficult to see | Easy |
| Retrofill | Approximate | Precise |

**Illumination and mirrors in surgery.** Microscope light is two to three times that of a surgical headlamp (Rubinstein, 2017). Mirrors are essential to examine the bevelled root-end preparation, although direct vision is preferred where possible (Sikri, 2019). Front-surface micro-mirrors avoid double images and come as round 2-5 mm, oval or rectangular heads chosen for the space available.

**Outcome.** Lins et al (2016) report success below 60% for the traditional technique against above 90% with microscope and ultrasonics. Rubinstein (2017) says the SOM is used to best advantage for osteotomy, apicoectomy, root-end preparation, retrofilling and documentation.

## 5A. Choosing the aid for each surgical task

#### ✎ Exam core

| Task | Suitable aid |
|---|---|
| Incision, flap reflection, simple sutures | Naked eye or low-power loupes; little is gained from the microscope (Rubinstein, 2017) |
| Papilla-base incision, 7-0 microsutures | Microscope at about ×4.3 or more |
| Osteotomy, apicoectomy, root-end preparation, retrofill | Microscope, mid-range (Rubinstein, 2017) |
| Inspection of the resected surface | Microscope, high range, with micro-mirror; or endoscope |
| Bone removal and root-end isolation with an endoscope | ×2-×2.5 loupes plus endoscope (Bahcall and Barss, cited by Rubinstein, 2017) |

The choice follows the principle of usable power: the lowest magnification that shows the detail needed.

## 6. Endoscopy in surgical endodontics

### Explanation

A 0.9 mm endoscope optic gives up to 20× (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. For surgery Lins et al and Rubinstein recommend a 2.7 mm, 70° lens with a 3 cm rod lens, whereas Sikri recommends a 2.7 mm, 3 cm rod lens for nonsurgical use (Lins et al, 2016; Rubinstein, 2017; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Endoscopes give no image in blood; antifog solutions are advised; the image is 2D; close approach gives ×30-×40; Bahcall and Barss pair the endoscope with ×2-×2.5 loupes; orascopy complements rather than replaces the microscope (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. The endoscope should not retract gingiva, and haemostasis comes first (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Design:** rod-lens telescope with camera, light source and monitor. For surgery a 2.7 mm, 70° lens with a 3 cm rod lens is recommended (Lins et al, 2016; Rubinstein, 2017). Sikri (2019) assigns the 2.7 mm, 3 cm rod lens to nonsurgical use, so the books disagree.
- **Advantages:** the lens is brought close to the root end, giving about ×30-×40 and a non-fixed focus. Bahcall and Barss pair it with ×2-×2.5 loupes for flap and bone work (Rubinstein, 2017).
- **Limits:**
  - no discernible image in blood, so haemostasis is essential;
  - fogging, so antifog solution is needed;
  - a two-dimensional image;
  - the endoscope must not be used to retract the gingiva (Sikri, 2019).
- It complements, rather than replaces, the microscope (Rubinstein, 2017).

## 7. Ergonomics and the surgical team

### Explanation

Two assistants; the suction assistant watches through an assistant binocular and sees what the operator sees; job satisfaction rises; operating position depends on six factors; upright posture keeps the spine's S-curve (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

The surgeon sits upright, and the spine keeps its double S-curve, which loupes and endoscopes do not allow (Rubinstein, 2017). Two assistants are advised. The primary assistant suctions while watching through an assistant binocular, and so sees stereoscopically what the operator sees. The secondary assistant passes instruments into the surgeon's hand, so the surgeon never looks away from the eyepieces.

The operating position is set by six factors: the patient's head, the chair, the microscope, the doctor, the assistants and any observation devices. Bringing the assistant into the visual field raises job satisfaction (Rubinstein, 2017).

## 8. Limitations and documentation

### Explanation

The SOM does not improve physical access; the field darkens at high power; more than ×30 is of little value; learning depth perception takes time (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Documentation serves education, communication and legal records (Sikri, 2019; Lins et al, 2016) [SRC]. 3D vision systems are under development (Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Limitations:**
- The microscope does not improve physical access; where access is poor it is even harder with the microscope in place (Rubinstein, 2017).
- The field darkens as magnification rises.
- More than ×30 is of little practical value.
- Learning depth perception, and passing instruments under the lens, takes time and patience.
- Cost and set-up time. Rubinstein (2017) advises a university-based microsurgical course before buying a microscope, to avoid costly mistakes.
- Infection control: adhesive barriers and sterilisable rubber knobs cover the controls (Rubinstein, 2017).

**Documentation.** Images captured through the beam splitter serve patient and student education, communication with the referring dentist, and legal records (Sikri, 2019; Lins et al, 2016). After surgery, video stills can be reviewed with the patient alongside the digital radiographs (Rubinstein, 2017).

**Recent advance.** Heads-up 3D vision systems are being developed and may in time replace the endoscope (Rubinstein, 2017).

## 9. Conclusion

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** Magnification, with coaxial light and micro-instruments, has made nonsurgical endodontics more conservative and predictable, and has turned apical surgery into apical microsurgery. Isthmuses, cracks, missed canals and portals of exit can now be found and treated, so the cause of failure is removed rather than only the lesion.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

1. **Success of microsurgery.** Lins et al (2016) report success above 90% against below 60% for traditional surgery, and Rubinstein (2017) cites 57.7% at 10 years for amalgam-sealed surgery. The modern figure combines magnification with ultrasonic root-end preparation, a shallow bevel and modern retrofill materials, so magnification alone cannot be credited with the whole gain. [Likely]
2. **Anatomy as the reason.** Weller (an isthmus at the 4 mm level 100% of the time), the 93% apical ramification figure and West's unfilled portals of exit, all reported by Rubinstein (2017), explain why seeing the resected surface matters. This is mechanistic reasoning, not outcome evidence. [Likely]
3. **Bevel angle.** Sikri (2019) gives about 10° and Lins et al (2016) "perpendicular". Both mean a near-flat resection, and the difference is one of wording. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Microsurgical triad: magnification, illumination, micro-instruments.
- Surgical ranges: ×2.5-8 orient, ×10-16 operate, ×20-30 inspect; above ×30 little value.
- Osteotomy 0.5 cm; bevel about 10°; isthmus at 4 mm in MB roots 100% (Weller).
- 93% of ramifications in the apical 3 mm.
- >90% vs <60% success; amalgam surgery 57.7% at 10 years.
- Endoscope for surgery: 2.7 mm, 70°, 3 cm; needs haemostasis and antifog.
