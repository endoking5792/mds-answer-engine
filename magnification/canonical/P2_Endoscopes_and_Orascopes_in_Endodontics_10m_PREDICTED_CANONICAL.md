## Explanation

Endoscopy means "to see inside"; endoscopes were introduced into endodontics in 1979 to diagnose root fractures (Lins et al, 2016; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. The flexible endoscope has a Nitinol coating and a 0.9 mm optic giving 20× (Lins et al, 2016; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Lins et al and Rubinstein recommend 4 mm, 30°, 4 cm rods for nonsurgical use and 2.7 mm, 70°, 3 cm for surgery; Sikri recommends the 2.7 mm, 3 cm rod for nonsurgical use (Lins et al, 2016; Rubinstein, 2017; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Orascopes have been used since 1990: optical fibre, 0.8 mm tip, 0° lens, 15 mm working portion, focus from 0 to infinity; canal preparation to #90 over the cervical 15 mm (Lins et al) or the coronal half to 80-90 (Sikri); difficult in curves (Lins et al, 2016; Sikri, 2019; Rubinstein, 2017) [SRC]. Close approach gives ×30-×40; no image in blood; antifog needed; 2D images; endoscope not used to retract gingiva and haemostasis first; Bahcall and Barss pair it with ×2-×2.5 loupes; it complements the microscope (Rubinstein, 2017; Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definitions.** Endoscopy (Greek *endon*, inside; *skopion*, to see) means "to see inside". An endoscope is a set of tubular lenses coupled to a camera, light source and monitor. Endoscopes were first used in endodontics in 1979 to diagnose root fractures (Lins et al, 2016; Rubinstein, 2017). The orascope is a fibre-optic endoscope designed for intracanal viewing, in use since 1990 (Lins et al, 2016). "Orascopy" covers either device used in the mouth.

**Endoscope:**
- Rigid, semi-flexible or flexible. The flexible type has a Nitinol coating and a 0.9 mm optic giving 20× (Lins et al, 2016).
- Recommended lenses differ between books:

| Use | Lins et al (2016); Rubinstein (2017) | Sikri (2019) |
|---|---|---|
| Nonsurgical | 4 mm, 30°, 4 cm rod lens | 2.7 mm, 3 cm rod lens |
| Surgical | 2.7 mm, 70°, 3 cm rod lens | not given |

**Orascope (Lins et al, 2016):**
- Plastic or glass optical fibre, 0.8 mm tip, 0° lens, 15 mm working portion; light and flexible.
- Its focus runs from 0 to infinity, so it images the apical canal without reaching it (Sikri, 2019).
- The canal is enlarged first and dried. Lins et al (2016) specify the cervical 15 mm to #90; Sikri (2019) specifies the coronal half to size 80-90.

**Uses:**
- diagnosis of fractures and cracks;
- locating canals;
- checking cleaning, obturation and retreatment [RECALL];
- inspecting the resected root end, isthmus and portals of exit in surgery.

**Advantages:** the lens sits close to the object, giving about ×30-×40; a non-fixed focus; viewing at many angles (Rubinstein, 2017).

**Limitations:**
- no discernible image in blood, so haemostasis is essential, and the endoscope must not be used to retract gingiva (Sikri, 2019);
- condensation, so antifog solution is needed;
- a two-dimensional image (Rubinstein, 2017);
- difficult in curved canals.

**Role.** Bahcall and Barss pair the endoscope with ×2-×2.5 loupes for flap and bone work. Orascopy complements, but does not replace, loupes and the microscope (Rubinstein, 2017).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Endoscope in endodontics 1979 (fractures); orascope 1990.
- Orascope: 0.8 mm tip, 0° lens, 15 mm working portion, focus 0 to infinity.
- Surgical endoscope 2.7 mm, 70°, 3 cm.
