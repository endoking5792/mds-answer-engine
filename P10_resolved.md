## STUDY MODE

### S1. What each agent is [basic science]
- **SDF:** Craig's chapter describes a solution of about 30% silver diamine fluoride (about 25% silver and 5% fluoride) in water at a pH of about 10 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Sikri describes Ag(NH3)2F as a combination of silver nitrate and sodium fluoride and quotes a 38% solution (Sikri, 2020). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia edition, adapted from the 7th edition, also gives SDF 38% (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **CPP-ACP:** casein is the main phosphoprotein of bovine milk and CPP-ACP stabilises high concentrations of calcium and phosphate in a bioavailable form (Sikri, 2020). Mount's chapter says the nano-complexes stabilise calcium and phosphate ions in amorphous form, and fluoride can be added to give CPP-ACFP (Mount et al, 2016).
- **P11-4 (Curodont Repair):** the book names it as a self-assembling peptide and gives only review and trial results, with no composition or mechanism (Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **Glass ionomer:** a water-based cement that holds fluoride, calcium, strontium and aluminium ions that can migrate (Mount et al, 2016).

### S2. Mechanisms [mechanism]
- **SDF:** the high pH neutralises acids, and the strong antibacterial effect is mainly due to silver (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). The book passage read gives no collagen-protection mechanism (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- **Fluoride in general:** fluorapatite forms at the surface while hydroxyapatite dissolves beneath; fluoride cannot supply calcium and phosphate from biofilm, and high concentrations deposit calcium fluoride that releases fluoride slowly, faster at low pH (Sikri, 2020).
- **CPP-ACP:** the bound complex buffers free calcium and phosphate and raises their level in plaque, which inhibits demineralisation and enhances remineralisation; it binds the adhesin of S. mutans and is pH-responsive (Sikri, 2020). The complexes dissolve in saliva, localise in plaque and release ions into the lesion fluid, or enter the lesion intact and release bound ions there (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Dentine:** remineralisation of dentine means mineral precipitating between collagen fibrils, with collagen acting as the scaffold; overall mechanical properties do not change (Sikri, 2020).
- **GIC:** ion exchange needs water and GIC is water-based, so Mount calls it the ideal material for remineralisation (Mount et al, 2016). Fluoride can leave the cement into saliva and return when the saliva fluoride is high, so the cement acts as a fluoride reservoir (Mount et al, 2016). Strontium ions can penetrate partly demineralised dentine (Mount et al, 2016).
- **GIC, qualifications:** Braga (2021, Oral Biofilms and Modern Dental Materials) writes that fluoride does not prevent caries lesion development but slows its progression; that clinical evidence for the remineralising and antibacterial effects of GIC is scant and most often shows no significant effect on lesion development; that fluoride from GIC enters caries-affected dentine by ion exchange, but ultrastructural studies found mineral uptake rather than actual remineralisation; that a freshly mixed cement is antibacterial through high fluoride and low pH but this is lost with time; that raised enamel fluoride beside a restoration reflects de- and remineralisation and not a strengthening effect of the material; and that bioactivity of ion-releasing materials is expected to fall over time, as with glass ionomers (Braga, 2021). Braga's abstract states that glass ionomers show no evidence of intrinsic antimicrobial activity (Braga, 2021). Mount calls GIC the ideal material for remineralisation because it is water-based (Mount et al, 2016); the two statements concern different points and are given side by side.
- **GIC, fluoride time course, side by side:** a strong initial release declining over 2-3 months to a steady level (Mount, 1994); most ions in the first 2 days (5 to 155 ppm in different studies) and slow release for up to 3 years, with resin-modified GIC lower than conventional (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021); burst complete in the first 24 hours, then a sustained release likely far below the therapeutic level, with only restorative glass ionomers having legitimate potential against secondary caries and a luting film, base or liner unlikely to receive enough fluoride (Sturdevant 2nd South Asia edition; Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Dentine hypersensitivity text:** Mosquim et al (2024) list SDF among agents acting by tubule occlusion through protein precipitation, and also under mineral deposition (Mosquim et al, 2024); the same chapter notes a light-cured resin-modified GIC varnish (Vanish XT) with one 6-month clinical trial showing benefit (Mosquim et al, 2024).

### S3. Where glass ionomer fits [treatment relevance]
Mount says GIC seals the cavity by ion-exchange adhesion, prevents ingress of bacterial nutrients and reduces bacteria on the cavity floor to a dormant form (Mount et al, 2016). Sikri notes that set GIC is rechargeable from fluoride varnishes and toothpastes, with the disadvantages of technique sensitivity and opacity (Sikri, 2020). Summitt describes interim therapeutic restorations (ITRs) with conventional or resin-modified GIC, used to reduce cariogenic bacteria and make surfaces more cleansable (Hilton et al, 2013). Hill and Gillam list GIC among remineralising products (Hill and Gillam, 2024).

### S4. Indications and contraindications [treatment relevance]
- **SDF:** cavitated lesions, with advantages for children and geriatric populations (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); trained auxiliaries can apply it (Sikri, 2020); Crystal and Niederman say it suits cases where aesthetics are not a primary concern (Crystal and Niederman, 2019, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **CPP-ACP:** adjunct for high-risk patients, erosion with reflux, white spot lesions, orthodontic decalcification, desensitising (Sikri, 2020).
- **Restoration is avoided** for non-cavitated white spot lesions, secondary caries at the margin and root surface lesions, even if cavitated, which are managed with topical remineralising agents (Sikri, 2020). With infection control and remineralising agents, restoration placement can be reduced to about 50% (Sikri, 2020).

### S5. Staining and soft tissue [treatment relevance]
Craig says SDF stains tooth structure, skin and clothing, so after arrest the tooth needs covering with an aesthetic material to block the black stain (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Crystal and Niederman say it stains the lesions dark as it arrests them (Crystal and Niederman, 2019, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). Muntean and colleagues report that the black spot is diminished by immediate potassium iodide, but this may affect effectiveness (Muntean et al, 2024, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). The Sikri passage read does not mention staining (Sikri, 2020).

### S6. Clinical protocol as the books give it [treatment relevance]
- **SDF:** Sikri says twice-yearly application of the 38% solution "might be able to arrest" the carious process (Sikri, 2020). Craig gives no frequency and only the cover-up advice (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia edition (Ritter et al, 2019) says SDF 38% needs only removal of the biofilm and application with a microbrush for 3 minutes, then rinsing with water or covering with fluoride varnish, with petroleum jelly on gingiva and lips; the lesion is reassessed 1-2 weeks after the first application, re-applied if not arrested, and lesions not restored get a new application every 6 months (Ritter et al, 2019). The same edition reports that up to 50% of arrested lesions were reactivated by 24 months after one application and so supports restoring them (Ritter et al, 2019). Sikri and Craig give no step list.
- **Fluoride varnish (comparison):** Craig gives 5% sodium fluoride (22,600 ppm F) or 1% difluorosilane (1000 ppm F), semiannual application (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- **GIC:** hand excavation, conditioning, placement; see Q09 and P5.

### S8. Evidence statements [prognosis]
- **SDF, root caries in older adults:** three RCTs; prevented fraction 71% in a 3-year study and 25% in a 2-year study; no severe adverse effects (McReynolds and Duane, 2018, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **SDF, primary dentition:** 6 reports of 4 trials, 1118 randomised; at 24 months SDF reduced new dentine caries against placebo, no treatment and fluoride varnish; one trial compared SDF with high-viscosity GIC, and GIC was more effective than SDF at 12 months but the difference was not statistically significant (Oliveira et al, 2019, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). All studies had at least one domain of unclear or high risk of bias (Oliveira et al, 2019, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **SDF, children:** 16 RCTs, ages 18 months to 13 years, 12-30 months; SDF is effective for preventing and arresting caries in primary and permanent teeth and needs regular monitoring (Muntean et al, 2024, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **P11-4:** Wierichs and colleagues: may be a viable option for enamel caries, with caution because of few trials, short follow-up and limited grade of evidence (Wierichs et al, 2021, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). Keeper and colleagues: 6 trials; large increase in caries arrest (RR 1.82; NNT 2.8); all trials had elevated risk of bias (Keeper et al, 2023, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). Xie and colleagues: P11-4 with fluoride varnish and resin infiltration had advantages over fluoride varnish (Xie et al, 2023, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). Kobeissi and colleagues: recovery of white spot lesions better with P11-4 than with TCP fluoride varnish (Kobeissi et al, 2020, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **CPP-ACP, listed as each author states it:** Li and colleagues: long-term remineralising effect against placebo, not significantly different from fluorides, synergy with fluoride unclear (Li et al, 2014, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). Golzio and colleagues: CPP-ACP plus fluoride did not overcome fluoride alone, certainty very low (Golzio et al, 2024, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). Llena and colleagues: CPP-ACFP superior to fluoride varnish at 4 weeks on smooth surfaces; CPP-ACP not superior (Llena et al, 2015, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). Ekambaram and colleagues: CPP-ACP superior to fTCP and ACP forms (Ekambaram et al, 2017, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). Craig: sugar-free gum with CPP-ACP gave an 18% reduction in caries progression at 24 months (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Mount: no long-term clinical evidence for caries control yet (Mount et al, 2016).
- **GIC as a remineralising material:** RMGICs remineralise affected dentine similarly to conventional GICs, with differences between products; adding bioactive glass or CPP-ACP improves this; the review supports RMGIC after selective caries removal (Ghilotti et al, 2023, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024). Bioactive glass in GIC improves bioactivity but more glass decreases strength (De Caluwe et al, 2017, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **Abstract only:** in an in-vitro study of SDF-treated simulated carious dentine, the highest bond strength of self-cure high-viscosity GIC was with polyacrylic acid conditioning (Koh et al, 2024; abstract only, no clinical conclusion).
- **Overall:** Hill and Gillam say the consensus from reviews "appears somewhat inclusive [sic]" for some products, with lack of standardisation of studies (Hill and Gillam, 2024).

### S9. Limits [prognosis]
Most statements above are from reviews summarised in one table, several with unclear or elevated risk of bias, short follow-up or low certainty (Hill and Gillam, 2024). Fluoride alone cannot supply calcium and phosphate and is limited in fissures (Sikri, 2020). Mount's chapter says there is no clinical data for calcium sodium phosphosilicate bioactive glass (Mount et al, 2016).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 22 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Remineralisation and arrest aim to manage caries without drilling; agents are fluoride, silver diamine fluoride, CPP-ACP, P11-4 and glass ionomer (Sikri, 2020; Hill and Gillam, 2024).

### 2. Silver diamine fluoride
- **Composition:** about 30% SDF, about 25% silver and 5% fluoride, pH about 10 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); Sikri quotes 38% (Sikri, 2020); also 38% (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Action:** high pH neutralises acid; silver is strongly antibacterial (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- **Use:** cavitated lesions, children and the elderly; twice-yearly application might arrest caries; auxiliaries can apply it (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
- **Drawback:** black staining of tooth, skin and clothing; cover with an aesthetic material (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); potassium iodide reduces the stain but may affect effectiveness (Muntean et al, 2024, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).

### 3. CPP-ACP
Casein phosphopeptide stabilises calcium and phosphate; it buffers plaque, inhibits demineralisation and binds S. mutans adhesin (Sikri, 2020). Uses: white spot lesions, high-risk adjunct, orthodontic decalcification (Sikri, 2020). Evidence differs: not significantly different from fluorides (Li et al, 2014, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024); not superior to fluoride alone, very low certainty (Golzio et al, 2024, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024); no long-term clinical evidence (Mount et al, 2016).

### 4. Self-assembling peptide P11-4
Large increase in caries arrest, RR 1.82, NNT 2.8, all trials at elevated risk of bias (Keeper et al, 2023, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024); limited evidence grade (Wierichs et al, 2021, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).

### 5. Role of glass ionomer
- Water-based, so ion exchange is possible; fluoride reservoir; seals the cavity and makes bacteria dormant (Mount et al, 2016).
- Rechargeable from varnish and toothpaste (Sikri, 2020).
- ITR with GIC reduces bacteria and makes surfaces cleansable (Hilton et al, 2013).
- RMGIC remineralises affected dentine like GIC (Ghilotti et al, 2023, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).

### 6. Evidence table
| Question | Statement |
|---|---|
| SDF against placebo, 24 months | fewer new dentine lesions (Oliveira et al, 2019, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024) |
| SDF against high-viscosity GIC | GIC better at 12 months, not significant (Oliveira et al, 2019, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024) |
| SDF root caries, elderly | prevented fraction 71% (3 years), 25% (2 years) (McReynolds and Duane, 2018, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024) |

### 7. Limits
Evidence mostly from reviews with unclear or high risk of bias; fluoride gives no calcium or phosphate (Sikri, 2020; Hill and Gillam, 2024).

### 8. Conclusion
SDF arrests cavitated caries but stains; CPP-ACP and P11-4 are for non-cavitated lesions with limited evidence; GIC releases and recharges fluoride and seals the cavity; Mount et al (2016) call it ideal for remineralisation, Braga (2021) reports scant clinical evidence (Sikri, 2020; Hill and Gillam, 2024).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- SDF: about 30% (Craig, 25% Ag + 5% F, pH about 10) or 38% (Sikri), twice yearly; black stain (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
- SDF against high-viscosity GIC: GIC better at 12 months, not significant (Oliveira et al, 2019, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- P11-4: RR 1.82, NNT 2.8 for arrest, all trials elevated bias (Keeper et al, 2023, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- CPP-ACP plus fluoride not superior to fluoride alone (Golzio et al, 2024, quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- GIC: water-based, fluoride reservoir, ideal for remineralisation (Mount et al, 2016).
