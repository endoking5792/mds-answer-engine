# Discuss the different types of retentive pins used in restorative dentistry.

**Marks:** 25 · Essay

## Introduction

A pin-retained restoration is **"any restoration requiring the placement of one or more pins in dentin to provide adequate resistance and retention forms"** (Ritter et al, 2019). Pins are auxiliary features, used when slots, grooves or undercuts alone cannot give enough retention; **pins should be used only as an auxiliary means of retention** (Marzouk et al, n.d.). There are three types: **cemented, friction-locked and self-threading** (Ritter et al, 2019; Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013). They differ in how the pin is held in dentin, and that difference governs their retention, the stress they create and their current use.

**Diagram:** three pins side by side in dentin. (A) Cemented: channel wider than the pin, with a cement layer. (B) Friction-locked: channel slightly narrower, pin tapped in. (C) Self-threading: channel narrower, threads cut into dentin.

## Classification

| Type | Pinhole relative to pin | Retained by | Described by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cemented | 0.025–0.05 mm **larger** | Luting cement | Markley in 1958 |
| Friction-locked | 0.025 mm **smaller** | Resiliency of dentin (pin tapped in) | Goldstein in 1966 |
| Self-threading | 0.03–0.10 mm **smaller** | Threads engaging dentin | Going in 1966 |

(Ritter et al, 2019)

## 1. Cemented pins

- Threaded or serrated stainless steel pins in a slightly wider hole; **any standard dental luting agent** may be used (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Stress:** little or none. Even at the DEJ, cemented pins cause no enamel crazing (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- **Retention:** the least of the three. It rises in a straight line with depth, and failure occurs at the cement–dentin interface. Copper phosphate cement (non-vital teeth only) retains best, then zinc phosphate, polycarboxylate and ZOE; varnish under zinc phosphate cuts retention by almost 40% (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- **Niche use:** Marzouk et al advise against threaded or friction-grip pins in endodontically treated teeth and dead-tract dentin, and recommend cemented pins there (Marzouk et al, n.d.).

## 2. Friction-locked pins

- Tapped into a slightly smaller hole and held by dentinal resiliency; **2–3 times more retentive than cemented pins** (Ritter et al, 2019; Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- **Stress:** the greatest of any type, because impact forces add to the size mismatch. They cause the most crazing and have no practically safe distance from the DEJ (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- Their smooth surface makes them **the least retentive in amalgam and composite** (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- Friction-locked and cemented pins are still available but **rarely used** (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 3. Self-threading pins

- **The most frequently used parapulpal pin** (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Mechanism:** the elasticity of dentin lets a threaded pin enter a smaller hole (Ritter et al, 2019), with the thread acting as its own tap. In practice the cut thread is poor, and the pin is held by **tightly packed dentine chips** (McCabe and Walls, 2008). On pull-out the **dentinal threads shear, not the metal threads**, so current pins space their threads wider (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- **Retention:** the greatest in dentin. Its shape also gives the greatest retention of amalgam (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Stress:** intermediate (Marzouk et al, n.d.). Craze lines increase with pin size, and pulpal stress is greatest when the pin is inserted perpendicular to the pulp (Ritter et al, 2019).

**The books differ on the size of the advantage.** Sturdevant's puts self-threading pins at 3–6 times the retention of cemented pins (Ritter et al, 2019). Marzouk et al put it at 5–6 times, for pins of equal diameter and depth (Marzouk et al, n.d.). Both give the same order.

### Thread Mate System (TMS)

The most widely used self-threading system (Ritter et al, 2019):

| Size | Colour | Diameter | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular | Gold | 0.78 mm | Most cracks reaching the pulp; rarely used |
| Minim | Silver | 0.61 mm | Backup for an over-prepared Minikin hole |
| Minikin | Red | 0.48 mm | Pin of choice in badly broken-down posterior teeth |
| Minuta | Pink | 0.38 mm | Usually too small for posterior teeth |

**Designs:** standard, self-shearing, two-in-one, Link Series (self-aligning in a plastic sleeve) and Link Plus (sharper thread, shoulder stop at 2 mm) (Ritter et al, 2019). A shoulder stops the pin end from stressing dentin at the base of the channel (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013; McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## Pin materials

- **Gold-plated stainless steel or titanium alloy** (Ritter et al, 2019; Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- Plating does less than expected: mercury dissolves a silver layer, and gold must be pure and clean to react. **All-silver pins are ineffective** because they are plastic and have an affinity for mercury (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- A metal pin conducts heat and cold through an insulating lining (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## Comparison

| Property | Cemented | Friction-locked | Self-threading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retention in dentin | Least | Intermediate | Greatest |
| Stress and crazing | Least | Greatest | Intermediate |
| Hold on the restorative material | Good (serrated) | Least (smooth) | Greatest |
| Current use | Rare | Rare | Routine |

(Ritter et al, 2019; Marzouk et al, n.d.)

## Clinical significance

Every pin weakens the restoration: pins reduce tensile and horizontal strength (Ritter et al, 2019) and **should be used sparingly** (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Pins do not increase compressive strength; they only hold the material mechanically (Marzouk et al, n.d.). The usual choice is the smallest self-threading pin that gives enough retention.

## Conclusion

Cemented pins give the least stress and retention, friction-locked pins the most stress, and self-threading pins the best retention for moderate stress. Self-threading pins (mainly TMS Minikin and Minim) are therefore standard, and cemented pins keep a niche in non-vital dentin. Every type stays auxiliary to the preparation's own retention.
