# Discuss in detail pin – retained restorations.

**Marks:** 20 · Long Essay

**Answer blueprint:** Definition → Indications → Advantages and disadvantages → Types of pins → Factors governing retention → Pin placement (size, number, location) → Technique → Failure → Pins vs slots → Conclusion

## Definition

A pin-retained amalgam is **"any restoration requiring the placement of one or more pins in dentin to provide adequate resistance and retention forms"** (Ritter et al, 2019). **Pins should be used only as an auxiliary means of retention** (Marzouk et al, n.d.).

## Indications

- Loss of **4 mm or more of vertical coronal height** from caries or cusp fracture (Ritter et al, 2019).
- When slots, grooves or undercuts alone cannot provide resistance and retention (Ritter et al, 2019).
- As an **economic provisional restoration** while an endodontic or periodontal prognosis is uncertain (Marzouk et al, n.d.).

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Significantly greater retention than boxes alone or bonding alone (Ritter et al, 2019) | Craze lines, fractures and internal stresses in dentin (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| | Risk of pulpal penetration or external perforation (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| | Lower tensile and horizontal strength of the amalgam (Ritter et al, 2019) |

## Types of pins

**Cemented** (pinhole larger than the pin), **friction-locked** (slightly smaller; 2–3 times more retentive than cemented) and **self-threading** (smaller; the most retentive and most used). Cemented and friction-locked pins are now rarely used. The Thread Mate System (TMS), in gold-plated stainless steel or titanium, is the most widely used (Ritter et al, 2019).

## Factors governing retention

1. **Type and surface:** self-threading > friction-locked > cemented; threads also hold amalgam best (Ritter et al, 2019).
2. **Orientation:** non-parallel pins retain better, but **bending pins to improve their retention in amalgam is not advised** (Ritter et al, 2019).
3. **Number and diameter:** more pins and wider pins retain more, but crazing, the risk of fracture and the weakening of the amalgam all increase (Ritter et al, 2019).
4. **Extension:** about **1.5–2 mm into dentin and into amalgam** (Ritter et al, 2019). Marzouk et al add an ideal dentinal-to-protruding ratio of **2:1** (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
5. **Dentin:** young, vital dentin holds best; sclerosed or non-vital dentin holds poorly (Marzouk et al, n.d.).

## Pin placement

- **Size:** the Minikin (0.48 mm) is the pin of choice in badly broken-down posterior teeth, with the Minim as backup. The Minuta is usually too small and the Regular causes the most cracks (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Number:** **one pin per missing axial line angle**, and as few as possible (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Location:** near line angles, parallel to the external surface, away from fluted and furcal areas, with 2 mm of amalgam over the pin and none directly under an occlusal contact (Ritter et al, 2019).

**The books differ on distance and spacing:**

| Parameter | Sturdevant's (Ritter et al, 2019) | Summitt's (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) | Marzouk et al (n.d.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distance from DEJ | 0.5–1 mm (or 1–1.5 mm from the external surface, whichever is greater) | At least 0.5 mm; 1.0 mm preferable | 1.5–2.0 mm for threaded pins |
| Pinhole depth | 2 mm | 2–3 mm optimum (Dilts' study) | — |
| Minimum interpin distance | 3 mm (Minikin), 5 mm (Minim) | — | 4 mm for small threaded pins |

Marzouk's distances are the most cautious, set to avoid enamel crazing.

## Technique (TMS)

1. Rubber dam (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013); pilot dimple with a No. ¼ round bur.
2. Twist drill aligned with the external surface, run at **300–500 rpm** in one or two movements, withdrawn while still rotating.
3. Pin threaded in until it resists, then turned back **a quarter to a half turn** to relieve dentinal stress.
4. Any length beyond 2 mm removed with a bur perpendicular to the pin; bending only with the bending tool (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Perforation:** a pulpal perforation is covered with calcium hydroxide or MTA and a new site is chosen. An external perforation above the epithelial attachment is filled with amalgam (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).

## Failure

Failure can occur in five places: within the restoration, at the pin–restoration interface, within the pin, at the pin–dentin interface, or within dentin. It is **more likely at the pin–dentin interface** (Ritter et al, 2019).

## Pins vs slots

Slots suit short clinical crowns and cusps reduced by only 2–3 mm, cause fewer microfractures and perforations, and **the retention potential of slots and pins is similar** (Ritter et al, 2019). Amalgapins and bonding are further alternatives (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).

## Conclusion

Pins reliably restore a badly broken-down vital tooth when used as auxiliaries: the smallest suitable self-threading pin, the fewest pins, correct location and angulation, and adequate amalgam bulk.
