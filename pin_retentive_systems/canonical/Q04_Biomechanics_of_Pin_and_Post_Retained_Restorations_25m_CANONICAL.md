## Introduction

**Retention** is the prevention of dislodgment along the path of insertion (tensile forces); **resistance** is the prevention of dislodgment or fracture by oblique or compressive forces (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013). Pins (in vital dentin) and posts (in root canals) add retention but stress the dentin holding them, so their biomechanics balances retention against induced stress (Marzouk et al, n.d.).

## Biomechanics of pin-retained restorations

### 1. Stresses induced in dentin

Inserting a pin always stresses dentin. **If these stresses exceed the elastic limit of the dentin, permanent (plastic) deformation will occur**, followed by micro- or macrocracks (Marzouk et al, n.d.). Factors (Marzouk et al, n.d.):

- **Pin type:** little or no stress with cemented pins, maximum with friction-grip pins, intermediate with threaded pins.
- **Diameter and depth:** greater diameter and deeper engagement give greater stress. Modern pins therefore have fewer, wider-spaced, sharper threads.
- **Dentin:** more bulk spreads stress; non-vital or sclerosed dentin tolerates it poorly, so threaded and friction-grip pins are avoided in endodontically treated teeth.
- **Interpin distance:** pins closer than 4 mm (small threaded pins) concentrate stress.
- **Lever ratio:** the ideal ratio of dentinal engagement to protrusion is **2:1**. Below 1:1, the dentinal part of the pin becomes **the short side of a type one lever**.

Sturdevant's adds that pulpal stress peaks when a pin is inserted perpendicular to the pulp (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 2. Retention of the pin in dentin

Self-threading pins are the most retentive, friction-locked pins intermediate and cemented pins the least (Ritter et al, 2019). Marzouk et al put the self-threading advantage at 5–6 times and friction grip at 2–3 times the retention of cemented pins (Marzouk et al, n.d.). For small threaded pins retention levels off after 2 mm in dentin and failure then occurs within the pin; for cemented pins it rises linearly with depth (Marzouk et al, n.d.).

### 3. Pins and the restorative material

- **Pins will not increase the compressive strength** of amalgam or composite; they only hold it mechanically (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- **Tensile strength depends on pin orientation** (Marzouk et al, n.d.):

| Pin relative to the tensile stress | Loss of tensile strength |
|---|---|
| At right angles | 30–40% |
| At 45° | 10% |
| Parallel | None |

This is why a gingival pin is avoided in the isthmus of a preparation (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- Strength is also lost when pins are closer than 2 mm (Marzouk et al, n.d.); Sturdevant's asks for 2 mm of amalgam over the pin (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 4. Failure sites

Failure can occur within the restoration, at the pin–restoration interface, within the pin, at the pin–dentin interface, or within dentin. It is **most likely at the pin–dentin interface** (Ritter et al, 2019).

## Biomechanics of post-retained restorations

### 1. Features that reduce stress in the root

Marzouk et al describe (Marzouk et al, n.d.):

- **Ferrule:** a cast collar embracing the tooth near the crown–root junction. It is the **major protecting feature** against induced stress.
- **Flat planes** at right angles to occlusal forces (tables, floors, ledges) are **the second major feature**.
- **Root-to-crown portion of the post 2:1**. Less than 1:1 concentrates stress at the post apex.
- **Length:** **one-half to two-thirds** of the root canal. The thin apical third must be avoided, because stress concentrated at the post tip there leads to catastrophic failure.
- A space between post and root filling; lateral vents against **hydraulic pressure** at cementation.
- **Threads:** continuous threads create more stress than interrupted threads.

### 2. Retention of posts

| Factor | Effect (Marzouk et al, n.d.) |
|---|---|
| Surface | Threaded posts six to eight times, serrated three to four times as retentive as smooth posts |
| Shape | Parallel-sided posts 2–3 times as retentive as tapered posts |
| Forces | Torsion is the most disengaging force, so a rounded post needs an anti-rotation device (lateral pin, groove) |

van Noort and Barbour agree on the trade-off: **self-threading posts have excellent retention but a high incidence of tooth fracture**, and tapered posts are the least retentive (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

## Methods of stress analysis

Stresses in teeth and restorations, **endodontic posts** included, have been studied by **brittle-coating analysis, strain gauges, holography, two- and three-dimensional photoelasticity and finite element analysis** (Craig, 2002).

**1. Photoelasticity**
- A transparent isotropic model **becomes anisotropic when stressed** and shows **double refraction** under polarised light.
- The isochromatic fringes **"represent locations where the difference in the principal stresses is a constant"**. Higher fringe orders mean higher stress, and closely spaced fringes mean a steep stress gradient.
- **Advantages:** quantifies stress and gradients. **Limits:** needs a birefringent material; difficult with complex shapes (Craig, 2002).

**2. Finite element analysis (FEA)**
- The structure is divided into **a finite number of discrete elements interconnected at nodes**, each with the elastic modulus and Poisson's ratio of its material. Loads and boundary constraints are then applied.
- **Advantages:** handles complex geometry in three dimensions. **Limits:** purely numerical, assumption-based and costly; best for **predicting trends and parametric analyses**, needing experimental validation (Craig, 2002).

**3. Strain gauges, brittle coatings and holography** are the other methods Craig lists (Craig, 2002). Because a strain gauge is bonded at one spot, it can only report strain at that spot; photoelasticity and FEA map the whole field.

## Conclusion

Pins and posts both act as levers in dentin and are safe only while induced stress stays within what dentin tolerates: small, spaced, well-oriented pins; long, parallel, well-fitting posts with a ferrule. Photoelasticity and FEA underpin these rules, FEA mainly for trends.
