# Critically evaluate the status of pin-retained restoration in view of recent restorative materials.

**Marks:** 20 · Long Essay

**Answer blueprint:** Introduction → What pins offer → Problems with pins → Pins with tooth-coloured materials → Recent alternatives (mechanical, adhesive, indirect) → Evidence comparing pins with bonding → Present status → Conclusion

## Introduction

A pin-retained restoration uses one or more pins in dentin to provide resistance and retention form (Ritter et al, 2019). **Threaded pins or retentive grooves placed in dentin have served well for years**, but adhesive monomers have since achieved clinical success (Anusavice et al, 2013). Pins must now be weighed against their biological cost and against adhesive and non-pin methods.

## What pins offer

- **Significantly greater retention** than boxes alone or bonding alone (Ritter et al, 2019).
- In laboratory tests loaded at 90°, stainless steel pins give significantly more resistance than amalgam inserts (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).

## Problems with pins

- **Dentin:** craze lines, fractures and internal stress, worse with larger pins (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Perforation** into the pulp or periodontium (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- **Pulp:** medium-sized self-threading pins may cause inflammation if placed within 0.5 mm of the pulp (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Restoration:** pins lower tensile and horizontal strength (Ritter et al, 2019). Placed at right angles to the tensile stress, they cut the material's tensile strength by 30–40% (Marzouk et al, n.d.). **All dentine pins weaken the restoration** (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- **Thermal:** a pin bypasses an insulating lining (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## Pins with tooth-coloured materials

- Composite adapts to pins less well than amalgam and has more voids, so **pin-retained composites fail more often than pin-retained amalgams** (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- **GICs should not be used for pin-retained cores**, or for other high-tensile uses (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- Pins have therefore not moved well to the newer aesthetic materials; their partner is still amalgam.

## Recent alternatives

**1. Non-pin mechanical features**

| Feature | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Slots | Retention similar to pins; fewer microfractures and perforations; indicated in short crowns (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| Circumferential slot | No significant difference from four TMS pins in vitro; pin restorations slipped on their bases more (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) |
| Amalgapins | Resistance similar to pins at a depth of 1.5–2.0 mm (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013) |

**2. Adhesive (bonded) amalgam.** McCabe and Walls call chemically active adhesive resins **the most recent innovation for retention of amalgam** (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Filled resins work best (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).

**3. Indirect restorations.** For cuspal coverage, many regard a gold casting as the restoration of choice, and high-strength ceramics are viable alternatives. Amalgam remains the option when an indirect restoration cannot be chosen (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).

**4. Endodontically treated teeth.** Foundations are retained by posts, or by the pulp chamber in molars, not by pins (Ritter et al, 2019).

## Evidence: pins vs bonding

- A six-year clinical trial compared 32 bonded and 28 TMS-pin complex amalgams, each replacing at least one cusp. **After 5 years, seven of the pin-retained and two of the bonded restorations had failed. None of the bonded amalgams had debonded**, and results were equal at 6 years (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- Summitt's concludes that **properly bonded amalgam restorations will be as successful as pin-retained amalgam restorations** (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- **Pins and bonding can have an additive effect**: in one in vitro study, the two together roughly equalled the sum of each alone (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).
- **Weakness of the evidence:** most resistance data come from flattened molars loaded at 45°. These are lab models and not as informative as long-term clinical trials (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013).

**Two views:** Sturdevant's says pins give more retention than bonding alone (Ritter et al, 2019); Summitt's says bonded amalgam survives as well clinically (Overton, Summitt and Osborne, 2013). These do not conflict. One compares retention values and the other clinical survival, and in the trial reported the higher retention of pins did not give better survival.

## Present status

1. **No longer the first choice** for routine complex amalgams: slots, amalgapins and bonding give similar resistance with less risk.
2. **Still indicated** in preparations with few or no vertical walls (Ritter et al, 2019), especially where bonding is unreliable.
3. **Best combined with bonding**, because the effects add up.
4. **Limited to amalgam in vital teeth.** Composite and GIC work poorly with pins, and endodontically treated teeth get posts.

## Conclusion

Pins remain mechanically effective but cost dentin, pulp and restoration strength. Adhesive amalgam, slots, amalgapins and indirect cuspal coverage now achieve comparable results more safely, so the pin is today a **selective adjunct**: the smallest, fewest pins, used with bonding where little tooth remains.
