## STUDY MODE
### S1. Why glass-ionomer should seal
GIC bonds chemically: carboxyl groups of the cement bond to calcium ions of the tooth. Being hydrophilic it bonds to a slightly wet surface. Three further properties limit leakage: a coefficient of thermal expansion close to tooth, fluoride release, and the ability to renew broken ionic bonds (Sikri, 2020). Craig adds that adhesion enhances marginal sealing compared with non-adhesive cements, though it does little for retention (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Mount goes furthest: ion exchange is "the only material" that gives a union "proof against microleakage" (Mount et al, 2016), and the Atlas states there is "no microleakage at the margin" (Mount, 1994).

### S2. Why it can still leak: the moisture problem
- **Early water exposure:** matrix-forming ions leach out during the initial set and may interfere at the tooth-restoration interface. Sikri asks for isolation from moisture for at least 30 minutes after placement; solubility in oral fluids remains a problem for 24 hours after insertion (Sikri, 2020). These are two different figures: 30 minutes is isolation, 24 hours is solubility.
- **Desiccation:** excessive drying crazes and cracks the surface; if the crazing reaches the margin, marginal leakage is considerable (Sikri, 2020). Phillips: the cement stays vulnerable to dehydration until it matures over a few weeks, so a type II restoration is varnished before dismissal (Anusavice et al, n.d.).
- **Maturation time differs by book:** McCabe and Walls say maturation continues for at least an hour, up to 24 hours with some materials, and the surface must be protected for that time (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Mount advises maturation under a waterproof sealant for a minimum of 24 hours (Mount, 1994).
- **Early finishing:** sharp hand instruments before full set harm the margin (Sikri, 2020); hand carving damages marginal integrity, so trim from restoration towards tooth (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Long term:** Pickard lists marginal dissolution and shrinkage of GIC as a cause of loss of marginal integrity (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). Goldstein 3rd ed reports laser studies in which GIC and RMGI "failed to prevent microleakage" (Goldstein, 2018).

### S3. The isolation dilemma
McCabe and Walls describe a "dichotomy": rubber dam gives the best moisture control but risks desiccating the cement; cotton rolls or dry guards are usually adequate. Saliva on the cavity leaves a pellicle that interferes with bonding; it is removed with poly(acrylic) acid for 15 seconds (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### S4. Resin-modified GIC
Rapid light-activated setting reduces the effect of moisture contamination, but RMGI has "occasionally" leaked more than chemically cured GIC: resin polymerisation shrinkage, fast setting with no stress relief, and less water and carboxylic acid reducing wetting. Water uptake expansion partly compensates (Sikri, 2020). Phillips states the same mechanism without the "occasionally" qualifier (Anusavice et al, n.d.). McCabe and Walls: the resin protects RMGI from moisture, so varnish or resin coating is not advocated for most products, and RMGI can be finished immediately, whereas conventional GIC finishing is best delayed 24 hours (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Under composite, a light-cured GIC base performs better than a chemically cured one, because it adheres at once and gains early strength (Sikri, 2020). The books disagree on RMGI leakage: Sikri (2020) and Phillips 12th (Anusavice et al, n.d.) say it can leak more than conventional GIC, whereas Seltzer and Bender 2nd ed reports a quantitative study ranking RMGI first for preventing bacterial leakage in nonexposed Class V restorations (100%, no leakage) (Hargreaves et al, 2012).

### S5. Surface protection: which agent
Sikri: two coats of varnish or a layer of unfilled resin. Unfilled resin resists water better, yet Sikri still prefers varnish because it adheres closely to the surrounding enamel (Sikri, 2020). McCabe and Walls: unfilled methacrylate resins are "by far the most effective" (McCabe and Walls, 2008). The Atlas shows a varnished GIC dyed at 7 minutes with severe dye penetration, and a resin-sealed one with none (Mount, 1994). Sturdevant: GIC lutes must be protected immediately with petroleum jelly or varnish, because they are quite soluble in the first 24 h after mixing (Ritter, 2019).

## EXAM MODE
**(399 words; about 18 min at 22 words/min)**

**Introduction.** Marginal integrity is the continuity of the seal between restoration and cavity wall. Glass-ionomer seals by chemical adhesion: carboxyl groups bond to tooth calcium (Sikri, 2020).

**Factors favouring a seal**
- Ion-exchange adhesion; Mount calls it "proof against microleakage" (Mount et al, 2016).
- Coefficient of thermal expansion close to tooth.
- Fluoride release and renewal of broken ionic bonds (Sikri, 2020).
- Bonds to a slightly moist surface.

**Factors causing leakage**
1. *Early water contact:* matrix ions leach during the initial set. Isolate for at least 30 minutes; solubility persists for 24 hours (Sikri, 2020).
2. *Desiccation:* crazing that reaches the margin leaks (Sikri, 2020).
3. *Early hand finishing:* tears margins, causing ditching (Sikri, 2020); hand carving damages marginal integrity (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
4. *Low powder:liquid ratio:* raises solubility (Sikri, 2020).
5. *Long term:* marginal dissolution (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

**Resin-modified GIC.** Quick light-cure limits moisture damage, but polymerisation shrinkage and poorer wetting can increase leakage over conventional GIC (Sikri, 2020; Anusavice et al, n.d.). Varnish is usually unnecessary and finishing can be immediate (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**Measures to reduce microleakage**
- Clean cavity; condition with polyacrylic, tannic or citric acid (Sikri, 2020); polyacrylic acid for 15 s removes salivary pellicle (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Correct powder:liquid ratio and manipulation (Sikri, 2020).
- Isolation: rubber dam risks desiccation, so cotton rolls usually suffice (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Petroleum jelly during finishing; rotary, not hand, instruments (Sikri, 2020).
- Surface sealing: two coats of varnish or unfilled resin (Sikri, 2020). Unfilled resin is the most effective (McCabe and Walls, 2008); a varnished GIC showed severe dye penetration, a resin-sealed one none (Mount, 1994).
- Delay final finishing of conventional GIC by 24 hours (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Under composite, prefer light-cured GIC base (Sikri, 2020).

**Where books differ**
| Point | Sikri 2020 | McCabe and Walls 2008 | Mount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protect for | 30 min isolation | 1 to 24 h | 24 h sealant |
| Sealant | Varnish preferred | Resin best | Resin |

Phillips says protection is needed for a few weeks (Anusavice et al, n.d.).

**Conclusion.** Glass-ionomer seals well when moisture balance is controlled and the surface is sealed; claims that it never leaks are Mount's position, and the dye images in his Atlas (courtesy of Smales) show leakage under varnish alone (Mount, 1994).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Chemical bond: carboxyl groups to tooth calcium (Sikri, 2020).
- Isolate at least 30 min; solubility for 24 h; two figures, not one (Sikri, 2020).
- Both water contact and desiccation damage the margin (Sikri, 2020; McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Rotary finishing with petroleum jelly; no hand carving (Sikri, 2020; van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- Sealant: Sikri prefers varnish; McCabe and Walls and Mount favour unfilled resin.
- RMGI can leak more than conventional GIC (Sikri, 2020; Anusavice et al, n.d.) but needs no varnish (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- "Proof against microleakage" is Mount's view, not shared by Pickard or Goldstein.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Writing "keep GIC dry for 24 hours" as Sikri's figure; Sikri says isolate 30 minutes, solubility lasts 24 hours.
2. Treating desiccation as harmless; crazing at the margin causes leakage.
3. Recommending hand instruments for finishing.
4. Stating that GIC "never leaks" as settled fact.
5. Recommending varnish for RMGI routinely.
