## STUDY MODE

### S1. Principle
A dye with a colour contrasting with tooth and restoration is allowed to enter any gap at the interface; the depth it reaches is taken as a measure of leakage. Sikri calls it "one of the oldest and most commonly used methods" (Sikri, 2020, p.445), and Coluzzi and Parker call the two-dimensional dye test the most common in vitro method because of low cost and ease of use (Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).

### S2. Dyes and tracers
- Sikri lists methylene blue, India ink, crystal violet, fluorescein, rhodamine B, eosin, basic fuchsin and erythrosin, as solutions or particle suspensions; the ideal dye does not bond to tooth or restoration and is colour stable (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- Coluzzi and Parker list methylene blue, silver nitrate, basic fuchsin, propylene glycol and acid red (Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).
- Craig's names methylene blue or silver nitrate (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Silver nitrate (50% aqueous) is reduced to metallic silver, which stays in place and "cannot diffuse away or fade as is common with water-soluble dyes" (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).
- Radioisotope molecules are only 40 nm against 120 nm for the smallest dye particles, so isotopes penetrate finer gaps (Sikri, 2020, p.445).

### S3. Method
- Restored teeth are stored, usually with thermal cycling, in dye or another marker; then sectioned, and the seal is judged by how far the dye has run down the margin toward the cavity floor (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Sikri: immerse for a set period, remove, wash, section, examine under a microscope, and score penetration by assigning numbers to defined depths (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- Craig's: score qualitatively (scores) or measure quantitatively (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- A three-dimensional method needs no sectioning and gives an objective reading (Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).
- Endodontic variant: roots coated with nail varnish except the area under test, then sectioned or cleared [Likely].

### S4. Limitations
- Different particle sizes give inconsistent results even with standard technique; penetration times of two dyes ranged from 5 minutes to 1 hour (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- Dye may bind tooth or material: basic fuchsin binds carious dentin, so stained caries can be read as a larger gap (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- Trapped air is hard to remove from small spaces even under vacuum (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- Colour instability: aniline blue becomes colourless in alkaline conditions such as calcium hydroxide (Sikri, 2020, p.445). In the same way, methylene blue is discoloured by calcium hydroxide, so it should not be used to test MTA and other cements that release it (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- Formalin storage and dye pH can alter results (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- Scoring is subjective, "a major disadvantage" (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- Tests disagree: grey MTA resisted bacteria better than CEM cement, but dye penetration showed no difference (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017). Comparing Thermafil with lateral condensation, fluid filtration, dye/electrochemical and bacterial tests gave three different verdicts (Karagenç et al, 2006) [LIT, PubMed abstract].
- All in vitro: thermocycling cannot reproduce the dynamic pulpo-dentinal complex (Sikri, 2020, p.444).

### S5. What dye studies have shown (endodontics)
- Lateral condensation gave more apical dye leakage than heated multiphase gutta-percha in vitro (Gilhooly et al, cited in Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- MTA leaked significantly less than amalgam and Super EBA in dye and bacterial leakage studies (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019); most root-end studies found MTA more resistant to dye than Super EBA, amalgam or IRM, with no difference between white and grey MTA (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- All obturation materials and techniques leak to some degree, and radiographs correlate poorly with leakage (Berman et al, 2021).
- Coronal leakage: teeth exposed to artificial saliva for 3 to 56 days leaked 79 to 85% of root length, with no difference between periods (Swanson and Madison, 1987) [LIT, full text]. Sealer study: AH26 leaked significantly more than Sealapex and Roth's (Madison et al, 1987) [LIT, full text]. In monkeys, dye entered all groups with no difference among sealers, unlike the in vitro ranking (Madison and Wilcox, 1988) [LIT, full text].

## EXAM MODE
**(364 words; about 17 min at 22 words/min)**

**Definition.** A dye leakage study is an in vitro test of marginal seal: a restored or root-filled extracted tooth is exposed to a coloured tracer, and the depth the dye travels along the interface is measured. It is the oldest and most widely used leakage test, being cheap and simple (Sikri, 2020; Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).

**Dyes.** Methylene blue, India ink, crystal violet, fluorescein, rhodamine B, eosin, basic fuchsin, erythrosin (Sikri, 2020); silver nitrate as a chemical tracer (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Ideal dye: contrasting colour, no binding to tooth or material, colour stable.

**Method.**
1. Restore or obturate extracted teeth; thermocycle (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
2. Coat surfaces with varnish except the margin under test (apical or coronal in endodontics) [Likely].
3. Immerse in dye for a set time, preferably under vacuum.
4. Wash, section (or clear), examine under a microscope.
5. Score depth on a numbered scale or measure linearly (Sikri, 2020).

**Limitations.**
- Particle size and concentration differ; two dyes took 5 minutes to 1 hour to penetrate (Sikri, 2020).
- Dye binding: basic fuchsin stains carious dentin and exaggerates the gap (Sikri, 2020).
- Trapped air blocks entry (Sikri, 2020).
- Colour loss in alkali; methylene blue is unsuitable for MTA (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- Water-soluble dyes diffuse and fade; silver does not (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).
- Subjective scoring (Sikri, 2020).
- In one study, dye and bacterial penetration results did not match (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- Radiographs do not predict leakage (Berman et al, 2021).

**Textbooks disagree.** Sikri (2020) says dye type changes results; Coluzzi and Parker (2023) say it does not. Berman et al (2021): no standardised technique; McCabe and Walls (2008): ISO TR 11405 gives guidelines.

**Endodontic findings.**
- Lateral condensation leaked more dye apically than heated gutta-percha (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- MTA leaked less than amalgam and Super EBA (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- All obturation techniques leak to some degree (Berman et al, 2021).
- Saliva-exposed root fillings leaked 79 to 85% of root length within days (Swanson and Madison, 1987).

**Conclusion.** Dye studies rank materials within one protocol, but they are in vitro, unstandardised and not proof of clinical outcome.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Oldest, commonest, cheapest leakage test (Sikri, 2020; Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).
- Score = depth of dye; subjective (Sikri, 2020).
- Basic fuchsin binds carious dentin; aniline blue fades in alkali (Sikri, 2020).
- Methylene blue not for MTA (calcium hydroxide) (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- Silver nitrate does not fade or diffuse (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).
- MTA leaks less than amalgam and Super EBA (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- No standardised technique (Berman et al, 2021); ISO TR 11405 guidelines exist (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Presenting dye depth as a measure of clinical failure.
2. Using methylene blue results for MTA without the calcium hydroxide caveat.
3. Saying dye choice does not matter without noting that Sikri says it does.
4. Quoting gutta-percha "poor sealant" from Weine as a root-filling finding (the sentence is about temporary fillings).
5. Omitting that dye, bacterial and fluid filtration tests disagree.
