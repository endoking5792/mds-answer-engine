## ANSWER

### Definition and principle
A dye leakage study is an in vitro test of marginal seal: a restored or root-filled extracted tooth is exposed to a coloured tracer, and the depth the dye travels along the interface is measured. A dye with a colour contrasting with tooth and restoration is allowed to enter any gap at the interface; the depth it reaches is taken as a measure of leakage. Sikri calls it "one of the oldest and most commonly used methods" (Sikri, 2020, p.445), and Coluzzi and Parker call the two-dimensional dye test the most common in vitro method because of low cost and ease of use (Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).

### Dyes and tracers
- Sikri lists methylene blue, India ink, crystal violet, fluorescein, rhodamine B, eosin, basic fuchsin and erythrosin, as solutions or particle suspensions (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- The ideal dye has a colour contrasting with tooth and material, does not bond to tooth or restoration, and is colour stable (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- Coluzzi and Parker list methylene blue, silver nitrate, basic fuchsin, propylene glycol and acid red (Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).
- Craig's names methylene blue or silver nitrate as the tracer or dye solution (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Silver nitrate (50% aqueous) acts as a chemical tracer: it is reduced to metallic silver, which stays in place and "cannot diffuse away or fade as is common with water-soluble dyes" (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).
- Radioisotope molecules are only 40 nm against 120 nm for the smallest dye particles, so isotopes penetrate finer gaps (Sikri, 2020, p.445).

### Method
1. Restore or obturate extracted teeth, then store them, usually with thermal cycling (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
2. In the endodontic variant, coat roots with nail varnish except the area or margin under test (apical or coronal) [Likely].
3. Immerse in dye or another marker for a set period, preferably under vacuum (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
4. Remove, wash and section (or, in endodontic studies, clear [Likely]), then examine under a microscope (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
5. Judge the seal by how far the dye has run down the margin toward the cavity floor (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Score penetration by assigning numbers to defined depths (Sikri, 2020, p.445), or score qualitatively (scores) or measure quantitatively (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- A three-dimensional method needs no sectioning and gives an objective reading (Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).
- Guidelines for leakage testing are included in ISO TR 11405 (McCabe and Walls, 2008), although Berman et al (2021) state there are "no standardized techniques to evaluate and measure microleakage".

### Limitations
- **Particle size.** Different particle sizes and concentrations give inconsistent results even with standard technique; penetration times of two dyes ranged from 5 minutes to 1 hour (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- **Dye binding.** Dye may bind tooth or material: basic fuchsin binds carious dentin, so stained caries can be read as a larger gap (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- **Trapped air.** Trapped air is hard to remove from small spaces even under vacuum and blocks dye entry (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- **Colour instability.** Aniline blue becomes colourless in alkaline conditions such as calcium hydroxide (Sikri, 2020, p.445). In the same way, methylene blue is discoloured by calcium hydroxide, so it should not be used to test MTA and other cements that release it (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- **Diffusion and fading.** Water-soluble dyes diffuse and fade; silver does not (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).
- **Storage and pH.** Formalin storage and dye pH can alter results (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- **Subjective scoring.** Scoring is subjective, "a major disadvantage" (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- **Tests disagree.** Grey MTA resisted bacteria better than CEM cement, but dye penetration showed no difference; results from dye and bacterial penetration did not match (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017). Comparing Thermafil with lateral condensation, fluid filtration, dye/electrochemical and bacterial tests gave three different verdicts (Karagenç et al, 2006) [LIT, PubMed abstract].
- **Radiographs.** Radiographs correlate poorly with leakage and do not predict it (Berman et al, 2021).
- **In vitro only.** Thermocycling cannot reproduce the dynamic pulpo-dentinal complex (Sikri, 2020, p.444).

### What dye studies have shown in endodontics
- Lateral condensation gave more apical dye leakage than heated multiphase gutta-percha in vitro (Gilhooly et al, cited in Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- MTA leaked significantly less than amalgam and Super EBA in dye and bacterial leakage studies (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). Most root-end studies found MTA more resistant to dye than Super EBA, amalgam or IRM, with no difference between white and grey MTA (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- All obturation materials and techniques leak to some degree (Berman et al, 2021).
- Coronal leakage: teeth exposed to artificial saliva for 3 to 56 days leaked 79 to 85% of root length, with no difference between periods (Swanson and Madison, 1987) [LIT, full text].
- Sealers: AH26 leaked significantly more than Sealapex and Roth's (Madison et al, 1987) [LIT, full text].
- In vivo: in monkeys, dye entered all groups with no difference among sealers, unlike the in vitro ranking (Madison and Wilcox, 1988) [LIT, full text].

### Conclusion
Dye studies rank materials within one protocol, but they are in vitro, unstandardised and not proof of clinical outcome.

## TEXTBOOK DISAGREEMENTS
- **Does the dye chosen matter?** Sikri, Textbook of Conservative and Restorative Dentistry, edition not stated (2020): yes, particle size varies, so consistent results cannot be expected. Coluzzi and Parker, Lasers in Dentistry, 2nd ed (2017, 2023): variation in dye type "does not affect the results". Nakabayashi and Pashley, Hybridization of Dental Hard Tissues, edition not stated (1998): water-soluble dyes diffuse and fade; silver does not. Torabinejad and Rubinstein, Surgical Endodontics, edition not stated (2017): methylene blue unsuitable for calcium-hydroxide-releasing cements.
- **Is there a standard?** Berman et al, Cohen's Pathways of the Pulp, 12th ed (2021): "no standardized techniques to evaluate and measure microleakage". McCabe and Walls, Applied Dental Materials, 9th ed (2008): guidelines for leakage testing are included in ISO TR 11405.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Oldest, commonest, cheapest leakage test (Sikri, 2020; Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).
- Score = depth of dye; subjective (Sikri, 2020).
- Basic fuchsin binds carious dentin; aniline blue fades in alkali (Sikri, 2020).
- Methylene blue not for MTA (calcium hydroxide) (Torabinejad and Rubinstein, 2017).
- Silver nitrate does not fade or diffuse (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).
- MTA leaks less than amalgam and Super EBA (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- No standardised technique (Berman et al, 2021); ISO TR 11405 guidelines exist (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Presenting dye depth as a measure of clinical failure.
2. Using methylene blue results for MTA without the calcium hydroxide caveat.
3. Saying dye choice does not matter without noting that Sikri says it does.
4. Quoting gutta-percha "poor sealant" from Weine as a root-filling finding (the sentence is about temporary fillings).
5. Omitting that dye, bacterial and fluid filtration tests disagree.
