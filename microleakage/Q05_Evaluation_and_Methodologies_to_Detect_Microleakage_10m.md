## STUDY MODE
### S1. What "evaluation" means
Microleakage is the "clinically undetectable passage of bacteria and bacterial products, fluids, molecules or ions" along gaps at the cavity-restoration interface (Sikri, 2020). Nakabayashi and Pashley (1998) define it as diffusion of a substance into a fluid-filled gap or defect between filling and tooth. Because the passage is clinically undetectable, it is evaluated mostly in the laboratory. Sikri states that most methods are in vitro: thermocycling imitates the mouth, but the dynamic pulpo-dentinal complex, its defences and plaque cannot be simulated (Sikri, 2020). McCabe and Walls (2008) describe the standard protocol: restored teeth are stored, normally with thermal cycling, in a dye or other marker (for example a radioisotope), sectioned, and judged by how far the marker has run down the margin towards the cavity floor; guidelines are in ISO TR 11405. Poli et al (2023) call thermocycling "imperative" in in vitro studies.

### S2. Sikri's classification (Sikri, 2020)
1. **Penetration methods**
   - *Dye penetration:* the oldest and commonest. Dyes: methylene blue, India ink, crystal violet, fluorescein, rhodamine B, eosin, basic fuchsin, erythrosin. Tooth immersed, washed, sectioned, depth scored. Validity questioned: particle sizes differ (penetration time 5 min to 1 h); basic fuchsin binds carious dentin and exaggerates the gap; trapped air despite vacuum; colour instability (aniline blue goes colourless with calcium hydroxide); scoring subjective.
   - *Chemical tracers:* two colourless compounds react to give an opaque silver precipitate (50% silver nitrate plus photographic developer; 1.0% silver chloride also used); both must penetrate. Limitations as for dyes.
   - *Radioactive isotopes:* 45Ca, 131I, 32P, 14C, 35S, 86Rb; autoradiography. Isotope molecule 40 nm vs smallest dye particle 120 nm, so minute leaks are detected. Limits: subjective reading (reduced with a stereomicroscope; Sikri, 2020), scatter from high-energy isotopes, 45Ca affinity for tooth, cost and safety.
   - *Neutron activation analysis:* non-radioactive manganese salt, activated 55Mn to 56Mn in a reactor; cannot locate the leak; costly.
   - *Bacterial penetration:* qualitative only; gaps passing bacteria are usually 0.5-1.0 µm (micro sign lost in extraction; µm from context), so smaller gaps that pass toxins are missed.
2. **Air pressure technique:** compressed air at the interface; bubbles at the margin. Tooth not destroyed, result can be quantified; not usable in vivo, dries the specimen.
3. **Fluid filtration:** water forced through the gap under constant pressure; volume read from movement of an air bubble in a capillary. Non-destructive, automatic, accurate, no tracer; pressure hard to standardise.
4. **Electrochemical method:** current measured across the interface in an electrolytic bath; not suitable for metallic restorations; not in vivo.
5. **Microscopic examination:** SEM (direct view; artefacts in preparation), fluorescent microscopy (UV-excited dye; reproducible, cheap), optical coherence tomography (width and depth of marginal gaps).
6. **Miscellaneous:** artificial caries (outer lesion and cavity-wall lesion; depth quantifiable), pain perception (calcium chloride on the margin; pain in 30-60 s; subjective), reverse diffusion (tracer placed on the floor, measured leaking out; "time-dependent leakage"), three-dimensional computer analysis (now CBCT), laser microspectral analysis, resin infiltration.

### S3. What other books add
- Craig: interfacial gaps measured by SEM; dye or tracer (methylene blue or silver nitrate) scored qualitatively or measured quantitatively; nanoleakage is silver nitrate viewed under TEM (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Nakabayashi and Pashley: SEM of the interface needs fracturing or polishing, which "can produce artifacts", so indirect tracer methods were developed; silver nitrate is preferred because reduced silver "cannot diffuse away or fade" like water-soluble dyes (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).
- Lasers in Dentistry: two-dimensional dye penetration is the commonest in vitro method because it is cheap and easy; a three-dimensional method needs no sectioning and gives an objective reading (Poli et al, 2023). Swept-source OCT detected gaps of half a micron in Class I resin restorations, reporting Makishi et al and Bakhsh et al, 2011 (Muruppel and Fried, 2023).
- Mahmoud (root-end fillings): tests include dye penetration, fluid filtration, protein leakage, glucose concentration, bacterial penetration and marginal adaptation, all with limitations; methylene blue should not be used with calcium-hydroxide-forming materials such as MTA (Parirokh and Shabahang, 2017).

### S4. How far results can be trusted
- Sturdevant: in vitro microleakage values for an adhesive "often do not correlate with clinical behaviour of the same material" (Ritter, 2019).
- Phillips: leakage data vary widely between laboratories, and bond strength and microleakage often do not correlate (Anusavice et al, n.d.).
- Seltzer and Bender: in vitro marginal quality is "unlikely to predict" clinical outcome (Hargreaves et al, 2012).
- Cohen: there are "no standardized techniques to evaluate and measure microleakage" (Berman and Hargreaves, 2021).
- Journal reviews kept from the old answers: methods vary widely between dye studies (Taylor and Lynch, 1992, full text); artificial caries and SEM are among established methods (Kidd, 1976, full text); in vitro bond and gap data correlate only weakly with clinical outcome (Peutzfeldt et al, 2024, full text).

## EXAM MODE
**(374 words; about 17 min at 22 words/min)**

**Definition.** Microleakage is the clinically undetectable passage of bacteria, fluids, molecules or ions along the cavity-restoration interface (Sikri, 2020). Because it cannot be seen clinically, it is evaluated mainly in vitro.

**Basic protocol.** Restored extracted teeth are thermocycled, stored in a dye or other marker, sectioned and scored by depth of penetration towards the cavity floor; ISO TR 11405 gives guidelines (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**Classification (Sikri, 2020)**
1. *Penetration methods*
   - Dye penetration (methylene blue, India ink, basic fuchsin, fluorescein): oldest and commonest. Faults: variable particle size, binding to carious dentin, trapped air, colour instability, subjective scoring.
   - Chemical tracers: silver nitrate plus developer gives a silver precipitate.
   - Radioisotopes (45Ca, 131I, 14C): 40 nm molecule detects minute leaks; autoradiography; costly, safety needed.
   - Neutron activation: quantifies but cannot locate the leak.
   - Bacterial penetration: qualitative; misses gaps under 0.5-1.0 µm.
2. *Air pressure:* bubbles at the margin; non-destructive; not in vivo.
3. *Fluid filtration:* fluid volume through the gap read from a bubble in a capillary; no tracer, quantitative; pressure hard to standardise.
4. *Electrochemical:* current across the interface; not for metal restorations.
5. *Microscopy:* SEM (direct view, preparation artefacts), fluorescent microscopy, optical coherence tomography.
6. *Miscellaneous:* artificial caries, pain perception with calcium chloride, reverse diffusion (time-dependent leakage), 3D analysis or CBCT, laser microspectral analysis, resin infiltration.

**Textbooks disagree**
- Dye choice: Sikri (2020) says different dyes give inconsistent results; Poli et al (2023) say dye type does not affect results.
- Scoring: Sikri (2020) calls dye scoring only subjective; Craig scores qualitatively or quantitatively (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Tracer: Nakabayashi and Pashley (1998) prefer silver nitrate because it does not fade or diffuse; Sikri (2020) gives it the same limits as dyes.
- Fluid filtration: accurate (Sikri, 2020) but inconsistent with other tests (Parirokh and Shabahang, 2017).

**Evaluation of results**
- In vitro leakage often does not correlate with clinical behaviour (Ritter, 2019).
- Bond strength and microleakage often do not correlate, and data vary between laboratories (Anusavice et al, n.d.).
- No standardised technique exists (Berman and Hargreaves, 2021).

**Conclusion.** No single test suffices. Penetration tests show where leakage occurs, fluid filtration measures how much, microscopy shows the gap. Laboratory rankings of materials must be confirmed by clinical follow-up.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Most methods are in vitro; the pulpo-dentinal complex and plaque cannot be simulated (Sikri, 2020).
- Dye penetration: commonest; subjective scoring; dye-specific errors (Sikri, 2020).
- Isotope molecule 40 nm vs smallest dye particle 120 nm (Sikri, 2020).
- Bacterial method misses gaps under 0.5-1.0 µm (Sikri, 2020).
- Fluid filtration: no tracer, non-destructive; pressure hard to standardise (Sikri, 2020).
- Electrochemical: not for metallic restorations (Sikri, 2020).
- Guidelines: ISO TR 11405 (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- In vitro leakage often does not correlate with clinical behaviour (Ritter, 2019).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing tests without saying what each measures (location, volume, or structure of the gap).
2. Calling dye penetration precise or objective without naming the dye errors.
3. Saying the electrochemical method suits amalgam or cast restorations; it does not work with metal.
4. Presenting in vitro rankings of materials as clinical proof.
5. Writing "bacteria pass a 10 µm gap" for the bacterial method; Sikri's figure for that method is 0.5-1.0 µm.
