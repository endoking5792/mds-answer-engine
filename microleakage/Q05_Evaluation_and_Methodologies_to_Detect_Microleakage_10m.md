## ANSWER

### Definition
Microleakage is the "clinically undetectable passage of bacteria and bacterial products, fluids, molecules or ions" along gaps at the cavity-restoration interface (Sikri, 2020). Nakabayashi and Pashley (1998) define it as diffusion of a substance into a fluid-filled gap or defect between filling and tooth. Because the passage is clinically undetectable, it is evaluated mainly in the laboratory (in vitro).

### Why evaluation is mainly in vitro
Sikri states that most methods are in vitro. Thermocycling imitates the mouth, but the dynamic pulpo-dentinal complex, its defences and plaque cannot be simulated (Sikri, 2020). Poli et al (2023) call thermocycling "imperative" in in vitro studies.

### Basic protocol
Restored extracted teeth are stored, normally with thermal cycling, in a dye or other marker (for example a radioisotope), sectioned, and judged by how far the marker has run down the margin towards the cavity floor. Guidelines for leakage testing are given in ISO TR 11405 (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### Classification of methods (Sikri, 2020)

**1. Penetration methods**
- *Dye penetration.* The oldest and commonest method. Dyes: methylene blue, India ink, crystal violet, fluorescein, rhodamine B, eosin, basic fuchsin, erythrosin. The tooth is immersed, washed, sectioned and the depth of penetration is scored. Two-dimensional dye penetration is the commonest in vitro method because it is cheap and easy (Poli et al, 2023). Validity is questioned (Sikri, 2020):
  - particle sizes differ between dyes (penetration time 5 min to 1 h);
  - basic fuchsin binds carious dentin and exaggerates the gap;
  - air is trapped despite vacuum;
  - colour is unstable (aniline blue goes colourless with calcium hydroxide);
  - scoring is subjective.
- *Chemical tracers.* Two colourless compounds react to give an opaque silver precipitate (50% silver nitrate plus photographic developer; 1.0% silver chloride is also used). Both must penetrate. Sikri gives the limitations as similar to those of dyes (Sikri, 2020). Nakabayashi and Pashley prefer silver nitrate because reduced silver "cannot diffuse away or fade" like water-soluble dyes (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998). Craig names methylene blue or silver nitrate as the dye or tracer, scored qualitatively or measured quantitatively (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- *Radioactive isotopes.* 45Ca, 131I, 32P, 14C, 35S, 86Rb, read by autoradiography. The isotope molecule is 40 nm against 120 nm for the smallest dye particle, so minute leaks are detected. Limits: subjective reading (reduced with a stereomicroscope; Sikri, 2020), scatter from high-energy isotopes, affinity of 45Ca for tooth, cost, and the need for safety measures.
- *Neutron activation analysis.* A non-radioactive manganese salt is used and activated from 55Mn to 56Mn in a reactor. It quantifies the leak but cannot locate it, and it is costly.
- *Bacterial penetration.* Qualitative only. Gaps that pass bacteria are usually 0.5-1.0 µm (micro sign lost in extraction; µm from context), so smaller gaps that pass toxins are missed.

**2. Air pressure technique.** Compressed air is applied at the interface and bubbles appear at the margin. The tooth is not destroyed and the result can be quantified. It cannot be used in vivo and it dries the specimen.

**3. Fluid filtration.** Water is forced through the gap under constant pressure; the volume is read from the movement of an air bubble in a capillary. It is non-destructive, automatic, accurate, quantitative and needs no tracer. Pressure is hard to standardise.

**4. Electrochemical method.** Current is measured across the interface in an electrolytic bath. It is not suitable for metallic restorations and cannot be used in vivo.

**5. Microscopic examination**
- *Scanning electron microscopy (SEM):* direct view of the gap, with artefacts from specimen preparation. Craig measures interfacial gaps by SEM (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). SEM of the interface needs fracturing or polishing, which "can produce artifacts", and this is why indirect tracer methods were developed (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).
- *Fluorescent microscopy:* UV-excited dye; reproducible and cheap.
- *Optical coherence tomography:* measures width and depth of marginal gaps. Swept-source OCT detected gaps of half a micron in Class I resin restorations, reporting Makishi et al and Bakhsh et al, 2011 (Muruppel and Fried, 2023).
- *Nanoleakage:* silver nitrate viewed under TEM (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

**6. Miscellaneous**
- *Artificial caries:* produces an outer lesion and a cavity-wall lesion; depth can be quantified.
- *Pain perception:* calcium chloride on the margin; pain within 30-60 s; subjective.
- *Reverse diffusion:* tracer placed on the cavity floor and measured as it leaks out; shows "time-dependent leakage".
- *Three-dimensional computer analysis:* now done with CBCT. A three-dimensional method needs no sectioning and gives an objective reading (Poli et al, 2023).
- *Laser microspectral analysis.*
- *Resin infiltration.*

### Tests used for root-end filling materials
For root-end fillings, tests include dye penetration, fluid filtration, protein leakage, glucose concentration, bacterial penetration and marginal adaptation, all with limitations. Methylene blue should not be used with calcium-hydroxide-forming materials such as MTA (Parirokh and Shabahang, 2017).

### Critical appraisal: how far results can be trusted
- In vitro microleakage values for an adhesive "often do not correlate with clinical behaviour of the same material" (Ritter, 2019).
- Leakage data vary widely between laboratories, and bond strength and microleakage often do not correlate (Anusavice et al, n.d.).
- In vitro marginal quality is "unlikely to predict" clinical outcome (Hargreaves et al, 2012).
- There are "no standardized techniques to evaluate and measure microleakage" (Berman and Hargreaves, 2021).
- Journal reviews: methods vary widely between dye studies (Taylor and Lynch, 1992) [LIT, full text, earlier session]; artificial caries and SEM are among established methods (Kidd, 1976) [LIT, full text, earlier session]; in vitro bond and gap data correlate only weakly with clinical outcome (Peutzfeldt et al, 2024) [LIT, full text, earlier session].

### Conclusion
No single test suffices. Penetration tests show where leakage occurs, fluid filtration measures how much, and microscopy shows the gap. Laboratory rankings of materials must be confirmed by clinical follow-up.

## TEXTBOOK DISAGREEMENTS
- **Does dye choice matter?** Sikri, Textbook of Conservative and Restorative Dentistry, First eBook Edition (2020): different particle sizes mean consistent results cannot be expected even with a standard technique. Poli et al in Coluzzi and Parker, Lasers in Dentistry, 2nd ed (2023): variation in dye type "does not affect the results".
- **Is dye scoring only subjective?** Sikri, First eBook Edition (2020): "only subjective", a major disadvantage. Sakaguchi et al, Craig's Restorative Dental Materials, 14th ed (2019): scored qualitatively or measured quantitatively. Poli et al, Lasers in Dentistry, 2nd ed (2023): a 3D method gives an objective reading.
- **Silver tracers.** Sikri, First eBook Edition (2020): limitations "similar to dye penetration". Nakabayashi and Pashley, Hybridization of Dental Hard Tissues (1998; edition not stated): silver preferred because it cannot diffuse or fade.
- **Fluid filtration.** Sikri, First eBook Edition (2020): accurate, automatic, "no bias". Parirokh and Shabahang in Torabinejad and Rubinstein, The Art and Science of Contemporary Surgical Endodontics (2017; edition not stated): results "not consistent with other leakage tests".
- **Is a standard method available?** McCabe and Walls, Applied Dental Materials, 9th ed (2008): guidelines in ISO TR 11405. Berman and Hargreaves, Cohen's Pathways of the Pulp, 12th ed (2021): no standardised techniques (endodontic context).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Most methods are in vitro; the pulpo-dentinal complex and plaque cannot be simulated (Sikri, 2020).
- Dye penetration: commonest; subjective scoring; dye-specific errors (Sikri, 2020).
- Isotope molecule 40 nm vs smallest dye particle 120 nm (Sikri, 2020).
- Bacterial method misses gaps under 0.5-1.0 µm (Sikri, 2020).
- Fluid filtration: no tracer, non-destructive; pressure hard to standardise (Sikri, 2020).
- Electrochemical: not for metallic restorations (Sikri, 2020).
- Guidelines: ISO TR 11405 (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- In vitro leakage often does not correlate with clinical behaviour (Ritter, 2019).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing tests without saying what each measures (location, volume, or structure of the gap).
2. Calling dye penetration precise or objective without naming the dye errors.
3. Saying the electrochemical method suits amalgam or cast restorations; it does not work with metal.
4. Presenting in vitro rankings of materials as clinical proof.
5. Writing "bacteria pass a 10 µm gap" for the bacterial method; Sikri's figure for that method is 0.5-1.0 µm.
