## ANSWER

### Introduction
Marginal integrity is the continuity and durability of the junction between restoration, luting medium and tooth. It is built by margin design: the circumferential tie and the finish line.

### Marginal integrity: what it means
- **Working meaning** (author synthesis, [Likely]): the continuity and durability of the junction between restoration, luting medium and tooth at the margin.
- **A closed margin is not possible.** Summitt's frames it clinically: "A completely closed margin is unattainable clinically"; caries bacteria are 4 to 5 µm and pass under every margin, so patient resistance matters more than the opening; the best margin is one the patient can floss (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Practical criterion of fit.** Since 100 µm is the smallest detectable ledge, that is a practical criterion of fit (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Weight of the margin in failure.** The same page reports that defective margins explain only 10% of failed restorations (Hilton et al, 2013), so fit must be judged with caries risk and hygiene. Peutzfeldt et al (2024, full text) found marginal gap in enamel correlated only weakly with clinical outcome of Class II composites; bevels were not tested.
- **Cast restorations.** Sikri and Marzouk both call the tooth/cement/cast junction the weakest link, and say an accurate wax pattern and casting do not by themselves give an adapted margin (Sikri, 2020; Marzouk et al, n.d.). Sikri's other book puts the residual luting gap at 50 to 100 µm even with a precise restoration and says poorly adapted indirect restorations should be remade (Sikri, 2017).

### Circumferential tie
- **Definition.** The peripheral marginal anatomy of the preparation, created by bevels along the cavosurface margins (Sikri, 2020); Sikri 2017 adds "relation with the casting and luting cement" (Sikri, 2017). Singh and Marzouk use the same wording and make the tie one of the salient features of a cast preparation (Singh, 2020; Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- **Requirements.** Enamel supported by sound dentin; enamel rods at the margin continuous with sound dentin (Sikri, 2020); angular cavosurface angles trimmed (Sikri, 2017). Marzouk adds that tie junctions are rounded and the tie is "the smoothest" part of the preparation (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- **Components.** Bevels (occlusal and gingival) and flares (primary and secondary). Sikri 2020: the secondary flare is "as good as the occlusal bevel given to create a circumferential tie" in gold inlays, exploiting gold's malleability. Garg: the primary flare "is basic part of circumferential tie" (Garg and Garg, 2011).
- **Who uses the term.** The term is used only by the Indian and Marzouk operative texts; Summitt's, Sturdevant and the materials books do not use it.

### Why bevels make the tie work
- **Marginal angles.** Obtuse marginal tooth angle (strong enamel) and acute marginal metal angle (burnishable): Sikri gives 140° enamel and 40° metal, occlusal bevel depth about one quarter of the wall (Sikri, 2017); Singh gives 140° to 150° tooth and 30° to 40° metal, with metal below 30° too thin and above 40° too thick to burnish (Singh, 2020).
- **Error factor.** Bevels reduce the space between casting and tooth at the margin relative to the internal dimension, "depending on the bevel's angulation" (Sikri, 2020). Singh and Marzouk quantify this as threefold or more (Singh, 2020; Marzouk et al, n.d.). Sikri's Fig 16.11 shows microgap "virtually nil" with a bevelled inlay (Sikri, 2020).
- **Retention.** Bevels indirectly improve retention by decreasing the direct frictional component (Sikri, 2020); Sturdevant says retention "may be slightly improved" with opposing bevels, the main purpose being a better junction between metal and tooth (Ritter, 2019).

### Types of bevel
- **Sikri 2020 lists four:**
  - Partial: part of the enamel wall; direct filling gold.
  - Short: entire enamel wall; preferred for cast gold.
  - Inverted: labial shoulder of metal ceramic crowns, for aesthetics.
  - Reverse: dentinal part of the cervical wall toward the axiogingival line angle, resisting rotational displacement from hydrostatic pressure during cementation (Sikri, 2020).
- **Marzouk and Singh list six:** partial, short, long, full, counter and hollow-ground (Marzouk et al, n.d.; Singh, 2020). Marzouk calls the long bevel the most frequently used for the first three classes of cast alloys, says the full bevel should be avoided because it "deprives the preparation of its internal resistance and retention", and makes hollow-ground (concave) bevels ideal for Class IV and V alloys, giving bulk (Marzouk et al, n.d.).

### Finish lines: the basic forms
- **Sikri 2020:** bevel, chamfer and shoulder are the common margins; chamfer is under 0.5 mm wide and rounded, shoulder over 0.5 mm and sharp; chamfer where the metal is malleable, shoulder where it is not. Table 16.2: bevel removes unsupported enamel and allows finishing but extends into the sulcus if apical (facial margin of maxillary metal ceramic, intracoronal casts, reverse bevel in gold inlays); chamfer gives a distinct, easily controlled margin but risks an unsupported enamel lip (lingual of cast metal and metal ceramic); shoulder gives bulk but is less conservative and difficult in mandibular incisors (labial metal ceramic, cervical margins of all-ceramic crowns).
- **Sikri 2017:** shoulder is 0.75 to 1.0 mm (not "more than 0.5 mm"); heavy chamfer 0.75 mm for some all-ceramics; knife-edge and feather-edge "should be avoided"; shoulder preferred for all-ceramic, chamfer for porcelain laminates.
- **Marzouk:** chamfer is "the most universally used design" for Class I, II and III cast alloys; knife-edge uses least tooth; bevelled shoulder uses most and suits subgingival margins (Marzouk et al, n.d.).
- **Summary table** (Sikri, 2020; Sikri, 2017):

| Finish line | Features | Indication |
|---|---|---|
| Bevel | Removes unsupported enamel, allows finishing | Facial metal ceramic, intracoronal casts |
| Chamfer | Under 0.5 mm, rounded; distinct margin | Lingual of metal and metal ceramic; malleable metal |
| Shoulder | Over 0.5 mm, sharp; bulk; less conservative | Labial metal ceramic; all-ceramic |
| Knife-edge or feather-edge | Least tooth removal | Avoid |

### Finish lines for crowns (metal-ceramic and all-ceramic)
- **Summitt's:** traditionally margin design has no significant effect on the seal of metal-ceramic crowns; shoulder, shoulder-bevel and chamfer all fit acceptably when fully seated. In two studies the shoulder gave the least marginal discrepancy after cementation, significantly better than shoulder-bevel or chamfer. Chamfer and internally rounded shoulder had the lowest stress concentration under vertical load. Bevel only with metal-ceramic, where metal covers it; porcelain on a bevel may break at cementation. A 90° shoulder 1.0 to 1.5 mm deep gives a precise margin; a chamfer does not give enough reduction for metal-ceramic. For Procera crowns a feather-edge gave over twice the marginal opening of a chamfer or rounded shoulder (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Sturdevant:** shoulder and shoulder-bevel margins "distort relatively little" (Ritter, 2019).
- **Goldstein:** labial shoulder, chamfer and bevelled shoulder all adequate for metal-ceramic; porcelain butt joint where tissue is thin; collarless crown 90° facial shoulder 1.5 mm deep (Goldstein, 2018).
- **Craig:** metal margins can be bevelled, ceramic margins cannot; ceramic designs are 90° shoulder, 120° shoulder or shoulder bevel with shoulder depth at least 1.2 mm (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- **Pickard's Guide:** indirect margins should be a chamfer or rounded shoulder, depending on material, remaining tooth and margin position; metal allows finer tolerances (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### Finish lines by type of restoration
- **Cast gold inlay or onlay:** short bevel occlusally and gingivally, secondary flare proximally; burnish (Sikri, 2020).
- **Metal ceramic crown:** labial shoulder 1.0 to 1.5 mm or shoulder-bevel; chamfer lingually (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020). Bevel only where metal covers it (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **All-ceramic crown:** shoulder (Sikri, 2017) or chamfer and rounded shoulder (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); ceramic margins are never bevelled (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Feather-edge gives over twice the marginal opening (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Porcelain laminate:** chamfer (Sikri, 2017).
- **Amalgam, GIC, ceramic:** 90° cavosurface; bevels contraindicated because of low edge strength (Hilton et al, 2013; Ritter, 2019). Summitt's gives a 90° butt joint about 1 mm deep for GIC (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Composite, Class II (Summitt's):** "Bevel placement is a point of controversy"; where facial and lingual proximal walls meet the surface at a right angle, 0.5 mm bevels at about 45°, and research shows these significantly reduce marginal leakage. Gingival bevel only in enamel well away from the CEJ; within about 1 mm of the CEJ adhesion is no better than to dentin. An inverse bevel significantly reduces microleakage against a butt margin, but is not cut deliberately; keep it if left after excavation. Occlusal bevels not indicated (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Composite, Class II (Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed):** no occlusal bevels; bevels "rarely used" on any proximal box wall; not recommended on the gingival margin; bevelling indicated mainly for large restorations to gain bonding area (Ritter, 2019).
- **Composite (Sikri 2020 and Singh):** role of bevels "remained controversial"; occlusal bevelling usually not required (Sikri, 2020). Singh: bevels indicated for composite (Singh, 2020).
- **Composite, anterior:** Goldstein calls a 45° enamel bevel the commonest finish line and cites Moore and Vann (secondhand) that bevelling posterior composites reduces microleakage (Goldstein, 2018). Summitt's Class 3: one silver-nitrate study found no difference bevelled versus not (Hilton et al, 2013). Alani: facial bevels open enamel prisms in cross-section; palatal chamfers give bulk (Alani and Patel, 2025).

### Gold restorations: finishing the margin
Short bevel aids burnishing and close adaptation of direct gold (Sikri, 2020). For castings, burnish over zinc phosphate after 24 hours, resin lutes at once; marginal gaps over 0.1 mm are not burnished but remade (Sikri, 2020).

### Laboratory evidence on bevels
Class V laboratory studies disagree: bevels gave no benefit in two (Ameri et al, 2010; Bagheri and Ghavamnasiri, 2008) and less leakage in one (Uno et al, 1997). These are in vitro dye studies, so they do not prove clinical superiority. Details are in the critical appraisal below.

### Conclusion
The circumferential tie reduces, but never closes, the marginal gap. The finish line must suit the material: bevels for malleable metal and enamel bonding, butt joints for brittle materials, and shoulder or chamfer for ceramics.

### Critical appraisal
Full synthesis, three claims.

**Claim A. "Bevels reduce leakage at composite margins."**
- **What the books state:** Hilton et al, 2013 (Summitt's 4th ed): controversial, but 0.5 mm proximal bevels significantly reduce leakage; Class 3 tracer study found no difference. Ritter, 2019 (Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed): proximal bevels rarely used, gingival not recommended. Sikri, 2020: controversial. Singh, 2020: indicated. Goldstein, 2018 (3rd ed): commonest finish line, reduces microleakage.
- **Evidence:** Ameri et al (2010, abstract) and Bagheri and Ghavamnasiri (2008, abstract) found no benefit from enamel bevels in Class V composite; Ameri found non-bevelled cavities leaked significantly less after load cycling. Uno et al (1997, abstract) found bevelled RMGI and composite Class V cavities leaked less than 90° cavities. Peixoto et al (2002, abstract), on indirect composite inlays, found bevel and chamfer leaked less than butt joint at one margin, while chamfer did worst in maxillary premolars.
- **Interpretation:** the effect depends on margin site, material and loading; there is no general rule.
- **Limitation:** abstracts only, in vitro dye studies, small samples, different protocols; the books cite primary studies that were not read.
- **Clinical implication:** bevel accessible proximal enamel when it is thick; no occlusal bevel; no gingival bevel near the CEJ.

**Claim B. "Finish-line design decides crown marginal fit."**
- **What the books state:** Hilton et al, 2013: traditionally no significant effect for metal-ceramic, yet shoulder best after cementation in two studies, and feather-edge over twice the opening in Procera crowns. Sikri, 2017 and Sikri, 2020: shoulder for all-ceramic; feather-edge to be avoided. Banerjee and Watson, 2015: chamfer or rounded shoulder. Sakaguchi et al, 2019: shoulder depth at least 1.2 mm.
- **Evidence:** only textbook summaries of in vitro fit and stress studies.
- **Interpretation:** the books agree that knife-edge and feather-edge are poor for ceramics; they disagree on chamfer versus shoulder.
- **Limitation:** no primary study read; fit in vitro is not clinical success, and Summitt's notes defective margins cause only 10% of failures.

**Claim C. "The circumferential tie closes the gap."**
- **What the books state:** Sikri, 2020: bevels reduce the error factor depending on angulation; Marzouk et al and Singh, 2020: threefold or more. Sikri, 2017: 50 to 100 µm remains; some authors find no correlation between gap and leakage.
- **Interpretation:** the tie reduces, not removes, the gap; cement solubility and burnishing still decide the seal.
- **Limitation:** the threefold figure is geometric and given without a primary source.

## TEXTBOOK DISAGREEMENTS
- **Shoulder width.** Sikri, Textbook of Conservative and Restorative Dentistry, first eBook ed (2020): over 0.5 mm. Sikri, Indirect Restorations in Dental Practice, 2nd ed (2017): 0.75 to 1.0 mm.
- **Number of bevel types.** Sikri, first eBook ed (2020): four. Marzouk et al, Operative Dentistry, Indian reprint (edition and year not stated, n.d.) and Singh, 2nd ed (2020): six.
- **Bevel angles.** Sikri, 2nd ed (2017): 140° tooth, 40° metal. Singh, 2nd ed (2020): 140° to 150° tooth, 30° to 40° metal.
- **Error reduction by the bevel.** Sikri, first eBook ed (2020): "depending on the bevel's angulation", no figure. Marzouk et al (n.d.) and Singh, 2nd ed (2020): threefold or more.
- **All-ceramic finish line.** Sikri, 2nd ed (2017) and Sikri, first eBook ed (2020): shoulder. Hilton et al, Summitt's 4th ed (2013) and Banerjee and Watson, Pickard's Guide 10th ed (2015): chamfer or rounded shoulder.
- **Metal-ceramic margin and fit.** Hilton et al, Summitt's 4th ed (2013): "traditionally" design has no significant effect on seal. Two studies cited by the same book, Summitt's 4th ed (2013): shoulder significantly better than shoulder-bevel or chamfer.
- **Composite proximal bevels.** Hilton et al, Summitt's 4th ed (2013): 0.5 mm, reduce leakage. Ritter, Sturdevant's 2nd South Asia ed (2019): "rarely used". Sikri, first eBook ed (2020): controversial.
- **Composite bevels generally.** Singh, 2nd ed (2020): indicated. Goldstein, Esthetics in Dentistry 3rd ed (2018): commonest finish line, reduces microleakage. Hilton et al, Summitt's 4th ed (2013): no leakage difference in Class 3.
- **Chamfer for cast alloys.** Marzouk et al (n.d.): most universal for Class I to III alloys. Sikri, first eBook ed (2020): lingual margins only.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Circumferential tie = peripheral marginal anatomy created by bevels (Sikri, 2020).
- Chamfer under 0.5 mm rounded; shoulder over 0.5 mm sharp (Sikri, 2020); 0.75 to 1.0 mm in Sikri, 2017.
- Bevel: 140° to 150° enamel, 30° to 40° metal (Singh, 2020).
- 100 µm is the smallest detectable ledge (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Feather-edge: over twice the marginal opening (Hilton et al, 2013).
- No bevel for amalgam, GIC or ceramic (Hilton et al, 2013; Ritter, 2019; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- No occlusal bevel for composite; proximal bevels contested.
- Cast gap over 0.1 mm: remake (Sikri, 2020).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Defining chamfer and shoulder without the width threshold or the metal malleability reason.
2. Saying bevels always reduce composite leakage; the books and the Class V studies disagree.
3. Bevelling amalgam, GIC or ceramic margins.
4. Confusing inverted bevel (labial shoulder, metal ceramic) with reverse bevel (cervical dentin, inlay) or with Summitt's inverse bevel (composite gingival enamel).
5. Writing that the tie closes the gap; it reduces the error factor only.
