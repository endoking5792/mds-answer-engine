## STUDY MODE

### S1. Definition and why it matters
Sikri defines microleakage as the "clinically undetectable passage of bacteria and bacterial products, fluids, molecules or ions" along the restoration interface (Sikri, 2020). Other books frame it differently. Phillips calls it fluid ingress along "gaps wider than 1 µm" (Anusavice et al, n.d.). Summitt calls it a "flux of substances" at the restoration-tooth interface and says no material gives a hermetic seal (Hilton et al, 2013). Nakabayashi and Pashley (1998) call it diffusion into a fluid-filled gap.

The reason it matters is the pulp. Sikri says bacterial leakage, not material toxicity, is the greater threat to the pulp (Sikri, 2020). Walton and Torabinejad (2002) call bacterial colonisation of cavity walls "the major cause" of pulpal injury, and Mount et al (2016) call microleakage "the principal reason for pulpal inflammation following restoration". Sturdevant adds a qualifier: Class V studies with etch-and-rinse adhesives found no pulpal inflammation or necrosis despite a probably incomplete dentin seal (Ritter, 2019).

### S2. Clinical implications
1. **Postoperative sensitivity.** Fluid movement in tubules; worse in proximal and cervical cavities and with resin (Sikri, 2020). Summitt lists the range "from marginal stains to sensitivity and chronic pulpitis" (Hilton et al, 2013).
2. **Recurrent (secondary) caries.** Sikri: bacteria under 1.0 µm pass easily, and a gap of more than 10 µm gives them room and nutrients (Sikri, 2020). Summitt: caries risk next to composite rises when gaps exceed 100 to 150 µm (Hilton et al, 2013). Brambilla and Ionescu (2021): wall lesions formed at about 30 µm in vitro (Maske) but needed a mean of 225 µm (range 80 to 560 µm) in situ (Thomas).
3. **Pulpal pathology.** Bacterial products pass the tubules to the pulp (Sikri, 2020); persistent leakage can end in necrosis and periradicular disease (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
4. **Marginal discoloration.** Interfacial stain that cannot be polished out, often leading to replacement (Sikri, 2020). Goldstein treats the stain as a clinical sign: composite lets you "see microleakage in the form of marginal stain" (Goldstein et al, 2018).
5. **Breakdown of luting cements** (Sikri, 2020).
6. **Replacement burden.** Leakage and staining are a frequent reason for replacement (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S3. Methods to assess microleakage (Sikri's classification)
Sikri says most methods are in vitro and that the pulpo-dentinal complex "cannot be easily simulated" (Sikri, 2020).
1. **Penetration methods.**
   - Dye: methylene blue, India ink, crystal violet, fluorescein, rhodamine B, basic fuchsin and others; immerse, section, score depth. "One of the oldest and most commonly used methods", but its validity "is, however, questioned": particle size and penetration time differ (5 min to 1 h), basic fuchsin binds carious dentin, air is trapped, some dyes lose colour (e.g. aniline blue becomes colourless with calcium hydroxide), and scoring is subjective (Sikri, 2020).
   - Chemical tracers: silver nitrate with developer; both chemicals must penetrate (Sikri, 2020).
   - Radioisotopes (45Ca, 131I, 32P and others) by autoradiography; isotope molecules 40 nm against the smallest dye particles, 120 nm; limits are subjectivity, scatter, affinity of 45Ca for tooth, cost and safety (Sikri, 2020).
   - Neutron activation (manganese): cannot show where the leak is; costly (Sikri, 2020).
   - Bacterial penetration: qualitative; bacteria need gaps of about 0.5 to 1.0 µm, so smaller gaps that pass toxins are missed (Sikri, 2020).
2. **Air pressure:** tooth not destroyed, result quantifiable; not in vivo, and the air dries the specimen (Sikri, 2020).
3. **Fluid filtration:** no tracer, automated and precise; pressure is hard to standardise (Sikri, 2020).
4. **Electrochemical:** current across the interface; not for metallic restorations (Sikri, 2020).
5. **Microscopy:** SEM (preparation artefacts), fluorescent microscopy (reproducible, cheap), optical coherence tomography (Sikri, 2020). Swept-source OCT detected gaps down to half a micron (Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).
6. **Miscellaneous:** artificial caries, pain perception with calcium chloride, reverse diffusion (time-dependent leakage), three-dimensional analysis now by CBCT, laser microspectral analysis, resin infiltration (Sikri, 2020).
7. **Nanoleakage:** refers specifically to dentin bonding (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); a silver tracer in the hybrid layer is read by SEM or TEM (Sikri, 2020).

Other books add: thermocycling and storage in dye or radioisotope, then sectioning, with guidelines in ISO TR 11405 (McCabe and Walls, 2008); qualitative scoring or quantitative measurement after methylene blue or silver nitrate (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); in endodontics, root-end seal tests by dye, fluid filtration, protein leakage, glucose concentration, bacteria and SEM or confocal adaptation (Parirokh and Shabahang, 2017).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
Full synthesis on three claims where the books diverge.

### Claim 1. Dye penetration is a valid measure of microleakage
- Textbook: commonest method, validity questioned (Sikri, 2020); the two-dimensional dye test is the commonest in vitro method because of low cost, and dye type "does not affect the results" (Coluzzi and Parker, 2023).
- Other evidence: silver nitrate is preferred because dyes diffuse and fade (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998). Methylene blue is discoloured by calcium hydroxide and should not be used for MTA and calcium-hydroxide-releasing cements (Parirokh and Shabahang, 2017). Taylor and Lynch (1992, full text) found isotopes give finer detail than dyes and that scoring is subjective [LIT].
- Agreement or challenge: Sikri, Nakabayashi and Pashley, and Parirokh and Shabahang challenge the Coluzzi and Parker statement. The books are not reconciled here.
- Strength: all are laboratory reasoning; no clinical validation is cited.
- Conclusion: dye results compare materials only within one protocol; they are not an absolute leakage value. [Likely]

### Claim 2. Laboratory leakage predicts clinical performance
- Textbook: in vitro tests cannot reproduce the pulpo-dentinal complex (Sikri, 2020); in vitro values "often do not correlate with clinical behaviour" (Ritter, 2019); "unlikely to predict their clinical outcomes" (Hargreaves et al, 2012); large inter-laboratory variation and often no correlation between bond strength and microleakage (Anusavice et al, n.d.); there are "no standardized techniques" to measure it (Berman and Hargreaves, n.d.).
- Other evidence: in vitro results are a "theoretical maximum level of leakage" (Bajabaa et al, 2021, full text) [LIT]; lab data correlated only weakly with clinical results (Peutzfeldt et al, 2024, full text) [LIT]. Fluid filtration results have not agreed with other leakage tests (Parirokh and Shabahang, 2017).
- Agreement: the books agree; the method tests also disagree with each other.
- Conclusion: lab leakage ranks techniques; it does not give a clinical failure rate. Never infer clinical superiority from it.

### Claim 3. Microleakage causes secondary caries
- Textbook: gap over 10 µm supports bacteria (Sikri, 2020); caries risk rises above 100 to 150 µm (Hilton et al, 2013); bacterial leakage is the principal cause of pulpal inflammation (Mount et al, 2016).
- Challenge: the microleakage theory "has been questioned"; Lima 2009 is reported as showing that microleakage did not influence secondary caries; thresholds range from about 30 µm in vitro to 225 µm in situ (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021).
- Strength: the thresholds come from different models (microcosm, in situ) and cannot be pooled.
- Conclusion: leakage is a plausible contributor, but the gap size that matters clinically is not settled. Clinically, judge the margin with plaque and caries risk, not stain alone. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(749 words; about 34 min at 22 words/min)**

**Introduction**
Microleakage is the clinically undetectable passage of bacteria, their products, fluids, molecules and ions along the restoration-tooth interface (Sikri, 2020). No restorative material gives a hermetic seal (Hilton et al, 2013). Its importance is biological: bacteria leaking along the gap, rather than material toxicity, are the main threat to the pulp (Sikri, 2020); Mount calls microleakage the principal reason for pulpal inflammation after restoration (Mount et al, 2016).

**A. Clinical implications**
1. *Postoperative sensitivity.* Fluid moves in exposed tubules. It is worse in proximal and cervical cavities and with resin restorations (Sikri, 2020).
2. *Recurrent caries.* Bacteria are under 1 µm, so they pass easily; a gap over 10 µm lets them grow (Sikri, 2020). Summitt places the caries risk next to composite at gaps over 100 to 150 µm (Hilton et al, 2013).
3. *Pulpal inflammation and necrosis.* Bacterial products reach the pulp through tubules (Sikri, 2020; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
4. *Marginal discoloration.* Stain at the interface cannot be polished away and often leads to replacement. Goldstein treats visible marginal stain as a clinical sign of leakage (Goldstein et al, 2018).
5. *Breakdown and dissolution of luting cements* (Sikri, 2020).
6. *Replacement burden.* Leakage and staining are a frequent reason for replacement (Hilton et al, 2013).

**B. Methods to assess microleakage**
Almost all are in vitro; the living pulp cannot be simulated (Sikri, 2020).

| Group | Method | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Penetration | Dye (methylene blue, basic fuchsin, rhodamine B) | Commonest, cheap; subjective scoring |
| | Chemical tracer (silver nitrate) | Silver does not fade (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998) |
| | Radioisotopes (45Ca, 131I) | 40 nm, finer than dye; cost, safety |
| | Neutron activation | Cannot locate the leak |
| | Bacterial | Qualitative; misses gaps under 0.5 to 1 µm |
| Air pressure | Bubbles at margin | Non-destructive; not in vivo |
| Fluid filtration | Volume through interface | No tracer, quantitative; pressure hard to standardise |
| Electrochemical | Current across interface | Not for metal restorations |
| Microscopy | SEM, fluorescence, OCT | SEM artefacts; OCT non-destructive |
| Miscellaneous | Artificial caries, CaCl2 pain test, reverse diffusion, 3D/CBCT | Reverse diffusion shows time-dependent leakage |

(Sikri, 2020.) Specimens are thermocycled, stored in dye or isotope, sectioned and scored; guidelines are in ISO TR 11405 (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Nanoleakage, which refers specifically to dentin bonding (Sakaguchi et al, 2019), is shown by a silver tracer in the hybrid layer read by SEM or TEM (Sikri, 2020). One clinical study used caries indicator paste to show marginal leakage around crowns on endodontically treated molars (Goldstein et al, 2018).

**C. Critical appraisal**
- *Dye validity.* Sikri says dye validity is questioned because dyes differ in particle size and binding (Sikri, 2020). Coluzzi and Parker say dye type does not affect results (Coluzzi and Parker, 2023). Methylene blue is bleached by calcium hydroxide, so it is unsuitable for MTA (Parirokh and Shabahang, 2017).
- *Lab versus clinic.* In vitro leakage often does not correlate with clinical behaviour (Ritter, 2019; Hargreaves et al, 2012), does not correlate with bond strength (Anusavice et al, n.d.), and tests disagree with each other (Parirokh and Shabahang, 2017).
- *Leakage as cause of secondary caries.* Sikri attributes wall lesions to existing microleakage (Sikri, 2020). Brambilla and Ionescu report that the microleakage theory of secondary caries has been questioned, with wall-lesion thresholds from about 30 µm in vitro to 225 µm in situ (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021).

**D. Choosing a method**
Each method answers a different question. Dye, tracer and isotope methods show that a pathway exists and how deep it runs, but need sectioning, which destroys the specimen and records only one plane. Fluid filtration and air pressure measure flow through the whole interface and keep the tooth, so the same specimen can be retested over time. Microscopy shows the gap itself but not whether fluid crosses it. Bacterial and artificial caries models come closest to the biological question but are qualitative and slow. A sound study therefore states the dye or tracer, the thermocycling regime, the storage time and the scoring scale, and compares groups only within one protocol. [Likely]

**Clinical assessment**
In the mouth, leakage is inferred, not measured: brown marginal stain, a gap that admits a sharp explorer tip (Hilton et al, 2013), sensitivity, and caries at the margin. Stain from leakage may signal decay under the restoration; if the stain can be removed, the margin can be refinished and resealed (Goldstein et al, 2018).

**Conclusion**
Microleakage explains sensitivity, staining, recurrent caries and pulpal disease, but its measurement is method-dependent. Laboratory tests rank materials within one protocol; clinical decisions rest on clinical examination and follow-up.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Definition: clinically undetectable passage of bacteria, fluids, molecules or ions at the interface (Sikri, 2020).
- Bacterial leakage, not material toxicity, is the main pulpal threat (Sikri, 2020); microleakage is the principal reason for post-restoration pulpal inflammation (Mount et al, 2016).
- Gap figures differ by book: over 10 µm (Sikri, 2020); 100 to 150 µm (Hilton et al, 2013); 30 µm in vitro, 225 µm in situ (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021).
- Isotope molecules 40 nm against the smallest dye particles, 120 nm (Sikri, 2020).
- Bacterial method misses gaps under 0.5 to 1 µm (Sikri, 2020).
- Fluid filtration: no tracer, quantitative, pressure hard to standardise (Sikri, 2020).
- ISO TR 11405 gives leakage-test guidelines (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- In vitro leakage often does not predict clinical behaviour (Ritter, 2019).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Merging the two Sikri numbers: bacteria are under 1 µm; the gap that supports their growth is over 10 µm.
2. Calling dye penetration a precise quantitative test; the books disagree on its validity and dye choice.
3. Using methylene blue as a universal dye; it is unsuitable for calcium-hydroxide-releasing materials such as MTA.
4. Presenting lab leakage as a clinical failure rate, or high bond strength as proof of seal.
5. Stating "micro-CT" as a Sikri method; Sikri names CBCT for three-dimensional analysis.
