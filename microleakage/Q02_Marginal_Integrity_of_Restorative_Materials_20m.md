## ANSWER

### Definition and scope
No book in the repository defines "marginal integrity" as a separate term. The working meaning used here is the author's synthesis: the continuity and durability of the junction between restoration and cavity margin, judged by the gap that remains and what passes through it [Likely]. The clinical question is whether that gap admits bacteria, fluids and ions, that is, microleakage. Sikri defines microleakage as the clinically undetectable passage of bacteria, fluids, molecules or ions along the cavity and restoration interface (Sikri, 2020).

Every book agrees that no material gives a perfect seal. Sikri: "no restorative material can chemically bond to the tooth structure providing perfect seal", so a gap of at least 10 µm remains even with adhesive liners (Sikri, 2020). Summitt's: "No restorative material or technique can ensure a complete hermetic seal" (Hilton et al, 2013). Marzouk: every restoration is surrounded by a microleakage space of 20 to 120 microns "even in clinically successful restorations" (Marzouk et al, n.d.).

### Properties that decide the gap
Sikri names coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE), polymerisation shrinkage and adhesion as the main properties; creep, elasticity, fatigue and solubility contribute to a minor extent (Sikri, 2020).
- **CTE (Table 22.1, ×10⁻⁶/°C):** tooth (crown) 11.4, dentin 8.3, aluminous porcelain 6.6, Type II glass-ionomer 11.0, pure gold 14.0, silver amalgam 25.0, composites 20 to 25, denture resin 81 (Sikri, 2020). The greater the mismatch with tooth, the more percolation. The row label is "tooth (crown)"; the caption says values are relative to tooth enamel.
- **Polymerisation shrinkage (Table 22.2):** acrylic heat cure 7%, acrylic cold cure 3.5%, conventional composite 1.5 to 2.0%, microfilled 1.3 to 1.5%, hybrid 2.2 to 2.5% (Sikri, 2020). The same chapter's text gives composite volumetric shrinkage as 1.67 to 5.68%, which does not match its own table; both are quoted here as printed.
- **Adhesion:** governed by wetting, surface energy, water and smear layer, and composition (Sikri, 2020).
- **Minor properties:** creep, elasticity, fatigue, solubility (Sikri, 2020).
- **Operator:** poor packing, condensation and insertion, or poor cavity design, increase dimensional change, dissolution and marginal misfit (Sikri, 2020). Isolation and finishing are also in the operator's hands.

McCabe and Walls caution that the thermal effect should not be overstated, because intraoral thermal stimuli are brief and affect only surface layers (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### Factors controlling bacterial entry and gap size
Sikri's three-factor model (Fig 22.5) has gap size, host defence and material quality as inputs and bacterial penetration as the output. Sikri gives interfacial gaps of 10 to 50 µm and says even 10 µm admits oral organisms; whether bacteria survive depends also on host defence (dentinal fluid, sclerosis, smear plugs) and material quality (fluoride, corrosion products) (Sikri, 2020). Sikri's Fig 22.1 locates the routes of leakage at the interface: within the smear layer, between smear layer and liner (or varnish), and between liner and restoration (Sikri, 2020).

Phillips puts the threshold for fluid ingress lower, at gaps wider than 1 µm (Anusavice et al, n.d.). Clinical thresholds are far larger: caries risk beside composite rises when gaps exceed 100 to 150 µm, and 100 µm is the smallest ledge an explorer detects (Hilton et al, 2013); caries is unlikely with gaps under 250 µm (Ritter, 2019).

### Amalgam
The initial gap is 10 to 15 µm. Tin oxide and chloride corrosion products seal it, which is why amalgam is called a "self-sealing restoration"; high-copper alloys corrode more slowly and so leak for longer (Sikri, 2020). Summitt's calls amalgam "the only restorative material in which the marginal seal improves with time" (Hilton et al, 2013). Garg states microleakage occurs with a 2 to 20 µm gap between amalgam and tooth (Garg and Garg, 2011).

Net dimensional change of modern alloys is ±0.2% by volume, with ADA Specification No. 1 allowing 20 µm/cm; creep under 3.0% is acceptable (Sikri, 2020). Spherical alloys leak more than lathe-cut (Sikri, 2020; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

Measures: alloy choice, incremental condensation, burnishing, unfilled resin over margins, bonded amalgam. Varnish under high-copper amalgam "did not effectively reduce the microleakage" (Sikri, 2020). Summitt's reports that two coats of copal varnish cut dentin permeability by 69% and leakage for 4 to 6 months, though some studies show no benefit or increased leakage, and adhesive resin sealers reduce leakage compared with unsealed or varnished amalgam when evaluated in the short term (24 hours to 14 days) (Hilton et al, 2013). Phillips: varnish benefit is not confirmed in vivo, and a base cement does not prevent microleakage under amalgam (Anusavice et al, n.d.).

### Glass-ionomer
GIC has an ion exchange bond (carboxyl to calcium), a CTE near that of tooth, fluoride release and renewal of ionic bonds (Sikri, 2020). Isolate it from moisture "at least 30 minutes after placement"; solubility "continues to be a problem for 24 hours"; desiccation crazing at the margin causes leakage (Sikri, 2020). RMGI has "occasionally" leaked more than chemically cured GIC because of resin shrinkage (Sikri, 2020); Phillips states this without "occasionally" and adds poorer wetting (Anusavice et al, n.d.). Pickard's lists marginal dissolution and shrinkage of GIC as a cause of lost marginal integrity (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). A marginal gap matters less around anticariogenic GIC (Hilton et al, 2013).

### Composite
Shrinkage and CTE mismatch break the dentin bond first; enamel etching reduces leakage "to almost nil" (Sikri, 2020). Composites have no intrinsic defence against caries, so once a gap forms recurrent caries follows quickly (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Full analysis is in Q01. Kidd (1976, full text) reported an early composite with a good initial seal that leaked more after temperature cycling. Peutzfeldt et al (2024, full text) found marginal gap and dentin bond strength differed between adhesive and composite combinations, with weak correlation to clinical outcome.

### Direct gold
Malleability, a short bevel and insolubility give good adaptation, and the material can be burnished. Mat and powdered golds are porous, so they are used inside and cohesive foil forms the surface veneer (Sikri, 2020). Mount's view is harsher: direct gold is "highly prone to microleakage in the absence of a sealing base" (Mount et al, 2016).

### Cast restorations
Gaps of 10 to 160 µm are reported, so a luting cement must seal the interface. Burnish zinc phosphate margins after 24 hours and resin lutes at once; gaps over 0.1 mm mean remaking the casting (Sikri, 2020). Sikri's other book notes some authors find no correlation between gap and microleakage, and leakage is higher with dentin margins (Sikri, 2017). Its 50 to 100 µm figure is a gap present at every tooth and restoration interface irrespective of material, not a cast-specific value (Sikri, 2017). Marzouk calls the tooth, cement and cast joint the weakest link (Marzouk et al, n.d.).

### Ceramic
Ceramic relies on etch, silane and resin cement. The silane bond weakens by hydrolysis, with a marked fall in bond strength after one year; the most vulnerable site of leakage is wear of the cement lute and the inlay and tooth interface (Sikri, 2020). Ceramic inlay gaps are clinically acceptable but larger than with gold (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Porcelain CTE is close to tooth, so percolation is less of a problem if the seal is good (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). For CAD/CAM, margin openings under 100 µm are desirable (Ritter, 2019).

### Smear layer (Sikri's unresolved point)
One view keeps the smear layer as a barrier (Pashley 1991, as cited by Sikri, reports an 86% fall in dentin permeability); the other removes it because it harbours bacteria and impairs adaptation. Sikri does not settle it (Sikri, 2020). The Pashley study itself was not read.

### Clinical relevance of the gap
Laboratory gaps of a few micrometres differ from clinical thresholds: caries risk beside composite rises above 100 to 150 µm (Hilton et al, 2013), and caries is unlikely under 250 µm (Ritter, 2019). In vitro leakage "often do[es] not correlate with clinical behaviour" (Ritter, 2019), and some authors find no correlation between marginal gap and microleakage at all (Sikri, 2017). Leakage is consistently higher where the margin lies in dentin rather than enamel (Sikri, 2017). A gap matters less beside fluoride-releasing GIC (Hilton et al, 2013).

### Prevention
- **Bond:** etch enamel; bonded amalgam; resin lutes with tooth bonding first.
- **Choose the material for the site:** GIC where caries risk is high, with early moisture protection (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020).
- **Manipulate correctly:** incremental condensation and placement, low-creep alloys.
- **Protect GIC early:** 30 minutes isolation, sealant.
- **Seal margins:** unfilled resin over amalgam and composite margins; bonded amalgam rather than varnish under high-copper alloys, where varnish did not reduce leakage (Sikri, 2020).
- **Keep margins in enamel where possible:** leakage is higher at dentin margins (Sikri, 2017) and enamel etching reduces it almost to nil (Sikri, 2020).
- **Finish castings:** burnish zinc phosphate margins after 24 hours; remake gaps over 0.1 mm.
- **Review:** recall, and check for ditching, staining and secondary caries.

### Conclusion
Marginal integrity is a balance of material properties and operator skill. No material gives a permanent seal. Textbooks agree that leakage falls under amalgam and is least at enamel margins; they disagree on timing, on high-copper alloys and on glass-ionomer (see TEXTBOOK DISAGREEMENTS). The clinician aims for a gap that bacteria cannot use and checks the margin over time.

### Critical appraisal
Three claims, textbooks only except where marked.

**Claim A. "Amalgam is self-sealing."**
- *What the books state:* Sikri, 2020: corrosion products seal a 10 to 15 µm gap; high-copper leaks longer. Hilton et al, 2013 (Summitt's 4th ed): "the only restorative material in which the marginal seal improves with time", and high-copper prolongs post-placement leakage. Ritter, 2019 (Sturdevant 2nd South Asian ed): seal usually by 6 weeks, high-copper slightly longer. Mount et al, 2016 (3rd ed): sealed "within the first few days". Anusavice et al (Phillips 12th ed, n.d.): both low- and high-copper seal, high-copper more slowly. Bonsor and Pearson, 2013: high-copper alloys "have been associated with greater microleakage". McCabe and Walls, 2008 (9th ed): laboratory leakage falls with time in a corrosive environment.
- *Evidence:* a corrosion mechanism plus laboratory storage findings (McCabe and Walls, 2008); Kidd (1976, review, full text, from old file) reports leakage falling as amalgams age.
- *Interpretation:* the books agree on direction (leakage falls) and disagree on time (days versus weeks) and on whether high-copper alloys are merely slower or worse.
- *Limitation:* no textbook gives a primary longitudinal source for the time course; the two figures cannot be reconciled from the books.
- *Clinical implication:* a fresh amalgam, especially high-copper, needs an interim seal; which sealer works is itself disputed (Claim C).

**Claim B. "Glass-ionomer seals the margin."**
- *What the books state:* Mount et al, 2016: ion exchange gives "a union which is proof against microleakage". Sikri, 2020: chemical bond and tooth-like CTE, but RMGI has "occasionally" leaked more than conventional GIC. Anusavice et al (n.d.): RMGI causes "more microleakage than with conventional glass ionomers". Goldstein, 2018 (3rd ed): all studies found GIC and RMGI "failed to prevent microleakage", gingival worse than occlusal. Banerjee and Watson, 2011 (Pickard's 9th ed): marginal dissolution and shrinkage of GIC is a cause of lost marginal integrity. Mount, 1994 (Atlas 2nd ed): a varnished GIC immersed in dye 7 minutes after placement shows severe dye penetration into the cement, while one sealed with a resin bonding agent shows none (Figs 1.20 and 1.21).
- *Interpretation:* "proof against microleakage" is Mount's position. The dye images in Mount's own Atlas show the seal depends on early protection.
- *Limitation:* the opposing statements come from dye studies, whose validity Sturdevant questions: in vitro leakage "often do[es] not correlate with clinical behaviour" (Ritter, 2019).
- *Clinical implication:* protect GIC early (Sikri: 30 min isolation; Mount: 24 h sealant) and do not claim it never leaks.

**Claim C. "A smaller gap means better marginal integrity, and varnish closes it."**
- *What the books state:* the minimum gap varies from 1 µm (Anusavice et al, n.d.) to 10 µm (Sikri, 2020) to 20 to 120 µm (Marzouk et al, n.d.) and 50 to 100 µm (Sikri, 2017); clinical caries thresholds are 100 to 150 µm (Hilton et al, 2013) and 250 µm (Ritter, 2019). Sikri, 2017 notes some authors find no correlation between marginal gap and microleakage. On varnish: ineffective under high-copper amalgam (Sikri, 2020); 69% permeability reduction but mixed leakage results (Hilton et al, 2013); not confirmed in vivo (Anusavice et al, n.d.).
- *Interpretation:* laboratory gap figures and clinical thresholds differ by one to two orders of magnitude; they are different measures and should not be ranked against each other.
- *Limitation:* none of these are clinical trials of marginal integrity; Peutzfeldt et al (2024, full text) also found only weak correlation between laboratory marginal measures and clinical results.
- *Clinical implication:* judge margins clinically (explorer, staining, caries), not by laboratory gap size alone.

## TEXTBOOK DISAGREEMENTS
- **Amalgam seal time.** Ritter (ed), Sturdevant's Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, 2nd South Asia ed (2019): "usually 6 weeks", high-copper slightly longer. Mount et al, Preservation and Restoration of Tooth Structure, 3rd ed (2016): "within the first few days".
- **High-copper amalgam.** Anusavice et al, Phillips' Science of Dental Materials, 12th ed (n.d.); Sikri, Textbook of Conservative and Restorative Dentistry, first eBook ed (2020); Hilton et al, Summitt's Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, 4th ed (2013): it seals, but more slowly. Bonsor and Pearson, A Clinical Guide to Applied Dental Materials, edition not stated (2013): "associated with greater microleakage", microgap persists without γ2.
- **GIC seal.** Mount et al, 3rd ed (2016): "proof against microleakage". Goldstein (ed), Esthetics in Dentistry, 3rd ed (2018): GIC and RMGI "failed to prevent microleakage" in all laser and bur studies.
- **RMGI versus conventional GIC.** Sikri, first eBook ed (2020): RMGI has "occasionally" leaked more. Anusavice et al, Phillips' 12th ed (n.d.): more microleakage than conventional GIC, without the qualifier, and adds poorer wetting.
- **GIC moisture window.** Sikri, first eBook ed (2020): isolate 30 min; solubility 24 h. Mount, An Atlas of Glass-Ionomer Cements, 2nd ed (1994): sealant for a minimum of 24 h.
- **Universal gap.** Sikri, Textbook of Conservative and Restorative Dentistry, first eBook ed (2020): at least 10 µm. Marzouk et al, Operative Dentistry (edition and year not stated, n.d.): 20 to 120 µm. Sikri, Indirect Restorations in Dental Practice, 2nd ed (2017): 50 to 100 µm "irrespective of the material choice". Anusavice et al, Phillips' 12th ed (n.d.): fluid ingress along gaps wider than 1 µm.
- **Varnish.** Hilton et al, Summitt's 4th ed (2013): two coats cut permeability by 69% and leakage for 4 to 6 months. Sikri, first eBook ed (2020): ineffective under high-copper amalgam. Anusavice et al, Phillips' 12th ed (n.d.): benefit not confirmed in vivo.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- No material seals perfectly; at least 10 µm gap remains (Sikri, 2020).
- Three properties: CTE, polymerisation shrinkage, adhesion (Sikri, 2020).
- Amalgam self-seals with tin corrosion products; high-copper slower; time 6 weeks (Ritter, 2019) or days (Mount et al, 2016).
- GIC: isolate 30 min, soluble 24 h (Sikri, 2020); RMGI may leak more than conventional GIC (Sikri, 2020; Anusavice et al, n.d.).
- Composite: dentin bond fails first; enamel bond gives almost nil leakage (Sikri, 2020).
- Cast: 10 to 160 µm gap; over 0.1 mm remake (Sikri, 2020).
- Clinical gap thresholds: 100 to 150 µm (Hilton et al, 2013); 250 µm (Ritter, 2019).
- In vitro leakage often does not predict clinical behaviour (Ritter, 2019).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Writing that GIC needs 24 hours of moisture control. Sikri's figure is isolation for at least 30 minutes; 24 hours refers to solubility (Sikri, 2020).
2. Stating amalgam's dimensional change limit as a percentage. ADA Specification No. 1 allows 20 µm/cm; ±0.2% is the net volume change of modern alloys (Sikri, 2020).
3. Presenting "GIC never leaks" or "high-copper amalgam seals better" as settled. The textbooks disagree.
4. Caution on 11.4 ×10⁻⁶/°C: the row label is "tooth (crown)"; the caption says values are relative to tooth enamel (Sikri, 2020).
5. Quoting laboratory gap sizes as clinical failure thresholds.
