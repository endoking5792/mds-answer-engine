# Q06. Microleakage and its clinical significance.

**Marks:** 5 (Short Note) — Oct 2016 (Endodontics PIII 16-21)
**Repeats absorbed:** "Micro leakage: What are the clinical implications?" (5m, Oct 2003, Conservative Dentistry PI 01-07; wording marked "reconstructed" in the repository)
**Scope note:** Restorative and endodontic (coronal) implications. Methods of assessment are in Q04; dye studies are in Q07.

## ANSWER

### Definition
Microleakage is the clinically undetectable passage of bacteria and their products, fluids, molecules or ions along the gaps at the cavity and restoration interface (Sikri, 2020). The definition is written for restorations, not root fillings. No restorative material gives a hermetic seal, and the effects of leakage range "from marginal stains to sensitivity and chronic pulpitis" (Hilton et al, 2013).

### Clinical significance in restorative dentistry

**1. Postoperative sensitivity.** Leakage creates a direct link between oral fluids and the pulp. Fluid moving in the dentinal tubules causes sensitivity, which is worse in proximal and cervical cavities and under resin restorations (Sikri, 2020). Leakage around provisional crowns is a common cause of postoperative sensitivity (Ingle's Endodontics, 7th ed, n.d.).

**2. Recurrent (secondary) caries.** Sikri gives two separate figures that are often merged by mistake: bacteria with an average diameter under 1.0 µm pass easily, and a gap of more than 10 µm gives room for nutrients that sustain them (Sikri, 2020). Leakage at the margin therefore supports bacterial growth and recurrent caries.

**3. Pulpal pathology.** Bacteria and their products reach the pulp through the dentinal tubules. Bacterial leakage, rather than the toxicity of the restorative material, is the greater threat to the pulp (Sikri, 2020). Mount et al (2016) call microleakage the principal reason for pulpal inflammation after a restoration is placed.

**4. Marginal discoloration.** Stains form along the leaking margin. They are difficult to remove by conventional means and often lead to replacement of the restoration (Sikri, 2020). Kidd (1976) also describes discoloration of tooth tissue under amalgam restorations [LIT, full text, earlier session].

**5. Breakdown of materials.** Leakage hastens dissolution of materials at the interface, such as cement lutes (Sikri, 2020), and of some filling materials (Kidd, 1976).

### Clinical significance in endodontics (coronal leakage)

**1. Reinfection of the root canal.** Leakage can occur within a short time, even through well-obturated canals (Berman and Hargreaves, 2021). Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011) state that the apical tissues can be reinfected in as little as 19 days, and with relative certainty within 3 months, if the coronal seal is lost. In vitro, more than 50% of canals were fully contaminated after 19 days of coronal exposure (Torabinejad et al, 1990) [LIT, full text, earlier session].

**2. Outcome of treatment.** In a cross-sectional study, absence of periapical inflammation was 91.4% when both the root filling and the coronal restoration were good (Ray and Trope, 1995) [LIT, full text, earlier session]. This shows association, not cause.

**3. The coronal seal matters as much as the apical seal.** The practical rule is an immediate, permanent coronal restoration after root canal treatment (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). Intra-orifice barriers reduce coronal leakage in vitro with no clear difference between materials (Chen et al, 2023), although one review reports MTA as the least leaking barrier (Mehta et al, 2022) [LIT, full text, earlier session].

### Conclusion
Microleakage matters clinically because it causes sensitivity, recurrent caries, pulpal inflammation, discoloration and breakdown of materials in restorations, and can reinfect a root-filled canal. Much of the evidence, especially for coronal leakage, comes from laboratory studies, and its clinical weight is debated (see below).

## TEXTBOOK DISAGREEMENTS
- **How much coronal leakage matters.** Gutmann and Lovdahl, 5th ed (2011): reinfection in as little as 19 days, near certain in 3 months. Cohen's 12th ed (Berman and Hargreaves, 2021): leakage "has been proposed" to cause failure on in vitro evidence, and its clinical significance "has been challenged". Torabinejad, Fouad and Shabahang, 6th ed (2021): "clinically less significant" than laboratory studies suggest.
- **Main cause of post-treatment disease.** Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011): coronal leakage reinfects the canal. Siqueira et al, Treatment of Endodontic Infections, 2nd ed (2022): persistent infection is the most common cause.
- **When to retreat after a lost seal.** Torabinejad et al (2021): one guideline says 2 to 3 months of exposure, others 2 to 3 weeks. Cohen's 12th ed (2021): no clear evidence to retreat solely because a temporary restoration was in place for more than 3 months.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Definition: clinically undetectable passage at the cavity and restoration interface (Sikri, 2020).
- Five restorative effects: sensitivity, recurrent caries, pulpal disease, discoloration, breakdown of materials.
- Bacteria under 1 µm; gap over 10 µm holds nutrients. Two figures, not one.
- Coronal reinfection in as little as 19 days (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- The clinical weight of coronal leakage is disputed.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Merging Sikri's two numbers into "bacteria pass 10 µm gaps".
2. Extending Sikri's definition to root fillings.
3. Stating coronal leakage as a proven cause of endodontic failure.
4. Listing causes of leakage instead of its consequences.
