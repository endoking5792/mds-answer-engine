## 1. Composition and breakdown

### Explanation

Carbamide peroxide (urea hydrogen peroxide, CH6N2O3) is available as white crystals "that contain H2O2 in a concentration of about 35%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's says "approximately 35% H2O2" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. It was first used in a tray by Klusmier in the late 1960s, and "In 1989, Haywood and Heymann wrote the first article on an at-home bleaching technique" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Breakdown of 10% carbamide peroxide — nine books, five figures (the same set is shown in B02, B04, B05, B10 and B11): Summitt's "6.5% urea and 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Torabinejad's "approximately 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" with urea, ammonia and carbon dioxide (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's 10-30% "(equivalent to 3.5% to 8.6% hydrogen peroxide)", although its home-bleaching section gives 10-22% "with an effective yield of 4% to 7.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's "approximately 3.4% hydrogen peroxide and 6.5% urea", although its reaction scheme a page earlier writes "+ 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson's "release 3.3% hydrogen peroxide", the one-third rule (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's "3% hydrogen peroxide (active ingredient) and 7% urea" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri's "10% carbamide peroxide is equal to 3.0% hydrogen peroxide" in both his books (Sikri, 2020; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Alani and Patel's "approximately 3% hydrogen peroxide over a period of 4 h" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Reasoning: the crystals "contain H2O2 in a concentration of about 35%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], so 10% gives about 3.5%; 3.3% and 3% are rounded one-third figures [Likely]. At higher strengths the books fall below that proportion: Cohen's 30% = 8.6% (proportionally 10.5%) and Summitt's "A 35% carbamide peroxide product is approximately equivalent to a 9.5% hydrogen peroxide product" (proportionally about 12%) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; neither book explains the shortfall [Likely; the arithmetic is the auditor's]. Luk and Anagnostaki: "a carbamide peroxide concentration of 16% is equivalent to 5.76% of hydrogen peroxide" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. All give about one-third; Summitt's: "This 3:1 reduction of carbamide peroxide to the active hydrogen peroxide ion explains why" carbamide products are dosed at three times the hydrogen peroxide strength, and "A 35% carbamide peroxide product is approximately equivalent to a 9.5% hydrogen peroxide product" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. "The urea degrades into ammonia and carbon dioxide" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], although Andreasen's cautions "It is, however, unclear how much ammonia will be formed during tooth bleaching using carbamide peroxide" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; urea maintains "an alkaline environment (pH 8) which potentiates the action of hydrogen peroxide" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], though products themselves "have an average pH of 5 to 6.5" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].

Craig's 11th ed, a materials text, takes carbamide peroxide as the usual vital agent: "These agents usually contain some form of peroxide (generally carbamide peroxide) in a gel" (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; the 14th ed widens this to "carbamide or hydrogen peroxide" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

Additives: "The most significant additive to carbamide peroxide is carbopol" — "This ingredient thickens the material and prolongs the release of peroxide over time" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri's gives carbopol "in a concentration of 0.5 to 1.5%, is commonly used thickening agent" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Ingle's: "Carbopol also prolongs the release of active peroxide and improves shelf life", with glycerine or propylene glycol, sodium stannate, phosphoric or citric acid and flavours (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** carbamide peroxide (urea hydrogen peroxide, CO(NH2)2·H2O2) is a white crystalline compound containing about 35% H2O2 (Setzer, 2021). Introduced for tray (nightguard) bleaching by Klusmier (1968) and Haywood and Heymann (1989).

**Breakdown (about one-third H2O2):**
Carbamide peroxide → urea + H2O2; urea → ammonia + CO2; H2O2 → water + oxygen radicals (bleach).
- 10% CP ≈ 3.5% H2O2 + 6.5% urea (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 3.4% (Gopikrishna, 2021); 3% + 7% urea (Ritter et al, 2019).
- 22% CP ≈ 7.5% H2O2 (Sikri, 2020); 35% CP ≈ 9.5% H2O2 (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Urea raises pH to about 8, potentiating H2O2 and possibly retarding caries.

**Composition of gel:** carbamide peroxide; carbopol 0.5-1.5% (thickener, slow release, shelf life); glycerine/propylene glycol (vehicle); sodium stannate (stabilizer); phosphoric or citric acid; flavours; potassium nitrate and fluoride (desensitizers) (Rotstein, 2019; Sikri, 2020).

## 2. Concentrations, uses, advantages and side effects

### Explanation

Concentrations — books differ: "at about 10% strength, but it can reach up to 30%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's 10-30% (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Torabinejad's "varying between 3% and 15%" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's 3 to 45% across home and in-office use (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sturdevant's recommends 10%-15% for nightguard bleaching (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's: "the best combination of safety, limited side effects, and speed of action is obtained with a 10% solution" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Activity time — books disagree (the same set is shown in B02, B05 and B11): Summitt's "Carbamide peroxide is active for up to 10 hours, with about 50% of the active agent being used up in the first 2 hours", although "the agent has lost most of its potency after 5 hours" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson: "active even after 10 hours of use" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Grossman's: it "can be active up to several hours" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Alani and Patel: 3% hydrogen peroxide released "over a period of 4 h" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; against Cohen's "whereas carbamide peroxide remains active for 40 to 90 minutes" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha's home regimen, in which "The gel is replenished each hour" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], fits the shorter figure. Summitt's Table 16-2 gives "2 to 10 hours due to carbopol amount" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC], so carbopol content probably explains the gap and Cohen's figure may describe unthickened gels [Likely]; Cohen's also notes that the reaction "takes place at a slower rate compared to hydrogen peroxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Stability: shelf life "of 1–2 years, while hydrogen peroxide has a shelf life of few weeks" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Intracoronal use: "the recommended treatment for internal bleaching is sodium perborate mixed with water, or 10% carbamide peroxide alone" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Safety and effects: FDA lists "3% hydrogen peroxide and 10% to 15% carbamide peroxide as GRAS"; carbamide peroxide "may retard the progression of caries"; sensitivity in "25% to 70% of patients" but "the pulp remains healthy and the sensitivity is completely reversible"; mercury release from amalgam (van Noort agrees in vitro: "carbamide-based bleaching agents can increase the release of mercury from amalgam restorations", van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; bond strength reduced "25% to 50%" with a delay of "1 to 2 weeks or more" needed (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; PMMA provisionals turn yellow-orange "on exposure to carbamide peroxide" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Concentrations and uses**
- 10% (most used, overnight 2-6 weeks), 15-22% home (Ritter et al, 2019; Setzer, 2021).
- 35-45% in-office (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Intracoronal: 10% sealed in the chamber, inside-outside and closed techniques, modified walking bleach (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Tetracycline stains: prolonged tray use.

**Advantages over H2O2:** lower effective concentration, safer to soft tissue; stable (shelf life 1-2 years); slow, sustained release (active up to 10 h, Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Cohen's: 40-90 min, Setzer, 2021); urea raises pH; FDA "generally recognized as safe" at 10-15%.

**Side effects:** reversible tooth sensitivity (25-70%), gingival irritation from trays, mercury release from amalgam, yellowing of PMMA provisionals, 25-50% fall in composite bond strength (wait 1-2 weeks) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Carbamide peroxide = urea + H2O2; about one-third becomes H2O2 (10% ≈ 3.5%).
- Carbopol is the key additive: thickens and prolongs release.
- 10% is the gold standard for nightguard vital bleaching.
- Urea raises pH (about 8).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Equating 10% carbamide peroxide with 10% hydrogen peroxide.
- Forgetting carbopol and its role.
- Writing that carbamide peroxide is used mainly in-office; its main use is home tray bleaching.

## MNEMONIC
**"CUP"**: **C**arbopol, **U**rea (pH up), **P**eroxide one-third.
