## 1. Definition, mechanism and classification

### Explanation

Sturdevant's definition: "The lightening of the colour of a tooth through the application of a chemical agent to oxidize the organic pigmentation in the tooth is referred to as bleaching" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Bleaching and whitening are not strictly the same: Sikri says "Whitening restores teeth to their natural tooth color", whereas bleaching makes them lighter than that (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; the FDA usage quoted by Anagnostaki reserves "bleaching" for whitening teeth beyond their natural colour with products containing bleach (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]; Summitt's authors note that in practice "cosmetic products typically use the word whitening for the removal of surface stains" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Vital in-office bleaching was "first reported in 1868" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Mechanism: the action of hydrogen peroxide "is considered to be oxidation of organic pigments, although the chemistry is not well understood" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. "Hydrogen peroxide diffuses through the organic matrix of the enamel and dentin because of its low molecular weight"; 10% carbamide peroxide "breaks down into 6.5% urea and 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. A newer view (Eimar et al, cited by Anagnostaki) is that "the peroxide whitens teeth by oxidizing its transparent organic matrix into an opaque whiter material" rather than simply breaking chromogens (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. The mechanism is detailed in B10.

Classification. Summitt: "The three types of bleaching therapy are in-office bleaching, at-home bleaching, and over-the-counter products" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant divides vital bleaching into the "in-office vital bleaching technique referred to as power bleaching" and the dentist-prescribed, home-applied (nightguard) technique (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's fuller scheme lists in-office (thermocatalytic, power, non-thermocatalytic, microabrasion-assisted) and home bleaching under vital bleaching, plus laser-assisted bleaching, "Bleaching with non-thermal atmospheric plasma", OTC products and advanced techniques (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Lightening of tooth colour by a chemical agent that oxidizes organic pigment in the tooth (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Mechanism.** Low-molecular-weight hydrogen peroxide diffuses through enamel and dentin and oxidizes pigments; 10% carbamide peroxide → 3.5% H₂O₂ + 6.5% urea (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Classification.** (1) In-office (power) bleaching, with or without heat/light/laser; (2) dentist-supervised at-home (nightguard) bleaching; (3) over-the-counter products (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).

## 2. Indications

### Explanation

"The primary indication for bleaching is patient dissatisfaction with tooth color" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri likewise lists "Persons dissatisfied with the original color of their otherwise sound teeth" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Sturdevant's list: "Teeth intrinsically discoloured because of ageing, trauma or certain medications"; failed walking bleach; harmonising shades before and after restorations; yellow-orange stains ("Teeth exhibiting yellow or orange intrinsic discolouration seem to respond best to vital bleaching", bluish-grey much harder); traumatised but vital teeth and "teeth that have a poor endodontic prognosis because of the absence of a radiographically visible canal" (calcific metamorphosis); and "Brown fluorosis stains also are often responsive to treatment, but white fluorosis stains may not be resolved effectively" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Response rate by stain: "teeth discolored by age, genetics, smoking, or coffee are the fastest to respond", "In general, tetracycline-stained teeth are the slowest to respond to bleaching", and "White spots are not removed by bleaching but may be less noticeable" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** dissatisfaction with colour; ageing, genetic, smoking/coffee yellowing (fastest); trauma-darkened vital teeth, calcific metamorphosis; brown fluorosis; mild tetracycline (slowest); shade matching around restorations (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 3. Case selection: contraindications and guarded cases

### Explanation

Summitt's Box 16-1 contraindications: "Unrealistic expectations", "Unwillingness to comply with treatment", extensive tooth-coloured restorations the patient will not replace, intolerance of taste or technique, and pregnancy or lactation (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] — "it has been recommended that pregnant and lactating women avoid bleaching" because of gingival irritation, although no fetal harm is shown; "In-office bleaching is not recommended for children with large pulps or teeth with cracks"; and "it may be prudent to have the patient forego tobacco use during the period of the bleaching process" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Guarded prognosis (Box 16-2): history of sensitivity, translucent teeth ("some that are already translucent at the incisal edge may become more translucent"), an "Extremely dark gingival third of tooth that is visible when smiling", exposed roots, extensive white spots, and TMD or bruxism ("Patients with a history of temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) may not be good candidates for at-home bleaching") (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Goldstein's contraindications to in-office vital bleaching add "extremely large pulps, which may increase sensitivity", exposed roots, "severe loss of enamel due to attrition, abrasion, or erosion", "teeth exhibiting gross or microscopic enamel cracking", very dark and banded tetracycline teeth, restorations that must be matched, and "patients who are perfectionists: bleaching is not perfect" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson add kidney disease ("Patients with kidney disorders or on kidney dialysis"), allergy, heavy smoking, deciduous teeth, untreated pathology and "Amalgam stains in tooth tissue. Such stains are resistant to bleaching" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri: "Severe discoloration from amalgam corrosion", deep tetracycline, severe fluorosis, extensive restorations or caries (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

**Books disagree on age.** Summitt: "the age of a bleaching patient can range from 10 years to 80 years or older", citing a psychological benefit at 10-14 years (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson contraindicate "Children under 14 years of age" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri: "Tooth bleaching in younger individuals (less than 18 years) should be avoided" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Cohen's and Goldstein's middle position is caution, not prohibition, because of the larger pulp (Setzer, 2021; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Note that Bonsor and Pearson write for UK practice, where legal limits apply (see B08). [Likely] Elderly patients, conversely, are "excellent candidates" because pulps have receded (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

Pre-treatment examination: "A radiograph should always be taken of a single dark tooth, and pulp testing may also be indicated", because otherwise "the disease process occurring in the tooth may be masked by removal of the only clinical symptom" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein's workup includes photographs, radiographs and "an intensive prophylaxis" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Record the shade with a tab and photographs, and "the best way to ensure patient awareness of the effect of bleaching is to bleach one arch at a time"; a natural end-point is when "the white of the teeth matches the white of the eyes" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Case selection.** Examination, radiograph and pulp test of any single dark tooth (bleaching may mask pathology), prophylaxis, baseline shade and photographs (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Contraindications:** unrealistic expectations, poor compliance, pregnancy/lactation, untreated caries or hypersensitivity, cracked or worn enamel, large pulps (in-office), amalgam stains, severe tetracycline (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). **Guarded:** translucent incisal edges, dark gingival third, exposed roots, TMD.
**Age (books differ):** from 10 years (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); not under 14 (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); avoid under 18 (Sikri, 2020).

## 4. Procedures: in-office (power) bleaching

### Explanation

Isolation: "In-office vital bleaching requires an excellent rubber dam technique and careful patient management"; light-cured paint-on dams "cannot provide the same degree of protection and isolation as a conventionally applied rubber dam" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein takes the opposite view for gels: "The conventional rubber dam has been replaced by the use of cheek retractors and gingival protectors", with a resin "barrier to cover approximately 0.5 mm of the cervical area of the tooth and extend 2–3 mm onto the gingiva" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. **Books differ** on whether sheet dam is still required; both demand complete soft-tissue protection. "The teeth should not be anesthetized for in-office bleaching" so that the patient reports discomfort (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); "Local anesthesia is not required" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

Agent and steps: gels "most commonly contain 30–35% hydrogen peroxide" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; "In-office bleaching of vital teeth generally uses a 35% hydrogen peroxide solution" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Cohen's range is 25% to 38% (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. "Etching of teeth with 37% phosphoric acid, once considered a required part of this technique, is unnecessary" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. "If the patient reports a tingling of the gingival tissues, the procedure should be immediately aborted" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein finishes with "a 2% neutral sodium fluoride gel for 5–10 min" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Schedule: "Bleaching treatments generally are rendered weekly for two to six treatments, with each treatment lasting 30–45 min" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt gives an average of three sessions, 1 week apart (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Light and heat: Sturdevant says credible research shows "the addition of light during the bleaching procedure does not improve the whitening result beyond what the bleach alone can achieve" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's Table 16-1 answers "No" to whether lights make a difference (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] (details in B12). Dehydration misleads: it "has been shown to cause a transient 3- to 12-shade change", so the shade "must be evaluated after 1 to 2 weeks" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; patients are warned teeth look chalky at first (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. "A nonsteroidal analgesic and anti-inflammatory drug may be administered if sensitivity is anticipated" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Advantages: under the dentist's control with soft tissue protected, and potentially faster. Disadvantages: cost, "Unknown duration of the treatment" and "The unpredictable outcome" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt recommends that in-office bleaching "be followed by at-home bleaching to enhance the effect and to minimize rebound" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC], and Sturdevant that the combination "has better results than either technique used alone" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**In-office technique.** No anaesthesia; rubber dam or resin gingival barrier; 30-35% H₂O₂ gel, no etching, light optional; abort if gingiva tingles; neutral fluoride gel; 30-45 min visits weekly, two to six (Ritter et al, 2019; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Judge shade after 1-2 weeks (dehydration) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
**Advantages:** dentist-controlled, rapid. **Disadvantages:** cost, unknown duration, unpredictable outcome.

## 5. Procedures: at-home and over-the-counter

### Explanation

Dentist-prescribed at-home (nightguard) bleaching uses "A 10%–15% carbamide peroxide bleaching material" in a custom vacuum-formed tray (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], "a 10% solution of carbamide peroxide (approximately equal to a 3.5% solution of hydrogen peroxide)" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC], worn "for several hours or overnight every day for 2 to 6 weeks" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Full detail in B05.

OTC: "Over-the-counter (OTC) products are available in the form of tray systems, trayless systems, and paint-on products"; "The fundamental problem with OTC products is that there is no examination and diagnosis of the cause of discoloration"; strips carry "approximately 6% to 14%" hydrogen peroxide and "Patients are instructed to wear the strips for 30 minutes twice a day" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Summary ranking: "overnight tray bleaching produces the best results" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**At-home (nightguard):** 10-15% carbamide peroxide in a custom tray, overnight or several hours daily for 2-6 weeks; one arch at a time (Ritter et al, 2019; Setzer, 2021).
**OTC:** strips (6-14% H₂O₂, 30 minutes twice a day), paint-on gels, trays; no diagnosis, so pathology may be masked (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 6. Side effects and longevity

### Explanation

Sensitivity is the commonest effect. Summitt: "Double-blind studies have reported sensitivity in 25% to 70% of patients", but "the pulp remains healthy and the sensitivity is completely reversible" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson: thermal sensitivity "occurs in between 25% and 50% of patients" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri: 10% carbamide peroxide raised sensitivity "in at least 50% of the patients" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. **Craig's is more cautious:** "In vivo studies have demonstrated adverse pulpal effects from bleaching" and "a legitimate concern exists about the long-term use of these products on vital teeth" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. The books do not agree whether the pulpal effect is fully benign; the operative texts call it reversible, the materials text flags long-term uncertainty. [Certain that both views are printed; Likely that the reversible view applies to supervised 10% carbamide peroxide.]

Soft tissue: in-office 30-35% hydrogen peroxide "can easily produce tissue burns, turning the tissue white"; at-home irritation "is most likely the result of an ill-fitting tray rather than the agent itself" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bonding: "A 25% to 50% reduction in bond strength of composite to etched enamel" from residual oxygen (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. **Delay before bonding differs:** 1-2 weeks or more (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); at least 1 week (Ritter et al, 2019); at least 2 weeks (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); 2-3 weeks (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); Sikri says agents should not be used "at least 24 hours prior" to resin treatment (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Restorations: methyl methacrylate provisionals yellow (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; mercury release from some amalgams (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Low-pH solutions cause slight surface softening "to a depth of approximately 25 μm", which remineralizes (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Longevity: "Bleaching generally has an approximate lifespan of 1–3 years" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson describe relapse after 1-3 years (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein: "it may be 3 years before retreatment is desirable" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Cohen: at-home results "tend to remain stable for 1 to 10 years" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Side effects:** reversible sensitivity in 25-70% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) or 25-50% (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013), though Craig's flags long-term pulpal concern (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019); gingival burns or tray irritation; 25-50% fall in bond strength — delay bonding 1-2 weeks (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013), 1 week (Ritter et al, 2019) or 2 weeks (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); PMMA temporaries yellow.
**Longevity:** about 1-3 years; retreatment (touch-up) needed (Ritter et al, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Vital bleaching = in-office (30-35% H₂O₂), at-home nightguard (10-15% carbamide peroxide), OTC (strips 6-14% H₂O₂).
- 10% carbamide peroxide ≈ 3.5% H₂O₂ + 6.5% urea (Summitt; other books give 3-3.3%).
- Radiograph any single dark tooth before bleaching.
- No anaesthesia and no etching for in-office bleaching; shade judged after 1-2 weeks.
- Lights add no proven benefit beyond the peroxide.
- Delay composite bonding at least 1-2 weeks.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing that acid etching is part of in-office vital bleaching (it is unnecessary).
- Recording the final shade immediately after power bleaching (dehydration gives a false light shade).
- Bleaching a single dark tooth without a radiograph (masks necrosis or resorption).
- Giving one "correct" bonding delay; the books range from 24 hours (Sikri) to 2-3 weeks (Goldstein).
- Claiming lights or lasers do the bleaching; the peroxide does.

**"POT"** for the three vital methods: **P**ower (in-office), **O**vernight tray (nightguard), **T**ill-bought (over-the-counter).
