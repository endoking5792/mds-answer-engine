## 1. Basis of classification

### Explanation

Cohen's definition is broad — "any change in the hue, color, or translucency of a tooth due to any cause" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Most books classify by location of the stain: "Tooth discoloration can be classified as either extrinsic or intrinsic" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; "Extrinsic stains are located on the outer surfaces of teeth, whereas intrinsic stains are internal" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri adds a third: "The etiology of tooth discoloration is broadly divided into three categories, viz. intrinsic, extrinsic and internalized stains" (Sikri, 2020; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's keeps two classes but notes that "extrinsic stains can become intrinsic over time" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC] — the same idea that Sikri names "internalized". Classification matters because location predicts treatment: "In general, extrinsic stains respond well to scaling, polishing, or bleaching" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], whereas developmental intrinsic stains of AI and DI "are impossible to eliminate because they originate from the developmental defects of the enamel and dentin" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** any change in the hue, colour or translucency of a tooth from any cause (Setzer, 2021).

**Basis:** location of the stain. Grossman's and Sturdevant's use two classes, extrinsic and intrinsic (Gopikrishna, 2021; Ritter et al, 2019); Sikri adds internalized (Sikri, 2020). Location decides treatment: extrinsic stain polishes off, intrinsic needs bleaching or restoration (Setzer, 2021).

## 2. Extrinsic discoloration

### Explanation

"Extrinsic discolorations are found on the outer surface of teeth and are usually of local origin" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; "Extrinsic discoloration implies color changes on the external tooth surface" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; "The most common cause of extrinsic stains is poor oral hygiene" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Old scheme: "Direct or nonmetallic staining occurs when the stain from a chemical compound gets incorporated into the acquired pellicle"; "Indirect or metallic staining occurs by the means of metal salts and cationic antiseptics" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Grossman's objects that "The problem with this classification is that it does not explain the mechanism of staining" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Nathoo (1997), "The most common classification for tooth discoloration" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]: N1 "N1‐type colored material (chromogen) binds to the tooth surface"; N2 "The stains actually are N1‐type food stains that darken with time"; N3 "N3‐type stains are caused by carbohydrate‐ rich foods (e.g., apples, potatoes), stannous fluoride, and chlorhexidine" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. **Terminology conflict:** Grossman's box labels N2 "N2 type or direct dental stain" and N3 "N3 type or indirect dental stain" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], so N1 and N2 are direct, N3 indirect. Metallic examples: "Black staining seen in people using iron supplement and in iron foundry workers"; "Potassium permanganate produces violet to black color"; "Stannous fluoride causes golden-brown discoloration" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; green stain "in association with remnants of Nasmyth’s membrane" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Extrinsic** — on the outer surface, local origin, commonest cause poor oral hygiene (Gopikrishna, 2021; Goldstein et al, 2018a).

1. *By nature (older):* non-metallic or direct (chromogens in the pellicle: tea, coffee, wine, tobacco, chromogenic bacteria, chlorhexidine) and metallic or indirect (metal salts, cationic antiseptics: iron black, copper green, potassium permanganate violet-black, stannous fluoride golden brown, silver nitrate grey) (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Sikri, 2020). Criticised because it does not explain mechanism (Gopikrishna, 2021).
2. *Nathoo (1997)* (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Setzer, 2021):
   - **N1** (direct): coloured chromogen binds to the surface; colour same as the chromogen — tea, coffee, wine, metals.
   - **N2** (direct): N1 stain that darkens after binding.
   - **N3** (indirect): colourless prechromogen binds and reacts chemically — chlorhexidine, stannous fluoride, carbohydrate foods.
3. *By colour:* green (Nasmyth's membrane), orange, black line, brown-black (tobacco, betel).

## 3. Intrinsic discoloration

### Explanation

"Intrinsic discolorations are due to the presence of chromogenic material within the enamel or dentin, incorporated either during odontogenesis or after tooth eruption" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Goldstein's adds structure: "Intrinsic stains result from the change in structural composition or the thickness of hard dental tissues" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Sikri: "The factors causing intrinsic discoloration are classified as local and systemic factors"; "A few authors have categorized causes of intrinsic stains as pre-eruptive and post-eruptive" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Ingle's alternative: "patient- or dentist-related causes" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; the latter "are usually preventable" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Systemic examples: fluorosis "(more than 4.0 ppm)"; DI "brownish-violet to yellowish-brown color of the dentin with a translucent hue"; porphyria "red brown/purplish-red discoloration" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; bilirubin "creating yellow-green and blue-green discolorations" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Local: "Trauma leads to pulpal hemorrhage, causing grayish discoloration of the teeth" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; "Decomposition of pulp tissue is probably the most common cause of tooth discoloration" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**B. Intrinsic** — chromogen within enamel or dentin, or altered structure or thickness (Gopikrishna, 2021; Goldstein et al, 2018a).

1. *Systemic (pre-eruptive):* fluorosis, tetracycline, amelogenesis and dentinogenesis imperfecta, dentin dysplasia, hyperbilirubinaemia and erythroblastosis fetalis (green), congenital erythropoietic porphyria (red-brown), alkaptonuria, high fever (Sikri, 2020; Setzer, 2021).
2. *Local (post-eruptive):* pulp necrosis, intrapulpal haemorrhage, pulp remnants, calcific metamorphosis, resorption, ageing, caries, restorative and endodontic materials (Sikri, 2020; Gopikrishna, 2021).
3. *Ingle's grouping:* patient-related (necrosis, haemorrhage, calcification, age, developmental defects, medication) and dentist-related (pulp remnants, medicaments, obturation and restorative materials — preventable) (Rotstein, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 4. Internalized discoloration

### Explanation

"Internalized discoloration occurs due to incorporation of extrinsic stains within the tooth substance following some dental pathology. It occurs in enamel defects and in the porous surface of exposed dentin"; "These types of defects can also be seen in tooth wear, gingival recession and caries" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Cohen's makes the same point without the name: "Both intrinsic and extrinsic discolorations can exist in combination and may affect enamel, dentin, or pulp" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**C. Internalized** — extrinsic chromogens enter the tooth through:
- developmental enamel defects: fluorosis, hypoplasia, hypocalcification;
- acquired defects: tooth wear, gingival recession, caries, cracks (Sikri, 2020).

Extrinsic and intrinsic types often coexist (Setzer, 2021).

**Clinical significance:** extrinsic — prophylaxis; internalized — abrasion, bleaching or composite; intrinsic — bleaching, veneers or crowns (Setzer, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Three classes (Sikri): extrinsic, intrinsic, internalized.
- Nathoo: N1 chromogen, N2 chromogen that darkens, N3 prechromogen (chlorhexidine).
- Intrinsic: systemic/pre-eruptive versus local/post-eruptive; Ingle's patient- versus dentist-related.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Listing only extrinsic and intrinsic when the question asks for internalized.
- Calling chlorhexidine stain N1 (it is N3, a prechromogen).
- Listing fluorosis only as intrinsic without noting that its porous enamel takes up stain (internalized).
