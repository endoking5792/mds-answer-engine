## 1. Definition and agents

### Explanation

Bleaching "may be defined as the lightening of the color of the tooth through the application of a chemical agent to oxidize the organic pigmentation in the tooth" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Active peroxides "primarily derive from hydrogen peroxide and carbamide peroxide for external, vital bleaching and sodium peroxide for internal, nonvital bleaching" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC] (Setzer's "sodium peroxide" here means sodium perborate, the agent the rest of his chapter describes [Likely]).

- **Sodium perborate.** "It is available as a monohydrate, trihydrate, and tetrahydrate, with varying contents of oxygen"; "Acids, water, and warm air initiate its decomposition to sodium metaborate, hydrogen peroxide, and nascent oxygen"; "it is considered safer to work with than hydrogen peroxide and more popular for intracoronal bleaching" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. "When fresh, it contains about 95% perborate, corresponding to 9.9% of the available oxygen" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Mixed with water it releases peroxide slowly — peak "within 27 hours of mixing it with distilled deionized water, reaching a plateau after about 3 days" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. "Therefore in most cases, it should be the material of choice for internal bleaching" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].
- **Hydrogen peroxide.** Used "but 30% to 35% stabilized solutions (Superoxyl," Perhydrol) are commonest; they "may explode unless they are refrigerated and stored in a dark container" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; on soft tissue "it is caustic and may cause chemical burns by free oxygen radicals" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; it "is not recommended for routine intracoronal use in non-vital teeth" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC].
- **Carbamide peroxide.** "Ten percent carbamide peroxide breaks down into urea, ammonia, carbon dioxide, and approximately 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. "Carbamide peroxide can be used for internal bleaching"; "At 35%, it shows very low extraradicular diffusion rates compared to hydrogen peroxide and sodium perborate" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].
- **Others.** "a preparation of sodium peroxyborate monohydrate (Amosan), which releases more oxygen than does sodium perborate"; sodium hypochlorite "does not release enough oxidizer to be effective and is not commonly used for routine tooth bleaching" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri lists Superoxol, "Pyrozone (25% H2O2 and ether)" and "Sodium percarbonate" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Rule: "The least potent form is preferred" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Mechanism: oxidation breaks double bonds of chromophores; afterwards "a shade rebound effect may be observed, resulting from a reformation of double bonds of the molecules" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** lightening of a tooth by a chemical that oxidizes the organic pigments (Gopikrishna, 2021). Peroxides break the double bonds of large chromophores into smaller, lighter molecules; shade may rebound (Setzer, 2021).

**Agents**
| Agent | Key facts |
|---|---|
| Sodium perborate (mono-, tri-, tetrahydrate) | With water releases H2O2 slowly; 9.9% available oxygen; safest — agent of choice (Rotstein, 2019) |
| Hydrogen peroxide 30–35% (Superoxol) | Caustic, unstable; not for routine intracoronal use |
| Carbamide peroxide 10–35% | 10% gives about 3.5% H2O2 + urea; low extraradicular diffusion; usable internally (Setzer, 2021) |
| Others | Amosan, Pyrozone, sodium percarbonate (Sikri, 2019) |

## 2. Case selection

### Explanation

Indications: "discolorations of pulp chamber origin; (2) dentin discolorations; and (3) discolorations that are not amenable to external bleaching"; contraindications "superficial enamel discolorations; (2) defective enamel formation; (3) severe dentin loss; (4) presence of caries" and discoloured composites (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; "this modality is contraindicated in teeth, which are discolored due to corrosion products and/or large restorations" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Kwon and Goldstein add "discoloration by metallic salts, particularly silver amalgam" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC], and Endodontology agrees "this does not hold true for discolorations caused by metal ions (silver pins, amalgam)" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. "the ideal tooth for nonvital bleaching is a discolored unrestored crown" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]. Bleaching before ceramics: "Intracoronal bleaching, prior to placement of bonded porcelain crowns or veneers, is advantageous" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Case selection:** indicated for pulp-chamber/dentine stains (necrosis, haemorrhage, remnants) with a sound root filling; contraindicated with enamel defects, severe dentine loss, caries, large restorations and metallic (amalgam, silver) stains (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021; Sikri, 2019).

## 3. Classification and techniques

### Explanation

Grossman lists walking bleach and "In-office thermocatalytic bleach" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri: "a. Thermocatalytic technique b. Walking bleach technique c. Modified walking bleach technique d. Combination technique (inside/outside bleaching)" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Walking bleach.** "remains the most commonly used technique for internal bleaching of nonvital teeth"; perborate "is mixed with an inert liquid (distilled water, saline, or anesthetic solution) to achieve the consistency of wet sand"; peroxide, if needed, "should be used at 3% strength"; "The long-term results of a sodium perborate and water mix do not significantly differ from mixes with hydrogen peroxide"; barrier: "A protective layer of at least a 2-mm thickness should be placed over the root filling material" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Evidence on efficacy is mixed: one in-vitro series found perborate with 30% peroxide better ("93% versus 53% of the artificially stained and bleached teeth recovered their initial shade"), whereas "Other studies found no difference in the bleaching efficacy using sodium perborate mixed with 30% hydrogen peroxide" or with 3% or water (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; Summitt reports "there was no difference in the efficacy of sodium perborate mixed with water and sodium perborate mixed with either 5% or 30% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Details in B07.

**Thermocatalytic.** "the pulp chamber is filled with 30% to" 35% hydrogen peroxide and heated; "This procedure is repeated in three to four office visits"; "the thermocatalytic technique is nowadays disfavored due to the high risk of external root resorption" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman: cotton soaked in 30% H2O2, light/heat, "The tooth is subjected to several, usually 5- to 6-minute" exposures; it found heat and walking bleach "showed no significant differences in bleaching efficacy" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. **Heat — books differ.** "Therefore the application of heat during internal bleaching is contraindicated" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; the 2002 edition said only "Therefore, the application of heat during bleaching should be limited" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; Sikri still gives "The recommended temperature for nonvital teeth is 60 to 70°C and 46 to 60°C for vital teeth" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Kwon and Goldstein allow higher temperatures in pulpless teeth "While you should remain within the upper limits of the normal range of heat due to possible internal or external resorption" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt: "Nonvital teeth should not be heated to a temperature higher than that acceptable for a vital tooth" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. The newer endodontic texts reject heat; Sikri's figures are out of step with them [Likely].

**Ultraviolet photo-oxidation.** 30–35% H2O2 on a pellet "followed by a 2-minute exposure to ultraviolet light" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; "this technique is not recommended for internal bleaching of nonvital teeth" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].

**Modified walking bleach.** Aldecoa and Mayordomo: "a mixture of 10% carbamide peroxide and sodium perborate is placed in the pulp chamber for 4 to 6 weeks" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Inside/outside (internal–external).** Alani and Patel: "It has been described as the ‘inside-outside’ bleaching technique as it applies a two-fold application of the bleaching agent" into the chamber and via a tray (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; "This technique was described by Settembrini and Liebenberg48 in 1997" with 10% carbamide peroxide, but "There is also possibility of tongue irritation from the margins of the open access cavity" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt: open internal–external bleaching "is very effective and may reduce the treatment time by as much as 50%", and the "internal-external closed bleaching technique" seals the CP in the chamber and continues with a single-tooth tray (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. **Disagreement:** Dahl and Pallesen reject the open version — "By this method the pulp chamber is left unsealed during the weeks of treatment"; "The saved chair-side time using this bleaching method does not compensate for the adverse biological consequences" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. Sikri's "combination technique" is different again: "This technique involves the combined use of thermocatalytic in-office nonvital bleaching technique along with nightguard bleaching" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**In-office non-vital (no heat).** Sturdevant's describes a more current in-office technique with 30–35% hydrogen peroxide pastes or gels that need no heat (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; even so an RMGI seal over the root filling is still imperative (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Dahl and Pallesen keep it as a supplement: walking bleach "technique can be supplemented with an in office bleaching procedure", using "25–40% hydrogen peroxide or" 35–40% carbamide peroxide inside and outside; "Heating or light device is not recommended" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Techniques**
1. **Walking bleach (choice):** gutta-percha removed below the gingival margin; barrier at least 2 mm; perborate + water (wet sand) sealed under IRM/Cavit, changed every few days; 3% H2O2 only for resistant stains (Setzer, 2021). Results match peroxide mixes (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
2. **Thermocatalytic:** 30–35% H2O2 in the chamber activated by heat/light, three to four visits; now disfavoured because of cervical resorption (Setzer, 2021). Heat: contraindicated (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) versus 60–70°C still quoted by Sikri (2019) — books differ.
3. **UV photo-oxidation:** H2O2 + 2 minutes of UV light; not recommended (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021).
4. **Modified walking bleach:** 10% carbamide peroxide + perborate for 4–6 weeks (Sikri, 2019).
5. **Inside/outside:** 10% carbamide peroxide placed by the patient in the open chamber and in a tray (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); faster, but Dahl and Pallesen (2007) reject the unsealed chamber; a closed version seals the agent.
6. **In-office non-heat:** 25–40% H2O2 gel inside and outside as a supplement, no heat or light (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007).

## 4. Current concepts

### Explanation

Summitt: "Other safer options for walking bleach include the use of sodium perborate mixed with distilled water or anesthetic, or 10% carbamide peroxide sealed in the pulp chamber"; "one of the benefits of carbamide peroxide is the elevation of the pH, which may combat resorption"; "Do not use high concentrations of peroxide, high heat, or light activation" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. "The use of a mixture of sodium perborate and water reduces the risk of the occurrence of cervical resorption" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The barrier follows the attachment: "The internal level of barrier should be one millimeter incisal to the corresponding external probing of the epithelial attachment" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], though Torabinejad's chapter says with perborate and water "a protective cement barrier and gingival protection are unnecessary" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Bleaching can start "at the same appointment as the obturation" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; definitive bonded restoration "about 1 to 3 weeks after the last appointment" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Prognosis: "More than 90% immediate success has been reported for intracoronal bleaching of non-vital teeth" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; trauma/necrosis stains "have a good prognosis of around 95%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; but "Within 1 to 5 years, only 35% to 50% of the teeth maintain their esthetically pleasing appearance" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC], and the thermocatalytic series fell to "45% success rate after 6 years, confirming the redarkening of bleached teeth in many instances" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Current concepts:** least potent agent — perborate + water or 10% carbamide peroxide; no 30% H2O2, no heat, no light, no routine acid etch; barrier over gutta-percha, 1 mm incisal to the probed attachment (Sikri, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); rubber dam; bonded composite over a light base after 1–3 weeks (Setzer, 2021); periodic radiographs. **Prognosis:** over 90% immediate success, but only 35–50% stable at 1–5 years; rebleaching usually works (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Sodium perborate = agent of choice for intracoronal bleaching; 30–35% H2O2 is caustic and resorption-linked.
- Walking bleach > thermocatalytic: same results, safer, less chair time.
- Barrier over gutta-percha shaped to the epithelial attachment.
- Inside/outside = 10% CP in chamber + tray; open chamber is controversial.
- Over 90% immediate success; relapse common, rebleach.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling sodium hypochlorite a bleaching agent for teeth (it does not release enough oxidizer).
- Recommending heat activation as current practice.
- Omitting the barrier and the radiographic check of the root filling.
- Confusing Sikri's "combination" (thermocatalytic + nightguard) with Settembrini–Liebenberg inside/outside (10% CP inside and in a tray).
