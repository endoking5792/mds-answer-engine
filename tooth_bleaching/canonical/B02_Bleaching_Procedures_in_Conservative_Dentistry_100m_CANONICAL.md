## 1. Definition and history

### Explanation

Sturdevant's defines bleaching as "The lightening of the colour of a tooth through the application of a chemical agent to oxidize the organic pigmentation in the tooth is referred to as bleaching" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's uses the same wording (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's puts it optically: "Bleaching is a treatment modality involving an oxidative chemical that alters the light-absorbing" and/or light-reflecting nature of the structure (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's: "Bleaching implies degradation of high molecular weight, complex organic molecules that reflect a specific wavelength of light" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's notes that whitening is often used for surface stain removal and true bleaching for change of the inherent colour (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

History. By the mid-1800s "the in-office bleaching agent of choice for nonvital teeth was chloride of lime"; oxalic acid, Pyrozone (ether peroxide), hydrogen dioxide, sodium peroxide and even cyanide of potassium were tried; by 1910 an in-office technique "where high concentrations of hydrogen peroxide are used on well-isolated teeth, occasionally assisted by heat or light, was well established"; in the late 1960s the tray method "employing a custom-fit tray containing 10% carbamide peroxide solution, was first used by Klusmier", but "In 1989, Haywood and Heymann wrote the first article on an at-home bleaching technique" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's gives the dates: "Bleaching of nonvital teeth was first reported in 1848; in-office bleaching of vital teeth was first reported in 1868", "a 3% ether and peroxide mouthwash used for bleaching in 1893", and home bleaching "began around 1968" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha: "Whitening teeth outside the dental office was introduced in 1989" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC] — this is the publication date, not the 1968 first use; the two are not in conflict once that is seen [Likely]. The term walking bleach "was first coined by Nutting and" Poe (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; cervical resorption after intracoronal bleaching "was first reported by Harrington and Natkin in 1979" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Andreasen's dates differ: "The first publication on bleaching of discolored, pulpless teeth appeared in 1864" (against Sturdevant's 1848), walking bleach was "introduced in 1961", and tray bleaching grew from an orthodontist's observation "made in the late 1960s" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; the 1848/1864 gap probably reflects which first report each book counts [Guessing]. Summitt's list of "Other agents used in the 1800s for nonvital tooth bleaching included aluminum chloride" and sodium hypophosphate (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt's sums up: "The effective ones all release oxidizing agents, whether supplied as hydrogen peroxide, carbamide peroxide, sodium percarbonate" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Discoloured teeth are a common esthetic complaint. In keeping with minimal intervention, bleaching is the first, most conservative option before veneers or crowns.

**Definition.** Bleaching is the lightening of the colour of a tooth through the application of a chemical agent that oxidizes the organic pigmentation in the tooth (Ritter et al, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021). It degrades large, light-absorbing organic molecules into smaller ones that reflect less of the stain colour (Sikri, 2020). "Whitening" strictly means surface stain removal; "bleaching" changes the inherent colour (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**History (milestones)**
- 1848: first report of non-vital bleaching (1864 in Dahl and Pallesen, 2007); 1868: in-office vital bleaching (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Mid-1800s: chloride of lime, oxalic acid, Pyrozone (ether peroxide), hydrogen dioxide; agents chosen by stain (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- 1893: 3% ether-peroxide mouthwash (Ritter et al, 2019); by 1910 in-office high-concentration hydrogen peroxide with heat or light on isolated teeth.
- Nutting and Poe coined walking bleach (sodium perborate sealed in the chamber) (Rotstein, 2019).
- Late 1960s: Klusmier — 10% carbamide peroxide in a tray; 1989: Haywood and Heymann publish nightguard vital bleaching (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- 1979: Harrington and Natkin report cervical resorption after intracoronal bleaching (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
- Since home bleaching (after 1989): resurgence of in-office power bleaching with lights and lasers, and over-the-counter (OTC) strips and paint-on products (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 2. Bleaching agents

### Explanation

Cohen's: the active peroxides "primarily derive from hydrogen peroxide and carbamide peroxide for external, vital bleaching and sodium peroxide for internal, nonvital bleaching" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. The internal agent named in Cohen's is almost certainly a slip for sodium perborate: the same chapter's next paragraphs and its walking-bleach protocol use sodium perborate throughout, and Ingle's and Torabinejad's (Rotstein's chapters) name sodium perborate as the intracoronal agent [Certain].

Hydrogen peroxide. Concentration range by book: Grossman's — "ranges in concentration between 5 and 35%", and "H2O2 concentrations ranging from 3 to 7.5% are used for home bleach" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's — "Typically, at-home concentrations range from 3% to 7.5%" (some up to 14%), and "In-office concentrations may range from 25% to 38%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Summitt's — in-office usually 35% "although concentrations range from 15% to 50%" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Superoxol is the 30-35% stabilized solution; Ingle's: it "is not recommended for routine intracoronal use in non-vital teeth" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Storage: refrigerated, it keeps potency "where it retains sufficient potency for approximately 3–4 months"; carbamide peroxide "has a shelf life of 1–2 years, while hydrogen peroxide has a shelf life of few weeks" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] — Grossman's gives both figures for hydrogen peroxide (months refrigerated, weeks otherwise), which probably refer to sealed refrigerated stock versus opened containers [Guessing].

Carbamide peroxide (urea hydrogen peroxide, CH6N2O3): white crystals "that contain H2O2 in a concentration of about 35%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's: "containing approximately 35% H2O2" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Concentrations by book: Cohen's 10% up to 30% "(equivalent to 3.5% to 8.6% hydrogen peroxide)" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's the same 10-30% (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Torabinejad's "varying between 3% and 15%" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's 3 to 45% across home and office (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri's "10–35%" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Breakdown of 10% carbamide peroxide — nine books, five figures (the same set is shown in B02, B04, B05, B10 and B11): Summitt's "6.5% urea and 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Torabinejad's "approximately 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" with urea, ammonia and carbon dioxide (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's 10-30% "(equivalent to 3.5% to 8.6% hydrogen peroxide)", although its home-bleaching section gives 10-22% "with an effective yield of 4% to 7.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's "approximately 3.4% hydrogen peroxide and 6.5% urea", although its reaction scheme a page earlier writes "+ 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson's "release 3.3% hydrogen peroxide", the one-third rule (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's "3% hydrogen peroxide (active ingredient) and 7% urea" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri's "10% carbamide peroxide is equal to 3.0% hydrogen peroxide" in both his books (Sikri, 2020; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Alani and Patel's "approximately 3% hydrogen peroxide over a period of 4 h" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Reasoning: the crystals "contain H2O2 in a concentration of about 35%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], so 10% gives about 3.5%; 3.3% and 3% are rounded one-third figures [Likely]. At higher strengths the books fall below that proportion: Cohen's 30% = 8.6% (proportionally 10.5%) and Summitt's "A 35% carbamide peroxide product is approximately equivalent to a 9.5% hydrogen peroxide product" (proportionally about 12%) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; neither book explains the shortfall [Likely; the arithmetic is the auditor's]. Summitt's explains "This 3:1 reduction of carbamide peroxide to the active hydrogen peroxide ion explains why" carbamide products are dosed at three times hydrogen peroxide products (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Carbopol: "The most significant additive to carbamide peroxide is carbopol" — "This ingredient thickens the material and prolongs the release of peroxide over time" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri's gives it "in a concentration of 0.5 to 1.5%, is commonly used thickening agent" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Ingle's adds that it "improves shelf life" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC].

Sodium perborate: "The perborate ion comprises 95% of the molecule and provides close to 10% of the available oxygen"; "Acids, water, and warm air initiate its decomposition to sodium metaborate, hydrogen peroxide, and nascent oxygen"; peak hydrogen peroxide release is reached "within 27 hours of mixing it with distilled deionized water" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson write instead that it "reacts with water to form sodium borate and hydrogen peroxide", and "The amount of hydrogen peroxide produced using sodium perborate is less than that from a similar amount of carbamide peroxide" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Grossman's text says metaborate, water and oxygen while its equation on the same page shows metaborate, hydrogen peroxide and oxygen (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] — a small internal inconsistency; perborate in water does yield hydrogen peroxide [Likely]. "There are three forms of sodium perborate which vary in their oxygen content" — mono-, tri- and tetrahydrate (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's: "it should be the material of choice in most intracoronal bleaching procedures" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Sturdevant's: "Although sodium perborate may bleach more slowly, it is safer and less aggressive to the tooth" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Others (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]: 0.5% chlorine dioxide, 18-36% hydrochloric acid (microabrasion), McInnes solution (36% HCl + 30% H2O2 + ether) and its modification with 20% NaOH, N-doped titanium dioxide with 3-10% hydrogen peroxide; inactive ingredients — carbopol, glycerine or propylene glycol, surfactants, sodium benzoate, flavours, potassium nitrate, fluoride and CPP-ACP.

#### ✎ Exam core

All effective agents release hydrogen peroxide or oxygen radicals (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**1. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)**
- Clear, colourless; unstable, kept refrigerated in amber bottles; decomposes with heat, light or organic debris (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- 30-35% (Superoxol) for in-office use; home 3-7.5% (Gopikrishna, 2021; Setzer, 2021). In-office range 25-38% in Cohen's, 15-50% in Summitt's.
- Caustic: burns soft tissue; not for routine intracoronal use (Rotstein, 2019).

**2. Carbamide peroxide (urea hydrogen peroxide, CH6N2O3)**
- White crystals containing about 35% H2O2 (Setzer, 2021).
- 10% (most popular), 15-22% home; 35-45% in-office.
- Breaks down to roughly one-third H2O2 plus urea. 10% CP ≈ 3.5% H2O2 + 6.5% urea (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 3.4% (Gopikrishna, 2021); 3% + 7% urea (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Additives: carbopol 0.5-1.5% (thickens, slows release, longer shelf life), glycerine/propylene glycol, flavours, potassium nitrate, fluoride (Sikri, 2020).

**3. Sodium perborate (NaBO3)**
- Stable white powder; mono-, tri- and tetrahydrate differing in oxygen content (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- About 95% perborate, close to 10% available oxygen; with water, acid or warmth it decomposes to sodium metaborate, H2O2 and nascent oxygen (Setzer, 2021).
- Alkaline, slow, safe: agent of choice for intracoronal bleaching (Rotstein, 2019).

**4. Others:** chlorine dioxide, McInnes and modified McInnes solutions, hydrochloric acid (microabrasion), nitrogen-doped titanium dioxide with low-concentration H2O2 (Sikri, 2020).

|---|---|---|

## 3. Mechanism of bleaching

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: the mechanism "is considered to be oxidation of organic pigments, although the chemistry is not well understood" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's: "Contemporary bleaching techniques rely on oxidative processes that actively break down large pigmenting molecules"; double bonds of high-molecular-weight pigments "are broken down by the chemical reactions with the free radicals", changing the wavelength of reflected light (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. "Hydrogen peroxide diffuses through the organic matrix of the enamel and dentin because of its low molecular weight" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Grossman's: "H2O2 has a low molecular weight and hence can penetrate dentin and release oxygen that breaks down the double bond" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's: "Ionization of H2O2 produces different types of free radicals" — hydroxyl and perhydroxyl ions (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Luk and Anagnostaki: "the unstable oxygen or perhydroxyl radicals are diffusing through the organic matrix of the enamel and dentine" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC].

Chromophore breakdown: "Bleaching agent opens the more highly pigmented carbon ring (yellow color) and converts it to carbon chains"; "When the carbon double bonds are converted to hydroxyl groups, the amount of light absorbed is reduced" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's places carbon dioxide and water at the end, beyond whitening: "Further degradation over this step may complete disintegration of the organic matrix with the release of carbon dioxide and water" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], so CO2 and water mark over-bleaching rather than normal whitening [Likely]. Andreasen's: the reactive molecules "attack the long chained, dark colored chromophore molecules and split them into smaller, less colored and more diffusible molecules", and "in a basic solution, lower activation energy is required for the formation of free radicals from hydrogen peroxide" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. Grossman's redox diagram labels the "Tooth (reducing agent takes up electrons)" and the "Bleaching agent (oxidizing agent gives free electrons)" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. A wording slip in two books: Bonsor and Pearson say "The smaller molecules so produced reflect less light" and Alani and Patel that they "are less able to reflect light than the previous large chromophore molecules" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; the smaller molecules absorb less light, as Luk and Anagnostaki put it, "which absorb less and therefore reflect more light" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC] — "reflect less" is backwards unless read as reflecting less of the stain colour [Likely]. Saturation point: "Saturation point is that point at which only hydrophilic colorless tooth structure exists"; "Bleaching beyond this point results in the breakdown of the organic matrix resulting in weakening of the enamel and surface porosities" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Luk and Anagnostaki likewise — "At this point, bleaching should be stopped immediately, or the next step would be a rapid loss of enamel" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Rebound: "a shade rebound effect may be observed, resulting from a reformation of double bonds of the molecules" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

The theory is not settled. Luk and Anagnostaki write that the stain-breakdown "hypothesis might be weak since" chromophore concentrations in enamel are below detection limits and enamel becomes more opaque after bleaching; they favour the view that "the peroxide whitens teeth by oxidizing its transparent organic matrix into an opaque whiter material" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Ingle's lists extra contributors — "other possible mechanisms include cleansing of tooth surface, temporary dehydration of enamel during the bleaching process" — and compares it to textile and paper bleaching (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's admits "it is not possible to state scientifically what exactly the bleaching process is changing within the tooth" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. The textbook chromophore model is therefore a working hypothesis, not established chemistry [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Mechanism: oxidation (redox reaction)**
1. H2O2 (released directly or from carbamide peroxide or sodium perborate) has a low molecular weight and diffuses through the organic matrix of enamel and dentin (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
2. It dissociates into free radicals — nascent oxygen, hydroxyl and perhydroxyl (HO2•) radicals (Sikri, 2020; Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023). The bleaching agent is the oxidizing agent; the stain is the reducing agent (Gopikrishna, 2021).
3. The radicals attack the unsaturated (double) bonds of chromophores. Pigmented carbon rings open into chains; double bonds become hydroxyl groups; large molecules become smaller, simpler ones that absorb less light (Gopikrishna, 2021; Setzer, 2021).
4. Further oxidation, beyond whitening, ends in CO2 and water (Setzer, 2021).

**Saturation point.** The point at which only hydrophilic, colourless structures remain. Bleaching beyond it breaks down the organic matrix of enamel: porosity, weakening, sensitivity, with little further lightening — so bleaching must stop there (Gopikrishna, 2021; Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023).

**Rebound.** Re-formation of double bonds and rehydration give some darkening after bleaching (Setzer, 2021).

**Limitation.** The exact chemistry is unknown (Ritter et al, 2019); an alternative theory holds that peroxide oxidizes the transparent enamel matrix into a more opaque, whiter material (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023). Dehydration and surface cleansing also contribute (Rotstein, 2019).

## 4. Factors affecting bleaching

### Explanation

Concentration: faster lightening up to a point, but "the effect is not linear (ie, 20% is not twice as fast as 10%)"; beyond the tooth's maximum rate, extra concentration adds only sensitivity (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Cohen's cites in vitro work in which "hydrogen peroxide gel concentrations above the 15% level did not increase bleaching effectiveness" but affected enamel hardness (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri's states it as established (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] — the evidence is an in vitro study, so "established" overstates it [Likely]. Temperature: "An increase of 10°C doubles the rate of chemical reaction" but not necessarily the rate of colour change (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Grossman's the same (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

pH — the books disagree. Grossman's: "The optimum pH for hydrogen peroxide ranges from 9.5 to 10.8" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's: hydrogen peroxide "is more effective as a bleaching agent at pH values that are close to the dissociation constant" and "Alkaline bleaching solutions may cause less demineralization damage than acidic solutions" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Yet commercial products are acidic: Summitt's — safety data sheets "list hydrogen peroxide products with a pH around 5" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha — home products "ranges from 4.6 to 6.7 when undiluted" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]; Torabinejad's — 10% carbamide preparations "have an average pH of 5 to 6.5" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; whereas Summitt's says ADA-accepted carbamide products are about pH 7 and "Urea is primarily responsible for raising the pH in the oral cavity above 8 for a number of hours" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. So: alkaline pH favours radical formation; products are stabilized acidic; carbamide's urea raises pH during use [Likely].

Time: longer contact gives more lightening up to the plateau but more sensitivity (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Others: surface cleanliness, sealed environment in non-vital teeth, additives, age, initial colour (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Concentration:** higher is faster, but not linear (20% is not twice 10%); in vitro, H2O2 above 15% did not add effectiveness, only enamel damage and sensitivity (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Setzer, 2021).
- **Temperature:** each 10°C rise doubles the reaction rate; excess heat causes sensitivity and irreversible pulpitis, so no local anaesthesia (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **pH:** H2O2 forms radicals best in alkaline pH (optimum 9.5-10.8, Gopikrishna, 2021); commercial H2O2 products are acidic (pH about 5); urea in carbamide peroxide raises oral pH above 8 (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- **Time:** more contact time, more effect, more sensitivity.
- **Surface cleanliness:** prophylaxis first.
- **Sealed environment** (non-vital) keeps the agent active.
- **Additives:** carbopol prolongs release; thickeners may reduce efficacy.
- **Type of stain:** yellow-brown, ageing, smoking stains respond fastest; tetracycline slowest (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 5. Classification of bleaching procedures

### Explanation

Grossman's classifies into intracoronal (walking bleach; "In-office thermocatalytic bleach") and extracoronal (in-office vital; at-home vital) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Summitt's: "The three types of bleaching therapy are in-office bleaching, at-home bleaching, and over-the-counter products" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri's gives the longest list, including microabrasion-assisted bleaching, "Combination bleaching technique (inside/outside bleaching)", laser and non-thermal plasma bleaching (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**I. Vital (extracoronal) bleaching**
- A. In-office (professional): thermocatalytic (heat/light), power bleaching (high-intensity light, laser), non-thermocatalytic (chemically activated), microabrasion-assisted.
- B. Dentist-prescribed home bleaching (nightguard vital bleaching).
- C. Over-the-counter (consumer-applied): strips, paint-on gels, trays, toothpastes.
- D. Combination (in-office plus home).

**II. Non-vital (intracoronal) bleaching**
- A. Walking bleach (and modified walking bleach with carbamide peroxide).
- B. Thermocatalytic.
- C. Ultraviolet photo-oxidation.
- D. Inside-outside (open) and internal-external closed techniques.

(Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2020; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013)

## 6. Indications, contraindications and case selection

### Explanation

"The primary indication for bleaching is patient dissatisfaction with tooth color"; bleaching can replace or precede veneers, lighten teeth to match lighter crowns, and the endpoint is that "Patients tend to look most natural when the color of the teeth matches the white of the sclera in their eyes" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Response by stain: "teeth discolored by age, genetics, smoking, or coffee are the fastest to respond"; "In general, tetracycline-stained teeth are the slowest to respond to bleaching"; "White spots are not removed by bleaching but may be less noticeable" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's: "Teeth exhibiting yellow or orange intrinsic discolouration seem to respond best to vital bleaching" while blue-grey responds poorly (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Prognosis is guarded for the gingival third: "Teeth heavily stained in the gingival third have the poorest prognosis for complete lightening" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Contraindications differ in strictness. Summitt's Box 16-1: unrealistic expectations, non-compliance, extensive tooth-coloured restorations, intolerance, pregnancy or lactation; "In-office bleaching is not recommended for children with large pulps or teeth with cracks"; pregnant and lactating women should avoid it "because of the increased potential for gingival irritation", not because of proven fetal harm; and "it may be prudent to have the patient forego tobacco use during the period of the bleaching process" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Grossman's in-office list adds hypersensitive teeth, carious teeth, exposed roots and "Children with large pulp chamber" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's: "Patients with suspected or confirmed bulimia should not receive bleaching procedures", and "no bleaching procedures should take place 2 to 3 weeks prior to any adhesive placement of brackets" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Age conflict: Sikri's says "Tooth bleaching in younger individuals (less than 18 years) should be avoided" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], while Summitt's places the age range from 10 years and finds in at-home bleaching "there were no predictors of which individuals would experience sensitivity other than a history of sensitive teeth and more than one bleach application per day" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. The difference is technique: high-concentration in-office bleaching in large-pulped teeth versus supervised 10% tray bleaching [Likely].

Sequencing: "bleaching should be performed before any esthetic restorative procedures", because existing restorations will not change shade (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Case selection: examination with radiographs — "A radiograph should always be taken of a single dark tooth" — because bleaching can mask necrosis or resorption; shade and photographs recorded; "the best way to ensure patient awareness of the effect of bleaching is to bleach one arch at a time" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Restorations will not bleach and need replacing after the shade stabilizes; only sensitive or deep caries is restored first, since "There is some evidence to suggest that the use of carbamide peroxide may retard the progression of caries" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications**
- Patient dissatisfied with tooth colour; generalized yellow, ageing, smoking and coffee stains (respond fastest) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Mild fluorosis (brown component), mild-moderate tetracycline stain (slow).
- Trauma or pulp-necrosis discoloration (non-vital bleaching); calcific metamorphosis (external tray bleaching).
- Before veneers or crowns (lighter base, less opaquer); to match an existing lighter crown.
- Retreatment of colour relapse (Sikri, 2020).

**Contraindications**
- Unrealistic expectations, poor compliance (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Pregnancy and lactation; bulimia (Setzer, 2021); allergy to the agent.
- Hypersensitive teeth, exposed roots, cracks, severe enamel loss, caries, defective restorations (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Children with large pulps for in-office bleaching; Sikri advises avoiding bleaching below 18 years.
- Extensive tooth-coloured restorations that the patient will not replace.
- Within 2-3 weeks before bracket bonding (Setzer, 2021).
- Stains that do not bleach: amalgam/metallic, deep tetracycline, white spots, amelogenesis/dentinogenesis imperfecta.

**Guarded prognosis:** dark gingival third, translucent incisal edges, blue-grey tetracycline stain, white spots, TMD or bruxism.

**Case selection protocol**
1. History: sensitivity, medical, tobacco, expectations.
2. Examination and radiographs — always for a single dark tooth (necrosis, resorption) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
3. Diagnose the cause and depth of discoloration; prophylaxis.
4. Record shade (tab, compare with sclera) and photographs.
5. Informed consent: duration, sensitivity, relapse, restorations will not change colour.
6. Bleach one arch at a time (maxillary first).
7. Treatment sequence: bleach first, wait for the shade to stabilize, then replace or place esthetic restorations, because restorations do not lighten (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 7. Vital bleaching: in-office (power) bleaching

### Explanation

"In-office bleaching of vital teeth generally uses a 35% hydrogen peroxide solution placed directly on the teeth" with light or heat optional (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's: most gels "most commonly contain 30–35% hydrogen peroxide"; heavy rubber dam ligated with floss or a paint-on resin dam; and "Etching of teeth with 37% phosphoric acid, once considered a required part of this technique, is unnecessary" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. "The teeth should not be anesthetized for in-office bleaching" so that the patient signals discomfort (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Heat — books differ: Grossman's "The temperature should be maintained between 125 and 140°F (52–60°C)" and "The treatment time should not exceed 30 minutes even if the result is not satisfactory" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri's "The recommended temperature is 46°C to 60°C" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Visits — books differ: Sturdevant's "Bleaching treatments generally are rendered weekly for two to six treatments, with each treatment lasting 30–45 min" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's "Authors suggest from one to six in-office treatments, with the average being about three visits" and "Appointments for subsequent treatment are scheduled 1 week apart to allow sensitivity to abate" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri's "Bleaching appointments are scheduled 2–4 weeks apart" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's indications for the in-office route include "Patient is unwilling or unable to use at-home tray application" and "Patient desires the process to be completed as quickly as possible due to an upcoming event" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Assessment is delayed because "a portion of the whitening effect is temporary, resulting from dehydration of teeth from the isolation technique" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Cohen's protocol: eye protection; "vitamin E capsules, a powerful antioxidant, should be ready at hand" for burns; gel 2-3 mm thick; neutral sodium fluoride afterwards; patients told to "refrain from tobacco, coffee, tea, cola, and wine for a period of 2 weeks" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Power bleaching "refers to accelerated vital inoffice tooth whitening procedures that employ xenon plasma arc-curing lights or lasers" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications for the in-office route:** patient unwilling or unable to wear trays, wants a quick result before an event, needs a motivating start, or has a single discoloured tooth; patient must accept the cost of several visits (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Agent:** 30-35% H2O2 (liquid or gel) or 35-38% carbamide peroxide (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).

**Procedure**
1. Prophylaxis with pumice; record shade and photographs.
2. No local anaesthesia — the patient's sensation is the safety monitor (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
3. Protect lips and gingiva with petroleum jelly/Orabase; isolate with heavy rubber dam, inverted and ligated, or a light-cured resin (liquid) dam; protective eyewear for patient and operator.
4. Acid etching is not needed (Ritter et al, 2019).
5. Apply the gel to the labial surfaces (2-3 mm thick). Activate chemically, or with heat (52-60°C, Gopikrishna, 2021; 46-60°C, Sikri, 2020) or a light; renew every few minutes.
6. Total time about 30-45 min per visit, never beyond 30 min of heat (Gopikrishna, 2021).
7. Remove gel with wet gauze and suction, rinse, remove dam, polish, apply neutral sodium fluoride.
8. Post-op: analgesic for sensitivity; avoid coloured foods, tea, coffee and tobacco for 2 weeks (Setzer, 2021).
9. Repeat at intervals of about 1 week: 2-6 visits, average 3 (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Evaluate the shade after 1-2 weeks, once dehydration has reversed.

**Emergency:** if the patient feels gingival tingling, stop, rinse with water or sodium bicarbonate; vitamin E oil to burns (Setzer, 2021).

**Advantages:** dentist control, soft tissue protected, rapid result, no compliance needed.
**Disadvantages:** cost, chair time, unpredictable outcome, more sensitivity, risk of burns, false lightening from dehydration (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 8. Vital bleaching: dentist-supervised nightguard (at-home) bleaching

### Explanation

Agent and concentration — books vary: Sturdevant's "A 10%–15% carbamide peroxide bleaching material generally is recommended" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's "with a 10% solution of carbamide peroxide (approximately equal to a 3.5% solution of hydrogen peroxide)" and "the best combination of safety, limited side effects, and speed of action is obtained with a 10% solution" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein's "Most matrix bleaching uses 10–15% carbamide peroxide" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Cohen's "10% to 22% carbamide peroxide with an effective yield of 4% to 7.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha "10% to 22% carbamide peroxide or 1.5% to 6% hydrogen peroxide" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Daytime hydrogen peroxide tray gels "range in concentration from 7.5% to 9.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Activity time — books disagree (the same set is shown in B02, B05 and B11): Summitt's "Carbamide peroxide is active for up to 10 hours, with about 50% of the active agent being used up in the first 2 hours", although "the agent has lost most of its potency after 5 hours" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson: "active even after 10 hours of use" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Grossman's: it "can be active up to several hours" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Alani and Patel: 3% hydrogen peroxide released "over a period of 4 h" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; against Cohen's "whereas carbamide peroxide remains active for 40 to 90 minutes" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha's home regimen, in which "The gel is replenished each hour" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], fits the shorter figure. Summitt's Table 16-2 gives "2 to 10 hours due to carbopol amount" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC], so carbopol content probably explains the gap and Cohen's figure may describe unthickened gels [Likely]; Cohen's also notes that the reaction "takes place at a slower rate compared to hydrogen peroxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

Wear time — books vary: overnight 1-2 weeks (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's "The tray should be worn for a time period of 4 hours for every session" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri's day regimen changing gel "every 1.5–2 hours" and "The daytime regimen requires one to three weeks" against 4-6 weeks at night (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Tray: vacuum-formed soft vinyl 0.75-1.5 mm; Sturdevant's prefers scalloped margins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], whereas Summitt's says "Scalloping is not necessary when low concentrations of carbamide peroxide (10%) are used" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Reservoirs 0.5-1 mm ending 1 mm short of the gingiva are optional; studies show no efficacy gain (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Tetracycline: "some tetracycline-stained teeth may require 2 to 12 months of daily treatment" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Agent:** 10% carbamide peroxide (about 3.5% H2O2) — best balance of safety and speed; 15-22% for faster results; 7.5-9.5% H2O2 for daytime wear (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Setzer, 2021). Active up to 10 h (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); Cohen's: 40-90 min (Setzer, 2021).

**Procedure**
1. *Visit 1:* examination, radiographs, shade, photographs; alginate impression of one arch (maxillary first); cast trimmed thin, without palate or vestibule.
2. *Tray:* vacuum-formed thin soft vinyl (ethyl vinyl acetate) sheet; optional 0.5-1 mm facial reservoirs ending 1 mm short of the gingiva; margins scalloped (Ritter et al, 2019) or non-scalloped for 10% gels (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
3. *Visit 2:* check fit, margins and occlusion; demonstrate loading — a thin bead on the facial aspect of each tooth; wipe excess; remove the tray by peeling from the molar region.
4. *Regimen:* overnight (single application) or day wear 2-4 h (Gopikrishna, 2021: 4 h per session); avoid drinking or rinsing while wearing.
5. *Duration:* normal teeth 1-2 weeks to 6 weeks; nicotine 1-3 months; tetracycline 2-12 months (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
6. *Recall* at 1-2 weeks: progress, sensitivity, tray adjustment.

**Advantages:** low concentration, safe, easy, cheap, little chair time, both arches possible, good long-term results.
**Disadvantages:** needs compliance, longer time, gingival irritation from ill-fitting trays, TMJ strain (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 9. Over-the-counter and combination bleaching

### Explanation

"The fundamental problem with OTC products is that there is no examination and diagnosis of the cause of discoloration" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Strips contain "a relatively high concentration (approximately 6% to 14%) of hydrogen peroxide", worn 30 minutes twice daily; a comparison found a six-shade change "required 7 nights of tray bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide, 16 days of a 6% hydrogen peroxide strip (OTC) worn twice a day, or 3 in-office treatments of 38% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Grossman's: OTC products "primarily work by removing extrinsic surface stain only" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] — Summitt's says recent strips do bleach, so Grossman's applies to toothpastes and gums rather than peroxide strips [Likely]. Toothpastes — the materials books disagree: Bonsor and Pearson, whitening toothpastes "cannot affect the colour of the tooth as this is determined by the underlying dentine" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Craig's 11th ed, "Bleaching toothpastes based on low-level peroxide formulations are also available to whiten teeth for esthetic purposes. They are effective in whitening teeth when used daily" (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Phillips', they "may contain an abrasive agent alone or both a chemical agent and an abrasive agent", the latter acting "through a combined mechanism of abrasion and bleaching", though "The therapeutic benefits of other additives such as peroxides and bicarbonates are under investigation" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Abrasive-only pastes remove stain; peroxide pastes may bleach slightly, with uncertain evidence [Likely]. A European review recommended "The maximum recommended concentration for products is 6% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Combination — a conflict: Sturdevant's and Grossman's say a combination of in-office and home bleaching "has better results than either technique used alone" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's reports that "One study found that in-office bleaching combined with at-home bleaching does not improve tooth whitening compared with at-home bleaching alone" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Luk and Anagnostaki cite Auschill: "7 days of home bleaching result to a similar effect of one session of in-office bleaching" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Combination gives a faster start; the end result appears similar [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Over-the-counter products:** strips (6-14% H2O2, 30 min twice daily), paint-on gels, pre-filled trays, whitening pens, toothpastes and gums (abrasives remove surface stain; low-peroxide pastes may whiten slightly, Craig and Powers, 2002) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Drawbacks: no diagnosis (necrosis, caries, resorption masked), ill-fitting trays, overuse with enamel erosion, strips miss posterior teeth, unknown composition.
- Efficacy: one six-shade change needed 7 nights of 10% carbamide peroxide tray, 16 days of 6% H2O2 strips or 3 in-office visits of 38% H2O2 (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- European limit: 6% H2O2 for consumer products.

**Combination (in-office jump-start + home maintenance):** an in-office session followed by nightguard bleaching. Sturdevant's and Grossman's report better results than either alone; Summitt's cites a study showing no added whitening over home bleaching alone — the advantage is speed and motivation, not a whiter end point.

## 9A. Bleaching of tetracycline-stained teeth

### Explanation

Children are at risk "from the time they are in utero until the age of" 8 (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's grades the stain (Jordan and Boksman, 1984): first degree, "Light yellow to light gray staining without banding"; second degree, darker yellow or grey without banding; third degree, "Severe staining characterized by dark gray or blue discoloration with banding" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: "Teeth stained with tetracycline require prolonged treatment durations of several months before any results are observed" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's: "some tetracycline-stained teeth may require 2 to 12 months of daily treatment", teeth "may retain some grayish-blue tint", "8 in 10 patients can expect to retain that lightening for at least 1 year", and "A follow-up study showed a very high patient satisfaction rate at 7.5 years after completion of bleaching"; a cheaper option "is to bleach tetracycline-stained teeth using 6.5% hydrogen peroxide strips"; and "Generally, teeth severely stained in gingival areas are candidates for porcelain veneers or crowns", though bleaching first is advised (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Intentional devitalization — a conflict: Sturdevant's reports that some clinicians advocate "intentional endodontic therapy along with the use of an intra-coronal nonvital bleaching technique" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and Sikri's cites Aldecoa and Mayordomo's carbamide peroxide-sodium perborate mix that "perborate is placed in the pulp chamber for 4 to 6 weeks" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Grossman's rejects it — veneers or crowns "are indicated instead of intentional devitalization of a tooth with a normal pulp" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's adds photochemical KTP bleaching at the "wavelength of KTP laser (532 nm)" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Tetracycline stain:** deposited in dentin during calcification (in utero to 8 years); blue-grey stains darken in sunlight. Grading (Jordan and Boksman): 1st degree light yellow/grey, no banding; 2nd degree darker yellow/grey, no banding; 3rd degree dark grey or blue with banding (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Management**
1. Extended nightguard bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide: 2-12 months of daily wear; partial lightening, some grey-blue tint may remain; 8 in 10 patients keep the result at least 1 year (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
2. 6.5% H2O2 strips as a cheaper option.
3. In-office/KTP laser (532 nm) photochemical bleaching (Sikri, 2020).
4. Intentional root canal treatment and intracoronal bleaching — effective but sacrifices a healthy pulp; Grossman's prefers veneers instead (Gopikrishna, 2021).
5. Severe or gingival-third stain: veneers or crowns, ideally after bleaching to reduce opaquer.

## 10. Light, heat and laser activation

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: "the addition of light during the bleaching procedure does not improve the whitening result beyond what the bleach alone can achieve"; lights dehydrate and add sensitivity, and the ADA advises against "Use of carbon dioxide laser to heat the bleaching mixture" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: for high-intensity lights "The preponderance of evidence at this time does not support their efficacy in producing a superior result"; "It is important to remember that the hydrogen peroxide performs the bleaching, not the laser"; "The ADA does not recommend CO2 laser use for bleaching"; argon at "a wavelength of 488 nm" with little pulpal heating, diode lasers potentially harmful (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Cohen's lists UV, halogen, plasma arc, LED ("cold blue light with a wavelength of around 465 nm") and lasers (810-980 nm diode with dyed gels), with mixed study results (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's describes KTP (532 nm) photochemical bleaching of tetracycline quinone (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Grossman's warns lights and lasers "can induce severe postoperative sensitivity" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The textbooks agree lights mainly heat the gel and speed the reaction; benefit to the final shade is unproven [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Rationale:** heat or light speeds H2O2 decomposition into radicals (each 10°C doubles the rate).

**Sources:** heated instruments, photoflood lamp, quartz-tungsten-halogen, plasma arc (short bursts, high heat), LED (cold blue, about 465 nm), lasers — argon 488 nm, diode 810-980 nm, CO2 (not recommended, pulpal damage), KTP 532 nm (photochemical, for tetracycline) (Setzer, 2021; Sikri, 2020; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Evidence:** lights do not improve the final result beyond the gel alone; the immediate extra whiteness is dehydration (Ritter et al, 2019). "The peroxide bleaches, not the laser" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Risks:** pulpal temperature rise, more sensitivity, dehydration, cost, UV exposure. Never anaesthetize; never heat a non-vital tooth more than a vital one.

## 11. Non-vital (intracoronal) bleaching

### Explanation

Walking bleach: "The walking bleach technique is an in-office bleaching procedure that does not require the use of heat and employs sodium perborate as the bleaching agent" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; sodium perborate is mixed with water, saline or anaesthetic "to achieve the consistency of wet sand" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], "(approximately 2 g/mL)" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Mixing liquid — books disagree: Cohen's — if needed, hydrogen peroxide "then it should be used at 3% strength" and not 30% (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's — 3% hydrogen peroxide for severe stains (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's — gradually 3% to 30% only if needed (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein's routine walking bleach still uses perborate "and enough 35% hydrogen peroxide to form a thick white paste" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt's: the historical paste of "watery 30% hydrogen peroxide and sodium perborate powder" is replaced by perborate with water or 10% carbamide peroxide (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Gutta-percha removal level — books disagree: Grossman's "condensed with finger pluggers to 1 mm apical to the cementoenamel junction" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sturdevant's "to approximately 1–2 mm apical of the clinical crown" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's "the gutta-percha is removed 2 mm below the" CEJ (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri's "Approximately 2.0–3.0 mm of root canal restorative material is also removed in an apical direction" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Barrier thickness and level — disagree: Cohen's "A protective layer of at least a 2-mm thickness" with "the vertical outline of the protective barrier should be 1 mm coronally to the CEJ" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's "at least 2 mm, of a protective white cement barrier" conforming to the epithelial attachment (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's "at least 1 mm intracoronal barrier" and "MTA/tricalcium silicate cements have been shown to be superior" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri's "0.5–1.0 mm thick calcium hydroxide" or GIC plug, its internal level "one millimetre incisal to the corresponding external" probing of the attachment (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Temporary seal "to a thickness of 3 mm" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Recall — differs: Grossman's 3-7 days ("The maximum bleaching effect is attained about 24 hours after the treatment") (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's 3-10 days (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's "Evaluate the patient 2 weeks later" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Sturdevant's "The sodium perborate should be changed weekly" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Acid etching of the chamber: "Etching the dentin with phosphoric acid is unnecessary and may not improve the prognosis" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's: "most discolorations (75%) can be handled in one to two appointments" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].

Thermocatalytic: 30-35% hydrogen peroxide in the chamber heated; "The application of heat will result in foaming of the hydrogen peroxide, with a subsequent release of free radical oxygen" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; historically heat "applied several times during a 30-minute period" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; "The thermocatalytic technique is not recommended, however, because of the potential for cervical resorption" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. UV photo-oxidation: hydrogen peroxide in the chamber "followed by a 2-minute exposure to ultraviolet light" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC].

Inside-outside: "This technique was described by Settembrini and Liebenberg" (1997), 10% carbamide peroxide placed by the patient into the open chamber and a tray (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt's: it "is very effective and may reduce the treatment time by as much as 50%", and the safer "internal-external closed bleaching technique" seals 10% carbamide peroxide in the chamber and continues with a single-tooth tray (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri's uses the name combination technique for thermocatalytic followed by walking bleach: "This technique involves the combined use of thermocatalytic non-vital bleaching technique followed by walking bleach regimen" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] — a different technique under the same label as Goldstein's and Summitt's inside-outside [Certain]. Summitt's recommendation: "the recommended treatment for internal bleaching is sodium perborate mixed with water, or 10% carbamide peroxide alone" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** discoloration from pulp necrosis, haemorrhage or pulp remnants in a root-filled tooth; discoloration not amenable to external bleaching. **Contraindications:** superficial enamel stain, defective enamel, severe dentin loss, caries, discoloured composites, poor obturation (Rotstein, 2019).

**A. Walking bleach (Nutting and Poe) — technique of choice**
1. Radiograph: adequate obturation (retreat if needed). Shade and photographs.
2. Rubber dam (ligated); protect gingiva.
3. Remove restoration; refine access; clean pulp horns; remove sealer with solvent. Do not etch.
4. Remove gutta-percha below the CEJ: 1 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021), 1-2 mm (Ritter et al, 2019), 2 mm (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013), 2-3 mm (Sikri, 2020).
5. **Cervical barrier** (essential): RMGI, GIC, Cavit, IRM, MTA; at least 2 mm thick (Setzer, 2021; Rotstein, 2019), its coronal edge about 1 mm incisal to the epithelial attachment/CEJ, following the external CEJ contour.
6. Paste of sodium perborate and water/saline/anaesthetic to wet-sand consistency (about 2 g/mL); 3% H2O2 only for resistant stains (Setzer, 2021). Pack against the labial wall, blot.
7. Seal with 3 mm temporary restoration (Cavit, IRM, GIC).
8. Recall in 3-7 days (Grossman) to 2 weeks (Ingle); renew up to three or four times; reassess the diagnosis if no change after three.

**B. Thermocatalytic:** 30-35% H2O2 on cotton in the chamber, heated (heat carrier or lamp) for up to 30 min, repeated in 3-4 visits; between visits a walking bleach. High resorption risk — not recommended (Ritter et al, 2019).

**C. UV photo-oxidation:** 30-35% H2O2 in the chamber, 2 min UV exposure to the labial surface (Rotstein, 2019).

**D. Inside-outside (Settembrini and Liebenberg, 1997):** chamber left open after barrier placement; patient places 10% carbamide peroxide in the chamber and in a tray; up to 50% faster; risks food impaction and caries (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). **Closed variant:** 10% carbamide peroxide sealed inside plus single-tooth tray.

## 12. Adverse effects

### Explanation

Sensitivity: "Double-blind studies have reported sensitivity in 25% to 70% of patients", but "20% to 30% of patients report sensitivity when a placebo is used"; peroxide reaches the pulp "in a matter of 5 to 15 minutes"; yet "the pulp remains healthy and the sensitivity is completely reversible" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Cohen's: "up to 50% of the teeth", lasting 2-3 days; 50,000 μg "of hydrogen peroxide are required to inhibit pulpal enzymes" while less than 30 μg reaches the pulp from 12% carbamide trays (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's: at least 50% with 10% carbamide, "(65–78%)" with heat (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Grossman's "Sensitivity is directly related to the concentration of hydrogen peroxide" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Craig's is more cautious: peroxides "traverse the dentin in sufficient concentrations to be cytotoxic" and "most reports agree that a legitimate concern exists about the long-term use of these products on vital teeth" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC] — a materials-science view based partly on in vitro cytotoxicity, against the operative texts' clinical reassurance [Likely]. Speed of penetration by book: Craig's 11th ed, "Peroxides in bleaching agents have been shown to permeate intact enamel in just a few seconds" (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Craig's 14th ed, peroxides "reach the pulp in a few minutes" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson, diffusion "starts within a matter of 15 minutes" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Summitt's 5-15 minutes to the pulp. Crossing enamel and reaching the pulp are different end points, so these are not true conflicts [Likely]. Bhat et al describe peroxides "capable of passing through the exposed dentin and cause cytotoxicity" (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha: they "are readily diffused through 0.5 mm of dentin in concentration to produce cytotoxicity" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Management: interrupt or shorten treatment, "the application of 3% to 5% potassium nitrate gel in the tray", fluoride, ACP (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Soft tissue: "The most commonly observed adverse effect after external bleaching is gingival irritation" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; "The more powerful in-office bleaching agents (30% to 35% hydrogen peroxide) can easily produce tissue burns" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Enamel: low-pH solutions cause slight hardness loss "to a depth of approximately 25" μm, quickly remineralized (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Restorations: "Bleaching has no effect on porcelain"; "It does encourage the release of mercury from some types of amalgam restorations"; methacrylate provisionals yellow (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Cohen's: "no health implications due to the induced release of mercury by tooth bleaching have been reported" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha: whitening agents "do not adversely affect gold alloys, amalgam, microfilled composites, or porcelain" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC] — van Noort, however, reports in vitro that "carbamide-based bleaching agents can increase the release of mercury from amalgam restorations" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC], siding with Summitt's; Grossman's, by contrast, says ceramics may be etched and avoids extracoronal bleaching of teeth with extensive amalgam (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Systemic: "The Food and Drug Administration recognizes 3% hydrogen peroxide and 10% to 15% carbamide peroxide as GRAS" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC], whereas Sikri's cites that "10% carbamide peroxide would act as a tumor promoter in the presence of mutated cells" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] — an animal-model finding that Summitt's treats only as a reason to avoid tobacco during bleaching [Likely].

Cervical resorption incidence — books differ widely: Summitt's "about 7% of the teeth that have undergone internal in-office bleaching", animal studies "18% to 25% when heat was applied" and "0% to 6% without heat application" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Cohen's "6% to 8% of cases when 35% hydrogen peroxide was used" and "18% to 25% if the hydrogen peroxide was heat activated" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Sturdevant's "a slight potential (i.e. 1%)" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri's "ranges from 5 to 10%" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Heithersay's series via Goldstein's: "and 3.9% by intracoronal bleaching" of all invasive cervical resorption cases (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC] — a different denominator (cases of resorption, not bleached teeth) [Certain]. Mechanism: hydrogen peroxide diffuses through unprotected tubules and cemental defects ("approximately 10% of the population"); in teeth lacking cementum 30% hydrogen peroxide "resulted in the diffusion of 82% of the bleaching agent" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; heat widens tubules (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; "The combination of bleaching and history of trauma seems to be the most important predisposing factor for cervical resorption" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Ingle's: "In none of the clinical reports of postbleaching root resorption was a protective barrier used" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC], though Summitt's warns the barrier "reduces the diffusion of hydrogen but does not prevent it" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Onset "generally has its onset in 1–7 years" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's adds that "The non-vital bleaching procedures adversely affect the marginal seal leading to marginal leakage" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Vital bleaching**
1. **Tooth sensitivity** — the commonest effect: 25-70% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013), up to 50% (Setzer, 2021), 65-78% with heat (Sikri, 2020). H2O2 reaches the pulp in 5-15 min, causing reversible pulpitis; transient (2-3 days), no long-term pulp damage. Related to concentration, time, heat, more than one application a day, history of sensitivity. *Management:* shorter or interrupted treatment, 3-5% potassium nitrate and fluoride in the tray, ACP/CPP-ACP, NSAIDs.
2. **Gingival irritation and burns:** most common soft tissue effect; 30-35% H2O2 burns (white, painful); ill-fitting trays. *Treat:* rinse, sodium bicarbonate, vitamin E, Orabase (Setzer, 2021).
3. **Enamel and dentin:** increased porosity, slight hardness loss (about 25 µm), mostly with acidic gels; reversible by remineralization; over-bleaching beyond the saturation point weakens enamel.
4. **Restorations:** no effect on porcelain and gold; mercury release from amalgam (clinical significance unknown); slight roughening of composite and GIC; PMMA provisionals turn yellow-orange — use bis-acryl (Ritter et al, 2019); existing restorations do not lighten and look dark.
5. **Bond strength:** reduced 25-50% (Section 13).
6. **Systemic:** swallowed low doses safe (FDA: 3% H2O2 and 10-15% carbamide peroxide GRAS); sore throat, nausea; possible co-carcinogen in animal studies — avoid tobacco during bleaching.
7. **TMJ** discomfort from trays.

**B. Non-vital bleaching**
1. **External cervical resorption** — the most serious complication. Incidence: 1% (Ritter et al, 2019); 5-10% (Sikri, 2020); 6-8% with 35% H2O2 and 18-25% with heat (Setzer, 2021); about 7% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). *Mechanism:* H2O2 diffuses through dentinal tubules and CEJ defects (cementum and enamel do not meet in about 10% of teeth), denatures dentin, lowers pH, inflames the PDL and activates osteoclasts. *Predisposing:* trauma, age below 25, heat, 30-35% H2O2, no barrier. *Prevention:* barrier, sodium perborate with water, no heat, no acid etching, radiographic recall (onset 1-7 years).
2. Chemical burns from Superoxol; tooth fracture (weakened crown); leakage of temporary seal.
3. Poorer marginal seal of the final composite, with microleakage and re-staining (Sikri, 2020).

## 13. Restoration after bleaching

### Explanation

Bond reduction: "A 25% to 50% reduction in bond strength of composite to etched enamel" from oxygen inhibition of resin tags (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Delay — books differ: Sturdevant's and Grossman's: no loss "if the composite restorative treatment is delayed at least 1 week after cessation of any bleaching" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Ingle's "at least 7 days" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's vital section: "must be postponed by 7–10 days" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Summitt's "a delay of 7 to 10 days" in one study, "at least 2 weeks for maximum bond strength" in another, and "A delay of 1 to 2 weeks or more" overall (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Cohen's "about 1 to 3 weeks after the last appointment", "wait for 2 to 3 weeks", and bonding "reestablished after 3 weeks" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri's "24 hours to 4 weeks have been quoted in various studies" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's also delays closure of a bleached access "for 2 weeks to allow the shade to stabilize" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Antioxidants: "Sodium ascorbate is a buffered form of vitamin C" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; "10% sodium ascorbate has been found to be effective in reversing the compromised bonding" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; catalase for "3 minutes" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; acetone drying (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Calcium hydroxide dressing — a direct conflict: Sturdevant's places calcium hydroxide paste and "allows the calcium hydroxide material to remain in the pulp chamber for 2 weeks" to reduce resorption (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], as does Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's says "this procedure, however, is unnecessary with walking bleach" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Torabinejad's says "this procedure is ineffective and unnecessary" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's "it may be unnecessary after a walking bleach technique" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. The endodontic texts by Rotstein reject it; the operative texts keep it; with sodium perborate and water there is little acidity to buffer [Likely]. Final restoration: light-shade acid-etched composite over a white base, "Light curing from the labial surface, rather than the lingual surface, is recommended" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Why delay?** Residual oxygen and peroxide in enamel and dentin inhibit resin polymerization and resin-tag formation: bond strength falls 25-50% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Shade also needs time to stabilize after rehydration.

**How long?** Books differ: at least 1 week (Ritter et al, 2019; Rotstein, 2019); 7-10 days (Gopikrishna, 2021); 1-2 weeks or more, 2 weeks for maximum bond (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 2-3 weeks, full recovery at 3 weeks (Setzer, 2021); 24 hours to 4 weeks in studies (Sikri, 2020). Safe rule: **2 weeks**, and select shade only then.

**If immediate bonding is needed:** 10% sodium ascorbate (antioxidant) for the same time as the bleach was applied, catalase (3 min), alcohol or acetone-based adhesives, alpha-tocopherol.

**Non-vital tooth**
- Calcium hydroxide dressing for 2 weeks to neutralize acidity and reduce resorption (Ritter et al, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) — but considered unnecessary after sodium perborate walking bleach (Rotstein, 2019; Setzer, 2021).
- Leave the cervical barrier; white base (GIC) in the chamber; light-shade acid-etched composite extended into the pulp horns for incisal support; cure from the labial side so shrinkage is toward the walls (Rotstein, 2019).
- A well-sealed restoration is the main defence against relapse.

**Existing restorations** do not lighten: replace them after the shade stabilizes.

## 14. Colour relapse and prognosis

### Explanation

Longevity — books differ: Sturdevant's "Bleaching generally has an approximate lifespan of 1–3 years" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's "Results with at-home bleaching techniques tend to remain stable for 1 to 10 years" and "External bleaching may show color regressions after 1 to 3 years" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; tetracycline: "8 in 10 patients can expect to retain that lightening for at least 1 year" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Non-vital: internal bleaching restores colour "in 83% to 91% of cases", but "only 35% to 50% of the teeth maintain their esthetically pleasing appearance" at 1-5 years; sodium perborate: 90% initially and 49% at 3 years (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; thermocatalytic "an 80% success rate after 1 year and a 45% success rate after 6 years"; trauma and necrosis stains "appear to have a good prognosis of around 95%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Causes: double bond re-formation (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], leakage "through marginal gaps between the tooth and the restoration" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC], and increased enamel permeability after overbleaching: "Teeth that are bleached a shade too light seem to revert to their former color shortly after bleaching" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Goldstein's: "It is also not known whether the color regression is a result of recolorizing of the oxidized stain molecule" or new stain and ageing (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Colour relapse (rebound, regression)**
- *Immediate:* rehydration after in-office dehydration (assess at 1-2 weeks).
- *Late causes:* re-formation of chromophore double bonds (Setzer, 2021); leakage of stains through restoration margins in non-vital teeth (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); new extrinsic stain, diet, tobacco, ageing; increased enamel permeability after bleaching.
- *Duration:* about 1-3 years (Ritter et al, 2019); home bleaching may be stable 1-10 years (Setzer, 2021).
- *Non-vital:* 83-91% initial success, only 35-50% maintained at 1-5 years (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); thermocatalytic 80% at 1 year and 45% at 6 years (Setzer, 2021).
- *Prevention/management:* well-sealed composite, slight over-bleaching in non-vital teeth, avoid chromogens, touch-up with the tray (single-tooth tray avoids reopening the access).

**Prognosis by stain:** best — trauma/necrosis stains (about 95%), yellow, ageing and smoking; moderate — brown fluorosis, mild tetracycline; poor — blue-grey tetracycline, metallic (amalgam, silver) stains, white spots, gingival-third stains (Setzer, 2021; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 15. Safety guidelines

### Explanation

Summitt's: the primary safety issue is "the concern for masking existing pathology"; a European review concluded low-concentration bleaching is safe, OTC use should follow a dental examination, and set the 6% hydrogen peroxide maximum (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Ingle's safe-bleaching list: isolate, protect mucosa, verify obturation, use barriers, avoid acid etching, avoid strong oxidizers, avoid heat, recall periodically (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's recommends for non-vital teeth "Use a low concentration of peroxide", a base over the root filling, no high heat or light activation (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Safety rules**
1. Diagnose before bleaching — radiograph every single dark tooth; never let bleaching mask necrosis, caries or resorption.
2. Use the lowest effective concentration: 10% carbamide peroxide for home, sodium perborate with water for non-vital teeth (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Rotstein, 2019).
3. Isolate and protect soft tissues; protective eyewear; vitamin E/bicarbonate at hand.
4. No local anaesthesia; avoid excessive heat; avoid CO2 laser.
5. Non-vital: verify obturation, place cervical barrier, avoid 30-35% H2O2 and heat, avoid acid etching, recall radiographically for resorption.
6. Stop at the saturation point; manage sensitivity early.
7. Delay bonding about 2 weeks; replace restorations afterwards.
8. Supervise; discourage unsupervised OTC use.

**Conclusion.** Bleaching is the most conservative treatment for discoloured teeth. Success depends on correct diagnosis of the stain, choice of agent and technique, and safe practice: low-concentration supervised home bleaching for vital teeth, sodium perborate walking bleach with a cervical barrier for non-vital teeth, and properly timed sealed restorations.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: light or heat activation improves in-office bleaching.**
- **Textbook position:** older methods and Grossman's/Sikri's protocols use heat (52-60°C or 46-60°C) and lights (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2020).
- **Challenge:** Sturdevant's states lights do not improve the result beyond the bleach; Summitt's says the evidence does not support superior results and the immediate extra whiteness is dehydration; Cohen's reports mixed studies, some favouring lasers (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Setzer, 2021).
- **Strength:** summaries of trials, not read in the original; no systematic review was opened this session. [Likely]
- **Conclusion:** heat speeds the chemistry but not, on present textbook evidence, the final shade; it adds sensitivity and pulpal risk. Light activation is optional, not a requirement. [Likely]

**Claim 2: the incidence of cervical resorption after intracoronal bleaching.**
- **Textbook figures:** 1% (Sturdevant's), about 7% (Summitt's), 5-10% (Sikri's), 6-8% and 18-25% with heat (Cohen's), 3.9% of resorption cases (Heithersay, via Goldstein's).
- **Refinement:** the high figures come from 30-35% hydrogen peroxide, heat, trauma and no barrier; animal data show 0-6% without heat (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); Ingle's notes no reported case had a barrier (Rotstein, 2019). Heithersay's figure is a share of resorption cases, not a risk per bleached tooth.
- **Conclusion:** the risk is technique-dependent; with sodium perborate and water, a barrier and no heat it is low but not zero (barriers reduce diffusion but do not prevent it). Long-term radiographic recall remains necessary. [Likely]

**Claim 3: calcium hydroxide should be placed after non-vital bleaching.**
- **For:** Sturdevant's and Grossman's (Ritter et al, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Against:** Ingle's, Torabinejad's and Cohen's call it unnecessary or ineffective after sodium perborate walking bleach (Rotstein, 2019; Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021; Setzer, 2021).
- **Conclusion:** the evidence is not resolved in these books; it is reasonable after Superoxol or thermocatalytic bleaching, and dispensable after sodium perborate-water. [Guessing]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Bleaching = oxidation of organic chromophores by free radicals from H2O2 (perhydroxyl, hydroxyl, nascent oxygen); stop at the saturation point.
- 10% carbamide peroxide = about 3.5% H2O2 + 6.5% urea (one-third rule); carbopol prolongs release; urea raises pH.
- In-office: 30-35% H2O2, rubber dam, no anaesthesia, about 3 visits; lights do not improve the end result.
- Nightguard: 10% carbamide peroxide, custom tray, overnight, 2-6 weeks; tetracycline 2-12 months.
- Walking bleach: sodium perborate + water, cervical barrier at least 2 mm, coronal to CEJ; avoid heat and 30% H2O2.
- Cervical resorption: 1-10% (books differ), up to 18-25% with heat; trauma is the key predisposing factor.
- Bond strength drops 25-50%; delay bonding 1-3 weeks (2 weeks is the safe rule) or use sodium ascorbate.
- Relapse: lifespan about 1-3 years; non-vital teeth only 35-50% stable at 5 years.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing that 10% carbamide peroxide equals 10% hydrogen peroxide (it is about 3-3.5%).
- Describing walking bleach with 30% Superoxol as routine; modern practice uses sodium perborate with water and a cervical barrier.
- Forgetting the cervical barrier, or placing it apical to the CEJ level instead of following the epithelial attachment.
- Claiming lights or lasers make teeth whiter in the end; they speed the reaction and dehydrate.
- Restoring with composite immediately after bleaching without mentioning the oxygen-inhibition delay or antioxidant.

## MNEMONIC
**"BARRIER"** for safe non-vital bleaching: **B**ase over gutta-percha, **A**void acid etching, **R**adiograph before and after, **R**ubber dam, **I**nert liquid with sodium perborate (no Superoxol), **E**liminate heat, **R**ecall for resorption.
