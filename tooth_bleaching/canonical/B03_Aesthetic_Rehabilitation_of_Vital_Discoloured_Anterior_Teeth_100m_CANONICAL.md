## 1. Introduction, definitions and the conservative principle

### Explanation

Sturdevant's defines bleaching as "The lightening of the colour of a tooth through the application of a chemical agent to oxidize the organic pigmentation in the tooth is referred to as bleaching." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri gives the formal definition "the treatment, usually involving an oxidative chemical that alters the light absorbing and/or light reflecting nature of the material structure, thereby increasing its value (whiteness)" and separates the terms: "Whitening restores teeth to their natural tooth color" by removing surface stains, while bleaching makes teeth lighter than their natural colour (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's notes that the distinction is loose: "cosmetic products typically use the word whitening for the removal of surface stains" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's lists the options for discoloured anterior teeth: "Treatment options include removal of surface stains, bleaching, micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion, veneering and placement of porcelain crowns." and observes that "increasing numbers of patients do not want their teeth" cut down for crowns (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Its governing principle is that "Mild discolourations are best left untreated, are bleached or are treated conservatively with micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion because no restorative material is as good as the healthy, natural tooth structure." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's chapter summary puts the same rule as a duty to "select the most conservative treatment option for the specific stain", after "the first step in the treatment of a patient whose chief complaint is stains or discolorations is the diagnosis of the cause" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Aesthetic rehabilitation is therefore read here as the whole diagnostic and treatment sequence, not only restorations. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Discoloured anterior teeth are among the commonest reasons for seeking aesthetic care. In a vital tooth colour must be improved without harming the pulp and with maximum preservation of enamel.

**Definitions**
- **Bleaching:** lightening of tooth colour by a chemical agent that oxidizes organic pigments in the tooth (Ritter et al, 2019); it increases the value (whiteness) of the tooth (Sikri, 2020).
- **Whitening:** restores natural colour by removing surface stain; bleaching makes teeth lighter than their natural colour (Sikri, 2020).

**Guiding principle.** First diagnose the cause, then choose the most conservative option for that stain, preparing the patient for further treatment if it fails (Goldstein et al, 2018a). Mild discolourations are left untreated, bleached or abraded, because no restorative material is as good as natural tooth structure (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Options (conservative → invasive):** prophylaxis → microabrasion/macroabrasion → resin infiltration → vital bleaching → direct composite → porcelain veneers → crowns (Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein et al, 2018a). Porcelain crowns were once the standard answer for badly discoloured teeth, but increasing numbers of patients refuse to have sound teeth cut down and accept less permanent, conservative alternatives (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 2. Classification and causes of discoloration in vital teeth

### Explanation

Goldstein's Ch.12: "Extrinsic discoloration is caused by the accumulation of stains on the enamel surface", aggravated by enamel pits, saliva and poor hygiene; "Nicotine stains start as tenacious extrinsic stains, but over time absorb into the tooth and become an intrinsic stain"; and "The most common drugs causing extrinsic discoloration include chlorhexidine", iron salts, essential oils and co-amoxiclav (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Ch.21 gives the Nathoo classification: "N1-type colored material (chromogen) binds to the tooth surface" (tea, coffee, wine, chromogenic bacteria, metals); "N2-type colored material changes color after binding to the tooth"; "N3-type colorless material or prechromogen binds to the tooth and undergoes a chemical reaction to cause a stain" (carbohydrates, stannous fluoride, chlorhexidine); and "The most common cause of extrinsic stains is poor oral hygiene." (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Sturdevant's notes that in the young, stains of any colour are "more prominent in the cervical areas of teeth" (Nasmyth membrane, plaque, chromogenic bacteria), while "In older patients, stains on the surfaces of teeth are more likely to be brown, black or grey" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri uses three groups — "broadly divided into three categories, viz. intrinsic, extrinsic and internalized stains" — where "Internalized discoloration occurs due to incorporation of extrinsic stains within the tooth substance" through enamel defects, wear, recession or caries (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's gives the clinically useful corollary: "Extrinsic stains are located on the surface of the tooth and are most easily removed by external cleaning." while "Intrinsic stains are located within the tooth and are accessible only by bleaching." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's lists the developmental causes in vital teeth as hereditary disorders, "medications (particularly tetracycline preparations)", excess fluoride, high fevers and trauma (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; ageing yellows teeth because "continuing deposition of secondary dentin occurs in older individuals" while enamel thins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and Goldstein's: "The combination of thinned enamel and darkened dentin creates an older-looking tooth." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Bartlett adds that the change "is believed to be caused by the gradual thinning of enamel from tooth wear", and that the canine naturally has "a higher saturation of the colour yellow than other incisor teeth", which some patients wrongly see as a defect (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Trauma to a vital tooth can lead to calcific metamorphosis — "Calcific metamorphosis is the process by which the tooth deposits darker yellow secondary dentin in the pulp chamber" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] — which Sturdevant's calls "extremely difficult to treat" ("This condition is extremely difficult to treat") (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Systemic pigments (bilirubin in jaundice, erythroblastosis fetalis, porphyria) and restorations also discolour vital teeth; for amalgam, Goldstein's says it "should be replaced with less visible materials such as composite resin before bleaching" and warns "Metallic stains are the most difficult to remove." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**
1. **Extrinsic** — on the enamel surface (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Nathoo (Goldstein et al, 2018a): N1 coloured chromogen binds (tea, coffee, wine, bacteria, metals); N2 chromogen darkens after binding; N3 colourless pre-chromogen reacts to form stain (chlorhexidine, stannous fluoride). Commonest cause: poor oral hygiene. Children: cervical stains; older patients: brown, black, grey (Ritter et al, 2019).
2. **Intrinsic** — within enamel/dentin; reachable only by bleaching or removal/covering (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
3. **Internalized** — extrinsic stain entering enamel defects or exposed dentin (Sikri, 2020); e.g. nicotine (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Intrinsic causes in vital teeth**
- *Developmental:* fluorosis, tetracycline, hypoplasia, hypocalcification, amelogenesis/dentinogenesis imperfecta, fever, bilirubin, porphyria (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
- *Acquired:* ageing — thin enamel plus darker secondary dentin (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Bartlett, 2011); trauma with calcific metamorphosis (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); caries, leaking restorations, amalgam shine-through.

## 3. Fluorosis, tetracycline and other developmental discolorations

### Explanation

**Fluorosis.** "Dental fluorosis is the most common cause of intrinsic discoloration"; histologically it shows "a hypomineralized, porous subsurface enamel below a well-mineralized surface layer"; "Bleaching is a good indication for fluorosis with brown pigmentation on a smooth enamel surface", but "Localized white spots frequently seen in fluorosis have an unpredictable prognosis", so with white spots or surface defects "bleaching should be performed as an adjunctive treatment prior to esthetic restorative treatments including bonding or veneering" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21 grades it: "Mild fluorosis is seen as flat gray or white flecks on the enamel surface.", moderate is chalky with pitting, and "Severe fluorosis causes marked tooth deformity"; it distinguishes fluoride from non-fluoride opacities: "Enamel opacities due to fluoride ingestion are poorly demarcated", non-fluoride ones round and well demarcated (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Sikri's mild form differs: "Mild fluorosis exhibits brown pigmentation on a smooth surface", and "The severe form of fluorosis may not respond to bleaching." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The threshold also differs: Summitt's "Excessive fluoride in drinking water, greater than 1 to 2 ppm, can cause metabolic alteration in the ameloblasts" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] versus Sikri's "high concentration of fluorides (more than 4.0 ppm) causes moderate to severe discoloration" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The two are not strictly contradictory — Sikri's figure is for moderate-to-severe staining — but they are often quoted as "the" threshold. [Likely]

**Tetracycline.** Goldstein's Ch.12: damage from "as little as 1 g of tetracycline" was recognised in the 1950s; teeth are susceptible "during the second trimester in utero to roughly 8 years of age"; binding "involves chelation with calcium, which forms tetracycline orthophosphate"; "When tetracycline-stained teeth are exposed to sunlight, they gradually turn to shades of dark gray or brown"; there are "more than 2000 variants have been patented" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's gives a different window — "if ingested by an expectant mother in the third trimester of pregnancy or by a child during the tooth-formation years, between the ages of 3 to 4 months and 7 to 8 years" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. The severity "depends on the dose, the duration of exposure to the drug and the type of tetracycline analogue used." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri says instead that "the dose of tetracycline is more important than the duration of intake of tetracyclines" and that "doxycycline does not produce any stain" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], whereas Summitt's lists "doxycycline, oxytetracycline, minocycline, chlortetracycline, demeclocycline" among the analogues producing grey, blue, brown or yellow (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] — a direct disagreement on doxycycline that the books do not resolve. [Guessing which is right; the literature behind Sikri's claim was not read.] Minocycline stains formed adult teeth: Goldstein's explains that "minocycline is absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract and combines poorly with calcium" and is thought to "chelate with iron and form insoluble complexes" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Sturdevant's cites "long-term exposure to minocycline, a tetracycline analogue" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Diagnosis: "Tetracycline staining can be clearly distinguished as the teeth fluorescence under ultraviolet light." (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; with time "the fluorescent yellow discoloration gradually changes over a period of months to years to a non-fluorescent brown color" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

**Jordan and Boksman grading.** Goldstein's presents "three major categories of tetracycline involvement first proposed by Jordan and Boksman" in 1984, plus a fourth added later: "First-degree tetracycline staining is a light yellow or light gray staining" without banding — "It is highly amenable to vital bleaching, with good results usually in fewer than four sessions of office bleaching"; second degree is darker without banding — "it may take five or more in-office treatments to obtain a satisfactory result", combined bleaching preferred and "Home bleaching alone may take 2–6 months."; "Third-degree tetracycline staining produces severe staining, characterized by dark gray or blue coloration, usually with banding" where "Veneering techniques with opaquers are often necessary to achieve satisfactory esthetic results"; fourth degree is "too dark" for some, although "these stains may not be too dark to try bleaching unless there is blue-gray stain at the gingivae" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

**Other defects.** Hypoplasia: "Cosmetic contouring, microabrasion, bonding resins, veneering, or crowning can be used to treat hypoplastic defects."; amelogenesis imperfecta: "Bleaching is difficult because the dentinal layer shows through, and it is usually painful because of the thinness of enamel."; hypocalcification: "Treatment can be cosmetic contouring, if the surface layer is not too deep, microabrasion, bonding, or porcelain veneers." (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. For enamel-structure defects generally, "Bleaching is usually a less appropriate treatment than bonding, veneering, or crowning for these problems involving the structure of enamel." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. In dentinogenesis imperfecta "there are bulbous roots with root canal obliteration", and with rapid wear in AI/DI "early intervention with multiple crowns is often required" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. **Slip (audit):** the grading is by Jordan and Boksman (1984), as Goldstein's prints it; Grossman's box reads "Tetracycline Discoloration Classification (Jordon and Boskman, 1984)" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and Walton's reference list "Jordan R, Boskman L: Conservative vital bleaching treatment of discolored dentition" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC] — misspellings to avoid in the exam [Certain].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Fluorosis** — commonest intrinsic cause; porous subsurface enamel under a well-mineralized surface (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Fluoride opacities are poorly demarcated (Goldstein et al, 2018a).
- Mild: grey/white flecks (Goldstein et al, 2018a) or brown pigment on smooth enamel (Sikri, 2020) → bleaching, microabrasion.
- Moderate: chalky enamel ± pits → bleach + microabrasion ± bonding.
- Severe: pitting, deformity; may not respond to bleaching (Sikri, 2020) → veneers/crowns.
Threshold: >1 to 2 ppm (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) versus >4.0 ppm for moderate-severe staining (Sikri, 2020).

**Tetracycline** — chelates calcium as tetracycline orthophosphate in dentin; sunlight darkens labial surfaces (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Window: second trimester to about 8 years (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) versus third trimester to 7–8 years (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Severity depends on dose, duration, analogue (Ritter et al, 2019); yellow UV fluorescence (Bartlett, 2011). Minocycline stains adult teeth (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Jordan and Boksman grading** (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018)

| Degree | Appearance | Bleaching prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| I | Light yellow/grey, no banding | Fewer than four office sessions |
| II | Darker, no banding | Five or more visits; combined best; home alone 2–6 months |
| III | Dark grey/blue, banded | Partial; veneers with opaquers |
| IV | Very dark | Veneers; try bleaching unless blue-grey at gingivae |

**Other defects:** hypoplasia/hypocalcification — contouring, microabrasion, bonding; amelogenesis imperfecta — bleaching difficult, painful (Goldstein et al, 2018a); dentinogenesis imperfecta — crowns (Bartlett, 2011).

## 4. Diagnosis and case assessment

### Explanation

Goldstein's Ch.12: "The diagnostic workup should include pretreatment photographs, X-ray films, and an intensive prophylaxis to remove superficial staining" that may compound intrinsic discoloration, with medical, medication and diet/tobacco history and sensitivity records (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. For a single dark tooth "The first step in the treatment of this tooth is to take a radiograph" and pulp test it (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Summitt's agrees ("A radiograph should always be taken of a single dark tooth") and explains why: "the disease process occurring in the tooth may be masked by removal of the only clinical symptom of the problem"; a traumatised tooth may necrose later — "It may take 1 to 20 years before this condition occurs." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt's states "The primary indication for bleaching is patient dissatisfaction with tooth color" and lists contraindications (Box 16-1) including "Unrealistic expectations" and "Pregnancy or lactation", and guarded prognoses including "Extremely dark gingival third of tooth that is visible when smiling", exposed roots, extensive white spots and TMD (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Translucent incisal edges: "some that are already translucent at the incisal edge may become more translucent"; test by placing a finger behind the tooth — "block the light transmission by placing a white-gloved finger on the lingual of the tooth"; and "Roots of teeth do not bleach as well as anatomical crowns" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's in-office contraindications include "extremely large pulps, which may increase sensitivity", severe enamel loss, cracks, severe banded tetracycline stain, white spots, restorations to be matched, extensive restorations (bleaching roughened tooth-coloured materials with "the greatest damage done to glass ionomer, the least to porcelain"), and perfectionists, because "bleaching is not perfect, in the way veneers can be" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's warns that many patients' teeth are normal: a shade tab "often shows these patients that the colour of their teeth is well within the normal range of shades" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's suggests an end point: "Patients tend to look most natural when the color of the teeth matches the white of the sclera in their eyes." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein's notes that although some say "the color of the teeth should closely match the color of the sclera", many patients ask for whiter (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**History:** complaint and expectation; onset; childhood fluoride, tetracycline/minocycline; trauma; diet, tobacco, chlorhexidine; sensitivity; pregnancy.

**Examination**
- Prophylaxis first, to separate extrinsic from intrinsic stain (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
- Distribution: generalized, single tooth, or spots; colour — yellow/brown respond, grey/blue, banded, dark cervical third resist.
- Enamel thickness, cracks, wear, exposed roots (do not bleach), translucent incisal edges (finger test) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); restorations, which will not bleach.
- Ultraviolet light: tetracycline-stained teeth fluoresce (Bartlett, 2011).
- Records: pretreatment photographs and shade, sensitivity history (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Investigations:** radiograph and pulp test of any single dark tooth — bleaching may mask necrosis or resorption (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Contraindications/guarded:** unrealistic expectations, non-compliance, pregnancy, restorations not to be replaced (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); large pulps, cracks, severe enamel loss, severe banded tetracycline stain, perfectionists (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). A shade tab often shows the teeth are normal (Ritter et al, 2019); a natural end point matches the sclera (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 5. Shade assessment and records

### Explanation

Goldstein's: "The VITA classical introduced to the dental profession in 1956 is still the most widely used", but its tabs are unevenly spaced, with "the lack of lighter shade tab than B1"; the 3D-Master works by "evaluating three components of color—value, chroma, and hue—separately"; Paravina in 2007 "introduced a new linear bleached shade guide for the purpose of monitoring color change during bleaching"; and spectrophotometers and colorimeters (Easyshade, SpectroShade) give data "not influenced by the human eye, environment, or light source" but are slow and costly (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's adds the perception problems: varying light sources — "This phenomenon is referred to as metamerism." — and eye fatigue with "loss of sensitivity to yellow-orange shades", relieved by looking at blue; so "the dentist, the assistant and especially the patient all be involved in shade selection" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's advises photographing the shade tab with the teeth and that "the best way to ensure patient awareness of the effect of bleaching is to bleach one arch at a time" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein's notes patients forget their original shade — Rosenstiel et al found "only 1 of 10 young adults who received one vital bleaching treatment were able to see the effects of the treatment past 1 month" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's warns that bleached teeth may go "lighter than the traditional lightest shade (B1 on the VITA Classic shade guide)" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

*Fundamentals of Color* (audit addition). The Bleachedguide Vita 3D-Master "is the only shade guide developed specifically for visual evaluation of tooth whitening"; the 3D-Master value groups begin with "Group 0 = 3 tabs (bleached shades, the lightest)"; and "Linearguide and Toothguide were both found to enable significantly better (closer) matches compared to Classical" (Chu et al, 2010) [SRC]. Bleached teeth are hard to match because they are achromatic, "leaving value as the only visual parameter"; bleaching removes pigment "allowing a tooth to become whiter yet remain highly translucent" (Chu et al, 2010) [SRC]. Protocol: light "with a color temperature of 5,500 K (D55) to 6,500 K (D65) and with a color-rendering index (CRI) of 90 or greater", and "An illuminance of 1,000 lux is considered optimal for visual color assessments."; eyes at "tooth level, 25 to 35 cm (10 to 14 inches) away"; "Dehydrated teeth become lighter and need time to regain their normal coloration", so shade comes at the start of the visit; "Five-second glances with periods of rest, instead of prolonged stares, are recommended." and "A neutral gray card should be observed between trials."; "Value is analyzed first, followed by chroma, then hue" (Chu et al, 2010) [SRC]. Sturdevant's blue-card advice and *Fundamentals*' grey card are two ways of resting the eye [Likely]. Alani and Patel add that "canine teeth are likely to require more bleaching than their neighbouring lateral incisor teeth" and recommend black-and-white photographs to judge value (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Methods** (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018)
- VITA Classical (1956): most used; uneven spacing, nothing lighter than B1 — bleached teeth often go lighter (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- 3D-Master: value, chroma, hue separately.
- Bleached shade guide (Paravina, 2007) — the Vita Bleachedguide, the only guide made for whitening; 3D-Master guides match better than Classical (Chu et al, 2010).
- Spectrophotometers/colorimeters: objective but costly and slow.
- Photographs with the tab beside the teeth, before, during and after.

**Why:** change is gradual and patients forget their original shade — in one study only 1 of 10 young adults could see the effect of a single treatment after 1 month (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Precautions:** correct light (metamerism); short looks, glance at blue against yellow fatigue; involve assistant and patient (Ritter et al, 2019); record shade at the start, before dehydration lightens teeth; D55-D65 light, CRI 90 or more, 25-35 cm, 5-second glances, grey card, value first (Chu et al, 2010). Bleached teeth are achromatic — match value. Bleach one arch at a time so the other is a control (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 6. Treatment sequence: from conservative to invasive

### Explanation

Goldstein's Ch.21 orders the options "in order of increasing aggressiveness" (Table 21.3): prophylaxis, air polisher, external bleaching with carbamide or hydrogen peroxide, sealants, macroabrasion, microabrasion, resurfacing or replacing restorations, partial or complete composite or ceramic veneers, and crowns (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Ch.12: "In most instances, bleaching teeth should be attempted before any other treatment is undertaken, with the exception of soft tissue management." and "In general, treat one arch at a time." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's agrees that "consideration should be given first to bleaching anterior teeth when intrinsic discolourations are encountered" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's calls the other methods "The alternatives to bleaching are more aggressive in relation to the tooth structure and/or to the gingival tissues." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bleaching also helps when restorations are inevitable: "Often, bleaching is the stepping-stone to veneers." and "bleaching lightens the underlying tooth, decreasing the masking needs of restoration" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Summitt's: "the lighter color of the bleached teeth allows lighter and more translucent veneers" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Order (increasing aggressiveness)** (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Ritter et al, 2019)
1. Hygiene, prophylaxis, air polishing.
2. Replace leaking restorations; treat caries.
3. Microabrasion/macroabrasion.
4. Resin infiltration (Sikri, 2020).
5. Vital bleaching — in-office, nightguard, OTC, combined.
6. Direct composite bonding/veneers.
7. Indirect veneers.
8. Crowns.

**Rules:** bleach before any other treatment except soft-tissue management; treat one arch at a time (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Evaluate each step before the next and warn the patient in advance that further treatment may be needed (Goldstein et al, 2018a). Even when veneers are planned, bleach first: the lighter substrate allows thinner, more translucent veneers (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 7. Prophylaxis and removal of extrinsic stains

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: "Most surface stains can be removed by routine prophylactic procedures", while superficial stains on restorations and decalcified areas may need micro- or macro-abrasion (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: "Superficial or extrinsic stains may be completely removed by a rubber cup with prophylaxis paste or by light abrasion with a rotary polishing device." and recommends that "bleaching should be delayed for several days after dental prophylaxis" so that sensitivity settles (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's: before diagnosis "a complete prophylaxis should be performed to remove minor surface staining"; of whitening toothpastes, abrasive ones remove stain but thin enamel, peroxide pastes fail because "the contact time on the tooth is too short to produce any noticeable whitening" (though useful for maintenance), and "Most whitening toothpastes contain titanium dioxide", a temporary coating (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. For chlorhexidine stain, some patients overcome the "disadvantage by employing 10% carbamide peroxide in a bleaching tray periodically", otherwise more frequent prophylaxis is needed (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.12 also notes that "tooth contouring can sometimes make stains disappear if they are only in the first cell layers of enamel" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Methods:** scaling, rubber-cup prophylaxis, air polisher for pits; light rotary polishing; cosmetic contouring for stain in the outermost enamel (Ritter et al, 2019; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); hygiene and diet advice.

**Whitening dentifrices:** abrasive (thin enamel with overuse), peroxide (too short a contact to bleach, useful for maintenance), titanium dioxide (temporary coating) (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

Chlorhexidine stain: periodic 10% CP in a tray, or more frequent prophylaxis (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

Prophylaxis also reveals the intrinsic component and precedes shade recording; delay bleaching a few days afterwards (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 8. Enamel microabrasion and macroabrasion

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: these techniques are "indicated only for stains or enamel defects that do not extend beyond a few tenths of a millimetre in depth". History: "In 1984, McCloskey reported the use of 18% hydrochloric acid swabbed on teeth for the removal of superficial fluorosis stains."; "in 1986, Croll and Cavanaugh modified the technique to include the use of pumice with 18% hydrochloric acid"; Croll later went on "reducing the concentration of the hydrochloric acid to approximately 11%" with "silicon carbide particles (in a water-soluble gel paste) instead of pumice" (Prema, Opalustre). The technique "does not remove stains or defects through any bleaching phenomena". For idiopathic spots, "an accurate prognosis for microabrasion cannot be given"; failure follows "the depth of the defect exceeding 0.2–0.3 mm". Fluorosis responds "if the discolouration is within the 0.2–0.3-mm removal depth limit"; near the CEJ "enamel is too thin to permit micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion". Technique: rubber dam, glasses, compound applied with a "special rubber cup that has fluted edges" in "A 10× gear reduction, low-speed handpiece", checked often, then "The treated areas are polished with a fluoride-containing prophy paste to restore surface lustre" and "a topical fluoride is applied to teeth to enhance remineralization". Macroabrasion uses "a 12-fluted composite finishing bur or a fine-grit finishing diamond in a high-speed handpiece" with water spray, because "Dehydration exaggerates the appearance of white spots", then "a 30-fluted finishing bur is used to remove any facets or striations". Comparison: "Micro-abrasion has the advantage of ensuring better control of the removal of tooth structure.", macroabrasion "can have catastrophic results if the clinician fails to use extreme caution" but is faster and needs no dam, and "micro-abrasion is recommended over macro-abrasion for the treatment of superficial defects in children" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

The books disagree markedly on enamel loss. Summitt's: the acid leaves "the outer 22 to 27 μm weakened enough so that the abrasive, with which the acid is mixed, quickly removes the stained outer surface", applied "is applied to the enamel with a rubber cup or stiff bristle brush for 20 to 30 seconds" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett, quoting Tong et al (1993): with 18% HCl and pumice "for approximately 100 seconds, about 360" ± 130 μm of enamel is lost (the extracted text has lost the ± and μ signs), so "rubber dam is therefore mandatory" and "it should not be used for defects in dentine" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Sikri's procedure applies the agent "for 60 seconds, followed by rinsing", repeated, and describes "clinically unrecognizable enamel loss" when the defect is "confined to the superficial 0.2–0.3 mm of enamel" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Reading them together: 22–27 μm is the depth weakened per application, 360 μm the cumulative loss after about 100 s, and 0.2–0.3 mm the depth limit of a treatable defect — consistent in substance, but a student quoting a single figure for depth removed will be wrong unless the time is stated. [Likely] Sikri contraindicates it for "Stains of amelogenesis imperfecta, dentinogenesis imperfecta and tetracycline stains" and notes "teeth sometimes appear more yellow after treatment" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Goldstein's treats brown fluorosis spots: "Usually, microabrasion is considered the primary treatment." yet bleaching first is preferred because "attempting bleaching first avoids the removal of the fluoride-rich enamel layer" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Summitt's: "It is best to bleach first unless the tooth enamel is of a chalky texture." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.12 describes the slurry as "a combination of hydrochloric acid (muriatic acid) and pumice with mechanical abrasion, which will etch the enamel slightly to facilitate stain removal" and macroabrasion "with friction grip diamonds or carbides with Soflex disk polishing" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Audit: Walton's insists "This is not a true bleaching (oxidizing) technique but decalcification and removal of a thin layer of stained enamel." (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], whereas Sikri's *Essentials* lists it among in-office bleaching methods — "a. Thermocatalytic bleaching b. Nonthermocatalytic bleaching c. Power bleaching d. Microabrasion" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC] — a misclassification [Certain]. Application times differ again: Cohen's — "the paste should be placed on the tooth for about 5 seconds, followed by the rubber cup activation for 15 seconds" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Bartlett's 360 figure is "about 360" ± "130 mm of enamel thickness is lost (Tong et al., 1993)" in the extract, where ± is a control character and μ has become m (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; read 360 ± 130 μm. Detail: B13.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Microabrasion:** simultaneous acid erosion and abrasion removing a thin layer of stained enamel (Bartlett, 2011); it does not bleach (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Agents:** 18% HCl + pumice (McCloskey 1984; Croll and Cavanaugh 1986); about 11% HCl + silicon carbide gel (Prema, Opalustre) (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Indications:** defects within 0.2–0.3 mm — mild fluorosis, idiopathic spots, decalcification, hypocalcification. **Contraindications:** dentin involvement, AI, DI, tetracycline, hypoplastic pits, thin cervical enamel (Sikri, 2020; Bartlett, 2011).

**Technique:** rubber dam, glasses → fluted rubber cup at 10:1 gear-reduced slow speed, short applications (20–30 s, Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; 60 s, Sikri, 2020) → rinse, inspect wet, repeat → fluoride prophy paste → topical fluoride (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Enamel loss — books differ:** 22–27 μm per application (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); about 360 ± 130 μm after about 100 s (Bartlett, 2011).

**Macroabrasion:** 12-fluted bur or fine diamond, high speed, water spray, then 30-fluted bur; faster but less controlled (Ritter et al, 2019).

Bleach first to spare fluoride-rich enamel (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

## 9. Resin infiltration

### Explanation

Sikri describes it under minimal intervention: "Resin infiltration is a microinvasive treatment regime for arresting early carious lesions." — a low-viscosity resin "marketed as ICON caries infiltrant", "first developed at the University of Kiel (Germany)"; "The infiltration procedure also provides optical blending of the carious lesion with the healthy enamel." Indications include white spot lesions, "Fluorosis stains (non-pitted)", amelogenesis imperfecta and "Molar-incisor hypomineralization". Kit: "The Etchant (acid) gel is composed of 15% hydrochloric acid"; "Drying agent is ethanol, which removes water retained in microporosities."; "The Infiltrant is composed of triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA)". The surface layer must be removed: "Etching with 15% HCl gel for 90–120 seconds was found to be more effective than 37% phosphoric acid gel". Sikri's table of white-spot types pairs trauma-related white patches with "Whitening, followed by resin infiltration" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Goldstein's, Sturdevant's and Summitt's chapters read for this answer do not describe resin infiltration for discoloration; it is included because Sikri does. [Certain about the sourcing; long-term colour stability of infiltrated lesions is not discussed in the book read.] Audit: *Modern Operative Dentistry* gives a different etch time — "the sole one able to sufficiently remove the surface layer is 15% hydrochloric acid, applied for 2 min"; in its case "the infiltrant was applied for 3 min"; and "The infiltration technique is indicated only to those lesions without cavitation" (Borges, Torres and Schlueter, 2020) [SRC]. Alani and Patel: "Acid etching of the white spot lesion followed by resin infiltration will stop lesion progression", used after vital bleaching (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. 90-120 s and 2 min are close; both are far longer than a phosphoric-acid etch [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** micro-invasive infiltration of a porous non-cavitated enamel lesion with low-viscosity resin (ICON, Kiel) (Sikri, 2020).

**Principle:** resin fills the light-scattering pores so the white lesion blends optically with sound enamel and is arrested.

**Indications:** white spot lesions, non-pitted fluorosis, mild MIH (Sikri, 2020).

**Steps:** rubber dam → 15% HCl gel 90–120 s (Sikri, 2020) or 2 min (Borges, Torres and Schlueter, 2020), better than phosphoric acid; non-cavitated lesions only → ethanol drying → TEGDMA infiltrant, light-cure, repeat → polish (Sikri, 2020). Whitening first, then infiltration.

**Advantages:** no drilling, no sensitivity, tissue preserved, lesion arrested. **Limitation:** pitted or deep defects still need composite (Sikri, 2020).

## 10. Bleaching agents and mechanism of action

### Explanation

Mechanism: Goldstein's — "the basic process almost certainly involves oxidation, during which the molecules causing the discoloration are released" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Sikri — "Bleaching implies degradation of high molecular weight, complex organic molecules" into smaller, less light-reflecting ones; hydrogen peroxide releases free radicals, "hydroxyl ions (OH–) and perhydroxyl ions (OOH–)" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. "Because hydrogen peroxide has such a low molecular weight, it easily passes through enamel and dentin." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Bartlett: "It is believed that most of the action of bleaching is on the dentine rather than the enamel" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Summitt's: "Carbamide peroxide penetrates through the enamel and the dentin to the pulp in 5 to 15 minutes." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Carbamide peroxide (CP) breakdown — three books, three figures: Summitt's "a solution of 10% carbamide peroxide agent breaks down into 6.5% urea and 3.5% hydrogen peroxide", which "bleaching formulations with carbamide peroxide typically are dosed at three times the concentration of those containing hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's "Carbamide peroxide degrades into 3% hydrogen peroxide (active ingredient) and 7% urea." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri "10% carbamide peroxide is equal to 3.0% hydrogen peroxide and 22% carbamide peroxide is equal to 7.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The difference (3.0 versus 3.5%) reflects rounding of the about one-third ratio; none is plainly wrong. [Likely] Urea raises pH: "raising the pH in the oral cavity above 8 for a number of hours" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Hydrogen peroxide gels for day use "range in concentration from 7.5% to 9.5% hydrogen peroxide" and "They are only active for 30 to 60 minutes", whereas "Carbamide peroxide is active for up to 10 hours, with about 50% of the active agent being used up in the first 2 hours." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Additives: "carbopol thickens the bleaching solution and extends the oxidation process" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri gives carbopol "in a concentration of 0.5 to 1.5%, is commonly used thickening agent", with glycerine carriers, preservatives, surfactants, flavours, and "Potassium nitrate and fluorides are added to decrease post-treatment sensitivity." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Other agents listed by Sikri: "0.5% chlorine dioxide when applied to enamel surface for 20 minutes" (roughens enamel), HCl — "The hydrochloric acid (18–36%) causes decalcification of tooth substance along with removal of stain." — McInnes solution ("5 parts of 36% HCl" with 30% H2O2 and ether), and nitrogen-doped TiO2 — "Titanium dioxide nanoparticle doped with nitrogen with less concentration of hydrogen peroxide" (3–10%); he also claims "hydrogen peroxide above 15% concentration does not increase bleaching effectiveness" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's is close: "the effect is not linear (ie, 20% is not twice as fast as 10%)", in-office "concentrations range from 15% to 50%" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Safety limits: "The Food and Drug Administration recognizes 3% hydrogen peroxide and 10% to 15% carbamide peroxide as GRAS", and the European committee's "The maximum recommended concentration for products is 6% hydrogen peroxide (which is approximately equivalent to 17% carbamide peroxide)." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sodium perborate belongs to non-vital (intracoronal) bleaching and is not covered here. Alani and Patel: "The most commonly utilised bleaching agent gel substances are 10% carbamide peroxide and 6% hydrogen peroxide."; "A solution of 10% carbamide peroxide results in approximately 3% hydrogen peroxide over a period of 4 h." (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Mechanism:** low-molecular-weight hydrogen peroxide diffuses through enamel into dentin (to the pulp in 5–15 min) and releases free radicals that oxidize large pigment molecules into smaller, less coloured ones (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Sikri, 2020; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Agents**
- Hydrogen peroxide: 30–35% in-office (range 15–50%); 7.5–9.5% day gels, active 30–60 min; 6–14% OTC strips.
- Carbamide peroxide (urea peroxide): 10–15% (up to 35–38%) nightguard; active up to 10 h, half used in 2 h.
- HCl 18–36%; 0.5% chlorine dioxide; N-doped TiO2 with low HP (Sikri, 2020).

**10% CP conversion — books differ:** 3.5% HP + 6.5% urea (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 3% HP + 7% urea (Ritter et al, 2019); 3.0% HP (Sikri, 2020). Urea raises pH above 8.

**Additives:** carbopol (prolongs release), glycerine, surfactants, preservatives, flavours, potassium nitrate and fluoride (Sikri, 2020).

**Concentration:** effect is not linear — 20% is not twice as fast as 10% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Limits: FDA GRAS 3% HP, 10–15% CP; EU maximum 6% HP for consumer products.

## 11. In-office (power) vital bleaching

### Explanation

Isolation is where the books disagree most clearly. Sturdevant's: "In-office vital bleaching requires an excellent rubber dam technique", and paint-on resin dams "cannot provide the same degree of protection and isolation as a conventionally applied rubber dam" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's: "The conventional rubber dam has been replaced by the use of cheek retractors and gingival protectors", with a light-cured resin barrier "to cover approximately 0.5 mm of the cervical area of the tooth and extend 2–3 mm onto the gingiva" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's takes the middle ground: rubber dam generally, but for one or a few teeth "it may be possible to protect the gingiva with cotton rolls and a so-called liquid rubber dam" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Both methods are in use; the choice depends on gel viscosity and number of teeth. [Likely]

Agent and protocol: Sturdevant's — gels "most commonly contain 30–35% hydrogen peroxide"; "Etching of teeth with 37% phosphoric acid, once considered a required part of this technique, is unnecessary."; "Bleaching treatments generally are rendered weekly for two to six treatments, with each treatment lasting 30–45 min." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's — "In-office bleaching of vital teeth generally uses a 35% hydrogen peroxide solution placed directly on the teeth"; "The teeth should not be anesthetized for in-office bleaching."; "Etching of the enamel is not indicated."; "Appointments for subsequent treatment are scheduled 1 week apart to allow sensitivity to abate."; "The average is three in-office sessions to reach maximum tooth" whiteness; results should be "evaluated after 1 to 2 weeks to allow rehydration of the teeth to a stable shade" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's — "For most bleaching, a 30–35% concentration of hydrogen peroxide is used.", covered with "Place a precut linear low-density polyethylene wrap (Saran wrap) onto the teeth to prevent evaporation", removed "Remove the wrap and the bleaching material after 40–60 min.", then "Finish the in-office bleaching session with a 2% neutral sodium fluoride gel for 5–10 min"; teeth "may appear chalky because of dehydration"; for tetracycline "stained teeth, three or more visits are generally required" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's disagrees on fluoride: "it is not essential to provide any fluoride treatment" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's thermocatalytic version: "The recommended temperature is 46°C to 60°C."; "Bleaching appointments are scheduled 2–4 weeks apart."; "A minimum of three treatments are usually sufficient; however, extra treatments can be carried out (not more than ten)."; "Instruct the patient to avoid coffee, tea and cola drinks for two weeks." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Interval therefore varies from 1 week (Sturdevant's, Summitt's) to 2–4 weeks (Sikri; Goldstein's "every 2–4 weeks" for tetracycline). [Certain that they differ]

Light and heat: Sturdevant's — "the addition of light during the bleaching procedure does not improve the whitening result beyond what the bleach alone can achieve"; heating "causes a greater level of tooth dehydration"; "Use of carbon dioxide laser to heat the bleaching mixture and accelerate the bleaching treatment has not been recommended" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: "Dehydration may give initial lighter appearance" and "It is important to remember that the hydrogen peroxide performs the bleaching, not the laser."; "An increase of 10°C doubles the rate of chemical reaction." but not necessarily the colour change (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri: "Use of carbon dioxide laser has been discontinued because of its thermal effect on the pulp.", but "Smartbleach is preferred choice for bleaching tetracycline-stained teeth." using the "wavelength of KTP laser (532 nm)" to photo-oxidize the tetracycline quinone (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] — a claim the other books do not make. [Guessing on its clinical superiority; not tested against another source here.] Goldstein's, in contrast, keeps heat/light as "a matter of preference" and notes in elderly patients "in most instances the pulp has shrunk back, making it possible to use higher bleaching temperatures", while in children "one should be especially careful to avoid irritation of the pulp, including not using heat" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. **Conflict — heat (audit).** Grossman's in-office protocol still heats: "The temperature should be maintained between 125 and 140°F (52–60°C)." and "The treatment time should not exceed 30 minutes even if the result is not satisfactory.", finishing with "Apply neutral sodium fluoride gel for 3–5 minutes." (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Andreasen's Ch.29 reports that light or heat "does not make teeth lighter than with bleach alone" once teeth rehydrate (Zachrisson and Toreskog, 2007), as do Sturdevant's and Summitt's. Alani and Patel use a blue light: "The gel is replenished in 3–4 cycles of 15 min." (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. The weight of the books read is against heat [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** quick result, poor tray compliance, selected or single teeth, jump-start (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Technique**
1. Prophylaxis, shade, photographs; no anaesthesia (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
2. Protect eyes, lips, soft tissue.
3. **Isolation — books differ:** heavy rubber dam (Ritter et al, 2019) versus cheek retractors and light-cured resin barrier over 0.5 mm of cervical enamel and 2–3 mm of gingiva (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
4. No etching (Ritter et al, 2019).
5. 30–35% HP gel, covered with plastic wrap, 40–60 min with renewals (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) or 30–45 min sessions (Ritter et al, 2019).
6. Suction, rinse, remove barrier.
7. Fluoride: 2% NaF 5–10 min (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) versus not essential (Ritter et al, 2019).
8. Repeat weekly, 2–6 sessions (average three) (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); Sikri: 46–60°C, 2–4 weeks apart; Grossman's: 52–60°C, at most 30 min (Gopikrishna, 2021). Judge shade after 1–2 weeks of rehydration.

**Lights and lasers** add dehydration and sensitivity, not whitening; CO2 laser not recommended (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); Sikri favours KTP for tetracycline. No heat in children (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Disadvantages:** cost, burns, sensitivity, unpredictable duration (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 12. Dentist-supervised nightguard (matrix) vital bleaching

### Explanation

History: Sturdevant's says the home technique "began around 1968, although it was not commonly known until the late 1980s" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Tray: Goldstein's cites Haywood that "the best prosthetic material is a 0.9 mm clear soft material"; Sturdevant's heats vinyl sheet until "the material sags approximately by 25 mm" and prefers scalloping: "This scalloped design is preferred because it allows the tray to cover only teeth" (Ritter et al, 2019; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's disagrees on scalloping and reservoirs: "Scalloping is not necessary when low concentrations of carbamide peroxide (10%) are used", and "there is little evidence that the creation of reservoirs actually improves bleaching efficacy" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Agent: "A 10%–15% carbamide peroxide bleaching material generally is recommended for this bleaching technique." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; "Most matrix bleaching uses 10–15% carbamide peroxide rather than the 35% hydrogen peroxide used in in-office procedures.", with three-tier products "beginning with a 5 or 6% solution", and "thicker gels generally work better than thinner ones" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt's: "the best combination of safety, limited side effects, and speed of action is obtained with a 10% solution", and "90% of the dentists surveyed used a 10% carbamide peroxide formulation" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Wear and duration: Sturdevant's "Total treatment time using an overnight approach is usually 1–2 weeks." and "It is recommended that only one arch be bleached at a time, beginning with the maxillary arch." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein's "The most common regimen is between 1 and 4 hours daily use from 4 weeks to 6 months (for tetracycline stain).", and success is "with 9 out of 10 patients experiencing a lightening of their teeth in 2–6 weeks" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Bartlett: "worn each night for 2–3 hours" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Sikri: "The daytime regimen requires one to three weeks; whereas, 4 to 6 weeks are required for" night-time use (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's Table 16-3: "Normal teeth 3 days to 6 weeks (average of 2 weeks) Nicotine-stained teeth 1 to 3 months Tetracycline-stained teeth 1 to 12 months (average of 3 to 4 months)" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21: "Typically, bleaching with 20% carbamide peroxide" in a tray "easily treats discolorations due to aging, smoking, or chromogenic foods and beverages"; "Nicotine staining of long-term duration may require as long as 3 months of nightly treatment."; "Tetracycline staining may take anywhere from 2 to 12 months of nightly treatment." (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Sturdevant's lists the disadvantages starting with "Reliance on patient compliance" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein's lists among contraindications "unwillingness to wear the appliance the necessary 1–3 h per day" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Restorations: "Polymethyl methacrylate restorations exhibit a yellow-orange discolouration on exposure to carbamide peroxide." so temporaries should be bis-acryl (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's states that overall "overnight tray bleaching produces the best results" and that 10% CP trays "are generally the safest, most cost-effective, best-researched whitening treatments available" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. **Conflict — tray margins and reservoirs (audit).** Goldstein's: "The tray is trimmed with sharp scissors approximately 0.5 mm away from the gingival margin" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Grossman's: "For highly viscous material, the margins can be scalloped, terminating just incisal to the free gingival margin."; "If the bleaching agent is nonviscous, then the margins have to be nonscalloped and extending on to the gingival tissues."; "The reservoir should terminate 1 mm short of the free gingival margin." (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Alani and Patel extend over the gingiva — "the tray should extend just over the visible gingival margin by approximately 1 mm" — because "The cervical area of a tooth is notoriously the most difficult area to bleach"; their tray material's "general minimum thickness should be 1 mm", and "there is no definitive evidence to show that the presence of bleach reservoirs (spacers) is more or less beneficial" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], agreeing with Summitt's. The choice follows gel viscosity and gingival tolerance [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Agent:** 10–15% carbamide peroxide with carbopol (Ritter et al, 2019; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); 10% gives the best balance of safety and speed (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Day alternative: 7.5–9.5% HP for 30–60 min. Tiered kits start at 5 or 6% and step up to limit sensitivity; thicker gels work better than thin ones (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Steps**
1. Examination, radiographs, prophylaxis, shade, photographs.
2. Alginate impression, thin cast, optional 0.5 mm reservoir.
3. Vacuum-form soft 0.9 mm sheet (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); margin — books differ: scalloped short of the gingiva (Ritter et al, 2019), 0.5 mm from it (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018), about 1 mm over it (Alani and Patel, 2025), or by gel viscosity (Gopikrishna, 2021); scalloping unnecessary with 10% CP (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
4. Try-in: fit, blanching, occlusion.
5. Instruct: small drop per tooth facially, wipe excess, wear overnight or 2–4 h by day; one arch at a time, maxillary first.
6. Recall at 1–2 weeks.

**Duration:** normal teeth 3 days–6 weeks (average 2 weeks); nicotine 1–3 months; tetracycline 1–12 months (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 9 of 10 lighten in 2–6 weeks (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Advantages:** low concentration, few side effects, low cost, tray reusable for touch-up. **Disadvantages:** compliance, time, PMMA temporaries turn yellow-orange, so provisional crowns should be bis-acryl (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 13. Over-the-counter products and combination bleaching

### Explanation

Summitt's: "The three types of bleaching therapy are in-office bleaching, at-home bleaching, and over-the-counter products."; "The fundamental problem with OTC products is that there is no examination and diagnosis of the cause of discoloration."; strips — "One whitening system utilizes adhesive strips containing a relatively high concentration (approximately 6% to 14%) of hydrogen peroxide." — "wear the strips for 30 minutes twice a day"; comparison: "To obtain a six-shade color change on a VITA shade guide required 7 nights of tray bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide, 16 days of a 6% hydrogen peroxide strip (OTC) worn twice a day, or 3 in-office treatments of 38% hydrogen peroxide."; conclusion "OTC nondentist bleaching does not have a good risk-benefit ratio" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett: "there are always dangers with patient-applied products that are not prescribed and supervised by a dentist" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Goldstein's describes the FDA history — "In 1991, the FDA ruled that the use of carbamide peroxide in the form advocated for home bleaching constituted a new drug use" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri lists "Polyethylene strips impregnated with 5.25% of H2O2 is also used without tray." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

On combination therapy the books conflict directly. Goldstein's: one in-office session plus home matrix "provides the most effective result seen to date" and "provides the most predictable of all the bleaching techniques" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Sturdevant's: "a combination of the in-office technique and the dentist-prescribed, home-applied technique has better results than either technique used alone" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: "One study found that in-office bleaching combined with at-home bleaching does not improve tooth whitening compared with at-home bleaching alone." and Table 16-1 says the final outcome is the same, the in-office part being only a paid "boost" — although the same chapter also recommends that in-office "bleaching be followed by at-home bleaching to enhance the effect and to minimize rebound" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett: "the result from both techniques is likely to be the same" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Reconciliation: combination gives a faster start and motivates the patient; the end point after long enough treatment is similar. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**OTC products:** strips (6–14% HP, 30 min twice daily), paint-ons, trays (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Problem: no diagnosis — may mask necrosis, resorption or caries; restorations ignored; strips may not reach past canines (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bartlett, 2011).
- Efficacy: a six-shade change took 7 nights of 10% CP tray, 16 days of 6% HP strips, or 3 office visits; overall, overnight tray bleaching gives the best results (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Regulation: the FDA in 1991 classed home carbamide peroxide as a new drug use, forcing manufacturers to prove safety and efficacy (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Combination (power + matrix) bleaching:** one in-office session then home trays.
- Most effective and predictable (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); better than either alone (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Same end point as trays alone; office session is only a faster start (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bartlett, 2011).
- Conclusion: it buys speed and motivation (useful in second-degree tetracycline), not a whiter end point.

## 14. Side effects of vital bleaching and their management

### Explanation

"Tooth sensitivity during bleaching is the most prevalent side effect to treatment"; passive treatment shortens or skips wear; active treatment — "The mechanism of action of fluoride is as a tubular blocker." — or "use of 5% or less concentrations of potassium nitrate applied in the bleaching tray", which acts to "chemically depolarize the nerve to inhibit refiring" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's: "Double-blind studies have reported sensitivity in 25% to 70% of patients." (placebo 20–30%); "the greatest sensitivity seems to occur in the first 2 weeks of treatment"; "the pulp remains healthy and the sensitivity is completely reversible"; "A more direct treatment is the application of 3% to 5% potassium nitrate gel in the tray."; for patients with maximum sensitivity Box 16-10 advises brushing with a potassium nitrate toothpaste "for 2 weeks prior to initiating bleaching" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett attributes sensitivity to low pH: "as the bleach has a low pH and opens up dentinal tubules" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], whereas Summitt's notes CP raises pH and says "Bleaching sensitivity is not directly related to tubule blockage" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Enamel: "a slight loss in surface hardness to a depth of approximately 25 μm" from low-pH solutions, quickly remineralized (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Restorations: "Bleaching has no effect on porcelain."; "It does encourage the release of mercury from some types of amalgam restorations." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; restorations do not lighten — "existing restorations tend to appear darker as the adjacent teeth lighten" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Bond strength: see Section 19. **Conflict — porcelain (audit).** Grossman's: "Bleaching agents are known to cause etching and weakening of ceramic prosthesis." (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], against Summitt's no effect on porcelain; Goldstein's found roughening of all tooth-coloured materials, least on porcelain (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Covering crowns out of the tray, as Grossman's advises, costs nothing [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Side effect | Management |
|---|---|
| Tooth sensitivity — commonest, 25–70%, worst in first 2 weeks, reversible (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) | Shorter/skipped wear; 3–5% potassium nitrate in tray; neutral fluoride; analgesics (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) |
| Gingival irritation, chemical burn | Trim tray; stop, rinse; baking soda or ZOE |
| Enamel softening about 25 μm | Remineralizes; neutral gels |
| Restorations: none on porcelain (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013), but etched and weakened per Grossman's (Gopikrishna, 2021); PMMA yellows; mercury release from amalgam | Replace mismatching restorations later (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019) |
| Reduced bond strength | Delay bonding (Section 19) |
| Relapse | Touch-up |

Potassium nitrate depolarizes the nerve and blocks refiring; fluoride acts as a tubule blocker (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Patients with sensitive teeth can brush with a potassium nitrate toothpaste for 2 weeks before starting. Low peroxide concentrations are systemically safe (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 15. Direct composite bonding and composite veneers

### Explanation

Goldstein's: "bonding remains the single fastest transformation of the mouth available to you as a dentist"; "the quickest and most economic method of obtaining an esthetic tooth transformation is through the direct resin labial veneer"; "The best candidate for the direct resin veneer is the monochromatic shaded tooth"; "Extremely dark tetracycline-stained teeth provide the most difficult challenge for direct resin full-veneer bonding.", handled by first applying "first mask the tetracycline discoloration with multiple layers of opaque", then tints and body shades (Goldstein and Vargas, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: "A veneer is a layer of tooth-coloured material that is applied to a tooth to restore localized or generalized defects and intrinsic discolourations"; "Partial veneers are indicated for the restoration of localized defects or areas of intrinsic discolouration"; direct full veneers suit cases "For cases involving young children or a single discoloured tooth" or limited budget; "Indirect veneers typically last much longer than do direct veneers". Intra-enamel preparation is recommended, among other reasons "To remove the outer, fluoride-rich layer of enamel that may be more resistant to acid etching"; "A window preparation is recommended for most direct composite veneers.", depth "ranging from approximately 0.5–0.75 mm midfacially and tapering down to a depth of about 0.3–0.5 mm along the gingival margin"; subgingival margins only for caries or "or if it involves significantly dark discolouration that presents a difficult esthetic problem"; "Discoloured mandibular anterior teeth are rarely indicated for veneers". For partial veneers "Microfills are excellent" enamel replacements, and for residual light stain "an intrinsically less translucent composite can be used rather than extending the preparation into dentin". Colour modifiers: "a violet colour modifier (the complementary colour of yellow)" tones down yellow calcific metamorphosis; but "Although opaque resin media can mask the underlying stain, esthetic vitality is usually lost because of reduced light penetration" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Fahl and Ritter: "Composites tend to need thicker opaquing layers to mask the discoloration", so "In general, the darker the tooth, the more preparation is needed." and "Any attempt to mask a dark tooth without adequate preparation will invariably result in a lifeless and often overcontoured veneer." (Fahl and Ritter, n.d.) [SRC]; "Opaquers are low-viscosity light-activated resins used for masking dark substrates", and "It is a common misconception that an opaquer must be 100% opaque" — "An opaquer that presents extremely high opacity may render a restoration lifeless and dull" (Fahl and Ritter, n.d.) [SRC]. Summitt's suggests opaque A2 under translucent enamel for unremoved white spots, and "pink composite may be helpful to neutralize remaining gray on the tooth from amalgam stains or severe" tetracycline banding (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21 illustrates microabrasion first, after which "Direct composite bonding masked the remainder of the white spots" (Figure 21.18) (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** localized spots and hypoplasia (partial veneers); mild–moderate generalized stain, single discoloured tooth, young patients, limited budget (full direct veneers) (Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein and Vargas, 2018).

**Preparation:** intra-enamel to avoid overcontour, remove fluoride-rich enamel, roughen and give a finish line; window design; 0.5–0.75 mm midfacially, 0.3–0.5 mm cervically; supragingival unless the cervical area is dark (Ritter et al, 2019). The darker the tooth, the deeper the preparation (Fahl and Ritter, n.d.).

**Technique:** shade on hydrated teeth, isolate, etch, bond → opaquer in thin layers → tints (violet against yellow, pink against grey) → dentin and enamel shades → finish, polish (Goldstein and Vargas, 2018; Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Opaquers** need not be 100% opaque; excess opacity gives a lifeless restoration (Fahl and Ritter, n.d.).

**Advantages:** one visit, little reduction, repairable, cheap. **Disadvantages:** staining, wear, shorter life; very dark tetracycline teeth are hardest to mask (Goldstein and Vargas, 2018).

## 16. Porcelain laminate veneers

### Explanation

Goldstein's Ch.15 places discoloration first in its indications — "Type I: moderate tooth discolorations/color corrections" (tetracycline, fluorosis, amelogenesis imperfecta) — and lists "Moderate discolorations, such as tetracycline staining, fluorosis, devitalized teeth, and teeth darkened by age which are not conducive to vital bleaching"; advantages include that "the highly glazed porcelain surface is very resistant to stain accumulation"; disadvantages "The color cannot be easily modified once bonded in position." and "Irreversibility of preparation versus little or no preparation for direct composite resin bonding."; contraindications include "Patients with certain tooth-to-tooth habits such as bruxism", insufficient enamel ("Ideally, enamel should be around the whole periphery of the veneer"), edge-to-edge bites, and "teeth that have been excessively fluoridated may not etch effectively"; for deep tetracycline or amalgam staining "the use of more opaque high-strength ceramics such as lithium disilicate" is indicated (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: "The preferred type of indirect veneer is the etched porcelain (i.e. feldspathic) veneer."; "Preparations into dentin should be avoided"; the depth gauge is a round-ended diamond — "A diamond with a tip diameter of 1–1.2 mm is recommended." — facial reduction in three zones "until the desired depth of approximately 0.6 mm is attained", incisal reduction "a minimum reduction of 1 mm or, more desirably, 1.5 mm". For darkly discoloured teeth: "Typically, 5%–15% opaque porcelain is required to achieve optimal masking.", but "Exceeding 15% opaque porcelain dramatically reduces light penetration"; "a slightly deeper tooth preparation can be used to allow greater veneer thickness" (still in enamel); "several coats of a die-spacing medium" for thicker resin cement; patients are told veneers "they may not entirely eliminate or mask extremely dark stains" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Conflict on pressed ceramics: Sturdevant's — "Because of the more translucent nature of pressed ceramic veneers, however, dark discolourations are best treated with etched porcelain veneers." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein's — lithium disilicate is indicated for severe staining, and "masking can be achieved with less amount of tooth structure removal" than feldspathic, provided one records the stump shade ("it is still essential to take the stump shade of the prepared teeth") (Stappert et al, 2018; Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. The difference partly reflects material generation: Sturdevant's discusses translucent pressed glass ceramics (IPS Empress) and notes newer pressed ceramics can be made thinner; Goldstein's discusses opaque high-strength lithium disilicate ingots. [Likely] Goldstein's Ch.21 also mentions "Some suggest the use of an opaque resin to mask the discolorations prior to the cementation of the porcelain veneers." (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Longevity: "an etched porcelain veneer should last 10–15 years or longer" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Darkening under veneers can be corrected: "Both vital and endodontically treated teeth under veneers may darken over time." (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; "the natural teeth can be bleached under the veneers from the lingual" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. *Modern Operative Dentistry* states the rule outright: "dental bleaching should always be the first treatment choice in those cases", veneers only for teeth that do not respond (Huhtala et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** moderate discoloration not responding to bleaching — tetracycline, fluorosis with defects, AI, aged teeth, amalgam shine-through (Stappert et al, 2018).
**Contraindications:** bruxism, insufficient enamel, edge-to-edge bite; heavily fluorosed enamel etches poorly (Stappert et al, 2018).

**Preparation (intra-enamel):** 1–1.2 mm round-ended diamond as depth gauge; facial reduction in three zones to about 0.6 mm (0.3–0.5 mm cervically); incisal 1–1.5 mm (butt-joint or lapping) or window; avoid dentin (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Dark teeth:** 5–15% opaque porcelain (above 15% loses vitality); slightly deeper enamel preparation; die spacer for thicker opaque cement; bleach first (Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein et al, 2018a).

**Material — books differ:** feldspathic preferred, pressed ceramic too translucent for dark stain (Ritter et al, 2019); opaque lithium disilicate masks with less reduction, stump shade to the laboratory (Stappert et al, 2018; Goldstein et al, 2018a).

**Bonding:** HF-etched, silanated porcelain; etched enamel; light-cured resin cement.

**Pros:** best aesthetics, stain resistance, 10–15 years. **Cons:** irreversible, colour fixed after cementation, cost (Stappert et al, 2018). Teeth darkening under veneers can be bleached from the lingual (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 17. Crowns

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: "Full porcelain coverage with all-ceramic crowns may be indicated in some patients with severe discolouration" because of greater capacity to restore vitality, though many patients refuse cutting down (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Bartlett: "Extensively restored teeth or severely discoloured teeth (Figure 5.21) are more appropriately treated with crowns" — here phrased "are more appropriately treated with crowns" — and in AI/DI "early intervention with multiple crowns is often required" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Goldstein's: for severe drug-induced and congenital defects "full crown coverage may be necessary to improve the appearance of the teeth" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; "Bleaching is usually a less appropriate treatment than bonding, veneering, or crowning for these problems involving the structure of enamel." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. When crowns are placed on bleached teeth Goldstein's advises: if the shade must be taken early, "choose a lighter shade, and be prepared to darken the crown if necessary" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** severe discoloration veneers cannot mask; extensively restored or weakened teeth; severe AI/DI with wear; gross fluorotic deformity (Ritter et al, 2019; Bartlett, 2011).

**Choice:** all-ceramic crowns restore vitality better on very dark teeth than thin veneers with opaque layers (Ritter et al, 2019); opaque high-strength cores (lithium disilicate, zirconia) block deep tetracycline or amalgam stain (Stappert et al, 2018); metal-ceramic where strength is paramount.

**Drawbacks:** most tooth removal, pulpal risk, cost — last resort for vital teeth.

**Shade:** bleach adjacent teeth first; if the shade must be taken early, choose lighter and darken later (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

## 18. Selection of treatment by cause and severity

### Explanation

Response to bleaching depends on colour: "Teeth exhibiting yellow or orange intrinsic discolouration seem to respond best to vital bleaching"; "Brown fluorosis stains also are often responsive to treatment, but white fluorosis stains may not be resolved effectively" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's ranks "teeth discolored by age, genetics, smoking, or coffee are the fastest to respond", "In general, tetracycline-stained teeth are the slowest to respond to bleaching", and "the yellow discoloration of aging responds quickly to bleaching in most cases"; "White spots are not removed by bleaching but may be less noticeable when the remainder of the tooth is lighter."; "Bleaching has been estimated to remove 80% of brown discolorations." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21: "It is estimated that 80% of these brown discolorations are amenable to bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide." with "with no return or need for additional treatment at seven years recall"; for white spots "Bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide is still the first treatment of choice", noting that "Teeth with white spots undergoing bleaching often develop a" splotchy look early on (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Tetracycline: "In-office bleaching is a possible treatment method but is generally contraindicated due to the number of treatments required"; "Treatment times may vary from 2 months to 1 year"; patients should "agree to a minimum of 2 months of nightly treatment before deciding to proceed to more aggressive treatment"; "Dark tetracycline stains located in the gingival third of the tooth or dark blue or gray stains have the least favorable prognosis." (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Note Ch.21 (home bleaching preferred for tetracycline) and Ch.12 (combined office/home preferred for second degree) differ in emphasis within the same book. [Certain] Sturdevant's: "Dark blue-grey, tetracycline-stained teeth are considerably more difficult to treat than are teeth with mild yellow-orange discolourations."; "Teeth stained with tetracycline require prolonged treatment durations of several months before any results are observed."; "Tetracycline-stained teeth may approach but never seem to achieve the appearance of normal teeth."; "some clinicians advocate intentional endodontic therapy along with the use of an intra-coronal nonvital bleaching technique", which carries endodontic risks (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: "some tetracycline-stained teeth may require 2 to 12 months of daily treatment"; "Teeth heavily stained in the gingival third have the poorest prognosis for complete lightening."; a cheaper option is to "bleach tetracycline-stained teeth using 6.5% hydrogen peroxide strips" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.12: with dark tetracycline stain "the majority of the bleaching will occur on the incisal one-half of the teeth" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri contraindicates bleaching for "Deep tetracycline stains" and "Severe discoloration from amalgam corrosion" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Calcific metamorphosis: the tooth is a poor endodontic candidate, and "External bleaching of the single dark tooth is best performed with tray application of the bleaching agent." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett's Table 5.1 of stains successfully bleached begins with "Mild generalized staining" and includes age-related yellowing, tobacco, diet, pulpal trauma, and mild forms of fluorosis, tetracycline and amelogenesis imperfecta (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. **Conflicts on prognosis (audit).** Brown: Grossman's — "Destaining of the yellow color is most successful, whereas brownish teeth are least successfully bleached." (Zachrisson and Toreskog, 2007) [SRC]. Grossman's statement concerns brown tetracycline stain, not brown fluorosis, which Goldstein's finds responsive [Likely]. ## 19. Combination therapy: timing and sequencing

### Explanation

Bond strength after bleaching: Sturdevant's — "This reduction in bond strength results from residual oxygen or peroxide residue in the tooth" and "No loss of bond strength is noted if the composite restorative treatment is delayed at least 1 week after cessation of any bleaching." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's — "A 25% to 50% reduction in bond strength of composite to etched enamel"; "a delay of 7 to 10 days after bleaching allows dissipation of the excess oxygen"; another study found "there is a need to wait at least 2 weeks for maximum bond strength"; "Placement of the restoration should be delayed at least 1 to 2 weeks"; and "A drying agent, such as acetone, can be used to diminish the oxygen in the outer layers of the tooth" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's — "restorative procedures should be postponed to 2–3 weeks after the last bleaching"; when combining, "for in-office bleaching alone, it is about 2 weeks but if at-home treatments are included then add an additional 4 weeks" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Bartlett — "most practitioners recommend a few days between completion of bleaching and any restorative work involving bonding" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. So the recommended delay ranges from "a few days" (Bartlett) through 1 week (Sturdevant's), 1–2 weeks (Summitt's), to 2–3 weeks (Goldstein's); Goldstein's also builds in shade stabilization, which explains its longer wait. [Likely] Audit additions: Grossman's, after in-office bleaching, "Any bonded restoration on bleached surfaces must be postponed by 7–10 days" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's, "Clinicians are generally advised to wait for 2 to 3 weeks prior to placing a definitive adhesive restoration" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; *Modern Operative Dentistry*, "A washout phase of minimum 2 weeks should be observed after the bleaching" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; Alani and Patel — bond strength is lower "by up to 60% if the bonding procedure is carried out immediately after tooth bleaching treatment" and restorations "should be delayed for at least 2–4 weeks after the bleaching treatment to allow for optimal adhesive bonding strength" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. The newest books lengthen the wait. Sequencing: "bleaching should be performed before any esthetic restorative procedures" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; "It is generally preferred to bleach teeth before orthodontia is initiated" because bonded resin hinders bleaching (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; restorations "tend to appear darker as the adjacent teeth lighten" and need replacement afterwards (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sequence:** disease control and periodontal therapy → bleach before orthodontic brackets (or after resin removal) (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) → bleaching → microabrasion/infiltration → wait → restorations matched to the final shade (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Delay before bonding — books differ:** a few days (Bartlett, 2011); at least 1 week (Ritter et al, 2019); 7–10 days, 2 weeks for maximum bond (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 2–3 weeks, about 2 weeks after in-office plus 4 weeks if home bleaching (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); at least 2 weeks (Torres et al, 2020); 2–4 weeks (Alani and Patel, 2025).

**Reasons:** residual oxygen inhibits resin polymerization (25–50% loss of enamel bond) and the shade must stabilize (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

Warn that existing restorations will look darker and may need replacing (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

## 20. Prognosis, maintenance and conclusion

### Explanation

Longevity differs between books: Sturdevant's — "Vital bleaching usually results in tooth lightening for only 1–3 years, whereas an etched porcelain veneer should last 10–15 years or longer." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Bartlett — "teeth that have bleached well can remain stable for 2–3 years" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Goldstein's — "Generally it may be 3 years before retreatment is desirable." and an annual touch-up "bleach usually will be recommended for the removal of any new accumulated stain" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt's for tetracycline — "8 in 10 patients can expect to retain that lightening for at least 1 year" and "a very high patient satisfaction rate at 7.5 years after completion of bleaching" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21 brown-fluorosis case stayed stable at seven years (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Maintenance: the tray can be reused; a peroxide toothpaste "may be useful in color maintenance after the dentist has whitened the teeth" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Summitt's summary: "OTC nondentist bleaching does not have a good risk-benefit ratio" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prognosis:** best for extrinsic, ageing, yellow/brown, mild fluorosis, first-degree tetracycline; poor for bleaching alone in banded or cervical tetracycline, white spots, deep defects, AI/DI and metallic stains (Ritter et al, 2019; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Books differ on brown (bleaches well: Ritter et al, 2019; least well in tetracycline: Gopikrishna, 2021) and on older teeth (excellent candidates: Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; harder to lighten: Alani and Patel, 2025).

**Longevity — books differ:** bleaching 1–3 years (Ritter et al, 2019), 2–3 years (Bartlett, 2011), about 3 years before retreatment (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); 8 in 10 tetracycline patients keep the result at least 1 year and satisfaction remains high at 7.5 years (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Porcelain veneers 10–15 years (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Maintenance:** keep the tray for touch-ups; peroxide or low-abrasive toothpaste; limit tea, coffee, tobacco; polish veneer margins, no air-polishing or stannous fluoride on porcelain; night guard for bruxists; recall radiographs of traumatized teeth (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Stappert et al, 2018).

**Conclusion.** Rehabilitation begins with diagnosis and the patient's expectation and proceeds from prophylaxis, microabrasion, infiltration and bleaching to bonding, veneers and crowns only as required. Bleaching is almost always first, either as definitive treatment or to lighten the substrate for thinner restorations (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Ritter et al, 2019).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: combined in-office plus home bleaching gives a better result than home bleaching alone.**
- **Textbook position:** Goldstein's calls it the most effective and most predictable technique (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); Sturdevant's says it beats either alone (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Challenge:** Summitt's cites a study showing no improvement over home bleaching alone and states the end point is the same; Bartlett agrees (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bartlett, 2011).
- **Refinement:** the office session produces quick visible change (partly dehydration) that motivates compliance; the tooth's own limit, not the technique, sets the final shade.
- **Strength:** clinical comparisons cited second-hand in Summitt's; the advocacy in Goldstein's is largely experience-based. [Likely]
- **Conclusion:** combination buys speed, not a whiter end point. [Likely]

**Claim 2: lights and lasers enhance vital bleaching.**
- **Textbook position:** Goldstein's treats activation as a matter of preference; Sikri describes many light systems and prefers the KTP laser for tetracycline (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Sikri, 2020).
- **Challenge:** Sturdevant's and Summitt's state light does not improve whitening, increases dehydration and sensitivity, and CO2 lasers are not recommended (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- **Conclusion:** the peroxide bleaches; light mainly heats and dehydrates. The KTP claim rests on one textbook. [Likely]

**Claim 3: microabrasion removes a negligible amount of enamel.**
- **Textbook position:** Sikri — clinically unrecognizable loss; Summitt's — about 22–27 μm weakened.
- **Challenge:** Bartlett (Tong et al, 1993) — about 360 ± 130 μm after about 100 s with 18% HCl.
- **Conclusion:** loss is cumulative with time and acid strength; short applications of lower-concentration HCl with silicon carbide are safer, and bleaching should come first to spare fluoride-rich enamel. [Likely]

**Claim 4: pressed ceramics are unsuitable for dark stains.**
- Sturdevant's (translucent pressed ceramic) versus Goldstein's (opaque lithium disilicate masks with less reduction). The disagreement is about different materials. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Sequence: prophylaxis → micro/macroabrasion → resin infiltration → bleaching → composite → porcelain veneer → crown. Bleach first, except soft-tissue care.
- Jordan and Boksman (1984) tetracycline degrees I–III (+IV); banding and grey-blue cervical stain = poor prognosis; 2–12 months nightguard bleaching.
- 10% CP ≈ 3–3.5% HP + about 7% urea (books differ); CP ≈ three times HP.
- In-office: 30–35% HP, no etch, no anaesthesia; rubber dam (Sturdevant's) versus resin barrier 0.5 mm cervical / 2–3 mm gingiva (Goldstein's).
- Nightguard: 10–15% CP, scalloped tray, one arch at a time; normal teeth average 2 weeks.
- Microabrasion: 18% HCl + pumice (McCloskey 1984, Croll 1986) or about 11% HCl + SiC (Prema); limit 0.2–0.3 mm.
- ICON: 15% HCl 90–120 s, ethanol, TEGDMA.
- Bonding delay: a few days / 1 week / 1–2 weeks / 2–3 weeks (books differ).
- Opaque porcelain 5–15%; above 15% loses vitality.
- Bleaching lasts 1–3 years; porcelain veneers 10–15 years.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Jumping to veneers or crowns without diagnosis, prophylaxis and a trial of bleaching.
- Forgetting the radiograph and pulp test of a single dark tooth.
- Saying bleaching removes white spots (it only reduces contrast).
- Quoting one figure for carbamide-peroxide conversion or microabrasion depth as if all books agree.
- Bonding immediately after bleaching (oxygen inhibition, unstable shade).
- Prescribing in-office bleaching with heat for children.
- Treating sodium perborate as a vital bleaching agent (it is intracoronal, for non-vital teeth).
- Omitting maintenance and the need to replace existing restorations after bleaching.
