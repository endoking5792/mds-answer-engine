## 1. Introduction, definitions and the conservative principle

### Explanation

Sturdevant's defines bleaching as "The lightening of the colour of a tooth through the application of a chemical agent to oxidize the organic pigmentation in the tooth is referred to as bleaching." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri gives the formal definition "the treatment, usually involving an oxidative chemical that alters the light absorbing and/or light reflecting nature of the material structure, thereby increasing its value (whiteness)" and separates the terms: "Whitening restores teeth to their natural tooth color" by removing surface stains, while bleaching makes teeth lighter than their natural colour (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's notes that the distinction is loose: "cosmetic products typically use the word whitening for the removal of surface stains" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's lists the options for discoloured anterior teeth: "Treatment options include removal of surface stains, bleaching, micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion, veneering and placement of porcelain crowns." and observes that "increasing numbers of patients do not want their teeth" cut down for crowns (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Its governing principle is that "Mild discolourations are best left untreated, are bleached or are treated conservatively with micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion because no restorative material is as good as the healthy, natural tooth structure." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's chapter summary puts the same rule as a duty to "select the most conservative treatment option for the specific stain", after "the first step in the treatment of a patient whose chief complaint is stains or discolorations is the diagnosis of the cause" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. "Aesthetic rehabilitation" is therefore read here as the whole diagnostic and treatment sequence, not only restorations. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Discoloured anterior teeth are one of the commonest reasons patients seek aesthetic care. In a vital tooth the pulp is healthy, so treatment must improve colour without harming the pulp, while preserving as much enamel as possible.

**Definitions**
- **Bleaching:** lightening of tooth colour by a chemical agent that oxidizes the organic pigments in the tooth (Ritter et al, 2019); an oxidative treatment that alters the light-absorbing/reflecting nature of the tooth and increases its value (Sikri, 2020).
- **Whitening:** restores the natural colour by removing surface stains; bleaching makes the tooth lighter than its natural colour (Sikri, 2020).
- **Aesthetic rehabilitation** of vital discoloured teeth = diagnosis of the cause, then the least invasive procedure that meets the patient's expectation, escalating only when needed.

**Guiding principle.** The first step is diagnosis of the cause; then the most conservative option for that stain is chosen, while preparing the patient for further treatment if it fails (Goldstein et al, 2018). Mild discolourations are best left untreated, bleached, or treated by micro/macro-abrasion, because no restorative material is as good as healthy natural tooth structure (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Options (conservative → invasive):** prophylaxis and stain removal → microabrasion / macroabrasion → resin infiltration → vital bleaching (in-office, nightguard, OTC, combined) → direct composite bonding/veneers → porcelain laminate veneers → crowns (Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein et al, 2018).

## 2. Classification and causes of discoloration in vital teeth

### Explanation

Goldstein's Ch.12: "Extrinsic discoloration is caused by the accumulation of stains on the enamel surface", aggravated by enamel pits, saliva and poor hygiene; "Nicotine stains start as tenacious extrinsic stains, but over time absorb into the tooth and become an intrinsic stain"; and "The most common drugs causing extrinsic discoloration include chlorhexidine", iron salts, essential oils and co-amoxiclav (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Ch.21 gives the Nathoo classification: "N1-type colored material (chromogen) binds to the tooth surface" (tea, coffee, wine, chromogenic bacteria, metals); "N2-type colored material changes color after binding to the tooth"; "N3-type colorless material or prechromogen binds to the tooth and undergoes a chemical reaction to cause a stain" (carbohydrates, stannous fluoride, chlorhexidine); and "The most common cause of extrinsic stains is poor oral hygiene." (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's notes that in the young, stains of any colour are "more prominent in the cervical areas of teeth" (Nasmyth membrane, plaque, chromogenic bacteria), while "In older patients, stains on the surfaces of teeth are more likely to be brown, black or grey" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri uses three groups — "broadly divided into three categories, viz. intrinsic, extrinsic and internalized stains" — where "Internalized discoloration occurs due to incorporation of extrinsic stains within the tooth substance" through enamel defects, wear, recession or caries (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's gives the clinically useful corollary: "Extrinsic stains are located on the surface of the tooth and are most easily removed by external cleaning." while "Intrinsic stains are located within the tooth and are accessible only by bleaching." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's lists the developmental causes in vital teeth as hereditary disorders, "medications (particularly tetracycline preparations)", excess fluoride, high fevers and trauma (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; ageing yellows teeth because "continuing deposition of secondary dentin occurs in older individuals" while enamel thins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and Goldstein's: "The combination of thinned enamel and darkened dentin creates an older-looking tooth." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Bartlett adds that the change "is believed to be caused by the gradual thinning of enamel from tooth wear", and that the canine naturally has "a higher saturation of the colour yellow than other incisor teeth", which some patients wrongly see as a defect (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Trauma to a vital tooth can lead to calcific metamorphosis — "Calcific metamorphosis is the process by which the tooth deposits darker yellow secondary dentin in the pulp chamber" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] — which Sturdevant's calls "extremely difficult to treat" ("This condition is extremely difficult to treat") (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Systemic pigments (bilirubin in jaundice, erythroblastosis fetalis, porphyria) and restorations also discolour vital teeth; for amalgam, Goldstein's says it "should be replaced with less visible materials such as composite resin before bleaching" and warns "Metallic stains are the most difficult to remove." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**
1. **Extrinsic** — on the enamel surface (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Nathoo classification (Goldstein et al, 2018):
   - N1: coloured chromogen binds to the tooth (tea, coffee, wine, chromogenic bacteria, metals).
   - N2: chromogen changes colour after binding (food stains darkening with time).
   - N3: colourless pre-chromogen binds and reacts to form a stain (chlorhexidine, stannous fluoride, carbohydrates).
   - Commonest cause: poor oral hygiene. In children, stains are cervical (Nasmyth membrane, green/orange stains); in older patients brown, black or grey (Ritter et al, 2019).
2. **Intrinsic** — within enamel or dentin; reachable only by bleaching or by removing/covering the tissue (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
3. **Internalized** — extrinsic stain incorporated into enamel defects, worn or exposed dentin (Sikri, 2020). Nicotine stain starts extrinsic and becomes intrinsic (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Causes of intrinsic discoloration in vital anterior teeth**
- *Developmental (pre-eruptive):* fluorosis, tetracycline/minocycline, enamel hypoplasia and hypocalcification, amelogenesis and dentinogenesis imperfecta, high fever, systemic pigments (bilirubin, erythroblastosis fetalis, porphyria) (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
- *Acquired (post-eruptive):* ageing (enamel thinning plus darker secondary dentin) (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Bartlett, 2011); trauma with blood pigment or calcific metamorphosis (darker yellow secondary dentin in the pulp chamber) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); caries, leaking or corroding restorations, amalgam shine-through (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); microcracks.

## 3. Fluorosis, tetracycline and other developmental discolorations

### Explanation

**Fluorosis.** "Dental fluorosis is the most common cause of intrinsic discoloration"; histologically it shows "a hypomineralized, porous subsurface enamel below a well-mineralized surface layer"; "Bleaching is a good indication for fluorosis with brown pigmentation on a smooth enamel surface", but "Localized white spots frequently seen in fluorosis have an unpredictable prognosis", so with white spots or surface defects "bleaching should be performed as an adjunctive treatment prior to esthetic restorative treatments including bonding or veneering" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21 grades it: "Mild fluorosis is seen as flat gray or white flecks on the enamel surface.", moderate is chalky with pitting, and "Severe fluorosis causes marked tooth deformity"; it distinguishes fluoride from non-fluoride opacities: "Enamel opacities due to fluoride ingestion are poorly demarcated", non-fluoride ones round and well demarcated (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri's mild form differs: "Mild fluorosis exhibits brown pigmentation on a smooth surface", and "The severe form of fluorosis may not respond to bleaching." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The threshold also differs: Summitt's "Excessive fluoride in drinking water, greater than 1 to 2 ppm, can cause metabolic alteration in the ameloblasts" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] versus Sikri's "high concentration of fluorides (more than 4.0 ppm) causes moderate to severe discoloration" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The two are not strictly contradictory — Sikri's figure is for moderate-to-severe staining — but they are often quoted as "the" threshold. [Likely]

**Tetracycline.** Goldstein's Ch.12: damage from "as little as 1 g of tetracycline" was recognised in the 1950s; teeth are susceptible "during the second trimester in utero to roughly 8 years of age"; binding "involves chelation with calcium, which forms tetracycline orthophosphate"; "When tetracycline-stained teeth are exposed to sunlight, they gradually turn to shades of dark gray or brown"; there are "more than 2000 variants have been patented" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's gives a different window — "if ingested by an expectant mother in the third trimester of pregnancy or by a child during the tooth-formation years, between the ages of 3 to 4 months and 7 to 8 years" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. The severity "depends on the dose, the duration of exposure to the drug and the type of tetracycline analogue used." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri says instead that "the dose of tetracycline is more important than the duration of intake of tetracyclines" and that "doxycycline does not produce any stain" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], whereas Summitt's lists "doxycycline, oxytetracycline, minocycline, chlortetracycline, demeclocycline" among the analogues producing grey, blue, brown or yellow (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] — a direct disagreement on doxycycline that the books do not resolve. [Guessing which is right; the literature behind Sikri's claim was not read.] Minocycline stains formed adult teeth: Goldstein's explains that "minocycline is absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract and combines poorly with calcium" and is thought to "chelate with iron and form insoluble complexes" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Sturdevant's cites "long-term exposure to minocycline, a tetracycline analogue" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Diagnosis: "Tetracycline staining can be clearly distinguished as the teeth fluorescence under ultraviolet light." (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; with time "the fluorescent yellow discoloration gradually changes over a period of months to years to a non-fluorescent brown color" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

**Jordan and Boksman grading.** Goldstein's presents "three major categories of tetracycline involvement first proposed by Jordan and Boksman" in 1984, plus a fourth added later: "First-degree tetracycline staining is a light yellow or light gray staining" without banding — "It is highly amenable to vital bleaching, with good results usually in fewer than four sessions of office bleaching"; second degree is darker without banding — "it may take five or more in-office treatments to obtain a satisfactory result", combined bleaching preferred and "Home bleaching alone may take 2–6 months."; "Third-degree tetracycline staining produces severe staining, characterized by dark gray or blue coloration, usually with banding" where "Veneering techniques with opaquers are often necessary to achieve satisfactory esthetic results"; fourth degree is "too dark" for some, although "these stains may not be too dark to try bleaching unless there is blue-gray stain at the gingivae" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

**Other defects.** Hypoplasia: "Cosmetic contouring, microabrasion, bonding resins, veneering, or crowning can be used to treat hypoplastic defects."; amelogenesis imperfecta: "Bleaching is difficult because the dentinal layer shows through, and it is usually painful because of the thinness of enamel."; hypocalcification: "Treatment can be cosmetic contouring, if the surface layer is not too deep, microabrasion, bonding, or porcelain veneers." (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. For enamel-structure defects generally, "Bleaching is usually a less appropriate treatment than bonding, veneering, or crowning for these problems involving the structure of enamel." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. In dentinogenesis imperfecta "there are bulbous roots with root canal obliteration", and with rapid wear in AI/DI "early intervention with multiple crowns is often required" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Dental fluorosis** — the commonest intrinsic cause (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Hypomineralized porous subsurface enamel under a well-mineralized surface layer. Fluoride opacities are poorly demarcated (non-fluoride opacities: round, well demarcated) (Goldstein et al, 2018).

| Grade | Appearance | Usual approach |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Flat grey/white flecks (Goldstein et al, 2018); brown pigment on smooth surface (Sikri, 2020) | Bleaching; microabrasion |
| Moderate | Chalky, dull enamel ± pitting and stain | Bleach + microabrasion ± bonding |
| Severe | Pitting, deformity, staining; may not respond to bleaching (Sikri, 2020) | Bleach as adjunct, then veneers/crowns |

Threshold quoted: >1 to 2 ppm (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) versus >4.0 ppm for moderate-severe staining (Sikri, 2020).

**Tetracycline** — chelates calcium to form tetracycline orthophosphate in dentin; sunlight darkens labial surfaces to grey/brown (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Window: second trimester in utero to about 8 years (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) versus third trimester to 7–8 years (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Severity depends on dose, duration and analogue (Ritter et al, 2019). Yellow fluorescence under ultraviolet light aids diagnosis (Bartlett, 2011). Minocycline stains adult teeth (iron chelation) (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Jordan and Boksman grading (1984)** (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018)

| Degree | Appearance | Prognosis with bleaching |
|---|---|---|
| First | Light yellow/grey, uniform, no banding | Excellent: fewer than four office sessions or one home series |
| Second | Darker yellow/grey, no banding | Five or more office visits; combined office/home best; home alone 2–6 months |
| Third | Dark grey/blue with banding | Partial; veneers with opaquers often needed |
| Fourth | Very dark | Usually veneers; try bleaching unless blue-grey at gingivae |

**Other developmental defects:** hypoplasia and hypocalcification — contouring, microabrasion, bonding, veneers; amelogenesis imperfecta — bleaching difficult and painful, so bonding or crowns (Goldstein et al, 2018); dentinogenesis imperfecta — crowns to prevent wear (Bartlett, 2011).

## 4. Diagnosis and case assessment

### Explanation

Goldstein's Ch.12: "The diagnostic workup should include pretreatment photographs, X-ray films, and an intensive prophylaxis to remove superficial staining" that may compound intrinsic discoloration, with medical, medication and diet/tobacco history and sensitivity records (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. For a single dark tooth "The first step in the treatment of this tooth is to take a radiograph" and pulp test it (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt's agrees ("A radiograph should always be taken of a single dark tooth") and explains why: "the disease process occurring in the tooth may be masked by removal of the only clinical symptom of the problem"; a traumatised tooth may necrose later — "It may take 1 to 20 years before this condition occurs." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt's states "The primary indication for bleaching is patient dissatisfaction with tooth color" and lists contraindications (Box 16-1) including "Unrealistic expectations" and "Pregnancy or lactation", and guarded prognoses including "Extremely dark gingival third of tooth that is visible when smiling", exposed roots, extensive white spots and TMD (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Translucent incisal edges: "some that are already translucent at the incisal edge may become more translucent"; test by placing a finger behind the tooth — "block the light transmission by placing a white-gloved finger on the lingual of the tooth"; and "Roots of teeth do not bleach as well as anatomical crowns" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's in-office contraindications include "extremely large pulps, which may increase sensitivity", severe enamel loss, cracks, severe banded tetracycline stain, white spots, restorations to be matched, extensive restorations (bleaching roughened tooth-coloured materials with "the greatest damage done to glass ionomer, the least to porcelain"), and perfectionists, because "bleaching is not perfect, in the way veneers can be" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's warns that many patients' teeth are normal: a shade tab "often shows these patients that the colour of their teeth is well within the normal range of shades" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's suggests an end point: "Patients tend to look most natural when the color of the teeth matches the white of the sclera in their eyes." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein's notes that although some say "the color of the teeth should closely match the color of the sclera", many patients ask for whiter (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**History:** chief complaint and expectation; onset (since eruption = developmental; later = acquired); childhood fluoride and tetracycline/minocycline exposure; trauma; diet, tobacco, chlorhexidine; sensitivity; pregnancy (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Examination:**
- Thorough prophylaxis first, to separate extrinsic from intrinsic stain (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
- Distribution: generalized (age, tetracycline, fluorosis, genetics) versus single tooth (trauma, calcific metamorphosis, resorption, restoration) versus spots (fluorosis, hypocalcification, demineralization).
- Colour and pattern: yellow/brown (responsive) versus grey/blue, banding, dark cervical third (resistant).
- Enamel thickness, cracks, wear, exposed roots, translucent incisal edges (finger test behind the tooth); roots do not bleach (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Existing restorations, which will not bleach.
- UV fluorescence for tetracycline (Bartlett, 2011).

**Investigations:** radiograph and pulp test of any single dark tooth — bleaching may mask pathology such as necrosis or resorption (Goldstein et al, 2018; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Contraindications / guarded cases:** unrealistic expectations, non-compliance, pregnancy or lactation, extensive restorations not to be replaced (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); very large pulps, cracks, severe enamel loss, severe banded tetracycline stain, perfectionists (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Many patients have teeth within the normal shade range; a shade tab beside the teeth shows this (Ritter et al, 2019). A natural end point is teeth matching the white of the sclera (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 5. Shade assessment and records

### Explanation

Goldstein's: "The VITA classical introduced to the dental profession in 1956 is still the most widely used", but its tabs are unevenly spaced, with "the lack of lighter shade tab than B1"; the 3D-Master works by "evaluating three components of color—value, chroma, and hue—separately"; Paravina in 2007 "introduced a new linear bleached shade guide for the purpose of monitoring color change during bleaching"; and spectrophotometers and colorimeters (Easyshade, SpectroShade) give data "not influenced by the human eye, environment, or light source" but are slow and costly (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's adds the perception problems: varying light sources — "This phenomenon is referred to as metamerism." — and eye fatigue with "loss of sensitivity to yellow-orange shades", relieved by looking at blue; so "the dentist, the assistant and especially the patient all be involved in shade selection" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's advises photographing the shade tab with the teeth and that "the best way to ensure patient awareness of the effect of bleaching is to bleach one arch at a time" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein's notes patients forget their original shade — Rosenstiel et al found "only 1 of 10 young adults who received one vital bleaching treatment were able to see the effects of the treatment past 1 month" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's warns that bleached teeth may go "lighter than the traditional lightest shade (B1 on the VITA Classic shade guide)" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Why record shade:** bleaching changes colour gradually and patients forget the starting shade; a baseline justifies treatment and guides later restorations (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Methods**
- **VITA Classical** (1956): most widely used, but uneven spacing and nothing lighter than B1 (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); bleached teeth often go lighter than B1 (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- **VITA 3D-Master:** value, chroma and hue assessed separately.
- **Bleached shade guide** (Paravina, 2007): linear, evenly spaced, very light tabs — designed for monitoring bleaching.
- **Instruments:** spot spectrophotometers/colorimeters and whole-tooth devices — objective, independent of eye and light, but costly and slow (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
- **Photographs** with the shade tab beside the teeth, before, during and after treatment.

**Precautions:** daylight-corrected light (metamerism); short looks and glance at a blue background to avoid yellow fatigue; involve assistant and patient (Ritter et al, 2019); record shade before rubber dam, as dehydration lightens teeth.

**One arch at a time** (usually maxillary first) leaves the other as a visible control (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).

## 6. Treatment sequence: from conservative to invasive

### Explanation

Goldstein's Ch.21 orders the options "in order of increasing aggressiveness" (Table 21.3): prophylaxis, air polisher, external bleaching with carbamide or hydrogen peroxide, sealants, macroabrasion, microabrasion, resurfacing or replacing restorations, partial or complete composite or ceramic veneers, and crowns (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Ch.12: "In most instances, bleaching teeth should be attempted before any other treatment is undertaken, with the exception of soft tissue management." and "In general, treat one arch at a time." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's agrees that "consideration should be given first to bleaching anterior teeth when intrinsic discolourations are encountered" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's calls the other methods "The alternatives to bleaching are more aggressive in relation to the tooth structure and/or to the gingival tissues." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bleaching also helps when restorations are inevitable: "Often, bleaching is the stepping-stone to veneers." and "bleaching lightens the underlying tooth, decreasing the masking needs of restoration" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt's: "the lighter color of the bleached teeth allows lighter and more translucent veneers" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Order of treatment (increasing aggressiveness)** (Goldstein et al, 2018; Ritter et al, 2019):

1. Oral hygiene, prophylaxis, air polishing, whitening dentifrice — extrinsic stain.
2. Replace leaking or discoloured restorations; treat caries.
3. Microabrasion / macroabrasion — superficial enamel stain or defect.
4. Resin infiltration — non-cavitated white spots (Sikri, 2020).
5. Vital bleaching — in-office, nightguard, OTC, or combined — intrinsic generalized stain.
6. Direct composite bonding: partial or full veneers.
7. Indirect veneers: composite, feldspathic porcelain, pressed ceramic.
8. All-ceramic or metal-ceramic crowns.

**Rules**
- Bleach before any other treatment except soft-tissue (periodontal) management (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); bleaching is the first choice for intrinsic discoloration (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Even when veneers are the goal, bleach first: it lightens the substrate, so thinner, more translucent veneers with less reduction are possible (Goldstein et al, 2018; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Each step is evaluated before the next; the patient knows in advance that further treatment may be needed (Goldstein et al, 2018).

## 7. Prophylaxis and removal of extrinsic stains

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: "Most surface stains can be removed by routine prophylactic procedures", while superficial stains on restorations and decalcified areas may need micro- or macro-abrasion (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: "Superficial or extrinsic stains may be completely removed by a rubber cup with prophylaxis paste or by light abrasion with a rotary polishing device." and recommends that "bleaching should be delayed for several days after dental prophylaxis" so that sensitivity settles (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's: before diagnosis "a complete prophylaxis should be performed to remove minor surface staining"; of whitening toothpastes, abrasive ones remove stain but thin enamel, peroxide pastes fail because "the contact time on the tooth is too short to produce any noticeable whitening" (though useful for maintenance), and "Most whitening toothpastes contain titanium dioxide", a temporary coating (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. For chlorhexidine stain, some patients overcome the "disadvantage by employing 10% carbamide peroxide in a bleaching tray periodically", otherwise more frequent prophylaxis is needed (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.12 also notes that "tooth contouring can sometimes make stains disappear if they are only in the first cell layers of enamel" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indication:** extrinsic stains (tea, coffee, tobacco, chlorhexidine, chromogenic bacteria, metallic dusts).

**Methods**
- Scaling and rubber-cup prophylaxis with pumice or prophy paste; air-polisher (sodium bicarbonate) for stained pits and grooves (Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein et al, 2018). Avoid air-polishing on composite and porcelain surfaces.
- Light rotary polishing (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); cosmetic contouring for stain confined to the outermost enamel (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
- Oral hygiene instruction; reduce chromogenic foods and tobacco.

**Whitening dentifrices** (Goldstein et al, 2018): abrasive (remove stain but thin enamel with overuse); chemical (anti-tartar, peroxide — contact time too short to bleach, but useful to maintain bleached colour); cosmetic (titanium dioxide coating, temporary).

**Role in rehabilitation:** prophylaxis is both treatment and diagnosis — it shows how much discoloration is intrinsic and must precede shade recording and bleaching. Delay bleaching for a few days after prophylaxis to avoid sensitivity (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 8. Enamel microabrasion and macroabrasion

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: these techniques are "indicated only for stains or enamel defects that do not extend beyond a few tenths of a millimetre in depth". History: "In 1984, McCloskey reported the use of 18% hydrochloric acid swabbed on teeth for the removal of superficial fluorosis stains."; "in 1986, Croll and Cavanaugh modified the technique to include the use of pumice with 18% hydrochloric acid"; Croll later went on "reducing the concentration of the hydrochloric acid to approximately 11%" with "silicon carbide particles (in a water-soluble gel paste) instead of pumice" (Prema, Opalustre). The technique "does not remove stains or defects through any bleaching phenomena". For idiopathic spots, "an accurate prognosis for microabrasion cannot be given"; failure follows "the depth of the defect exceeding 0.2–0.3 mm". Fluorosis responds "if the discolouration is within the 0.2–0.3-mm removal depth limit"; near the CEJ "enamel is too thin to permit micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion". Technique: rubber dam, glasses, compound applied with a "special rubber cup that has fluted edges" in "A 10× gear reduction, low-speed handpiece", checked often, then "The treated areas are polished with a fluoride-containing prophy paste to restore surface lustre" and "a topical fluoride is applied to teeth to enhance remineralization". Macroabrasion uses "a 12-fluted composite finishing bur or a fine-grit finishing diamond in a high-speed handpiece" with water spray, because "Dehydration exaggerates the appearance of white spots", then "a 30-fluted finishing bur is used to remove any facets or striations". Comparison: "Micro-abrasion has the advantage of ensuring better control of the removal of tooth structure.", macroabrasion "can have catastrophic results if the clinician fails to use extreme caution" but is faster and needs no dam, and "micro-abrasion is recommended over macro-abrasion for the treatment of superficial defects in children" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

The books disagree markedly on enamel loss. Summitt's: the acid leaves "the outer 22 to 27 μm weakened enough so that the abrasive, with which the acid is mixed, quickly removes the stained outer surface", applied "is applied to the enamel with a rubber cup or stiff bristle brush for 20 to 30 seconds" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett, quoting Tong et al (1993): with 18% HCl and pumice "for approximately 100 seconds, about 360" ± 130 μm of enamel is lost (the extracted text has lost the ± and μ signs), so "rubber dam is therefore mandatory" and "it should not be used for defects in dentine" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Sikri's procedure applies the agent "for 60 seconds, followed by rinsing", repeated, and describes "clinically unrecognizable enamel loss" when the defect is "confined to the superficial 0.2–0.3 mm of enamel" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Reading them together: 22–27 μm is the depth weakened per application, 360 μm the cumulative loss after about 100 s, and 0.2–0.3 mm the depth limit of a treatable defect — consistent in substance, but a student quoting a single figure for "depth removed" will be wrong unless the time is stated. [Likely] Sikri contraindicates it for "Stains of amelogenesis imperfecta, dentinogenesis imperfecta and tetracycline stains" and notes "teeth sometimes appear more yellow after treatment" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Goldstein's treats brown fluorosis spots: "Usually, microabrasion is considered the primary treatment." yet bleaching first is preferred because "attempting bleaching first avoids the removal of the fluoride-rich enamel layer" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt's: "It is best to bleach first unless the tooth enamel is of a chalky texture." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.12 describes the slurry as "a combination of hydrochloric acid (muriatic acid) and pumice with mechanical abrasion, which will etch the enamel slightly to facilitate stain removal" and macroabrasion "with friction grip diamonds or carbides with Soflex disk polishing" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Microabrasion:** simultaneous erosion (acid) and abrasion (abrasive) that removes a thin layer of discoloured or defective surface enamel (Bartlett, 2011). It removes tissue; it does not bleach (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Agents:** 18% HCl + pumice (McCloskey 1984; Croll and Cavanaugh 1986); about 11% HCl + silicon carbide in water-soluble gel (Prema, Opalustre) (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Indications:** defects within 0.2–0.3 mm of enamel — mild fluorosis (brown or white), idiopathic white/brown spots, post-orthodontic decalcification, hypocalcification (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
**Contraindications:** deep defects, dentin involvement, AI, DI, tetracycline stain, hypoplastic pits, thin cervical enamel (Sikri, 2020; Bartlett, 2011).

**Technique:** shade and photograph → rubber dam (mandatory), protective glasses → apply with fluted rubber cup at about 10:1 gear-reduced slow speed, moderate pressure, short applications (20–30 s, Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; 60 s, Sikri, 2020) → rinse and inspect wet, repeat → polish with fluoride prophy paste → topical fluoride (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Enamel removed — books differ:** about 22–27 μm per application (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); about 360 μm after about 100 s of 18% HCl-pumice (Bartlett, 2011); treatable limit 0.2–0.3 mm (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Macroabrasion:** 12-fluted finishing bur or fine diamond, high speed, light intermittent pressure, water spray to keep enamel hydrated, then 30-fluted bur and rubber points. Faster, no dam, but less controlled (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Sequence:** bleach first (preserves fluoride-rich enamel), microabrade residual spots, then bond what remains (Goldstein et al, 2018).

## 9. Resin infiltration

### Explanation

Sikri describes it under minimal intervention: "Resin infiltration is a microinvasive treatment regime for arresting early carious lesions." — a low-viscosity resin "marketed as ICON caries infiltrant", "first developed at the University of Kiel (Germany)"; "The infiltration procedure also provides optical blending of the carious lesion with the healthy enamel." Indications include white spot lesions, "Fluorosis stains (non-pitted)", amelogenesis imperfecta and "Molar-incisor hypomineralization". Kit: "The Etchant (acid) gel is composed of 15% hydrochloric acid"; "Drying agent is ethanol, which removes water retained in microporosities."; "The Infiltrant is composed of triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA)". The surface layer must be removed: "Etching with 15% HCl gel for 90–120 seconds was found to be more effective than 37% phosphoric acid gel". Sikri's table of white-spot types pairs trauma-related white patches with "Whitening, followed by resin infiltration" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Goldstein's, Sturdevant's and Summitt's chapters read for this answer do not describe resin infiltration for discoloration; it is included because Sikri does. [Certain about the sourcing; long-term colour stability of infiltrated lesions is not discussed in the book read.]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** a micro-invasive technique in which a low-viscosity resin infiltrates the porous body of a non-cavitated enamel lesion; developed at Kiel, marketed as ICON (Sikri, 2020).

**Aesthetic principle:** the porous lesion scatters light (looks white); filling the pores with resin of similar refractive index blends it optically with sound enamel, and also arrests the lesion (Sikri, 2020).

**Indications:** post-orthodontic and early carious white spots, non-pitted fluorosis, mild hypomineralization (MIH), some amelogenesis imperfecta (Sikri, 2020).

**Kit and steps:** rubber dam → ICON Etch (15% HCl gel) for 90–120 s, which removes the hypermineralized surface layer better than 37% phosphoric acid → rinse → ICON Dry (ethanol) to remove water from the pores → ICON Infiltrant (TEGDMA resin) applied, allowed to penetrate, light-cured, repeated → polish (Sikri, 2020).

**Advantages:** no drilling, no sensitivity, preserves tissue, arrests lesion. **Limit:** pitted or deep defects need composite.

**Place in sequence:** after bleaching of the whole tooth — whitening followed by infiltration for white patches (Sikri, 2020).

## 10. Bleaching agents and mechanism of action

### Explanation

Mechanism: Goldstein's — "the basic process almost certainly involves oxidation, during which the molecules causing the discoloration are released" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Sikri — "Bleaching implies degradation of high molecular weight, complex organic molecules" into smaller, less light-reflecting ones; hydrogen peroxide releases free radicals, "hydroxyl ions (OH–) and perhydroxyl ions (OOH–)" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. "Because hydrogen peroxide has such a low molecular weight, it easily passes through enamel and dentin." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Bartlett: "It is believed that most of the action of bleaching is on the dentine rather than the enamel" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Summitt's: "Carbamide peroxide penetrates through the enamel and the dentin to the pulp in 5 to 15 minutes." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Carbamide peroxide (CP) breakdown — three books, three figures: Summitt's "a solution of 10% carbamide peroxide agent breaks down into 6.5% urea and 3.5% hydrogen peroxide", which "bleaching formulations with carbamide peroxide typically are dosed at three times the concentration of those containing hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's "Carbamide peroxide degrades into 3% hydrogen peroxide (active ingredient) and 7% urea." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri "10% carbamide peroxide is equal to 3.0% hydrogen peroxide and 22% carbamide peroxide is equal to 7.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The difference (3.0 versus 3.5%) reflects rounding of the about one-third ratio; none is plainly wrong. [Likely] Urea raises pH: "raising the pH in the oral cavity above 8 for a number of hours" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Hydrogen peroxide gels for day use "range in concentration from 7.5% to 9.5% hydrogen peroxide" and "They are only active for 30 to 60 minutes", whereas "Carbamide peroxide is active for up to 10 hours, with about 50% of the active agent being used up in the first 2 hours." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Additives: "carbopol thickens the bleaching solution and extends the oxidation process" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri gives carbopol "in a concentration of 0.5 to 1.5%, is commonly used thickening agent", with glycerine carriers, preservatives, surfactants, flavours, and "Potassium nitrate and fluorides are added to decrease post-treatment sensitivity." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Other agents listed by Sikri: "0.5% chlorine dioxide when applied to enamel surface for 20 minutes" (roughens enamel), HCl — "The hydrochloric acid (18–36%) causes decalcification of tooth substance along with removal of stain." — McInnes solution ("5 parts of 36% HCl" with 30% H2O2 and ether), and nitrogen-doped TiO2 — "Titanium dioxide nanoparticle doped with nitrogen with less concentration of hydrogen peroxide" (3–10%); he also claims "hydrogen peroxide above 15% concentration does not increase bleaching effectiveness" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's is close: "the effect is not linear (ie, 20% is not twice as fast as 10%)", in-office "concentrations range from 15% to 50%" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Safety limits: "The Food and Drug Administration recognizes 3% hydrogen peroxide and 10% to 15% carbamide peroxide as GRAS", and the European committee's "The maximum recommended concentration for products is 6% hydrogen peroxide (which is approximately equivalent to 17% carbamide peroxide)." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sodium perborate belongs to non-vital (intracoronal) bleaching and is not covered here.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Mechanism:** hydrogen peroxide, of low molecular weight, diffuses through enamel into dentin (reaching the pulp in 5–15 min) and releases free radicals (hydroxyl, perhydroxyl) that oxidize large, coloured organic pigment molecules into smaller, less coloured ones (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Sikri, 2020; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Most of the effect is on dentin (Bartlett, 2011).

**Agents**

| Agent | Concentration / use |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen peroxide (HP) | 30–35% in-office (range 15% to 50%); 7.5–9.5% day-wear tray gels, active 30–60 min; 6–14% OTC strips |
| Carbamide peroxide (CP, urea peroxide) | 10–15% (up to 35–38%) nightguard; active up to 10 h, 50% used in 2 h |
| HCl | 18–36% (McInnes); 18% or about 11% for microabrasion |
| Others | 0.5% chlorine dioxide (roughens enamel); N-doped TiO2 with 3–10% HP (Sikri, 2020) |

**CP conversion — books differ:** 10% CP → 3.5% HP + 6.5% urea (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 3% HP + 7% urea (Ritter et al, 2019); 3.0% HP, and 22% CP = 7.5% HP (Sikri, 2020). Rule: CP is about three times the equivalent HP. Urea raises pH above 8.

**Additives:** carbopol 0.5–1.5% (thickens, prolongs oxygen release); glycerine/propylene glycol carriers; surfactants; preservatives; flavours; potassium nitrate, fluoride, ACP/CPP-ACP against sensitivity (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).

**Concentration:** higher concentration speeds bleaching only up to a point and increases sensitivity — 20% is not twice as fast as 10% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); Sikri says HP above 15% adds no effectiveness.

**Safety limits:** FDA GRAS 3% HP and 10–15% CP; EU maximum 6% HP (about 17% CP) for consumer products (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 11. In-office (power) vital bleaching

### Explanation

Isolation is where the books disagree most clearly. Sturdevant's: "In-office vital bleaching requires an excellent rubber dam technique", and paint-on resin dams "cannot provide the same degree of protection and isolation as a conventionally applied rubber dam" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's: "The conventional rubber dam has been replaced by the use of cheek retractors and gingival protectors", with a light-cured resin barrier "to cover approximately 0.5 mm of the cervical area of the tooth and extend 2–3 mm onto the gingiva" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's takes the middle ground: rubber dam generally, but for one or a few teeth "it may be possible to protect the gingiva with cotton rolls and a so-called liquid rubber dam" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Both methods are in use; the choice depends on gel viscosity and number of teeth. [Likely]

Agent and protocol: Sturdevant's — gels "most commonly contain 30–35% hydrogen peroxide"; "Etching of teeth with 37% phosphoric acid, once considered a required part of this technique, is unnecessary."; "Bleaching treatments generally are rendered weekly for two to six treatments, with each treatment lasting 30–45 min." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's — "In-office bleaching of vital teeth generally uses a 35% hydrogen peroxide solution placed directly on the teeth"; "The teeth should not be anesthetized for in-office bleaching."; "Etching of the enamel is not indicated."; "Appointments for subsequent treatment are scheduled 1 week apart to allow sensitivity to abate."; "The average is three in-office sessions to reach maximum tooth" whiteness; results should be "evaluated after 1 to 2 weeks to allow rehydration of the teeth to a stable shade" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's — "For most bleaching, a 30–35% concentration of hydrogen peroxide is used.", covered with "Place a precut linear low-density polyethylene wrap (Saran wrap) onto the teeth to prevent evaporation", removed "Remove the wrap and the bleaching material after 40–60 min.", then "Finish the in-office bleaching session with a 2% neutral sodium fluoride gel for 5–10 min"; teeth "may appear chalky because of dehydration"; for tetracycline "stained teeth, three or more visits are generally required" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's disagrees on fluoride: "it is not essential to provide any fluoride treatment" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's thermocatalytic version: "The recommended temperature is 46°C to 60°C."; "Bleaching appointments are scheduled 2–4 weeks apart."; "A minimum of three treatments are usually sufficient; however, extra treatments can be carried out (not more than ten)."; "Instruct the patient to avoid coffee, tea and cola drinks for two weeks." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Interval therefore varies from 1 week (Sturdevant's, Summitt's) to 2–4 weeks (Sikri; Goldstein's "every 2–4 weeks" for tetracycline). [Certain that they differ]

Light and heat: Sturdevant's — "the addition of light during the bleaching procedure does not improve the whitening result beyond what the bleach alone can achieve"; heating "causes a greater level of tooth dehydration"; "Use of carbon dioxide laser to heat the bleaching mixture and accelerate the bleaching treatment has not been recommended" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: "Dehydration may give initial lighter appearance" and "It is important to remember that the hydrogen peroxide performs the bleaching, not the laser."; "An increase of 10°C doubles the rate of chemical reaction." but not necessarily the colour change (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri: "Use of carbon dioxide laser has been discontinued because of its thermal effect on the pulp.", but "Smartbleach is preferred choice for bleaching tetracycline-stained teeth." using the "wavelength of KTP laser (532 nm)" to photo-oxidize the tetracycline quinone (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] — a claim the other books do not make. [Guessing on its clinical superiority; not tested against another source here.] Goldstein's, in contrast, keeps heat/light as "a matter of preference" and notes in elderly patients "in most instances the pulp has shrunk back, making it possible to use higher bleaching temperatures", while in children "one should be especially careful to avoid irritation of the pulp, including not using heat" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** fast result wanted, poor compliance with trays, single dark vital tooth or selected teeth, jump-start before home bleaching (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Agent:** 30–35% hydrogen peroxide gel (Ritter et al, 2019; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Technique**
1. Prophylaxis (pumice/Prophy-Jet), shade and photograph with tab.
2. No local anaesthesia — the patient's sensation warns of leakage or overheating (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
3. Protect eyes, lips (petroleum jelly), soft tissues.
4. **Isolation — books differ:** heavy rubber dam ligated and sealed (Ritter et al, 2019) versus cheek retractors and a light-cured resin barrier covering 0.5 mm of cervical enamel and 2–3 mm of gingiva (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
5. No acid etching (Ritter et al, 2019).
6. Apply gel uniformly, cover with plastic wrap, activate per manufacturer; leave 40–60 min in total, renewing gel (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) — or 30–45 min sessions (Ritter et al, 2019).
7. Remove gel with high suction, rinse copiously, remove barrier.
8. Fluoride: 2% neutral NaF 5–10 min (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) versus not essential (Ritter et al, 2019).
9. Review after 1–2 weeks (rehydration). Repeat: weekly, 2–6 sessions (average three) (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); Sikri spaces thermocatalytic sessions 2–4 weeks apart at 46–60°C.

**Heat, lights and lasers:** they add dehydration and sensitivity rather than whitening; the peroxide does the bleaching (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); CO2 laser not recommended. Sikri favours KTP (532 nm) for tetracycline. No heat in children; higher heat tolerated in the elderly (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Advantages:** dentist control, quick visible result. **Disadvantages:** cost, chair time, tissue burns, more sensitivity, unpredictable duration, false early lightening from dehydration (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 12. Dentist-supervised nightguard (matrix) vital bleaching

### Explanation

History: Sturdevant's says the home technique "began around 1968, although it was not commonly known until the late 1980s" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Tray: Goldstein's cites Haywood that "the best prosthetic material is a 0.9 mm clear soft material"; Sturdevant's heats vinyl sheet until "the material sags approximately by 25 mm" and prefers scalloping: "This scalloped design is preferred because it allows the tray to cover only teeth" (Ritter et al, 2019; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's disagrees on scalloping and reservoirs: "Scalloping is not necessary when low concentrations of carbamide peroxide (10%) are used", and "there is little evidence that the creation of reservoirs actually improves bleaching efficacy" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Agent: "A 10%–15% carbamide peroxide bleaching material generally is recommended for this bleaching technique." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; "Most matrix bleaching uses 10–15% carbamide peroxide rather than the 35% hydrogen peroxide used in in-office procedures.", with three-tier products "beginning with a 5 or 6% solution", and "thicker gels generally work better than thinner ones" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt's: "the best combination of safety, limited side effects, and speed of action is obtained with a 10% solution", and "90% of the dentists surveyed used a 10% carbamide peroxide formulation" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Wear and duration: Sturdevant's "Total treatment time using an overnight approach is usually 1–2 weeks." and "It is recommended that only one arch be bleached at a time, beginning with the maxillary arch." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein's "The most common regimen is between 1 and 4 hours daily use from 4 weeks to 6 months (for tetracycline stain).", and success is "with 9 out of 10 patients experiencing a lightening of their teeth in 2–6 weeks" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Bartlett: "worn each night for 2–3 hours" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Sikri: "The daytime regimen requires one to three weeks; whereas, 4 to 6 weeks are required for" night-time use (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's Table 16-3: "Normal teeth 3 days to 6 weeks (average of 2 weeks) Nicotine-stained teeth 1 to 3 months Tetracycline-stained teeth 1 to 12 months (average of 3 to 4 months)" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21: "Typically, bleaching with 20% carbamide peroxide" in a tray "easily treats discolorations due to aging, smoking, or chromogenic foods and beverages"; "Nicotine staining of long-term duration may require as long as 3 months of nightly treatment."; "Tetracycline staining may take anywhere from 2 to 12 months of nightly treatment." (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's lists the disadvantages starting with "Reliance on patient compliance" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein's lists among contraindications "unwillingness to wear the appliance the necessary 1–3 h per day" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Restorations: "Polymethyl methacrylate restorations exhibit a yellow-orange discolouration on exposure to carbamide peroxide." so temporaries should be bis-acryl (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's states that overall "overnight tray bleaching produces the best results" and that 10% CP trays "are generally the safest, most cost-effective, best-researched whitening treatments available" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle:** a low concentration of peroxide held against the teeth for long periods in a custom tray, worn by the patient at home under the dentist's supervision.

**Agent:** 10–15% carbamide peroxide with carbopol (Ritter et al, 2019; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); 10% CP gives the best balance of safety and speed and is used by 90% of dentists surveyed (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Alternatives: 7.5–9.5% HP day gels for 30–60 min; tiered 5–6% → 10–12% → 15–17% CP to limit sensitivity (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Steps**
1. Examination, radiographs, prophylaxis, shade and photographs.
2. Alginate impression; thin, trimmed stone cast; optional reservoir (0.5 mm spacer ending 1 mm short of the gingiva).
3. Vacuum-form a soft 0.9 mm sheet (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) or 0.75–1.5 mm vinyl sagging about 25 mm (Ritter et al, 2019); trim scalloped just short of the gingiva — or unscalloped for 10% CP (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
4. Try-in: check fit, blanching, occlusion.
5. Instruct: a small drop per tooth, facial surface; wipe off excess; overnight (one application) or 2–4 h by day; remove for meals; one arch at a time, maxillary first.
6. Recall after 1–2 weeks: shade, sensitivity, gingiva, compliance.

**Treatment time:** normal teeth 3 days–6 weeks (average 2 weeks); nicotine 1–3 months; tetracycline 1–12 months (average 3–4 months) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 9 of 10 patients lighten in 2–6 weeks (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Advantages:** low concentration, few side effects, low cost and chair time, easy touch-up with the same tray. **Disadvantages:** compliance needed, longer time, PMMA temporaries turn yellow-orange (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 13. Over-the-counter products and combination bleaching

### Explanation

Summitt's: "The three types of bleaching therapy are in-office bleaching, at-home bleaching, and over-the-counter products."; "The fundamental problem with OTC products is that there is no examination and diagnosis of the cause of discoloration."; strips — "One whitening system utilizes adhesive strips containing a relatively high concentration (approximately 6% to 14%) of hydrogen peroxide." — "wear the strips for 30 minutes twice a day"; comparison: "To obtain a six-shade color change on a VITA shade guide required 7 nights of tray bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide, 16 days of a 6% hydrogen peroxide strip (OTC) worn twice a day, or 3 in-office treatments of 38% hydrogen peroxide."; conclusion "OTC nondentist bleaching does not have a good risk-benefit ratio" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett: "there are always dangers with patient-applied products that are not prescribed and supervised by a dentist" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Goldstein's describes the FDA history — "In 1991, the FDA ruled that the use of carbamide peroxide in the form advocated for home bleaching constituted a new drug use" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri lists "Polyethylene strips impregnated with 5.25% of H2O2 is also used without tray." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

On combination therapy the books conflict directly. Goldstein's: one in-office session plus home matrix "provides the most effective result seen to date" and "provides the most predictable of all the bleaching techniques" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Sturdevant's: "a combination of the in-office technique and the dentist-prescribed, home-applied technique has better results than either technique used alone" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: "One study found that in-office bleaching combined with at-home bleaching does not improve tooth whitening compared with at-home bleaching alone." and Table 16-1 says the final outcome is the same, the in-office part being only a paid "boost" — although the same chapter also recommends that in-office "bleaching be followed by at-home bleaching to enhance the effect and to minimize rebound" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett: "the result from both techniques is likely to be the same" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Reconciliation: combination gives a faster start and motivates the patient; the end point after long enough treatment is similar. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**OTC products:** strips (6–14% HP, 30 min twice daily), paint-ons, trays, toothpastes (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); Sikri lists 5.25% HP strips.
- *Problems:* no examination or diagnosis — may mask necrosis, resorption or caries; restorations not identified; ill-fitting trays; unknown quality and concentration; strips may not reach beyond canines (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bartlett, 2011).
- *Efficacy:* a six-shade change needed 7 nights of 10% CP tray, 16 days of 6% HP strips, or 3 in-office 38% HP visits. Risk-benefit unfavourable without a dental examination.

**Combination (power + matrix) bleaching:** one in-office session followed by home tray bleaching.
- Goldstein: most effective and most predictable technique; immediate result, then continued home bleaching prevents regression (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Sturdevant: better than either alone (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Summitt and Bartlett: final outcome the same as tray bleaching alone; the office session is a quicker start at extra cost (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bartlett, 2011).
- Conclusion: combined bleaching suits second-degree tetracycline, impatient patients and those needing motivation; tray bleaching alone is equally effective given time.

**Comparison**

| | In-office | Nightguard | OTC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agent | 30–35% HP | 10–15% CP | 6–14% HP |
| Control | Dentist | Shared | Patient |
| Speed | Fast | Weeks | Variable |
| Cost | High | Moderate | Low |
| Diagnosis | Yes | Yes | No |

## 14. Side effects of vital bleaching and their management

### Explanation

"Tooth sensitivity during bleaching is the most prevalent side effect to treatment"; passive treatment shortens or skips wear; active treatment — "The mechanism of action of fluoride is as a tubular blocker." — or "use of 5% or less concentrations of potassium nitrate applied in the bleaching tray", which acts to "chemically depolarize the nerve to inhibit refiring" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's: "Double-blind studies have reported sensitivity in 25% to 70% of patients." (placebo 20–30%); "the greatest sensitivity seems to occur in the first 2 weeks of treatment"; "the pulp remains healthy and the sensitivity is completely reversible"; "A more direct treatment is the application of 3% to 5% potassium nitrate gel in the tray." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett attributes sensitivity to low pH: "as the bleach has a low pH and opens up dentinal tubules" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], whereas Summitt's notes CP raises pH and says "Bleaching sensitivity is not directly related to tubule blockage" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Enamel: "a slight loss in surface hardness to a depth of approximately 25 μm" from low-pH solutions, quickly remineralized (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Restorations: "Bleaching has no effect on porcelain."; "It does encourage the release of mercury from some types of amalgam restorations." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; restorations do not lighten — "existing restorations tend to appear darker as the adjacent teeth lighten" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Bond strength: see Section 19.

#### ✎ Exam core

| Side effect | Cause / features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Tooth sensitivity (commonest) | Peroxide reaches pulp; 25–70% of patients; worst in first 2 weeks; reversible (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) | Reduce wear time or skip days; 3–5% potassium nitrate in tray (nerve depolarization); neutral fluoride (tubule blocker); desensitizing toothpaste 2 weeks before; analgesics (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) |
| Gingival irritation / chemical burn | Ill-fitting tray; HP leakage in-office | Trim tray; stop and rinse; water and baking soda, ZOE or antiseptic ointment |
| Enamel surface change | Slight softening about 25 μm deep with low-pH gels | Remineralizes; neutral gels, fluoride |
| Restorations | No effect on porcelain; slight composite roughening; PMMA yellows; mercury release from amalgam (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019) | Replace mismatching restorations after bleaching |
| Reduced bond strength | Residual oxygen | Delay bonding (Section 19) |
| Colour relapse | Regression | Touch-up |
| TMJ discomfort | Tray wear | Day wear, tray adjustment |

**Systemic safety:** low concentrations are safe; FDA GRAS 3% HP and 10–15% CP (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 15. Direct composite bonding and composite veneers

### Explanation

Goldstein's: "bonding remains the single fastest transformation of the mouth available to you as a dentist"; "the quickest and most economic method of obtaining an esthetic tooth transformation is through the direct resin labial veneer"; "The best candidate for the direct resin veneer is the monochromatic shaded tooth"; "Extremely dark tetracycline-stained teeth provide the most difficult challenge for direct resin full-veneer bonding.", handled by first applying "first mask the tetracycline discoloration with multiple layers of opaque", then tints and body shades (Goldstein and Vargas, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: "A veneer is a layer of tooth-coloured material that is applied to a tooth to restore localized or generalized defects and intrinsic discolourations"; "Partial veneers are indicated for the restoration of localized defects or areas of intrinsic discolouration"; direct full veneers suit cases "For cases involving young children or a single discoloured tooth" or limited budget; "Indirect veneers typically last much longer than do direct veneers". Intra-enamel preparation is recommended, among other reasons "To remove the outer, fluoride-rich layer of enamel that may be more resistant to acid etching"; "A window preparation is recommended for most direct composite veneers.", depth "ranging from approximately 0.5–0.75 mm midfacially and tapering down to a depth of about 0.3–0.5 mm along the gingival margin"; subgingival margins only for caries or "or if it involves significantly dark discolouration that presents a difficult esthetic problem"; "Discoloured mandibular anterior teeth are rarely indicated for veneers". For partial veneers "Microfills are excellent" enamel replacements, and for residual light stain "an intrinsically less translucent composite can be used rather than extending the preparation into dentin". Colour modifiers: "a violet colour modifier (the complementary colour of yellow)" tones down yellow calcific metamorphosis; but "Although opaque resin media can mask the underlying stain, esthetic vitality is usually lost because of reduced light penetration" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Fahl and Ritter: "Composites tend to need thicker opaquing layers to mask the discoloration", so "In general, the darker the tooth, the more preparation is needed." and "Any attempt to mask a dark tooth without adequate preparation will invariably result in a lifeless and often overcontoured veneer." (Fahl and Ritter, n.d.) [SRC]; "Opaquers are low-viscosity light-activated resins used for masking dark substrates", and "It is a common misconception that an opaquer must be 100% opaque" — "An opaquer that presents extremely high opacity may render a restoration lifeless and dull" (Fahl and Ritter, n.d.) [SRC]. Summitt's suggests opaque A2 under translucent enamel for unremoved white spots, and "pink composite may be helpful to neutralize remaining gray on the tooth from amalgam stains or severe" tetracycline banding (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21 illustrates microabrasion first, after which "Direct composite bonding masked the remainder of the white spots" (Figure 21.18) (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** localized defects or stain (white/brown spots, hypoplasia, hypocalcification) — partial veneers; generalized mild–moderate discoloration, single discoloured tooth, young patients, limited budget — full direct veneers (Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein and Vargas, 2018).

**Types:** partial veneer; full veneer — window, butt-joint incisal, incisal-lapping (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Preparation:** intra-enamel to make space (avoid overcontour), remove fluoride-rich etch-resistant enamel, roughen surface and create a finish line; window preparation for direct veneers; 0.5–0.75 mm midfacially tapering to 0.3–0.5 mm cervically; margins supragingival unless the cervical area is dark or carious; stop facial to the contact (Ritter et al, 2019). The darker the tooth, the more preparation is needed (Fahl and Ritter, n.d.).

**Technique:** clean, shade with hydrated teeth, isolate, prepare with water spray → etch, bond → mask with opaquer in thin layers → tints (violet against yellow; pink/red against grey) → dentin and enamel shades in increments → finish, polish (Goldstein and Vargas, 2018; Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Opaquers:** low-viscosity resins with metal oxides; need not be 100% opaque — excess opacity gives a lifeless, dull, high-value restoration (Fahl and Ritter, n.d.; Ritter et al, 2019).

**Advantages:** single visit, minimal or no reduction, reversible, repairable, inexpensive. **Disadvantages:** technique sensitive, staining, wear, loss of lustre, shorter life than porcelain; very dark tetracycline teeth are the hardest to mask (Goldstein and Vargas, 2018).

## 16. Porcelain laminate veneers

### Explanation

Goldstein's Ch.15 places discoloration first in its indications — "Type I: moderate tooth discolorations/color corrections" (tetracycline, fluorosis, amelogenesis imperfecta) — and lists "Moderate discolorations, such as tetracycline staining, fluorosis, devitalized teeth, and teeth darkened by age which are not conducive to vital bleaching"; advantages include that "the highly glazed porcelain surface is very resistant to stain accumulation"; disadvantages "The color cannot be easily modified once bonded in position." and "Irreversibility of preparation versus little or no preparation for direct composite resin bonding."; contraindications include "Patients with certain tooth-to-tooth habits such as bruxism", insufficient enamel ("Ideally, enamel should be around the whole periphery of the veneer"), edge-to-edge bites, and "teeth that have been excessively fluoridated may not etch effectively"; for deep tetracycline or amalgam staining "the use of more opaque high-strength ceramics such as lithium disilicate" is indicated (Stappert et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: "The preferred type of indirect veneer is the etched porcelain (i.e. feldspathic) veneer."; "Preparations into dentin should be avoided"; facial reduction in three zones "until the desired depth of approximately 0.6 mm is attained", incisal reduction "a minimum reduction of 1 mm or, more desirably, 1.5 mm". For darkly discoloured teeth: "Typically, 5%–15% opaque porcelain is required to achieve optimal masking.", but "Exceeding 15% opaque porcelain dramatically reduces light penetration"; "a slightly deeper tooth preparation can be used to allow greater veneer thickness" (still in enamel); "several coats of a die-spacing medium" for thicker resin cement; patients are told veneers "they may not entirely eliminate or mask extremely dark stains" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Conflict on pressed ceramics: Sturdevant's — "Because of the more translucent nature of pressed ceramic veneers, however, dark discolourations are best treated with etched porcelain veneers." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein's — lithium disilicate is indicated for severe staining, and "masking can be achieved with less amount of tooth structure removal" than feldspathic, provided one records the stump shade ("it is still essential to take the stump shade of the prepared teeth") (Stappert et al, 2018; Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. The difference partly reflects material generation: Sturdevant's discusses translucent pressed glass ceramics (IPS Empress) and notes newer pressed ceramics can be made thinner; Goldstein's discusses opaque high-strength lithium disilicate ingots. [Likely] Goldstein's Ch.21 also mentions "Some suggest the use of an opaque resin to mask the discolorations prior to the cementation of the porcelain veneers." (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Longevity: "an etched porcelain veneer should last 10–15 years or longer" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Darkening under veneers can be corrected: "Both vital and endodontically treated teeth under veneers may darken over time." (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; "the natural teeth can be bleached under the veneers from the lingual" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** moderate discoloration not responding to bleaching — second/third-degree tetracycline, fluorosis with defects, AI, aged and worn teeth, amalgam shine-through, single discoloured tooth; with shape or spacing problems (Stappert et al, 2018).

**Contraindications:** bruxism and parafunction, insufficient enamel, edge-to-edge or Class III bite, poor hygiene; heavily fluorosed or primary enamel etches poorly (Stappert et al, 2018).

**Preparation (intra-enamel):** depth-orientation with a 1–1.2 mm round-ended diamond; facial reduction in three zones to about 0.6 mm (0.5–0.75 mm midfacial, 0.3–0.5 mm cervical); incisal reduction 1–1.5 mm (butt-joint or incisal lapping) or window design; margins just facial to contacts; avoid dentin (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Modifications for dark teeth** (Ritter et al, 2019): 5–15% opaque porcelain (above 15% kills vitality); slightly deeper preparation within enamel; die spacer for a thicker opaque resin cement; subgingival margin only if the cervical area is dark; bleach first to lighten the substrate (Goldstein et al, 2018).

**Material choice — books differ:** feldspathic preferred, pressed ceramics too translucent for dark stains (Ritter et al, 2019); opaque lithium disilicate masks tetracycline with less reduction, with stump shade sent to the lab (Stappert et al, 2018; Goldstein et al, 2018).

**Bonding:** HF etch and silane on porcelain; phosphoric acid etch of enamel; light-cured resin cement (colour stable).

**Advantages:** best aesthetics, stain resistance, durability (10–15 years), conservative versus crowns. **Disadvantages:** irreversible, colour cannot be changed after cementation, two visits, cost, fragile before bonding (Stappert et al, 2018). Teeth that darken under veneers can be bleached from the lingual (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 17. Crowns

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: "Full porcelain coverage with all-ceramic crowns may be indicated in some patients with severe discolouration" because of greater capacity to restore vitality, though many patients refuse "cutting down" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Bartlett: "Extensively restored teeth or severely discoloured teeth (Figure 5.21) are more appropriately treated with crowns" — here phrased "are more appropriately treated with crowns" — and in AI/DI "early intervention with multiple crowns is often required" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Goldstein's: for severe drug-induced and congenital defects "full crown coverage may be necessary to improve the appearance of the teeth" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; "Bleaching is usually a less appropriate treatment than bonding, veneering, or crowning for these problems involving the structure of enamel." (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. When crowns are placed on bleached teeth Goldstein's advises: if the shade must be taken early, "choose a lighter shade, and be prepared to darken the crown if necessary" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** severe discoloration that veneers cannot mask (fourth-degree tetracycline, very dark gingival thirds); extensively restored, fractured or weakened teeth; severe AI or DI with wear; severe fluorosis with gross deformity; malposition needing major change of form (Ritter et al, 2019; Bartlett, 2011).

**Choice:** all-ceramic crowns (lithium disilicate, zirconia with layered porcelain) give better translucency and vitality than veneers on very dark teeth; metal-ceramic where strength is needed but opaque layers reduce vitality (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Disadvantages:** most tooth removal, pulpal risk in young teeth, gingival response to margins, cost — the last resort for vital teeth.

**Shade matching:** bleach the remaining teeth first and wait for the shade to stabilize; if the shade must be taken early, choose a lighter shade and darken later; warn that adjacent teeth may need periodic touch-up bleaching to keep matching the crown (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

## 18. Selection of treatment by cause and severity

### Explanation

Response to bleaching depends on colour: "Teeth exhibiting yellow or orange intrinsic discolouration seem to respond best to vital bleaching"; "Brown fluorosis stains also are often responsive to treatment, but white fluorosis stains may not be resolved effectively" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's ranks "teeth discolored by age, genetics, smoking, or coffee are the fastest to respond", "In general, tetracycline-stained teeth are the slowest to respond to bleaching", and "the yellow discoloration of aging responds quickly to bleaching in most cases"; "White spots are not removed by bleaching but may be less noticeable when the remainder of the tooth is lighter."; "Bleaching has been estimated to remove 80% of brown discolorations." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21: "It is estimated that 80% of these brown discolorations are amenable to bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide." with "with no return or need for additional treatment at seven years recall"; for white spots "Bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide is still the first treatment of choice", noting that "Teeth with white spots undergoing bleaching often develop a" splotchy look early on (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Tetracycline: "In-office bleaching is a possible treatment method but is generally contraindicated due to the number of treatments required"; "Treatment times may vary from 2 months to 1 year"; patients should "agree to a minimum of 2 months of nightly treatment before deciding to proceed to more aggressive treatment"; "Dark tetracycline stains located in the gingival third of the tooth or dark blue or gray stains have the least favorable prognosis." (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Note Ch.21 (home bleaching preferred for tetracycline) and Ch.12 (combined office/home preferred for second degree) differ in emphasis within the same book. [Certain] Sturdevant's: "Dark blue-grey, tetracycline-stained teeth are considerably more difficult to treat than are teeth with mild yellow-orange discolourations."; "Teeth stained with tetracycline require prolonged treatment durations of several months before any results are observed."; "Tetracycline-stained teeth may approach but never seem to achieve the appearance of normal teeth."; "some clinicians advocate intentional endodontic therapy along with the use of an intra-coronal nonvital bleaching technique", which carries endodontic risks (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: "some tetracycline-stained teeth may require 2 to 12 months of daily treatment"; "Teeth heavily stained in the gingival third have the poorest prognosis for complete lightening."; a cheaper option is to "bleach tetracycline-stained teeth using 6.5% hydrogen peroxide strips" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.12: with dark tetracycline stain "the majority of the bleaching will occur on the incisal one-half of the teeth" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri contraindicates bleaching for "Deep tetracycline stains" and "Severe discoloration from amalgam corrosion" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Calcific metamorphosis: the tooth is a poor endodontic candidate, and "External bleaching of the single dark tooth is best performed with tray application of the bleaching agent." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett's Table 5.1 of stains successfully bleached begins with "Mild generalized staining" and includes age-related yellowing, tobacco, diet, pulpal trauma, and mild forms of fluorosis, tetracycline and amelogenesis imperfecta (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Condition | First line | If inadequate |
|---|---|---|
| Extrinsic stain | Prophylaxis, polishing, hygiene | Bleaching for internalized nicotine stain (1–3 months) |
| Ageing / yellow / genetic | Nightguard bleaching (fast responders) | Composite or porcelain veneers if enamel thin and worn |
| Mild fluorosis, brown | Bleaching (about 80% respond; stable at seven years) (Goldstein et al, 2018) | Microabrasion |
| Fluorosis, white spots | Bleach to lessen contrast → microabrasion → resin infiltration | Composite (opaque A2 under enamel shade) |
| Moderate–severe fluorosis with pitting | Bleach as adjunct + microabrasion | Composite/porcelain veneers; crowns if deformed |
| Tetracycline 1st degree | Nightguard or in-office bleaching | — |
| Tetracycline 2nd degree | Combined or long nightguard (2–12 months; at least 2 months trial) | Veneers |
| Tetracycline 3rd/4th degree, blue-grey, banded, dark gingival third | Bleach to lighten substrate | Porcelain veneers with opaque porcelain/opaque resin, lithium disilicate, or crowns |
| Hypoplasia / hypocalcification (localized) | Microabrasion, contouring | Partial composite veneer |
| Amelogenesis / dentinogenesis imperfecta | (Bleaching difficult, painful) | Veneers or crowns |
| Single dark vital tooth (trauma, calcific metamorphosis) | Radiograph, pulp test → single-tooth tray bleaching | Composite veneer with violet modifier; veneer/crown |
| Leaking restorations, amalgam shine-through | Replace restoration | Veneer or crown |

(Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Goldstein et al, 2018; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Sikri, 2020)

**Prognostic rule:** yellow/orange/brown respond best; grey/blue, banded and cervical stains worst (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). With dark tetracycline most lightening occurs in the incisal half (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Intentional root canal treatment for internal bleaching of tetracycline teeth works but adds endodontic risk (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 19. Combination therapy: timing and sequencing

### Explanation

Bond strength after bleaching: Sturdevant's — "This reduction in bond strength results from residual oxygen or peroxide residue in the tooth" and "No loss of bond strength is noted if the composite restorative treatment is delayed at least 1 week after cessation of any bleaching." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's — "A 25% to 50% reduction in bond strength of composite to etched enamel"; "a delay of 7 to 10 days after bleaching allows dissipation of the excess oxygen"; another study found "there is a need to wait at least 2 weeks for maximum bond strength"; "Placement of the restoration should be delayed at least 1 to 2 weeks"; and "A drying agent, such as acetone, can be used to diminish the oxygen in the outer layers of the tooth" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's — "restorative procedures should be postponed to 2–3 weeks after the last bleaching"; when combining, "for in-office bleaching alone, it is about 2 weeks but if at-home treatments are included then add an additional 4 weeks" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Bartlett — "most practitioners recommend a few days between completion of bleaching and any restorative work involving bonding" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. So the recommended delay ranges from "a few days" (Bartlett) through 1 week (Sturdevant's), 1–2 weeks (Summitt's), to 2–3 weeks (Goldstein's); Goldstein's also builds in shade stabilization, which explains its longer wait. [Likely] Sequencing: "bleaching should be performed before any esthetic restorative procedures" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; "It is generally preferred to bleach teeth before orthodontia is initiated" because bonded resin hinders bleaching (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; restorations "tend to appear darker as the adjacent teeth lighten" and need replacement afterwards (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sequence for a combined plan**
1. Disease control: caries, periodontal therapy (bleach after gingival swelling resolves); temporary GIC in deep cavities (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
2. Orthodontics: bleach before brackets, or after debonding and thorough resin removal (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
3. Bleaching (one arch at a time) → evaluate.
4. Microabrasion / resin infiltration of residual spots.
5. Wait, then restorations: composite, veneers, crowns — matched to the final bleached shade (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Delay before bonding — books differ:**

| Source | Delay |
|---|---|
| Bartlett, 2011 | A few days |
| Ritter et al, 2019 | At least 1 week (no bond loss) |
| Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013 | 7–10 days; 2 weeks for maximum bond; 1–2 weeks for shade |
| Kwon and Goldstein, 2018 | 2–3 weeks; about 2 weeks after in-office, add 4 weeks if home bleaching (about 8 weeks from start) |

**Reasons:** residual oxygen inhibits resin polymerization (25–50% loss of enamel bond strength), and the shade must rehydrate and stabilize (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Acetone-based drying agents can reduce surface oxygen.

**Patient warning:** existing restorations will not lighten and look darker; plan their replacement and its cost before bleaching (Goldstein et al, 2018).

## 20. Prognosis, maintenance and conclusion

### Explanation

Longevity differs between books: Sturdevant's — "Vital bleaching usually results in tooth lightening for only 1–3 years, whereas an etched porcelain veneer should last 10–15 years or longer." (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Bartlett — "teeth that have bleached well can remain stable for 2–3 years" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Goldstein's — "Generally it may be 3 years before retreatment is desirable." and an annual touch-up "bleach usually will be recommended for the removal of any new accumulated stain" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Summitt's for tetracycline — "8 in 10 patients can expect to retain that lightening for at least 1 year" and "a very high patient satisfaction rate at 7.5 years after completion of bleaching" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.21 brown-fluorosis case stayed stable at seven years (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Maintenance: the tray can be reused; a peroxide toothpaste "may be useful in color maintenance after the dentist has whitened the teeth" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's summary: "OTC nondentist bleaching does not have a good risk-benefit ratio" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prognosis**
- Best: extrinsic, ageing, yellow/brown, mild fluorosis, first-degree tetracycline.
- Fair: second-degree tetracycline, nicotine, brown fluorosis spots (need long treatment).
- Poor for bleaching alone: blue-grey, banded or gingivally concentrated tetracycline, white spots, deep defects, AI/DI, metallic stains — these need adjunctive restorations (Ritter et al, 2019; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**Longevity — books differ:** bleaching lasts 1–3 years (Ritter et al, 2019), 2–3 years (Bartlett, 2011), about 3 years before retreatment (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); 8 in 10 tetracycline patients retain lightening at least 1 year, satisfaction high at 7.5 years (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Porcelain veneers last 10–15 years or longer (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Maintenance**
- Keep the custom tray; touch-up bleaching as needed (annually to every few years) (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).
- Peroxide or low-abrasive whitening toothpaste; limit tea, coffee, tobacco; regular prophylaxis.
- Veneers and composites: polish margins, avoid air-polishing and stannous fluoride on porcelain, night guard for bruxists; bleach teeth darkening under veneers from the lingual (Stappert et al, 2018; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Recall radiograph of any traumatized tooth.

**Conclusion.** Aesthetic rehabilitation of vital discoloured anterior teeth begins with diagnosis of the cause and the patient's expectation, and proceeds from prophylaxis, microabrasion, resin infiltration and bleaching to bonding, veneers and crowns only as required. Bleaching is almost always the first step, either as the definitive treatment or to lighten the substrate for thinner, more natural restorations. Combined, staged treatment with adequate delay before bonding gives the most conservative and durable result (Goldstein et al, 2018; Ritter et al, 2019).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: combined in-office plus home bleaching gives a better result than home bleaching alone.**
- **Textbook position:** Goldstein's calls it the most effective and most predictable technique (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); Sturdevant's says it beats either alone (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Challenge:** Summitt's cites a study showing no improvement over home bleaching alone and states the end point is the same; Bartlett agrees (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bartlett, 2011).
- **Refinement:** the office session produces quick visible change (partly dehydration) that motivates compliance; the tooth's own limit, not the technique, sets the final shade.
- **Strength:** clinical comparisons cited second-hand in Summitt's; the advocacy in Goldstein's is largely experience-based. [Likely]
- **Conclusion:** combination buys speed, not a whiter end point. [Likely]

**Claim 2: lights and lasers enhance vital bleaching.**
- **Textbook position:** Goldstein's treats activation as a matter of preference; Sikri describes many light systems and prefers the KTP laser for tetracycline (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Sikri, 2020).
- **Challenge:** Sturdevant's and Summitt's state light does not improve whitening, increases dehydration and sensitivity, and CO2 lasers are not recommended (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- **Conclusion:** the peroxide bleaches; light mainly heats and dehydrates. The KTP claim rests on one textbook. [Likely]

**Claim 3: microabrasion removes a negligible amount of enamel.**
- **Textbook position:** Sikri — clinically unrecognizable loss; Summitt's — about 22–27 μm weakened.
- **Challenge:** Bartlett (Tong et al, 1993) — about 360 μm after about 100 s with 18% HCl.
- **Conclusion:** loss is cumulative with time and acid strength; short applications of lower-concentration HCl with silicon carbide are safer, and bleaching should come first to spare fluoride-rich enamel. [Likely]

**Claim 4: pressed ceramics are unsuitable for dark stains.**
- Sturdevant's (translucent pressed ceramic) versus Goldstein's (opaque lithium disilicate masks with less reduction). The disagreement is about different materials. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Sequence: prophylaxis → micro/macroabrasion → resin infiltration → bleaching → composite → porcelain veneer → crown. Bleach first, except soft-tissue care.
- Jordan and Boksman (1984) tetracycline degrees I–III (+IV); banding and grey-blue cervical stain = poor prognosis; 2–12 months nightguard bleaching.
- 10% CP ≈ 3–3.5% HP + about 7% urea (books differ); CP ≈ three times HP.
- In-office: 30–35% HP, no etch, no anaesthesia; rubber dam (Sturdevant's) versus resin barrier 0.5 mm cervical / 2–3 mm gingiva (Goldstein's).
- Nightguard: 10–15% CP, scalloped tray, one arch at a time; normal teeth average 2 weeks.
- Microabrasion: 18% HCl + pumice (McCloskey 1984, Croll 1986) or about 11% HCl + SiC (Prema); limit 0.2–0.3 mm.
- ICON: 15% HCl 90–120 s, ethanol, TEGDMA.
- Bonding delay: a few days / 1 week / 1–2 weeks / 2–3 weeks (books differ).
- Opaque porcelain 5–15%; above 15% loses vitality.
- Bleaching lasts 1–3 years; porcelain veneers 10–15 years.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Jumping to veneers or crowns without diagnosis, prophylaxis and a trial of bleaching.
- Forgetting the radiograph and pulp test of a single dark tooth.
- Saying bleaching removes white spots (it only reduces contrast).
- Quoting one figure for carbamide-peroxide conversion or microabrasion depth as if all books agree.
- Bonding immediately after bleaching (oxygen inhibition, unstable shade).
- Prescribing in-office bleaching with heat for children.
- Treating sodium perborate as a vital bleaching agent (it is intracoronal, for non-vital teeth).
- Omitting maintenance and the need to replace existing restorations after bleaching.
