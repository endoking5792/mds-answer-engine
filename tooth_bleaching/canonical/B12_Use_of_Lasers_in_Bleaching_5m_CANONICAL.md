## 1. Principle and mechanism

### Explanation

Laser-assisted bleaching was first described "in 1998 by Reyto" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Manufacturers claim lasers "provide a powerful energy source to enhance the action of the hydrogen peroxide", but Summitt stresses that "the hydrogen peroxide performs the bleaching, not the laser" and "The laser merely serves as a heat source to enhance the bleaching agent" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. George and Walsh list a wider range of actions: "photothermal effects (warming the gel to make hydrogen peroxide more active chemically), photochemical actions (such as the Fenton reaction)", photocatalytic and "photodynamic actions, where the laser energy activates a suitable" photosensitizer, plus photo-oxidation (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC]. Sikri: "With the exception of KTP, laser activated bleaching is based on heating of the bleaching gel" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The gel must contain "a proper photocatalyst, which is an absorber that matches the spectrum of the light used" — rhodamine for blue-green wavelengths, bluish-purple dyes for red-infrared — and filtering substances "to keep the energy inside the gel" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** 25-40% hydrogen peroxide gel containing a wavelength-matched absorber (rhodamine, blue dye) is irradiated; the laser only accelerates peroxide breakdown — photothermal, photochemical or photodynamic (KTP) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; George and Walsh, 2023; Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023).

## 2. Lasers used

### Explanation

- **Argon (blue):** Summitt — "an argon laser with a wavelength of 488 nm for 30 seconds", and "The argon laser, used appropriately, generates very little temperature rise in the pulp" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri gives both 480 nm in the text and "Argon laser (488 nm)" in his FDA list; its affinity for dark stains means "the yellow- brown color can be easily removed" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Sikri's 480 nm is likely a slip for 488 nm. [Likely]
- **CO₂ (10,600 nm):** used after argon for "deeper penetration" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); "penetrates only 0.1 mm into water and H2O2" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Abandoned: "Pulpal irritation or even necrosis has been demonstrated following CO2 laser use"; "The ADA does not recommend CO2 laser use for bleaching" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); Sturdevant agrees (Ritter et al, 2019); "no longer supported" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023); "discontinued because of its thermal effect on the pulp" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].
- **Diode:** Cohen — "around 810 to 830 nm and" 970-980 nm with 30-35% gel, "1 to 2 W of laser energy for 30 seconds per tooth" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri — 980 nm, pulpal temperature rise "from 4.3°C to 16°C" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Summitt — "the diode laser can generate high temperatures that are detrimental to the pulpal tissue" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. A 445-nm diode is new and unproven (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC].
- **KTP (532 nm):** argon/KTP action is "not only photo-thermal and photochemical but photolytic as well", effective "even in teeth not responding to other techniques (tetracycline-stained teeth)" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]; green light in "a particular narrow spectral range (512 to 540 nm)" photo-oxidizes the tetracycline quinone (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; KTP photodynamic bleaching lightened "78% of the teeth treated" with tetracycline staining (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC].
- **Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG:** Nd:YAG "possibly cause overheating due to their high peak power"; erbium use "is still under investigation" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]; Sikri does not recommend Nd:YAG, Er:YAG or CO₂ (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].
- **Device note:** Coluzzi's table of current visible lasers lists tooth whitening for a blue "Indium Gallium Nitride 445" nm diode and a green "Gallium Nitride 532" nm diode (Coluzzi, 2023) [SRC] — so a 532-nm whitening laser today may be a semiconductor diode rather than a KTP crystal laser; the wavelength, not the medium, matches the rhodamine gel [Likely].
- **Delivery:** whitening handpieces have an optic window that "typically falls into the range of 2.8" to 4.0 cm², and output is set to give an "average irradiance in close proximity to the surface of the target of 250–500 mW/cm2" (Cronshaw and Mylona, 2023) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Lasers used.**
- Argon 488 nm — little pulpal heating (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- CO₂ 10,600 nm — pulpal damage; not recommended (ADA).
- Diode 810-980 nm, 1-2 W, 30 seconds/tooth — risk of pulpal heating (Setzer, 2021).
- KTP 532 nm with rhodamine — photodynamic; useful for tetracycline stains (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023).
- Nd:YAG, erbium — overheating or still investigational (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023).

## 3. Advantages, disadvantages and the efficacy controversy

### Explanation

Possible advantages: shorter chair time ("the treatment time was significantly less for the laser-activated groups") and deeper peroxide penetration (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Disadvantages: "the cost of the laser itself, which translates to higher patient fees", pulpal and periodontal risk (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC], eye protection (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

**Efficacy — books and studies disagree.** Against: "Studies of laser bleaching to date have not indicated any better results than with other in-office techniques" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; light "does not improve the whitening result beyond what the bleach alone can achieve", and "Use of lights to heat the bleaching agent, however, causes a greater level of tooth dehydration", which makes the immediate result look better than it is (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Strobl et al found "the Nd:YAG laser did not enhance the bleaching success"; Surmelioglu et al, no better aesthetic or long-term stability; the only RCT found "no significant intergroup differences in color outcome" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. For: Gurgan et al found a diode laser gave "spectrophotometric measured better outcome, with less gingival and tooth sensitivity" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Summary: "the available studies concerning laser-assisted bleaching do not provide clear conclusions" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]; long-term data are "less documented" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

**Pulpal heat threshold — books differ:** "the pulp can only tolerate a temperature rise up to 5.5 °C" (Zach and Cohen) and 42 °C sustained for 1 min may be critical, yet Baldissara et al found rises of 8.9–14.7 °C did not induce pathology (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson: "an increase of intrapulpal temperature of 3°C will cause irreversible pulpal damage" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. The 3 °C figure is stricter than the widely quoted 5.5 °C and is unreferenced in that book. [Likely]

A separate use: photobiomodulation with an infrared diode "could be recommended as a suitable strategy to reduce the intensity of tooth sensitivity" after in-office bleaching (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Cronshaw and Mylona, in the same book, are more cautious: a systematic review "identified six studies investigating the potential benefits of PBMT to post-bleaching sensitivity"; all reported success, but "The wavelengths employed ranged from 660 to 810 nm with a fluence in the range of 12–60 J/cm2", no meta-analysis was possible, and "The weight of current available evidence is insufficient to offer guidelines on the prophylaxis of post-bleaching sensitivity" (Cronshaw and Mylona, 2023) [SRC]. The two chapters disagree on strength of recommendation, not on direction [Certain that both are printed].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages:** shorter treatment time; deeper peroxide penetration; KTP for tetracycline stains (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023).
**Disadvantages:** cost, pulpal heating (pulp tolerates about 5.5 °C), sensitivity, eye protection (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
Photobiomodulation (660-810 nm) may ease post-bleaching sensitivity; evidence insufficient (Cronshaw and Mylona, 2023).
**Controversy:** most studies show no better or more stable colour than non-activated bleaching, and dehydration exaggerates the immediate result; evidence remains inconclusive (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019; Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- The peroxide bleaches; the laser accelerates it.
- Argon 488 nm, KTP 532 nm, diode 810-980 nm; CO₂ abandoned (ADA).
- KTP + rhodamine = photodynamic; best for tetracycline stains.
- Pulpal temperature rise limit about 5.5 °C (Bonsor and Pearson say 3 °C).
- Evidence of extra efficacy is inconclusive.
