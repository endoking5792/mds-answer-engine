## 1. Basis: oxidation by free radicals

### Explanation

The mechanism "is considered to be oxidation of organic pigments, although the chemistry is not well understood" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; "Contemporary bleaching techniques rely on oxidative processes that actively break down large pigmenting molecules" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's frames it as a redox reaction in which "Free reactive radicals react with the unsaturated bonds" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. "Hydrogen peroxide diffuses through the organic matrix of the enamel and dentin because of its low molecular weight" and reaches the pulp "in a matter of 5 to 15 minutes" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. "Ionization of H2O2 produces different types of free radicals" — hydroxyl and perhydroxyl ions (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Luk and Anagnostaki describe "the unstable oxygen or perhydroxyl radicals are diffusing through the organic matrix of the enamel and dentine" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Heat speeds it: "An increase of 10°C doubles the rate of chemical reaction" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; alkaline pH favours it — "The optimum pH for hydrogen peroxide ranges from 9.5 to 10.8" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology lists the species: depending on pH, light, temperature and catalysts, "free hydrogen peroxide can release various radicals" (H•, O•, OH•, HO2•) and hydroxyl or perhydroxyl ions (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri ranks perhydroxyl first: "The perhydroxyl radical is produced as compared to superoxide, which is more reactive than the superoxide and other radicals" under alkaline conditions (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Andreasen's explains why alkalinity helps: "in a basic solution, lower activation energy is required for the formation of free radicals from hydrogen peroxide" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. Grossman's redox diagram: "Tooth (reducing agent takes up electrons)", "Bleaching agent (oxidizing agent gives free electrons)" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sodium perborate and carbamide peroxide act through the hydrogen peroxide they release: sodium perborate decomposes "to sodium metaborate, hydrogen peroxide, and nascent oxygen" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle:** bleaching is an oxidation-reduction reaction. The bleaching agent (oxidizer) gives up free radicals; the stain molecule (reducer) accepts them (Gopikrishna, 2021).

1. **Penetration:** H2O2 has a low molecular weight, so it diffuses through the organic matrix of enamel and dentin, reaching the pulp in 5-15 min (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
2. **Free radical formation:** H2O2 → nascent oxygen (O•), hydroxyl (OH•) and perhydroxyl (HO2•) radicals; perhydroxyl is the most reactive and forms in alkaline pH (optimum 9.5-10.8) (Sikri, 2020; Gopikrishna, 2021). Heat speeds the reaction (doubles per 10°C).

## 2. Chromophore breakdown and the saturation point

### Explanation

"Bleaching agent opens the more highly pigmented carbon ring (yellow color) and converts it to carbon chains"; "When the carbon double bonds are converted to hydroxyl groups, the amount of light absorbed is reduced" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's: double bonds of high-molecular-weight pigments "are broken down by the chemical reactions with the free radicals", changing the reflected wavelength; afterwards "a shade rebound effect may be observed, resulting from a reformation of double bonds of the molecules" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. "Saturation point is that point at which only hydrophilic colorless tooth structure exists"; "Bleaching beyond this point results in the breakdown of the organic matrix resulting in weakening of the enamel and surface porosities" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; "At this point, bleaching should be stopped immediately, or the next step would be a rapid loss of enamel" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. The same authors note the stain-breakdown "hypothesis might be weak since" chromophores in enamel are scarcely detectable and enamel grows more opaque; they propose "the peroxide whitens teeth by oxidizing its transparent organic matrix into an opaque whiter material" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Ingle's adds "other possible mechanisms include cleansing of tooth surface, temporary dehydration of enamel during the bleaching process" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Andreasen's: the radicals "attack the long chained, dark colored chromophore molecules and split them into smaller, less colored and more diffusible molecules" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. End products: Cohen's puts carbon dioxide and water beyond the whitening step — "Further degradation over this step may complete disintegration of the organic matrix with the release of carbon dioxide and water" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC] — so CO2 and water mark over-bleaching [Likely]. Slip: Bonsor and Pearson write "The smaller molecules so produced reflect less light" and Alani and Patel that they "are less able to reflect light than the previous large chromophore molecules" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; the smaller molecules absorb less light and so reflect more, as Luk and Anagnostaki put it, "which absorb less and therefore reflect more light" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC] [Likely]. The chromophore account is the textbook standard but remains a hypothesis [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

3. **Chromophore breakdown:** radicals attack the conjugated double bonds of pigment molecules. Pigmented carbon rings open into chains; double bonds become hydroxyl groups; large molecules become smaller, simpler, lighter ones that absorb less light (Gopikrishna, 2021; Setzer, 2021).
4. **End products:** further oxidation, beyond whitening, gives CO2 and water (Setzer, 2021).
5. **Saturation point:** only hydrophilic, colourless structures remain; lightening plateaus. Continuing breaks down the enamel organic matrix — porosity, weakness, sensitivity — so stop (Gopikrishna, 2021; Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023).
6. **Rebound:** re-formation of double bonds and rehydration cause slight darkening (Setzer, 2021).

## 3. Carbamide peroxide breakdown

### Explanation

Breakdown of 10% carbamide peroxide — nine books, five figures (the same set is shown in B02, B04, B05, B10 and B11): Summitt's "6.5% urea and 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Torabinejad's "approximately 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" with urea, ammonia and carbon dioxide (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's 10-30% "(equivalent to 3.5% to 8.6% hydrogen peroxide)", although its home-bleaching section gives 10-22% "with an effective yield of 4% to 7.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's "approximately 3.4% hydrogen peroxide and 6.5% urea", although its reaction scheme a page earlier writes "+ 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson's "release 3.3% hydrogen peroxide", the one-third rule (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's "3% hydrogen peroxide (active ingredient) and 7% urea" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri's "10% carbamide peroxide is equal to 3.0% hydrogen peroxide" in both his books (Sikri, 2020; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Alani and Patel's "approximately 3% hydrogen peroxide over a period of 4 h" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Reasoning: the crystals "contain H2O2 in a concentration of about 35%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], so 10% gives about 3.5%; 3.3% and 3% are rounded one-third figures [Likely]. At higher strengths the books fall below that proportion: Cohen's 30% = 8.6% (proportionally 10.5%) and Summitt's "A 35% carbamide peroxide product is approximately equivalent to a 9.5% hydrogen peroxide product" (proportionally about 12%) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; neither book explains the shortfall [Likely; the arithmetic is the auditor's]. "The carbamide peroxide dissociates into H2O2 and urea"; "The urea degrades into ammonia and carbon dioxide" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Luk and Anagnostaki describe "two parts of ammonia and carbon dioxide and one part of hydrogen peroxide" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. That figure caption is a slip: it calls the first step "The first phase is the reduction of carbamide peroxide into two parts of ammonia and carbon dioxide" and says "Hydrogen peroxide can be further reduced (A) into an oxygen radical and water" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC] — carbamide peroxide dissociates (it is not reduced) into urea and hydrogen peroxide, and only the urea breaks down to ammonia and CO2; the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen is not a reduction of the whole molecule [Likely]. Andreasen's is more cautious about the ammonia: urea "theoretically can be further decomposed", and "It is, however, unclear how much ammonia will be formed during tooth bleaching using carbamide peroxide" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. Urea raises pH: "Urea is responsible for maintaining an alkaline environment (pH 8) which potentiates the action of hydrogen peroxide" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Summitt's: above 8 "for a number of hours" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Carbamide peroxide:** CO(NH2)2·H2O2 → urea + H2O2 (about one-third). 10% CP ≈ 3.5% H2O2 + 6.5% urea (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 3.4% (Gopikrishna, 2021); 3% + 7% urea (Ritter et al, 2019). Urea → ammonia + CO2, raising pH to about 8, which potentiates H2O2 (Gopikrishna, 2021). H2O2 → water + oxygen radicals, which bleach as above.

**Limitation:** exact chemistry unknown; dehydration, surface cleansing and oxidation of the enamel matrix to a more opaque material also contribute (Rotstein, 2019; Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Redox: oxidizer = bleach, reducer = stain; low molecular weight H2O2 diffuses in.
- Radicals: O•, OH•, HO2• (perhydroxyl, most reactive, alkaline pH).
- Rings → chains → hydroxyl groups → CO2 + H2O; less light absorbed.
- Saturation point: stop, or the enamel matrix breaks down.
- 10% carbamide peroxide ≈ 3.5% H2O2 + 6.5% urea.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Saying the stain is "dissolved" or "washed out" rather than oxidized.
- Omitting the saturation point.
- Writing that carbamide peroxide bleaches directly; it acts through the hydrogen peroxide it releases.
