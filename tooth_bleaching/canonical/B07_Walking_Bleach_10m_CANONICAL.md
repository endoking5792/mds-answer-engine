## 1. Definition and history

### Explanation

Early accounts describe walking bleach as "leaving a mix of sodium perborate and distilled water in the pulp chamber for a period of several days", sealed under a temporary restoration; "Later, the use of 30% hydrogen peroxide instead of water was suggested to provide better bleaching effectiveness"; perborate with water or anaesthetic solution "remains the most commonly used technique for internal bleaching of nonvital teeth" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Dahl and Pallesen date it: "In the walking bleach technique that was introduced in 1961, the bleaching was performed between the visits", with perborate and water; "The method was later modified replacing the water with 30–35% hydrogen peroxide to improve the whitening effect" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. **Who gets the credit — books differ.** West et al: "This “walking bleach” procedure was introduced by Spasser over 50 years ago" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]; Rotstein says the term was first coined by Nutting and Poe, "referring to the bleaching action occurring between patients’ visits" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Kwon and Goldstein write that Nutting and Poe's technique "went another step: Superoxol and sodium perborate are sealed in the pulp chamber for as long as a week" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. The two accounts fit together: Spasser (1961) sealed perborate and water; Nutting and Poe (1963) added Superoxol and named the method [Likely]. The modern technique has gone back to perborate and water. "The walking bleach technique should be used in all situations requiring internal bleaching" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; "Walking bleach is preferred since it is safer, more comfortable for the patient, and requires less chair time" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** intracoronal bleaching of an endodontically treated tooth in which an oxidizing paste is sealed in the pulp chamber and works between visits, hence "walking".

**History:** introduced in 1961 (Spasser) as sodium perborate and water sealed in the chamber; Nutting and Poe added 30–35% hydrogen peroxide (Superoxol) and named it; today perborate with water is used again (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007; West et al, 2018; Rotstein, 2019). Technique of choice: safe, least chair time (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021).

## 2. Case selection

### Explanation

Indications: "discolorations of pulp chamber origin; (2) dentin discolorations; and (3) discolorations that are not amenable to external bleaching"; contraindications: "superficial enamel discolorations; (2) defective enamel formation; (3) severe dentin loss; (4) presence of caries" and discoloured composites (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Dahl and Pallesen's unquestionable contraindications "are unhealthy periodontal tissue, insufficient root filling or a heavily restored tooth" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; "The crowns of teeth to be bleached should be relatively intact" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. A failed root filling must be redone first: "retreatment of the existing endodontic filling is advised" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Timing: "the walking bleach technique may be initiated at the same appointment as the obturation" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** pulp-chamber and dentine discoloration (necrosis, haemorrhage, pulp remnants, materials) not amenable to external bleaching. **Contraindications:** superficial enamel stain, defective enamel, severe dentine loss, caries, heavily restored crown, unhealthy periodontium, inadequate root filling (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021; Dahl and Pallesen, 2007).

## 3. Technique, step by step

### Explanation

Assessment and isolation: radiograph, shade and photographs; "Rubber dam isolation is mandatory" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; gingival cream is needed only with peroxide — "This protection is not required with sodium perborate use" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Access: remove all restorative, sealer and pulp remnants including the pulp horns; "The practitioner should exercise care to avoid labial perforations"; "Intracoronal materials should be removed to a level just below the labial gingival margin" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

**How far to remove gutta-percha — books differ.** Just below the labial gingival margin (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman: "The remaining root canal filling should be vertically condensed with finger pluggers to 1 mm apical to the cementoenamel junction" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; West et al: "it should be removed 2 mm apical to the cervical line" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]; Dahl and Pallesen remove gutta-percha only when the root itself is stained — "If the root is discolored and needs bleaching, it is necessary to remove 2–3mm of the coronal guttapercha" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC].

**The protective barrier — thickness, level and whether it is needed.**
| Book | Barrier |
|---|---|
| Setzer, 2021 | "A protective layer of at least a 2-mm thickness should be placed over the root filling material"; "Ideally, the vertical outline of the protective barrier should be 1 mm coronally to the CEJ to cover lingual, labial, and proximal CEJ areas" [SRC] |
| Rotstein, 2019 | "at least 2 mm, of a protective white cement barrier" (GIC, IRM, Cavit, polycarboxylate, zinc phosphate, white MTA) [SRC] |
| Sikri, 2019; West et al, 2018 | "The internal level of barrier should be one millimeter incisal to the corresponding external probing of the epithelial attachment"; "The bleach barrier must be placed 1 mm incisal to the undulating attachment apparatus" [SRC] |
| Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021 | "If hydrogen peroxide is used, a sufficient layer of protective cement" barrier; "It should not extend incisal to the gingival margin"; with perborate and water "a protective cement barrier and gingival protection are unnecessary" [SRC] |
| Baumann and Beer, 2010 | the glass-ionomer cover "ends at the height of the gingival margin" [SRC] |

So Torabinejad's chapter makes the barrier optional with perborate-water, while Cohen's, Ingle's, Grossman's and Sikri's make it routine; and Torabinejad's "not incisal to the gingival margin" differs from Sikri's and West's "1 mm incisal to the epithelial attachment" [Certain that they differ]. Safest exam answer: always place it, following the attachment.

**Acid etching — books differ.** "Acid etching of dentin internally with phosphoric (or other) acid to remove the smear layer and open the tubules is not necessary" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Endodontology recommends "etching with 37.5% phosphoric acid for 15 seconds is recommended" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Setzer: "Acidic pretreatment may increase the risk adverse effects on the periodontium" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

**The paste.** Sodium perborate "is mixed with an inert liquid (distilled water, saline, or anesthetic solution) to achieve the consistency of wet sand"; peroxide, if needed, "should be used at 3% strength", and "The long-term results of a sodium perborate and water mix do not significantly differ from mixes with hydrogen peroxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; ratio "to the consistency of wet sand (approximately 2 g/mL)" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC], as in Endodontology: "a mixture of sodium perborate (mono-, tri-, or tetrahydrate) and water (ratio: 2 g/mL) is recommended" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Rotstein allows "gradually increasing concentrations of H2O2 (3% to 30%) instead of water" only for resistant cases (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Older wording persists: Sikri's technique uses "A thick paste of sodium perborate and 35% H2O2 is" placed (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and Kwon and Goldstein mix perborate "and enough 35% hydrogen peroxide to form a thick white paste" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC] — this is the superseded Nutting–Poe mix, not current practice [Likely].

**Seal and recall.** Temporary filling "ideally 3 mm thick with well sealing margins" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; IRM or Cavit "is packed carefully to a thickness of at least 3 mm to ensure a good seal" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Dahl and Pallesen accept "A 2mm layer of Cavit® or IRM® cement" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. **Recall interval — books differ:** "between 3 and 10 days" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; "The patient should return in 3–7 days for evaluation of the result" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; "Evaluate the patient 2 weeks later" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; "approximately 2 to 4 weeks later" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; "The bleaching agent is changed weekly" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; "The bleaching paste remains inside the tooth for about 7 days" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. "On average, most discolorations (75%) can be handled in one to two appointments" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; "If a clinical case does not show improvement after three to four attempts", re-diagnose (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Avoid overbleaching: "A tooth that is too light may be as unesthetic as one that is too dark" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Steps**
1. Radiograph (retreat a poor root filling), shade record, photographs.
2. Rubber dam (Setzer, 2021).
3. Remove restoration, sealer and pulp remnants including pulp horns.
4. Remove gutta-percha just below the labial gingival margin (Setzer) or 1 mm apical to the CEJ (Grossman) or 2 mm (West et al).
5. **Barrier** (GIC, RMGIC, IRM, Cavit, MTA) at least 2 mm thick over the root filling (Setzer, 2021; Rotstein, 2019), following the epithelial attachment — 1 mm incisal to the probed attachment (Sikri, 2019; West et al, 2018). Rotstein and Silvestrin (2021) consider it essential only when peroxide is used.
6. No acid etching (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021); Baumann and Beer (2010) etch — books differ.
7. Sodium perborate + water/saline/anaesthetic, wet-sand consistency (about 2 g/mL), packed; 3% hydrogen peroxide only for resistant stains (Setzer, 2021).
8. Temporary seal (IRM/Cavit) at least 3 mm thick (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021).
9. Recall: 3–7 days (Grossman), 3–10 days (Setzer), weekly (Dahl and Pallesen) or 2–4 weeks (Rotstein and Silvestrin); reassess after three to four attempts; avoid overbleaching.

## 4. Final restoration

### Explanation

**Delay before bonding — books differ:** "about 1 to 3 weeks after the last appointment" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; "only after an interval of a few days" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; "waiting for at least 7 days after bleaching, prior to restoring the tooth with resin composites, has been recommended" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; "an approximately 3-week interval before the" adhesive restoration (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Restore with a light base and composite: "the chamber must not be filled totally with composite; this may cause a loss of translucency of the tooth"; "It is easy and effective to fill the chamber with a light-colored gutta-percha temporary stopping, glass ionomer, or a light shade of zinc phosphate cement"; "Light curing from the labial, rather than the lingual, surface is recommended" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. **Calcium hydroxide — books differ:** Grossman: "To reduce the possibility of resorption, immediately after bleaching, a paste of calcium hydroxide powder and sterile water is placed in the pulp chamber" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and Sturdevant leaves it "to remain in the pulp chamber for 2 weeks" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Rotstein and Silvestrin: "however, this procedure is ineffective and unnecessary" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Follow-up: "The patient should be scheduled for yearly follow-ups" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Final restoration:** after the delay (a few days — Rotstein and Silvestrin; at least 7 days — Rotstein; 1–3 weeks — Setzer; about 3 weeks — Baumann and Beer), place a light base (GIC/zinc phosphate) and acid-etched composite, light-cured from labial; never fill the chamber entirely with composite (loss of translucency). Calcium hydroxide dressing: recommended by Grossman, called unnecessary by Rotstein and Silvestrin. Yearly recall.

## 5. Complications and prognosis

### Explanation

Perborate-water is the safe choice: "it did not cause cervical resorption, even 1 year after bleaching" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], whereas resorption "affects about 7% of the teeth that have undergone internal in-office bleaching" with high-concentration peroxide (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] (details in P1). Fracture: "It appears that bleached teeth are no more susceptible to fracture if properly restored" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Prognosis: "More than 90% immediate success has been reported for intracoronal bleaching of non-vital teeth"; "The need for retreatment increased with the observation time, 10% after 1–2 years", reaching "40% in teeth observed up to 8 years" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Complications:** cervical resorption (mainly 30–35% hydrogen peroxide with heat), chemical burns, fracture, rediscoloration (Setzer, 2021; Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021). **Prognosis:** over 90% immediate success; retreatment 10% at 1–2 years rising to 40% by 8 years (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Perborate + water (wet sand, about 2 g/mL) is the agent of choice; 3% H2O2 only if resistant.
- Barrier at least 2 mm over the gutta-percha, shaped to the epithelial attachment; the proximal CEJ is the weak point.
- Temporary seal at least 3 mm; recall 3–10 days (books range up to 2–4 weeks).
- No heat, no routine acid etching, no 30% H2O2.
- Delay composite (days to 3 weeks, books differ); light base under composite.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing "sodium perborate + 30% Superoxol" as the routine mix (that is the old Nutting–Poe mix).
- Putting the barrier flat at the labial CEJ, leaving the proximal tubules exposed.
- Forgetting rubber dam or the radiographic check of the root filling.
- Restoring with composite at the same visit the bleach is removed.
- Filling the whole chamber with composite.
