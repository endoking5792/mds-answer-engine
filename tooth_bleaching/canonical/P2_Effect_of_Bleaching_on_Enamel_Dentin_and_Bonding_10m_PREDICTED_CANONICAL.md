## 1. Effect on enamel

### Explanation

"A number of in vitro studies reported changes in enamel microhardness and morphology"; "A treatment with 10% carbamide peroxide and 7.5% hydrogen peroxide did alter the enamel surface micromorphology but not its microhardness"; "At least in vital teeth, however, these changes appear not to be permanent"; SEM porosity and erosion: "These changes were mostly seen when acidic prerinses or bleaching gels with a low pH had been applied"; "One study found no differences between bleached and unbleached human enamel over a 6-month period when a 10% carbamide peroxide gel was used"; "A few case reports described significant enamel damage after over-the-counter products had been applied by patients"; and "Bleaching with hydrogen peroxide increased the demineralization in teeth affected by incipient caries lesions" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Summitt: low-pH solutions produce calcium loss "along with a slight loss in surface hardness to a depth of approximately 25" µm, but "this loss has not been shown to be significant because the surface quickly remineralizes after the procedure is completed"; "no obvious morphological or chemical composition alterations of enamel surface were detected in the neutral or alkaline bleaching solutions"; 6 hours of 10% CP changes enamel less "than when a carbonated drink is consumed in a 2- to 3-minute period" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri is more cautious: "High concentrations of carbamide peroxide damage enamel surface integrity; however, lesser than phosphoric acid etch"; radicals act by "reducing calcium/phosphate ratio, enamel microhardness and surface morphology; subsequently, lead to increased caries risk" — yet "use of 10% carbamide peroxide daily for six months did not adversely affect the surface texture of human enamel" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman: "Caustic nature of hydrogen peroxide causes reduction in the enamel microhardness" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; over-bleaching: "Bleaching beyond this point results in the breakdown of the organic matrix resulting in weakening of the enamel and surface porosities" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Alani and Patel: "Some studies illustrate loss of the apris"matic layer, others reduced hardness, of uncertain clinical relevance (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. The books agree on in-vitro softening and disagree on its clinical weight: Summitt and Setzer call it transient; Sikri links it to caries risk [Certain that they differ].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Enamel**
- Reduced microhardness, increased porosity, slight erosion and loss of the aprismatic layer — mostly in vitro and mostly with low-pH (acidic) gels or prerinses (Setzer, 2021; Alani and Patel, 2025).
- Depth small (about 25 µm); calcium loss less than from a carbonated drink; remineralizes quickly (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). 10% CP for 6 months: no change in enamel texture (Sikri, 2019).
- Reduced Ca/P ratio may raise caries risk; incipient lesions demineralize more (Sikri, 2019; Setzer, 2021).
- Over-bleaching past the saturation point breaks down the organic matrix (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 2. Effect on dentin

### Explanation

"Ca and PO4 ions ratio of dentin was modified following 7 days of continuous treatment with 30% H2O2 or 10% carbamide peroxide solutions"; "A transient decrease in surface micro hardness has been observed; however, it is recovered following treatment with 0.05 fluoride solution"; "oxygen radicals damage the dentin substrate (hybrid layer)" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Internally, "internal bleaching with 30% hydrogen peroxide has been found to reduce the micro-hardness of dentin and enamel" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**2. Dentin:** altered Ca/PO4 ratio and transient loss of microhardness (recovered with fluoride); radicals damage the hybrid-layer substrate (Sikri, 2019); 30% H2O2 used internally softens dentine and enamel (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007).

## 3. Effect on bonding of new composite

### Explanation

Mechanism: "Polymerization and bonding strength of composite resins to dentin and enamel are temporarily compromised by the oxygen released during a bleaching process" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; "Consensus exists that the adhesive bond strength between glass-ionomer cements and composite resins to dentin and enamel is temporarily compromised" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; "This reduction in the bond strength results from the residual oxygen or peroxide residue in the tooth that inhibits the setting of the bonding resin", preventing resin-tag formation (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; "The interprismatic residual oxygen can result in a reduction of infiltration and polymeri"sation (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; "Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) examination has shown an increase in resin porosity" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Size: "A 25% to 50% reduction in bond strength of composite to etched enamel, due to oxygen inhibition of polymerization of the adhesive resin tags" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; "by up to 60% if the bonding procedure is carried out immediately after tooth bleaching treatment" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; "the dentin-composite bond was less sensitive as compared to enamel-composite bond" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Agent matters: "Sodium perborate mixed with water can result in much less loss of bond strength than does concentrated hydrogen peroxide" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**3. Bonding:** residual oxygen/peroxide in enamel and dentine inhibits resin polymerization and tag formation; enamel bond falls 25–50% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013), up to 60% if bonded immediately (Alani and Patel, 2025); the effect is temporary; the dentine bond is less affected (Sikri, 2019); perborate-water causes less loss than concentrated H2O2 (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021).

## 4. How long to wait — the books differ

### Explanation

| Book | Recommended delay before bonding |
|---|---|
| Sikri, 2019 | "tooth-bleaching agents should not be used at least 24 hours prior to treatment with resin-based materials"; also "24 hours to four weeks have been quoted in various studies" [SRC] |
| Dahl and Pallesen, 2007 | bond strength "was reduced in the first 24 hours after bleaching"; "Lag time from 24 hours (32, 33) up to 1–3 weeks (34, 35) has been suggested"; recommend final restoration "1–3 weeks after completion of the bleaching" [SRC] |
| Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021 | "only after an interval of a few days" [SRC] |
| Gopikrishna, 2021; Ritter et al, 2019 | "No loss of bond strength is noted if the composite restorative treatment is delayed at least 1 week after cessation of any bleaching" [SRC] |
| Rotstein, 2019 | "waiting for at least 7 days after bleaching" [SRC] |
| Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013 | "a delay of 7 to 10 days"; "there is a need to wait at least 2 weeks for maximum bond strength"; elsewhere "A delay of 1 to 2 weeks or more" [SRC] |
| Kwon and Goldstein, 2018 | "Placement of the final restoration is delayed for 2 weeks to allow the oxygen generated during bleaching to dissipate from the tooth and the shade to stabilize" [SRC] |
| Setzer, 2021 | "about 1 to 3 weeks after the last appointment"; "Optimal bonding to bleached dentin and enamel was shown to be reestablished after 3 weeks"; "wait for 2 to 3 weeks" [SRC] |
| Baumann and Beer, 2010 | "an approximately 3-week interval before the" adhesive restoration [SRC] |
| Alani and Patel, 2025 | "returns to normal after approximately 4 weeks"; delay "for at least 2–4 weeks" [SRC] |

The range is 24 hours to 4 weeks; 1–3 weeks is the commonest band. Two separate reasons favour the longer wait: bond recovery and shade stability — "Waiting 1 to 2 weeks is also important in resin bonding to allow the shade of the bleached teeth to stabilize" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; "there will be some rebound or relapse in colour approximately 2 weeks after" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Orthodontics: "no bleaching procedures should take place 2 to 3 weeks prior to any adhesive placement of brackets" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer's own chapter gives three figures (1–3, 2–3, 3 weeks) [Certain].

#### ✎ Exam core

**4. Waiting period (books differ):** 24 hours (Sikri, 2019); a few days (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021); at least 1 week (Gopikrishna, 2021; Ritter et al, 2019); 7–10 days to 2 weeks (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 2 weeks (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018); 1–3 weeks (Setzer, 2021; Dahl and Pallesen, 2007); about 3 weeks (Baumann and Beer, 2010); 2–4 weeks (Alani and Patel, 2025). Waiting also lets the shade stabilize (rebound at about 2 weeks).

## 5. Reversing the oxygen effect (when bonding cannot wait)

### Explanation

Setzer lists "dehydrating agents such as 80% alcohol and the use of acetone-containing adhesives", sodium hypochlorite, catalases, "the antioxidant sodium ascorbate" and alpha-tocopherol (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], noting that hypochlorite rinsing, "which, however, was demonstrated to have similar effects on bonding" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman: "Sodium ascorbate is a buffered form of vitamin C that consists of 90% ascorbic acid bound to 10% sodium" — an antioxidant that removes residual oxygen (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri: "10% sodium ascorbate has been found to be effective in reversing the compromised bonding of composite with enamel previously bleached with 10% carbamide peroxide", "the increase was proportional to the duration of application"; "tocopherol and grape seed extract have also been found to be effective"; "acetone based adhesive systems should be used because these have been shown to reverse the adverse effects of bleaching on enamel bond strengths" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Catalase: "One study reported that 3 minutes of catalase treatment e"ffectively removed residual H2O2 (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt: "A drying agent, such as acetone, can be used to diminish the oxygen in the outer layers of the tooth" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**5. Antioxidants and other aids:** 10% sodium ascorbate (buffered vitamin C) reverses the bond loss, more with longer application (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021); catalase (3 minutes, Rotstein, 2019); alpha-tocopherol, grape seed extract; acetone- or alcohol-based adhesives/drying (Setzer, 2021; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 6. Effect on existing restorations

### Explanation

"Bleaching has little or no effect on most of the common restorative" materials; "Although bleaching releases much oxygen into the tooth, the bond of existing restorations is not weakened"; composite surface "is roughened slightly, and the hardness may be slightly decreased, but neither effect is clinically" significant; "Bleaching has no effect on porcelain. It does encourage the release of mercury from some types of amalgam restorations"; "Bleaching does affect methyl methacrylate provisional restorative materials, causing them to yellow slightly"; and "previously placed restorations may appear comparatively dark" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Setzer: "The microhardness of methacrylate-based composite resins can be affected by carbamide peroxide in home bleaching concentrations" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri: GIC solubility increases (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and "The nonvital bleaching procedures adversely affect the marginal seal leading to marginal leakage" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Restore the access with light curing from labial: "Light curing from the labial, rather than the lingual, surface is recommended" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**6. Existing restorations:** bond of existing restorations not weakened; composite slightly roughened/softer; porcelain unaffected; mercury released from amalgam; acrylic provisionals yellow; GIC solubility and marginal leakage increase; old restorations look darker and may need replacing for colour (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Sikri, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Enamel changes are small, mostly in vitro, worst with acidic gels, and reversible.
- Residual oxygen = the cause of bond loss (25–60%); temporary.
- Wait: 24 hours to 4 weeks across books; 1–3 weeks commonest.
- Sodium ascorbate 10% reverses the effect.
- Existing restorations: no debonding, but colour mismatch.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Saying bleaching weakens existing bonded restorations.
- Giving one waiting period as if universal.
- Presenting in-vitro enamel softening as proven clinical damage.
- Forgetting shade stabilization as a reason to delay.

## MNEMONIC
**"ROAD"**: **R**esidual oxygen, **O**xygen inhibits polymerization, **A**scorbate reverses it, **D**elay 1–3 weeks.
