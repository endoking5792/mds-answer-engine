## Explanation

**Definition and mechanism.** "Acid abrasion or microabrasion is essentially a simultaneous process of erosion and abrasion" used for superficial enamel defects (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; it "does not remove stains or defects through any bleaching phenomena" and is "indicated only for stains or enamel defects that do not extend beyond a few tenths of a millimetre in depth" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's describes the slurry as "a combination of hydrochloric acid (muriatic acid) and pumice with mechanical abrasion, which will etch the enamel slightly to facilitate stain removal" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

**Agents.** "In 1984, McCloskey reported the use of 18% hydrochloric acid swabbed on teeth for the removal of superficial fluorosis stains."; "in 1986, Croll and Cavanaugh modified the technique to include the use of pumice with 18% hydrochloric acid"; Croll then went on "reducing the concentration of the hydrochloric acid to approximately 11%" with "silicon carbide particles (in a water-soluble gel paste) instead of pumice" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], sold as "microabrasion slurry (Prema, Premier, Opalustre, Ultradent)" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri keeps "preferably 18% HCl" with pumice ("Microabrasion is a procedure involving the surface dissolution of the stains of enamel by an acid (preferably 18% HCl) along with abrasives (pumice powder).") (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

**Indications and contraindications.** Defects "confined to the superficial 0.2–0.3 mm of enamel" give "clinically unrecognizable enamel loss" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; idiopathic spots fail if "the depth of the defect exceeding 0.2–0.3 mm", fluorosis responds "if the discolouration is within the 0.2–0.3-mm removal depth limit", "an accurate prognosis for microabrasion cannot be given" in advance, and near the CEJ "enamel is too thin to permit micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri lists "Enamel fluorosis (initial stages)" and "Small decalcified lesions, may be due to orthodontic bands." as indications and "Stains of amelogenesis imperfecta, dentinogenesis imperfecta and tetracycline stains" and "Hypoplastic lesion" as contraindications (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Bartlett: "it should not be used for defects in dentine" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

**Technique.** Isolation: "rubber dam is therefore mandatory" because of the acid (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Application with a "special rubber cup that has fluted edges" in "A 10× gear reduction, low-speed handpiece" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], or "special rubber agitators similar to a rubber point or a rotating rubber cup in a speed-reducing handpiece" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Application time differs: Summitt's — it "is applied to the enamel with a rubber cup or stiff bristle brush for 20 to 30 seconds" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri — "Microabrasive agent is applied to the tooth surface for 60 seconds, followed by rinsing." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Finish: "The treated areas are polished with a fluoride-containing prophy paste to restore surface lustre" and "a topical fluoride is applied to teeth to enhance remineralization" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Depth removed — books differ.** Summitt's: the acid leaves "the outer 22 to 27 μm weakened enough so that the abrasive, with which the acid is mixed, quickly removes the stained outer surface" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Bartlett, citing Tong et al (1993): with 18% HCl and pumice "for approximately 100 seconds, about 360" ± 130 μm of enamel is lost (± and μ lost in the extracted text) (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. The figures measure different things — loss per application versus cumulative loss over about 100 s — so the amount removed depends on time and acid strength. [Likely]

**Results and limits.** "A series of three microabrasion sessions (Opalustre, Ultradent) was sufficient to remove the hypocalcification" in Goldstein's case (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; but "Microabrasion failed to improve the appearance in this case." in Bartlett's fluorosis patient (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], and "teeth sometimes appear more yellow after treatment" as enamel thins (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. For brown fluorosis "Usually, microabrasion is considered the primary treatment.", yet "attempting bleaching first avoids the removal of the fluoride-rich enamel layer" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Summitt's: "It is best to bleach first unless the tooth enamel is of a chalky texture." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. "Micro-abrasion has the advantage of ensuring better control of the removal of tooth structure." than macroabrasion, and "micro-abrasion is recommended over macro-abrasion for the treatment of superficial defects in children" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** simultaneous erosion (acid) and abrasion (abrasive) that removes a thin layer of discoloured enamel; it does not bleach (Bartlett, 2011; Ritter et al, 2019).

**Agents:** 18% HCl + pumice (McCloskey 1984; Croll and Cavanaugh 1986); about 11% HCl + silicon carbide gel — Prema, Opalustre (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Indications:** defects within 0.2–0.3 mm of enamel — mild fluorosis, idiopathic white/brown spots, post-orthodontic decalcification (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
**Contraindications:** dentin defects, AI, DI, tetracycline, hypoplastic pits, thin cervical enamel (Sikri, 2020).

**Technique:** shade, photograph → rubber dam, glasses → fluted rubber cup, 10:1 gear-reduced handpiece, 20–30 s (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) or 60 s (Sikri, 2020) → rinse, inspect wet, repeat → fluoride prophy paste → topical fluoride (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Enamel removed — books differ:** 22–27 μm per application (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); about 360 μm after about 100 s (Bartlett, 2011).

**Results:** smooth, lustrous enamel; deeper residual defects need composite. Bleach first to spare fluoride-rich enamel (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- McCloskey 1984 (18% HCl); Croll and Cavanaugh 1986 (+ pumice); Prema about 11% HCl + SiC.
- Limit 0.2–0.3 mm; rubber dam mandatory; slow gear-reduced handpiece.
- Removes tissue, does not bleach; bleach first.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
- [DIAGRAM: Enamel section | stained surface layer within 0.2–0.3 mm removed by acid-abrasive in a fluted rubber cup; deeper defect left for composite]

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling microabrasion a bleaching technique.
- Using it on tetracycline stain or dentin defects.
- Giving one "depth removed" figure without the application time.

## MNEMONIC
**"Acid + Abrasive, Above 0.3 Abandon"**: acid plus abrasive; defects deeper than 0.3 mm need composite.
