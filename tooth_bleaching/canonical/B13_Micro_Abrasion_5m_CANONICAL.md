## Explanation

**Definition and mechanism.** "Acid abrasion or microabrasion is essentially a simultaneous process of erosion and abrasion" used for superficial enamel defects (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; it "does not remove stains or defects through any bleaching phenomena" and is "indicated only for stains or enamel defects that do not extend beyond a few tenths of a millimetre in depth" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's describes the slurry as "a combination of hydrochloric acid (muriatic acid) and pumice with mechanical abrasion, which will etch the enamel slightly to facilitate stain removal" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

**Agents.** "In 1984, McCloskey reported the use of 18% hydrochloric acid swabbed on teeth for the removal of superficial fluorosis stains."; "in 1986, Croll and Cavanaugh modified the technique to include the use of pumice with 18% hydrochloric acid"; Croll then went on "reducing the concentration of the hydrochloric acid to approximately 11%" with "silicon carbide particles (in a water-soluble gel paste) instead of pumice" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], sold as "microabrasion slurry (Prema, Premier, Opalustre, Ultradent)" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri keeps "preferably 18% HCl" with pumice ("Microabrasion is a procedure involving the surface dissolution of the stains of enamel by an acid (preferably 18% HCl) along with abrasives (pumice powder).") (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Grossman's dates the idea earlier — "McCloskey reported that Kane successfully removed fluorosis stains by applying acid and heat in 1916" — and describes PREMA as "an abrasive paste containing HCl, silicon gel, silicon carbide, and silica gel" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Walton's makes the 18% acid by dilution: "A 36% hydrochloric acid solution is mixed with an equal volume of distilled water to make an 18% hydrochloric acid solution." (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**McInnes versus acid-pumice.** The older McInnes solution (36% HCl, 30% H2O2 and ether, 5:5:1) does oxidize: "The acid-pumice method does not actually bleach the stain as in the McInnes technique" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Grossman's modified McInnes "Comprises 20% sodium hydroxide instead of 36% hydrochloric acid.", saying "it dissolves calcium of the enamel at a slower rate" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] — a doubtful mechanism, since an alkali does not dissolve hydroxyapatite the way an acid does [Likely; no book read explains it]. Grossman's also heads its microabrasion steps "The technique of vital bleaching is as follows:" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and Sikri's *Essentials* lists "a. Thermocatalytic bleaching b. Nonthermocatalytic bleaching c. Power bleaching d. Microabrasion" as in-office bleaching (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Both blur the rule that acid-pumice microabrasion "is not a true bleaching (oxidizing) technique but decalcification and removal of a thin layer of stained enamel." (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC] — a slip in classification [Certain]. Grossman's clinical note also prints "considerably faster and easier than microabrasion", which Sturdevant's gives as macro-abrasion (see B01) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Indications and contraindications.** Defects "confined to the superficial 0.2–0.3 mm of enamel" give "clinically unrecognizable enamel loss" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; idiopathic spots fail if "the depth of the defect exceeding 0.2–0.3 mm", fluorosis responds "if the discolouration is within the 0.2–0.3-mm removal depth limit", "an accurate prognosis for microabrasion cannot be given" in advance, and near the CEJ "enamel is too thin to permit micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri lists "Enamel fluorosis (initial stages)" and "Small decalcified lesions, may be due to orthodontic bands." as indications and "Stains of amelogenesis imperfecta, dentinogenesis imperfecta and tetracycline stains" and "Hypoplastic lesion" as contraindications (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Bartlett: "it should not be used for defects in dentine" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

**Technique.** Isolation: "rubber dam is therefore mandatory" because of the acid (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Application with a "special rubber cup that has fluted edges" in "A 10× gear reduction, low-speed handpiece" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], or "special rubber agitators similar to a rubber point or a rotating rubber cup in a speed-reducing handpiece" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Application time differs: Summitt's — it "is applied to the enamel with a rubber cup or stiff bristle brush for 20 to 30 seconds" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri — "Microabrasive agent is applied to the tooth surface for 60 seconds, followed by rinsing." (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Cohen's: "the paste should be placed on the tooth for about 5 seconds, followed by the rubber cup activation for 15 seconds", "repeated for up to 12 to 15 times", and if the stain persists "the procedure should be terminated to avoid permanent enamel damage" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Walton's: "the paste is worked into the enamel surface with a swirling motion for 5 seconds", then "The enamel surface is then rinsed for 10 seconds with water.", and at the end "The surface is neutralized with sodium bicarbonate and water." (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. So a single application ranges from 5 s (Walton's) to 60 s (Sikri) [Certain]. Finish: "The treated areas are polished with a fluoride-containing prophy paste to restore surface lustre" and "a topical fluoride is applied to teeth to enhance remineralization" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Depth removed — books differ.** Summitt's: the acid leaves "the outer 22 to 27 μm weakened enough so that the abrasive, with which the acid is mixed, quickly removes the stained outer surface" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Bartlett, citing Tong et al (1993): with 18% HCl and pumice "for approximately 100 seconds, about 360" ± "130 mm of enamel thickness is lost (Tong et al., 1993)" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. In the extract the ± is a control character and μ has become "m"; 360 ± 130 mm is impossible, so the figure is 360 ± 130 μm [Certain]. That cumulative loss exceeds the 0.2–0.3 mm limit quoted for the technique, which is why Cohen's caps the number of applications [Likely]. The figures measure different things — loss per application versus cumulative loss over about 100 s — so the amount removed depends on time and acid strength. [Likely]

**Results and limits.** "A series of three microabrasion sessions (Opalustre, Ultradent) was sufficient to remove the hypocalcification" in Goldstein's case (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; but "Microabrasion failed to improve the appearance in this case." in Bartlett's fluorosis patient (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], and "teeth sometimes appear more yellow after treatment" as enamel thins (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Walton's is more optimistic: "Usually the desired shade is obtained in a single appointment." and "The acid-pumice abrasion technique is relatively permanent if initial lightening is achieved." (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; Grossman's: it "may have to be repeated two or three times before the desired shade is obtained" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. For brown fluorosis "Usually, microabrasion is considered the primary treatment.", yet "attempting bleaching first avoids the removal of the fluoride-rich enamel layer" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Summitt's: "It is best to bleach first unless the tooth enamel is of a chalky texture." (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. "Micro-abrasion has the advantage of ensuring better control of the removal of tooth structure." than macroabrasion, and "micro-abrasion is recommended over macro-abrasion for the treatment of superficial defects in children" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** simultaneous erosion (acid) and abrasion (abrasive) that removes a thin layer of discoloured enamel; it does not bleach (Bartlett, 2011; Ritter et al, 2019).

**Agents:** 18% HCl + pumice (McCloskey 1984; Croll and Cavanaugh 1986); about 11% HCl + silicon carbide gel — Prema, Opalustre (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Indications:** defects within 0.2–0.3 mm of enamel — mild fluorosis, idiopathic white/brown spots, post-orthodontic decalcification (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
**Contraindications:** dentin defects, AI, DI, tetracycline, hypoplastic pits, thin cervical enamel (Sikri, 2020).

**Technique:** shade, photograph → rubber dam, glasses → fluted rubber cup, 10:1 gear-reduced handpiece, 20–30 s (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) or 60 s (Sikri, 2020) or 5 s paste plus 15 s cup, up to 12–15 times (Setzer, 2021) → rinse, inspect wet, repeat → neutralize with bicarbonate (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) → fluoride prophy paste → topical fluoride (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Enamel removed — books differ:** 22–27 μm per application (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); about 360 ± 130 μm after about 100 s (Bartlett, 2011).

**Results:** smooth, lustrous enamel; deeper residual defects need composite. Bleach first to spare fluoride-rich enamel (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- McCloskey 1984 (18% HCl); Croll and Cavanaugh 1986 (+ pumice); Prema about 11% HCl + SiC.
- Limit 0.2–0.3 mm; rubber dam mandatory; slow gear-reduced handpiece.
- Removes tissue, does not bleach; bleach first.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
- [DIAGRAM: Enamel section | stained surface layer within 0.2–0.3 mm removed by acid-abrasive in a fluted rubber cup; deeper defect left for composite]

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling microabrasion a bleaching technique.
- Using it on tetracycline stain or dentin defects.
- Giving one "depth removed" figure without the application time.

## MNEMONIC
**"Acid + Abrasive, Above 0.3 Abandon"**: acid plus abrasive; defects deeper than 0.3 mm need composite.
