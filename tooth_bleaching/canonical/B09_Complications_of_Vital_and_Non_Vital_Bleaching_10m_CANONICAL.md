## 1. Tooth sensitivity (vital bleaching)

### Explanation

Incidence — figures differ by book and regimen: "changes in sensitivity after external bleaching of vital teeth have been reported in up to 50% of the teeth", usually "mild to moderate and is transient over a period of 2 to" 3 days (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Summitt: "minor sensitivity may occur in as many as two-thirds of patients"; "Double-blind studies have reported sensitivity in 25% to 70%" of patients, though placebo and tray alone also produce it (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri: 10% carbamide peroxide "had led to increased tooth sensitivity in at least 50% of the patients" and "Higher incidences of tooth sensitivity (65–78%) have been reported after bleaching with H2O2 in combination with heat" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Mechanism: "Peroxide penetrates through the tooth to the pulp in a matter of 5 to 15" minutes; "However, the pulp remains healthy and the sensitivity is completely reversible" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; "Sensitivity is directly related to the concentration of hydrogen peroxide" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; heat-generating lights cause "a greater level of tooth dehydration" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; "the incidence of hypersensitivity increases with the frequency of changing the bleaching trays" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Pulp: "A 3.0% solution of hydrogen peroxide is capable of causing transient reduction in pulpal blood circulation" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; yet "it was shown that 50,000" µg of peroxide are needed to inhibit pulpal enzymes, far more than reaches the pulp from 12% CP trays (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Management: "A more direct treatment is the application of 3% to 5% potassium nitrate gel in the tray" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; "Potassium nitrate and sodium fluoride when added to" 10% CP reduce sensitivity; avoid "Tooth bleaching in younger individuals (less than 18 years) should be avoided" in Sikri's words (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Tooth sensitivity** — commonest. Up to 50% of teeth (Setzer, 2021); 25–70% of patients in double-blind trials (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 65–78% with H2O2 plus heat (Sikri, 2019). Peroxide reaches the pulp in 5–15 minutes; transient (2–3 days), reversible; worse with higher concentration, heat/dehydration and frequent tray changes. Manage with 3–5% potassium nitrate, fluoride, shorter or skipped sessions; avoid patients under 18 and exposed dentine.

## 2. Gingival and soft-tissue injury

### Explanation

"The most commonly observed adverse effect after external bleaching is gingival irritation"; "Warning signs other than the patient’s sensation are air bubbles rising from the gingival margins" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. "In clinical trials using 10% carbamide peroxide in custom made trays, 25–40% reported gingival irritation during the treatment" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. "The more powerful in-office bleaching agents (30% to 35% hydrogen peroxide) can easily produce tissue burns, turning the tissue white"; with trays "the irritation is most likely the result of an ill-fitting tray rather than the agent itself" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. In internal bleaching too, "The free radicals released by peroxides can cause chemical burns and tissue sloughing on gingiva, mucosa, or other oral soft tissues"; "clinicians should have vitamin E on hand" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. "Animal studies suggest that catalase applied to oral tissues before hydrogen peroxide treatment fully prevents the associated tissue damage" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**2. Gingival irritation / chemical burns** — 25–40% with 10% CP trays (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007), mostly from ill-fitting trays; 30–35% H2O2 blanches tissue (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Prevent with rubber dam, protective cream, scalloped trays; treat by copious rinsing and vitamin E (Setzer, 2021).

## 3. External cervical resorption (non-vital bleaching)

### Explanation

"External or cervical root resorption is a serious complication of whitening procedures with peroxide compounds" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. **Incidence — books differ:** "Cervical root resorptions were seen in 6% to 8% of cases when 35% hydrogen peroxide was used", and "18% to 25% if the hydrogen peroxide was heat activated" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Summitt: "This problem affects about 7% of the teeth that have undergone internal in-office bleaching" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri: "The incidence of cervical root resorption after nonvital bleaching ranges from 0 to 7%" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Sturdevant: "a slight potential (i.e. 1%) exists for a deleterious side effect termed external cervical resorption" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Causes: "The oxidizing agent, particularly 30% hydrogen peroxide, and heat may be the culprits" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; "Previous traumatic injury and age may act as predisposing factors"; "In none of the clinical reports of postbleaching root resorption was a protective barrier used" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Full discussion in P1.

#### ✎ Exam core

**3. External cervical resorption** — the most serious non-vital complication. Incidence: 6–8% with 35% H2O2 and 18–25% with heat (Setzer, 2021); about 7% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 0–7% (Sikri, 2019); about 1% (Ritter et al, 2019) — books differ. Causes: 30% H2O2, heat, CEJ defects, trauma, young age, no barrier (Rotstein, 2019). Prevent with perborate-water, barrier, no heat; radiographic recall.

## 4. Enamel and dentine changes

### Explanation

"A number of in vitro studies reported changes in enamel microhardness and morphology"; "At least in vital teeth, however, these changes appear not to be permanent"; "Bleaching with hydrogen peroxide increased the demineralization in teeth affected by incipient caries lesions" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Low-pH products cause "a slight loss in surface hardness to a depth of approximately 25" µm, but "this loss has not been shown to be significant because the surface quickly remineralizes after the procedure is completed" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri warns "Such altered enamel surface becomes the potential sites for caries progression" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Internally, "internal bleaching with 30% hydrogen peroxide has been found to reduce the micro-hardness of dentin and enamel" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. Over-bleaching: "Bleaching beyond this point results in the breakdown of the organic matrix resulting in weakening of the enamel and surface porosities" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Detail in P2.

#### ✎ Exam core

**4. Enamel/dentine** — reduced microhardness, porosity, surface erosion with acidic gels; mostly reversible by remineralization (Setzer, 2021; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Beyond the saturation point the organic matrix breaks down (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 5. Restorations and bonding

### Explanation

"Polymerization and bonding strength of composite resins to dentin and enamel are temporarily compromised by the oxygen released during a bleaching process" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; reduction "by up to 60% if the bonding procedure is carried out immediately after tooth bleaching treatment" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; Summitt quotes "A 25% to 50% reduction in bond strength of composite to etched enamel" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Existing restorations: "Bleaching has little or no effect on most of the common restorative" materials; "Bleaching has no effect on porcelain. It does encourage the release of mercury from some types of amalgam restorations"; composite "is roughened slightly, and the hardness may be slightly decreased, but neither effect is clinically" significant; "Bleaching does affect methyl methacrylate provisional restorative materials, causing them to yellow slightly"; and "previously placed restorations may appear comparatively dark" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Mercury: "no health implications due to the induced release of mercury by tooth bleaching have been reported" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], yet Grossman advises "It is not advisable to perform extracoronal bleaching for teeth with extensive amalgam restoration" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cements: "Bleaching may increase the solubility of glass-ionomer and other cements and reduce the bond strength between enamel and resin-based fillings, especially in the first 24 hours, but not later" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; "The nonvital bleaching procedures adversely affect the marginal seal leading to marginal leakage" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**5. Restorations and bonding** — residual oxygen cuts composite bond strength 25–50% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013), up to 60% (Alani and Patel, 2025): delay bonding. Existing composites appear darker and roughen slightly; mercury release from amalgam; acrylic provisionals yellow; porcelain unaffected; GIC solubility and marginal leakage increase (Sikri, 2019).

## 6. Other complications

### Explanation

Fracture: "Tooth crown fracture has also been observed after internal bleaching" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC], attributed mainly to excessive dentine removal — "the risk of fracture is increased if the tooth was previously weakened by the aggressive physical removal of hard structure" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; "It appears that bleached teeth are no more susceptible to fracture if properly restored" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Relapse: retreatment "10% after 1–2 years" rising to "40% in teeth observed up to 8 years" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; "External bleaching may show color regressions after 1 to" 3 years (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; overbleaching is a failure too — "A tooth that is too light may be as unesthetic as one that is too dark" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]. Toxicity: "hydrogen peroxide may act as a tumour-promoter", but products with "up to 3.6% hydrogen peroxide were unlikely to enhance the cancer risk" except in high-risk users (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; "Animal studies have indicated that ingestion of large amounts of bleaching gel is toxic and may cause irritation to the gastric and respiratory mucosa" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; "The maximum recommended concentration for products is 6% hydrogen peroxide" for consumer products (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Masking disease: "The primary safety issue is the concern for masking existing pathology" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**6. Others** — crown fracture (excess dentine removal; prevented by proper restoration); colour relapse (retreatment 10% at 1–2 years, 40% by 8 years) and overbleaching (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007); systemic toxicity on ingestion, tumour-promoting potential at high doses (consumer limit 6% H2O2); masking of caries or necrosis without prior examination (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Prevention (summary):** examine and radiograph first; lowest effective concentration; rubber dam/well-fitting trays; no heat; barrier in non-vital teeth; delay bonding; recall.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Vital: sensitivity (commonest), gingival irritation, enamel softening, bond inhibition, mercury release.
- Non-vital: cervical resorption (worst), chemical burns, fracture, relapse, marginal leakage.
- Resorption figures differ widely (1% to 25%) because they mix human and animal data, with and without heat.
- Sensitivity is reversible; potassium nitrate is the specific remedy.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Listing cervical resorption as a vital-bleaching complication.
- Quoting one incidence figure for resorption as if all books agree.
- Saying bleaching weakens the bond of existing restorations (it is new bonding that suffers).
- Omitting colour relapse and masking of pathology.
