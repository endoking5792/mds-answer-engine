## 1. Direction of development

### Explanation

Goldstein frames the field: "The history of bleaching has been one of continued improvements in bleaching materials, delivery systems, and devices to activate the bleaching action" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. For OTC products Summitt notes that, though new chemistry may appear, "their focus has been primarily on delivery systems that are easier and faster for the patient to apply" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. The advances below are grouped as (1) additives that reduce side effects, (2) pH control, (3) changes in concentration and viscosity, (4) activators/photocatalysts, (5) delivery systems, and (6) alternative (non-peroxide or low-peroxide) agents.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Recent advances aim at less sensitivity and enamel damage, faster or more convenient whitening, and controlled activation: changes in additives, pH, concentration, activators, delivery systems and alternative agents (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 2. Desensitising and remineralising additives

### Explanation

"Manufacturers have included potassium nitrate and fluoride or amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) in the bleaching gel"; these "have been shown to have varying degrees of effectiveness in decreasing tooth sensitivity" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson's ingredients table: ACP "Decreases sensitivity by occluding the dentinal tubules with calcium phosphate" and restores lustre; potassium nitrate "Decreases sensitivity by altering nerve conduction"; sodium fluoride occludes tubules, remineralizes and "Provides caries resistance" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein: "Some current bleaching products now incorporate a neutral fluoride with no apparent compromise of the bleaching process" (15% Opalescence with fluoride), and companies "have now introduced products of potassium nitrate with and without fluoride in a base barrier" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri: CPP-ACP remineralizing agents "are added to reduce sensitivity", and CPP-ACP used "during and after bleaching" has been advocated against demineralization; potassium nitrate and sodium fluoride added to 10% CP "considerably reduce sensitivity" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Cohen: fluoride, CPP-ACP, potassium nitrate "or nano-hydroxyapatite suspension" after or mixed into the agent recovered enamel microhardness after 14 days (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha: one in-office system "contains calcium, phosphate, and fluoride ions to allow remineralization during treatment" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Evidence level: Summitt's "varying degrees" wording and Cohen's in vitro microhardness data — modest. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Desensitising/remineralising additives** (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Sikri, 2020):
- Potassium nitrate — depolarises nerve, reduces sensitivity.
- Sodium fluoride — occludes tubules, remineralizes (fluoride-containing 15% CP gels).
- ACP / CPP-ACP — occludes tubules, restores lustre, counters demineralization.
- Nano-hydroxyapatite; calcium-phosphate-fluoride in-office gels (Setzer, 2021; Powers and Wataha, 2013).
Benefit varies between products.

## 3. pH-neutral and alkaline formulations

### Explanation

Bonsor and Pearson list "Neutralizers" — "Alkaline substances to create neutral pH" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. "Most carbamide peroxide materials approved by the ADA have a pH of approximately" 7, and urea raises oral pH "above 8 for a number of hours" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sikri: "A few pH buffered products are available, which can minimize the side effects" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Cohen: "Alkaline bleaching solutions may cause less demineralization damage than acidic solutions and are less likely to penetrate the pulp" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Hydrogen peroxide gels are kept acidic for shelf life ("The hydrogen peroxide per se is acidic"), whereas "Gels produced for use with the KTP laser contain a special buffering system and set the pH to around 9.5" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Older home products had pH "from 4.6 to 6.7 when undiluted" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**2. pH-controlled gels.** Neutral carbamide peroxide (pH about 7; urea raises pH above 8) and buffered/alkaline gels (KTP gels pH about 9.5) cause less enamel demineralization than acidic hydrogen peroxide (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Setzer, 2021; Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023).

## 4. Concentration, viscosity and stability

### Explanation

Viscosity: carbopol is "The most significant additive to carbamide peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; it "Increases viscosity, decreases breakdown in saliva and slows release of oxygen" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri gives 0.5-1.5% (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Goldstein: "thicker gels generally work better than thinner ones" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Stability: "Some products are now supplied in refrigerated packs" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

Higher-concentration home products: "high concentrations of carbamide peroxide (35% to 38%)" with tray application "only 3 to 15 minutes per day", but "There is no research at this time demonstrating clear comparisons" with 10% CP; daytime hydrogen peroxide gels at 7.5% to 9.5% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Graduated systems: Goldstein's "three‐tier bleaching approach beginning with a 5 or 6% solution" rising "finally to a 15–17% solution" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson's Ramp system — "11% carbamide peroxide is applied for 5 nights, then 16% carbamide peroxide for another 5 nights", then 6% hydrogen peroxide (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. In-office gels: 25% to 38% (Setzer, 2021) or "25% up to 40%" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]; 37% carbamide peroxide in-office gave less sensitivity than 35% hydrogen peroxide in an RCT (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Dual-component gels: LaserWhite 20 "is produced by mixing the base and activator gel contained in two syringes", giving 38% (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC].

**The books caution that a higher concentration is not simply better:** Sikri and Cohen both report that hydrogen peroxide above 15% did not increase effectiveness in vitro (Sikri, 2020; Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Summitt: "the effect is not linear (ie, 20% is not twice as fast as 10%)" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Yet in-office products remain at 25-40%. [Certain that this tension is printed; the in vitro data cannot settle clinical superiority.]

#### ✎ Exam core

**3. Concentration and viscosity.**
- Carbopol-thickened, viscous gels — slower oxygen release, stay in the tray (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- High-concentration home carbamide peroxide (35-38%, 3-15 minutes a day) and day-wear hydrogen peroxide 7.5-9.5% — little comparative research (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Graduated "three-tier/ramp" systems: 5-6% → 15-17%, or 11% then 16% CP then 6% H₂O₂, to limit sensitivity (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- Two-syringe base/activator in-office gels (38%); refrigerated packs.
- Caution: above 15% H₂O₂, no gain in vitro (Sikri, 2020; Setzer, 2021).

## 5. Activators and photocatalysts in gels

### Explanation

For light/laser activation "the gel should contain a proper photocatalyst, which is an absorber that matches the spectrum of the light used"; "bleaching gels for the blue and green wavelengths contain orange-red dyes like rhodamine", red-infrared gels contain bluish-purple dyes, and "Titanium-dioxide particles also act as a broadband absorber"; the JW gel "can be activated with any wavelength from 450 to 1064 nm since it contains a multiwavelength absorber" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Carotene "Converts light energy to heat so increasing the activation of hydrogen peroxide" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Cohen describes a premixed 35% gel with carotene and laser gels with "fumed silica and a blue dye", and photocatalysts "including methylene blue and UV or visible light-activated titanium dioxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri: laser gels with "thermal absorption crystals" in fumed silica and 35% H₂O₂, and "Titanium dioxide nanoparticle doped with nitrogen with less concentration of hydrogen peroxide" (3-10%), which "improves the biocompatibility of the solution reducing sensitivity during and after the procedure" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Whether light activation improves the result is contested (B12).

#### ✎ Exam core

**4. Activators/photocatalysts.** Rhodamine or blue dyes matched to the laser wavelength, carotene, thermal-absorption crystals, multiwavelength titanium dioxide; nitrogen-doped TiO₂ nanoparticles allow 3-10% H₂O₂ with less sensitivity (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023; Setzer, 2021; Sikri, 2020).

## 6. Delivery systems

### Explanation

Strips: "adhesive strips containing a relatively high concentration (approximately 6% to 14%) of hydrogen peroxide", worn "for 30 minutes twice a day" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson: "Flexible, polyethylene strips impregnated with hydrogen peroxide at concentrations ranging from 6% to 14%" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Paint-on gels "have been available since 2004"; they obviate a tray, allow single-tooth bleaching, and are applied "twice daily for 14 days"; one uses "19% sodium perborate" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. "Several companies also offer prefabricated-preloaded trays for home bleaching" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Sikri adds whitening pens ("a pen-like applicator"), the iPower ("the first wearable and portable bleaching device") that heats the gel, a microsensor embedded in trays to record wear, and light-cured polymerizable gingival barriers (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. A linear bleached shade guide (Paravina, 2007) monitors the result (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Whitening toothpastes remove extrinsic stain but "cannot affect the colour of the tooth as this is determined by the underlying dentine" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**5. Delivery systems.** Whitening strips (6-14% H₂O₂, 30 minutes twice daily); paint-on gels (since 2004, twice daily for 14 days, single teeth); prefilled disposable trays; whitening pens; wearable heated device (iPower); tray wear-time microsensors; light-cured liquid dams (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Sikri, 2020).

## 7. Alternative agents and regulation

### Explanation

Sikri lists "0.5% chlorine dioxide when applied to enamel surface for 20 minutes", but "its use may lead to roughness on enamel surface and decreased lustre" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; "Enzymatic dentifrices can be used as bleaching catalyst by generating hydrogen peroxide in situ"; "room temperature plasma could be used for tooth bleaching" (non-thermal atmospheric plasma, Lee et al 2009); and "A new bleaching agent containing tri-barrel hydremide-peroxide gel" for which "initial studies have not shown any advantage over traditional bleaching modalities" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Sodium perborate products were used in the UK because of legal limits (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Sodium chlorite products are not described in the books read. [RECALL: chlorite-based OTC whiteners exist; not verified here.]

Regulation shapes formulation: the 2011 EU ruling allows "less than 17% carbamide peroxide and between 0.5% and 6% hydrogen peroxide" when prescribed by a dentist (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Summitt quotes "The maximum recommended concentration for products is 6% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**6. Alternative agents (early evidence):** chlorine dioxide 0.5% (roughens enamel); enzymatic dentifrices generating H₂O₂ in situ; non-thermal atmospheric plasma; tri-barrel hydremide-peroxide gel (no advantage yet) (Sikri, 2020).
**Regulation:** EU limit 6% H₂O₂ / below 17% CP, dentist-prescribed (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Additives: potassium nitrate (nerve), fluoride and ACP/CPP-ACP (tubule occlusion, remineralization).
- Neutral/alkaline gels reduce demineralization; KTP gels buffered to pH about 9.5.
- Carbopol = viscosity and slow release; refrigerated packs for stability.
- Graduated (ramp) concentration systems limit sensitivity.
- Photocatalysts: rhodamine, carotene, TiO₂ (N-doped TiO₂ with low H₂O₂).
- Delivery: strips, paint-on, preloaded trays, pens, wearable devices.
- Above 15% H₂O₂ no extra effect in vitro.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing only "lasers" as the recent advance; the question asks about materials.
- Claiming higher concentration always bleaches faster (not linear; no gain above 15% in vitro).
- Presenting chlorine dioxide or plasma as established; evidence is early.
- Confusing whitening toothpaste (stain removal) with bleaching.
