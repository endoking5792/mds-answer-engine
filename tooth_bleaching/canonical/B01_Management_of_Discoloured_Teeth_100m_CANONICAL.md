## 1. Introduction: tooth colour and the meaning of discoloration

### Explanation

Grossman's frames tooth colour optically: "The color of teeth is determined by the translucency and thickness of the enamel, the thickness and color of the underlying dentin, and the color of the pulp" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's adopts Rotstein's broad definition of discoloration as "any change in the hue, color, or translucency of a tooth due to any cause", and stresses that "Both intrinsic and extrinsic discolorations can exist in combination and may affect enamel, dentin, or pulp" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Because a change in translucency counts, a calcified, opaque crown is "discoloured" even though no pigment has entered it — this matters later, because bleaching attacks pigment, not opacity [Likely].

Goldstein's chapter opens on the clinical rule that governs the whole answer: "The dentist needs to be able to correctly diagnose the cause of discolorations, as it is crucial for a successful treatment outcome", and its summary adds the ethical rule: "It is incumbent on the dentist to select the most conservative treatment option for the specific stain" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Torabinejad's textbook puts the sequence as diagnosis, treatment plan and prognosis: "there must be a diagnosis (to determine the cause and location of the discoloration), a treatment plan" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].

Normal colour: "The normal color of primary teeth is bluish white"; "The color of permanent teeth is grayish yellow, grayish white, or yellowish white"; teeth of elderly people are more yellow (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Terms. Bleaching: "lightening of the color of the tooth through the application of a chemical agent to oxidize the organic pigmentation in the tooth" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's gives the ADA-style wording, "an oxidative chemical that alters the light-absorbing and/or lightreflecting nature of a material structure" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri separates the two lay terms: "Whitening restores teeth to their natural tooth color" by removing surface stain, whereas bleaching makes teeth lighter than their natural colour (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Tooth colour depends on the translucency and thickness of enamel, the thickness and colour of dentin and the colour of the pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021). Discoloration is any change in the hue, colour or translucency of a tooth from any cause; extrinsic and intrinsic types often coexist (Setzer, 2021).

Normal shades: primary teeth bluish white; permanent teeth greyish yellow, greyish white or yellowish white; teeth become yellower with age as enamel thins and secondary dentin is laid down (Gopikrishna, 2021).

Management rests on three principles:
1. **Diagnose the cause and location** of the discoloration first — the cause decides the treatment and the prognosis (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021).
2. **Choose the most conservative effective option**, moving up a ladder of increasing aggressiveness only when the lower step fails (Goldstein et al, 2018a).
3. **Prepare the patient** for realistic results, cost, duration and longevity.

**Definitions.** Bleaching is the lightening of tooth colour by a chemical agent that oxidizes organic pigment (Gopikrishna, 2021). Whitening restores natural colour by removing surface stain; bleaching makes teeth lighter than their natural colour (Sikri, 2020).

## 2. Classification and aetiology of discoloration

### Explanation

**Two-way or three-way?** Grossman's and Sturdevant's use a two-way split: "Tooth discoloration can be classified as either extrinsic or intrinsic" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; "Extrinsic stains are located on the outer surfaces of teeth, whereas intrinsic stains are internal" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri uses three: "The etiology of tooth discoloration is broadly divided into three categories, viz. intrinsic, extrinsic and internalized stains" (Sikri, 2020; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's keeps two headings but notes the bridge between them: "extrinsic stains can become intrinsic over time" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Goldstein's Ch.39 uses the same three classes — "Intrinsic, extrinsic, and internalized tooth discoloration boast multifactorial etiologies" (Goldstein, Esfandiari and Schultz, 2018) [SRC]. The three-way scheme is the more useful for treatment choice because internalized stains behave like extrinsic stains in origin but resist polishing [Likely]. Full classification answer: P3.

**Extrinsic.** "Extrinsic discolorations are found on the outer surface of teeth and are usually of local origin" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Goldstein's defines them as stains "caused by topical or extrinsic agents" and names the predisposing factors — "including enamel defects, salivary dysfunction, and poor oral hygiene"; "The most common cause of extrinsic stains is poor oral hygiene" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].
- Older metallic/non-metallic (direct/indirect) scheme: "Direct or nonmetallic staining occurs when the stain from a chemical compound gets incorporated into the acquired pellicle"; "Indirect or metallic staining occurs by the means of metal salts and cationic antiseptics" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Grossman's criticises it: "The problem with this classification is that it does not explain the mechanism of staining" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].
- Nathoo (1997): "A newer classification based on chemistry of staining was put forth by Nathoo in 1997" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's calls it "The most common classification for tooth discoloration" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. N1: "N1‐type colored material (chromogen) binds to the tooth surface"; N2: "The stains actually are N1‐type food stains that darken with time"; N3: "N3‐type stains are caused by carbohydrate‐ rich foods (e.g., apples, potatoes), stannous fluoride, and chlorhexidine" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].
- Specific stains: "Green stain is most often found in children, in association with remnants of Nasmyth’s membrane" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; metals — "Iron yields a brown to greenish brown color; copper or brass yields green or bluish‐green" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; "Potassium permanganate produces violet to black color"; "Stannous fluoride causes golden-brown discoloration" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; betel — "Consistent chewing of betel nut and related substances may cause large-scale black discolorations" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Some penetrate: silver nitrate stains "are almost impossible to eliminate without grinding" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Intrinsic.** "Intrinsic discolorations are due to the presence of chromogenic material within the enamel or dentin, incorporated either during odontogenesis or after tooth eruption" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Goldstein's adds a structural route — "Intrinsic stains result from the change in structural composition or the thickness of hard dental tissues" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Sikri sorts the causes two ways: "The factors causing intrinsic discoloration are classified as local and systemic factors", and "A few authors have categorized causes of intrinsic stains as pre-eruptive and post-eruptive" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Ingle's uses a clinically handy split: "patient- or dentist-related causes" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's notes that "such dentist-related discolorations are usually preventable" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Walton's textbook makes the same split: "The first group, natural (acquired) discolorations, may be on the surface or incorporated into tooth structure."; "The second group, iatrogenic (inflicted) discolorations, which result from dental procedures, are usually incorporated into tooth structure and are preventable." (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].
- Systemic (Sikri, 2020 list) [SRC]: fluorosis, tetracycline, amelogenesis and dentinogenesis imperfecta (DI: "brownish-violet to yellowish-brown color of the dentin with a translucent hue"), dentin dysplasia, congenital hyperbilirubinaemia, congenital erythropoietic porphyria ("red brown/purplish-red discoloration"), alkaptonuria ("An inherited genetic disorder of phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism"). Cohen's adds erythroblastosis fetalis with bilirubin "creating yellow-green and blue-green discolorations", and "High fevers experienced during the ages of tooth development may cause chronologic enamel hypoplasia" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].
- Local (Grossman's list) [SRC]: pulp decomposition, trauma, haemorrhage during extirpation, calcific metamorphosis, filling materials, root canal medicaments, ageing, iatrogenic. Goldstein's adds internal resorption — "When the resorption nears the enamel surface, a pinkish discoloration is seen" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC] — and Cohen's the cervical "pink spot" of external resorption (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Ageing: "tooth wear thins the enamel, making it more transparent and causing dentin to show" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

**Colour as a diagnostic clue.** Grossman's reproduces "Tooth Discoloration: Causes and Colors (Abbott, 1997)": tobacco yellow-brown to black; fluorosis white to brown-black; tetracycline brown, grey, black; bilirubin blue-green; haemoglobin grey-black; pulp canal obliteration yellow; necrosis with haemorrhage grey-black and without haemorrhage yellow to grey-brown; restorative and endodontic materials grey to black (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Internalized.** "Internalized discoloration occurs due to incorporation of extrinsic stains within the tooth substance following some dental pathology. It occurs in enamel defects and in the porous surface of exposed dentin"; "These types of defects can also be seen in tooth wear, gingival recession and caries" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification.** Grossman's and Sturdevant's divide discoloration into extrinsic and intrinsic (Gopikrishna, 2021; Ritter et al, 2019); Sikri adds a third group, internalized (Sikri, 2020). Goldstein's notes that extrinsic stains can become intrinsic with time (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

**A. Extrinsic** — on the outer surface, local origin, removable by prophylaxis. Predisposed by poor oral hygiene (the commonest cause), enamel defects and reduced saliva (Goldstein et al, 2018a).
- *Old scheme:* non-metallic (direct, chromogen in pellicle) and metallic (indirect, metal salts, cationic antiseptics); criticised because it does not explain mechanism (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- *Nathoo (1997):* N1 — coloured chromogen binds to the surface (tea, coffee, wine, chromogenic bacteria, metals); N2 — N1 stain that darkens after binding; N3 — colourless prechromogen binds and reacts to form stain (chlorhexidine, stannous fluoride, carbohydrate foods) (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Setzer, 2021).
- *Examples:* green stain (Nasmyth's membrane, children), orange and black-line stains (chromogenic bacteria), tobacco, betel nut (black), iron (brown-black), copper (green), stannous fluoride (golden brown), potassium permanganate (violet-black), silver nitrate (grey, penetrating) (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Sikri, 2020; Setzer, 2021).

**B. Intrinsic** — chromogen within enamel or dentin, or altered structure or thickness of hard tissue (Gopikrishna, 2021; Goldstein et al, 2018a).

| Systemic (pre-eruptive) | Local (mostly post-eruptive) |
|---|---|
| Dental fluorosis | Pulp necrosis (decomposition products) |
| Tetracycline, minocycline | Trauma and intrapulpal haemorrhage |
| Amelogenesis imperfecta | Haemorrhage during pulp extirpation |
| Dentinogenesis imperfecta, dentin dysplasia | Calcific metamorphosis |
| Erythroblastosis fetalis, hyperbilirubinaemia | Internal and external (cervical) resorption |
| Congenital erythropoietic porphyria | Restorative materials (amalgam, pins, posts, leaking composite) |
| Alkaptonuria, high fever (chronologic hypoplasia) | Endodontic materials and medicaments; ageing; caries |

(Sikri, 2020; Gopikrishna, 2021; Setzer, 2021.) Ingle's groups the local causes as patient-related and dentist-related; the latter are preventable (Rotstein, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Colour clue (Abbott, 1997):**

| Colour | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Pink | Recent pulpal haemorrhage; internal or cervical resorption |
| Yellow, yellow-brown | Calcific metamorphosis, ageing, mild tetracycline, necrosis without haemorrhage |
| Grey, black | Necrosis with haemorrhage, amalgam, silver sealers, pins |
| Blue-grey, banded | Severe tetracycline |
| Blue-green, green | Bilirubin (erythroblastosis fetalis, biliary atresia); copper; green stain |
| Brown, chalky white | Fluorosis, hypomineralization |

(Gopikrishna, 2021; Goldstein et al, 2018a.)

**C. Internalized** — extrinsic stain incorporated into the tooth through enamel defects (fluorosis, hypoplasia, hypocalcification) or porous exposed dentin (wear, recession, caries) (Sikri, 2020).

## 3. Key causes in detail

### Explanation

**Tetracycline.** Mechanism: the drug "binds irreversibly with calcium ions, forming a stable complex in the hydroxyapatite crystals"; on light exposure "the fluorescent yellow discoloration gradually changes over a period of months to years to a non-fluorescent brown color" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Ingle's: ultraviolet exposure "leads to the formation of a reddish-purple oxidation by-product that discolors the teeth" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Window of risk: "Children are susceptible to tetracycline discolorations from the time they are in utero until the age of 8" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Determinants — **conflict**:
- Goldstein's: "The major factors impacting the amount of tetracycline deposition are dosage, duration of treatment, stage of tooth mineralization" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Sturdevant's: "The severity of the staining depends on the dose, the duration of exposure to the drug and the type of tetracycline analogue used" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].
- Sikri: "the dose of tetracycline is more important than the duration of intake of tetracyclines", and "however, doxycycline does not produce any stain" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].
- Grossman's: "the greater the amount of crown developed, the less severe the stain" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].
Sikri's ranking of dose over duration is not echoed by the other books, which list both side by side [Guessing that the difference is emphasis rather than contradiction]. Minocycline stains adult teeth: "permanent teeth in adults also can experience a graying discoloration as a result of long-term exposure to minocycline" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Severity classes — Grossman's box gives "Tetracycline Discoloration Classification (Jordon and Boskman, 1984)" with third degree as "Severe staining characterized by dark gray or blue discoloration with banding" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's describes the same three groups: "Tetracycline discoloration has been classified into three groups according to severity" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. **Slip — the authors' names.** Goldstein's spells them correctly — "three major categories of tetracycline involvement first proposed by Jordan and Boksman" — and adds a fourth degree "while not one of the original categories proposed by Jordan and Boksman" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Grossman's box ("Jordon and Boskman") misspells both names, and Walton's reference list also prints "Jordan R, Boskman L: Conservative vital bleaching treatment of discolored dentition" (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. The correct form is Jordan and Boksman (1984) [Certain]. Prevention: Alani and Patel state "tetracycline is not recommended for expectant mothers, those nursing neonates and children up to the age of 12 years" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC] — a prescribing limit, wider than Cohen's staining window to age 8 (Setzer, 2021).

**Fluorosis.** Grossman's: "The most common pre-eruptive staining is endemic fluorosis" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. **Conflict on the threshold:** Summitt's: "Excessive fluoride in drinking water, greater than 1 to 2 ppm, can cause metabolic alteration in the ameloblasts" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri: "high concentration of fluorides (more than 4.0 ppm) causes moderate to severe discoloration" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The two may be reconciled if Summitt's figure is the threshold for any fluorosis and Sikri's for moderate-severe [Guessing]. Severity: "The severe form of fluorosis may not respond to bleaching" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Window: "with the critical time between 20 and 30 months", and "Risk diminishes completely by the age of 8 years." (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

**Trauma and pulp necrosis.** "Blood disintegration products, mainly iron sulfides, flow to the pulp lumen and permeate into the dentin tubules" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; West: "Iron in the hemoglobin combines with hydrogen sulfide produced by bacteria to form dark‐pigmented iron sulfide" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]; Grossman's lists the haemoglobin products "hemin, hematin, hematoidin, hematoporphyrin, and hemosiderin" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Colour sequence: "Soon after the injury, the crown is pink; with time, the pink becomes orange, brown, or black" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Necrosis: "Disintegration by-products penetrate the dentinal tubules and discolor the surrounding dentin" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; "Decomposition of pulp tissue is probably the most common cause of tooth discoloration" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Calcific metamorphosis gives yellow loss of translucency (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

**Endodontic and restorative materials.** Obturation remnants are "a frequent and severe cause of single tooth discoloration" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's lists: "Silver points, historically used for root fillings, can cause gray, dark discolorations"; "Resorcinol-formaldehyde resin paste (“Russian Red”) was reported to cause discolorations ranging from pink to dark burgundy"; AH26 "contains bismuth trioxide as a filler and radiopacifier" which forms green-to-black bismuth compounds; MTA — "The discoloring effects were shown for both gray and white formulations"; triple antibiotic paste — "minocyline-containing pastes should be avoided for regenerative therapies"; and "In contact with chlorhexidine, sodium hypochlorite may form a dark brown precipitate containing para-chloroaniline"; silver diamine fluoride causes "dark gray or black discolorations" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's: "Phenolics or iodoform-based medicaments sealed in the root canal and chamber are in direct contact with dentin" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein's: root canal therapy "can cause intrinsic stains if eugenol and phenolic compounds are employed" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Amalgam: "Silver amalgam produces a stain ranging from slate gray to dark gray; copper amalgam produces a bluish black to black stain" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; two mechanisms — "(1) show through and (2) penetration of corrosion products", the dentinal pigment being "predominately corrosion products of tin" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Tetracycline.** Chelates calcium and binds in hydroxyapatite, mainly dentin; fluorescent yellow darkens to brown on light exposure (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Rotstein, 2019). Risk from in utero to 8 years (Setzer, 2021). Severity depends on dose, duration, stage of mineralization and type of analogue (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Ritter et al, 2019); Sikri ranks dose above duration and states doxycycline does not stain (Sikri, 2020). Minocycline greys adult teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021). Avoid prescribing tetracycline in pregnancy, nursing and up to 12 years (Alani and Patel, 2025). Jordan and Boksman (1984): first degree light yellow-grey without banding; second degree darker without banding; third degree dark grey-blue with banding (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Fluorosis.** Commonest pre-eruptive stain (Gopikrishna, 2021). Threshold given as above 1-2 ppm (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) but as above 4 ppm for moderate-severe discoloration (Sikri, 2020). Mild: white flecks; moderate: chalky, stained; severe: pitted, may not respond to bleaching (Sikri, 2020).

**Trauma and necrosis.** Haemorrhage → haemolysis → iron sulfide in tubules: pink, then orange, brown, grey-black (Goldstein et al, 2018a; West et al, 2018). Pulp decomposition is probably the commonest cause of single-tooth discoloration (Gopikrishna, 2021). Calcific metamorphosis: yellow, loss of translucency (Setzer, 2021).

**Frequency:** fewer than 5% of treated pulpless teeth become noticeably discoloured, and most respond to bleaching (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Endodontic materials.** Sealer and gutta-percha left in the chamber (frequent and severe cause), silver-containing sealers and silver points (grey-black), AH26 bismuth, MTA (grey and white), minocycline-containing triple antibiotic paste, Russian red (pink-burgundy), phenolic and iodoform medicaments, NaOCl-chlorhexidine precipitate, silver diamine fluoride (Rotstein, 2019; Setzer, 2021).

**Restorative materials.** Amalgam grey (show-through and tin corrosion products), copper amalgam bluish black, pins and posts, leaking or chemically cured composite (Gopikrishna, 2021; Goldstein et al, 2018a).

## 4. Diagnosis and treatment planning

### Explanation

History: Cohen's asks for "family history, genetic or systemic disorders, past and current medications" plus trauma and previous treatment; "Radiographic analysis, including periapical and bitewing radiographs (as well as, if applicable, cone-beam computed tomography [CBCT]) is indicated" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Single dark tooth: "The first step in the treatment of this tooth is to take a radiograph to determine whether there is any periapical pathology" and pulp test it (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Summitt's: "A radiograph should always be taken of a single dark tooth, and pulp testing may also be indicated" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Prophylaxis before diagnosis: "a complete prophylaxis should be performed to remove minor surface staining" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Adjuncts from West: "Transillumination through a fiber‐optic light may be of great assistance in detecting color shifts in a crown"; tetracycline "drug florescence and etiology may be verified by using an ultraviolet light or Woods black light"; grey-black may mean "silver precipitants from certain root canal sealers and filling materials" (West et al, 2018) [SRC].

Goldstein's Table 21.2 sorts presentations: "Single dark tooth (radiograph needed for diagnosis of pathology)" — vital (blood pigment, calcific metamorphosis, internal resorption) or nonvital (blood, pulp remnants, amalgam, leakage); generalized (smoking, food, tetracycline, disease, ageing, genetics); localized white or brown spots (fluorosis, demineralization); restoration-related; and dark incisal translucency, checked by a "Finger test on lingual to determine translucency" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

Expectations and contraindications. "The patient’s esthetic expectations should be critically evaluated and the prognosis for the treatment discussed thoroughly"; "Patients with suspected or confirmed bulimia should not receive bleaching procedures"; brackets — "no bleaching procedures should take place 2 to 3 weeks prior to any adhesive placement of brackets" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Summitt's first contraindication is "Unrealistic expectations"; its aesthetic end-point is natural colour — "Patients tend to look most natural when the color of the teeth matches the white of the sclera in their eyes" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: "Mild discolourations are best left untreated, are bleached or are treated conservatively with micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Prognostic flags: "Teeth heavily stained in the gingival third have the poorest prognosis for complete lightening" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Restorations will not change colour: "Bleaching does not change the color of other restorations" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**History:** onset and duration; single tooth or generalized; trauma; endodontic and restorative treatment; childhood medication (tetracycline), fluoride exposure, family history, systemic disease; diet, smoking, chlorhexidine; bulimia; expectations (Setzer, 2021).

**Examination:** prophylaxis first to separate extrinsic from intrinsic stain (Goldstein et al, 2018a); pattern (generalized, banded, single tooth, localized white or brown spots); shade with shade guide and photographs; transillumination; ultraviolet light for tetracycline fluorescence (West et al, 2018); restorations and caries; tooth wear, translucency, gingival recession.

**Pattern of presentation** (Goldstein et al, 2018a):
- *Single dark tooth* — radiograph and pulp test: vital (haemorrhage, calcific metamorphosis, internal resorption) or non-vital (pulp remnants, blood, materials, leakage).
- *Generalized* — ageing, smoking, food, tetracycline, fluorosis, genetic disorders.
- *Localized spots* — white or brown fluorosis, demineralization, hypoplasia.
- *Around restorations* — amalgam show-through, marginal leakage, discoloured composite.
- *Dark incisal edge* — translucency, confirmed by the finger test; bleaching may make it look darker.

**Investigations:** periapical and bitewing radiographs, CBCT where needed (Setzer, 2021); pulp sensibility tests for every single dark tooth (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Treatment planning:** identify cause, depth (enamel, dentin, pulp chamber) and pulp status; discuss realistic outcome, cost, duration, longevity and alternatives; begin with the most conservative option; warn that existing restorations will not bleach and may need replacement (Goldstein et al, 2018a). Contraindications to bleaching: unrealistic expectations, bulimia, untreated caries and leaking restorations, hypersensitivity, pregnancy (Setzer, 2021; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 5. The management ladder

### Explanation

Goldstein's tabulates options "in order of increasing aggressiveness": prophylaxis, air polisher, external and internal bleaching with carbamide or hydrogen peroxide, sealants, macroabrasion, microabrasion, resurfacing or replacing restorations, veneers and crowns (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Sturdevant's list: "Treatment options include removal of surface stains, bleaching, micro-abrasion or macro-abrasion, veneering and placement of porcelain crowns"; and, against the older habit, it notes "Many dentists recommend porcelain crowns as the best solution for badly discoloured teeth" while preferring conservative alternatives (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. "consideration should be given first to bleaching anterior teeth when intrinsic discolourations are encountered" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Resin infiltration is not in either ladder; it enters from Sikri's minimal-intervention chapter and Cohen's (Section 6).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Management ladder (least to most invasive)** (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Ritter et al, 2019):
1. Prevention and removal of cause (oral hygiene, diet, habits, replace leaking restorations).
2. Professional prophylaxis, scaling, polishing, air polishing; whitening toothpastes for maintenance.
3. Microabrasion; macroabrasion.
4. Resin infiltration (white spots, non-pitted fluorosis).
5. Vital (external) bleaching: dentist-supervised at-home (nightguard), in-office (power), combination.
6. Non-vital (internal) bleaching: walking bleach; inside-outside; thermocatalytic (now discouraged).
7. Direct composite: partial or full veneers, replacement of discoloured restorations.
8. Indirect veneers: processed composite, feldspathic porcelain, lithium disilicate.
9. Full-coverage crowns (with post and core in endodontically treated teeth).

Steps are often combined: bleaching first lightens the base so that a thinner, more translucent veneer will mask the rest (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 6. Removal of surface stain, microabrasion, macroabrasion and resin infiltration

### Explanation

Extrinsic stain: "In general, extrinsic stains respond well to scaling, polishing, or bleaching" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Toothpastes: abrasive pastes can thin enamel; peroxide pastes have too short a contact time; "Most whitening toothpastes contain titanium dioxide", a temporary cosmetic coating (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

Microabrasion. Sturdevant's history: "In 1984, McCloskey reported the use of 18% hydrochloric acid"; Croll and Cavanaugh (1986) added pumice; Croll then "reducing the concentration of the hydrochloric acid to approximately 11%" with silicon carbide (Prema); it "does not remove stains or defects through any bleaching phenomena"; limits — stains that "do not extend beyond a few tenths of a millimetre in depth", and restore if "the depth of the defect exceeding 0.2–0.3 mm" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's: "mixing 18% hydrochloric acid solution with fine pumice powder to form a thick paste", rubber dam mandatory, and "this procedure is repeated for up to 12 to 15 times" then fluoride (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri: "Microabrasion is a procedure involving the surface dissolution of the stains of enamel by an acid (preferably 18% HCl)"; disadvantage — "Since it removes enamel layer, teeth sometimes appear more yellow after treatment" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Grossman's gives the McInnes solution ("Five parts of hydrochloric acid (36%)", five parts 30% hydrogen peroxide, one part ether) and its modified version with sodium hydroxide; and lists exclusions under "Microabrasion cannot be used for the following conditions" — age-related and tetracycline stain, deep hypoplasia, most AI and DI (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Macroabrasion: "a 12- or 16-fluted composite finishing bur or a fine grit finishing diamond in high speed is used to remove the surface defect" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Comparison: "Macro-abrasion is considerably faster and does not require the use of a rubber dam or special instrumentation", but "micro-abrasion is recommended over macro-abrasion for the treatment of superficial defects in children" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's copy of the first sentence reads "It is considerably faster and easier than microabrasion and does not require the use of a rubber dam" under a microabrasion heading — a slip; it must mean macroabrasion (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] [Certain].

**Conflict — bleach or abrade first for brown fluorosis?** Goldstein's: "attempting bleaching first avoids the removal of the fluoride‐rich enamel layer", and "It is estimated that 80% of these brown discolorations are amenable to bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Sikri and Grossman's list fluorosis as a primary microabrasion indication without this ordering (Sikri, 2020; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Goldstein's argument (conserving enamel) is the more conservative and fits the ladder [Likely].

Resin infiltration. Sikri: "Resin infiltration is a microinvasive treatment regime for arresting early carious lesions"; "The infiltration procedure also provides optical blending of the carious lesion with the healthy enamel"; indications include "Fluorosis stains (non-pitted)", amelogenesis imperfecta and MIH; the etchant is "composed of 15% hydrochloric acid, water, silica", followed by ethanol drying, and "The Infiltrant is composed of triethylene glycol dimethacrylate" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Cohen's: "Resin infiltration has been proposed as an alternative treatment for the white opacities present in the hypomature enamel" in type 2 AI (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Extrinsic stain:** scaling, rubber-cup polishing, air polishing; responds well (Setzer, 2021). Whitening toothpastes (abrasive, chemical, titanium-dioxide cosmetic) only maintain the result (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

**Microabrasion** — acid plus abrasive removes a superficial layer of stained enamel; no bleaching action (Ritter et al, 2019).
- McCloskey (1984) 18% HCl; Croll and Cavanaugh (1986) 18% HCl plus pumice; later about 11% HCl with silicon carbide (Prema, Opalustre) (Ritter et al, 2019). McInnes solution: 36% HCl, 30% H2O2 and ether (5:5:1); modified with NaOH (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Technique: rubber dam, slurry applied with slow rubber cup, repeated up to 12-15 times, then polish and topical fluoride (Setzer, 2021).
- Indications: superficial fluorosis, white and brown spots, decalcification after orthodontics, superficial hypoplasia. Limit: 0.2-0.3 mm depth (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Contraindications: tetracycline and age-related stains, deep hypoplasia, most AI and DI (Gopikrishna, 2021). May leave teeth slightly more yellow (Sikri, 2020).

**Macroabrasion** — 12- or 16-fluted finishing bur or fine diamond at high speed with water spray, then 30-fluted bur and polishing (Gopikrishna, 2021). Faster, no rubber dam; microabrasion gives better control and is preferred in children; the two may be combined (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Brown fluorosis — order of treatment:** Goldstein's advises nightguard bleaching first (about 80% of brown spots respond to 10% carbamide peroxide), preserving fluoride-rich enamel, then abrasion if needed (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

**Resin infiltration (ICON):** 15% HCl etch, ethanol drying, low-viscosity TEGDMA infiltrant; masks white spot lesions optically and arrests them; for non-pitted fluorosis, AI and MIH opacities (Sikri, 2020; Setzer, 2021).

## 7. Vital (external) bleaching

### Explanation

Mechanism (Grossman's): hydrogen peroxide diffuses through enamel and dentin; "Bleaching agent opens the more highly pigmented carbon ring (yellow color) and converts it to carbon chains"; beyond the end-point, "Saturation point is that point at which only hydrophilic colorless tooth structure exists" and further bleaching degrades the organic matrix. Factors: concentration, time, "The optimum pH for hydrogen peroxide ranges from 9.5 to 10.8", and temperature — "The reaction gets doubled with an increase of 10°C"; at-home trays: "The tray should be worn for a time period of 4 hours for every session" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. In-office contraindications include "Children with large pulp chamber", pregnancy, hypersensitivity and exposed roots (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Indications (Sturdevant's): ageing, trauma, medication; "Teeth exhibiting yellow or orange intrinsic discolouration seem to respond best to vital bleaching"; vital teeth with "teeth that have a poor endodontic prognosis because of the absence of a radiographically visible canal" (calcific metamorphosis) (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's in-office indications: "Discolored teeth as a result of mild fluorosis, and tetracycline stains" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Nightguard bleaching: "A 10%–15% carbamide peroxide bleaching material generally is recommended"; "Total treatment time using an overnight approach is usually 1–2 weeks" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's gives longer times with 20%: "these types of stains generally require only 2–6 weeks of bleaching treatment"; "Tetracycline staining may take anywhere from 2 to 12 months of nightly treatment" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Chemistry: "10% carbamide peroxide is equal to 3.0% hydrogen peroxide" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. In-office: 30-35% hydrogen peroxide; "Bleaching treatments generally are rendered weekly for two to six treatments, with each treatment lasting 30–45 min"; light: "the addition of light during the bleaching procedure does not improve the whitening result beyond what the bleach alone can achieve" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri: "hydrogen peroxide above 15% concentration does not increase bleaching effectiveness" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Combination: "a combination of the in-office technique and the dentist-prescribed, home-applied technique has better results than either technique used alone" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. PMMA temporaries: "Polymethyl methacrylate restorations exhibit a yellow-orange discolouration on exposure to carbamide peroxide" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Conflict — white spots.** Goldstein's: "Bleaching with 10% carbamide peroxide is still the first treatment of choice because it can lighten the other portions of the tooth" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Summitt's agrees ("White spots do not bleach but may be less noticeable if the background color of the tooth is lightened") (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Cohen's warns that bleaching teeth with white or brown spot "discolorations could increase the existing contrast" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Both may happen — Goldstein's itself describes a temporary "splotchy" phase — so the patient must be warned [Likely].

**Slip — hydrogen or carbamide peroxide?** Andreasen's Ch.29 states "Nocturnal use of 10% hydrogen peroxide gel in a tray is a convenient way of bleaching teeth", but the study it cites is a "Clinical study of tooth shade lightening from dentist-supervised patient-applied treatment with two 10% carbamide peroxide gels" (Zachrisson and Toreskog, 2007) [SRC]. Since 10% carbamide peroxide releases only about 3% hydrogen peroxide (Sikri, 2020), the text should read carbamide peroxide [Likely].

Tetracycline: Goldstein's: "Dark tetracycline stains located in the gingival third of the tooth or dark blue or gray stains have the least favorable prognosis" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Sturdevant's: "Tetracycline-stained teeth may approach but never seem to achieve the appearance of normal teeth" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's: "the chemical cannot reach the real cause of the discoloration" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Mechanism:** H2O2 diffuses through enamel and dentin and releases free radicals that open pigmented carbon rings into lighter carbon chains; bleaching past the saturation point degrades the organic matrix. Efficacy rises with concentration, contact time, temperature (rate doubles per 10°C) and alkaline pH 9.5-10.8 (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Indications:** generalized yellow-orange stain (ageing, smoking, food), mild-moderate tetracycline, mild fluorosis (brown), vital traumatized or calcified tooth, before restorative work, matching teeth to existing crowns (Ritter et al, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021). Yellow-orange responds best; blue-grey worst (Ritter et al, 2019).

**1. Dentist-prescribed at-home (nightguard) bleaching:** custom vacuum-formed tray with reservoirs; 10-15% carbamide peroxide (10% CP ≈ 3% H2O2); overnight 1-2 weeks (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020); Goldstein's gives 2-6 weeks for ageing or food stains and 2-12 months for tetracycline (Goldstein et al, 2018a). One arch at a time, maxillary first.

**2. In-office (power) bleaching:** rubber dam or resin barrier; 30-35% H2O2 gel; 2-6 weekly visits of 30-45 min; added light does not improve the result and dehydrates teeth (Ritter et al, 2019).

**3. Combination** of in-office and at-home gives better results than either alone (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Contraindications:** caries, leaking restorations, hypersensitivity, exposed roots, children with large pulps, pregnancy and lactation, unrealistic expectations (Gopikrishna, 2021; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Special situations:**
- White spots: bleaching lightens the surrounding enamel so spots are less noticeable (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013), but contrast may increase (Setzer, 2021).
- Tetracycline: prolonged nightly bleaching; dark blue-grey and gingival-third stains have the poorest prognosis and may never match normal teeth (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Ritter et al, 2019).
- Existing restorations and PMMA provisionals do not bleach (PMMA yellows) (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 8. Non-vital (internal) bleaching

### Explanation

Indications/contraindications (Ingle's Table 40-2; Grossman's Table 23.3 lists the same items): indicated for discolorations of pulp-chamber origin, dentin discolorations and those not amenable to extracoronal bleaching; contraindicated for superficial enamel discoloration, defective enamel, severe dentin loss, caries and discoloured composites (Rotstein, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. West's ideal case: "the ideal tooth for nonvital bleaching is a discolored unrestored crown" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]. Agent: sodium perborate with water; Summitt's recommends "sodium perborate mixed with water, or 10% carbamide peroxide alone, used after the placement of a protective barrier material" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Heat: "application of highly concentrated H2O2 and heat during intracoronal bleaching is questionable" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC].

**Conflict — level of gutta-percha removal and barrier.**

(Gopikrishna, 2021; Rotstein, 2019; Setzer, 2021; Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; West et al, 2018; Kwon and Goldstein, 2018; Baumann and Beer, 2010; Dahl and Pallesen, 2007; Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. All agree that the barrier must reach coronal to the epithelial attachment; the commonest thickness quoted is 2 mm [Likely].

**Conflict — etching the chamber, the agent and heat.** Ingle's advises against acid etching of the chamber (Rotstein, 2019); Endodontology states "etching with 37.5% phosphoric acid for 15 seconds is recommended" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC], and Andreasen's protocol reads "The smear layer is removed by pulp chamber phosphoric acid-etch for 10 seconds" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. Agent: most books use perborate with water, but Goldstein's Ch.12 mixes perborate with "enough 35% hydrogen peroxide to form a thick white paste" and values, in pulpless teeth, "the ability to use higher temperatures without causing the patient discomfort" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC] — against Ingle's doubt about concentrated H2O2 and heat (Rotstein, 2019) and the resorption evidence (Section 13). Perborate with water and no heat is the safer reading; the value of etching is not settled by any book read [Likely].

Inside-outside: Sikri on the combination technique: "It provides synergistic effect and is significantly effective" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Summitt's closed variant uses a single-tooth tray, and "some single dark teeth may require 6 to 8 weeks of bleaching to resolve the discoloration" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** discoloration of pulp-chamber origin (necrosis, haemorrhage, remnants, materials) in an endodontically treated tooth with an intact crown; not for enamel defects, extensive restorations, caries or severe dentin loss (Rotstein, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021). Ideal: discoloured unrestored crown (West et al, 2018).

**Walking bleach — steps:**
1. Radiograph: adequate, well-condensed obturation; retreat if doubtful (Rotstein, 2019).
2. Shade and photographs; rubber dam.
3. Remove restorations; refine access to include pulp horns; remove gutta-percha below the cervical level (books vary from 1 mm below the CEJ to 2-3 mm) (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2020; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
4. Cervical barrier (GIC, RMGI, MTA, IRM, Cavit) about 2 mm thick, its coronal edge 1 mm incisal to the epithelial attachment as measured by probing (Rotstein, 2019; Setzer, 2021; West et al, 2018).
5. Sodium perborate with water (or 10% carbamide peroxide) packed into the chamber; seal with about 3 mm of temporary material (Rotstein, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
6. Review at 1-2 weeks; repeat 2-4 times; reassess diagnosis if no response (Rotstein, 2019).
7. Final restoration after a delay (Section 11).

**Safe bleaching rules** (Rotstein, 2019; West et al, 2018):
- Rubber dam with ligatures; protect mucosa with petroleum jelly or Orabase.
- Confirm adequate obturation; always place the cervical barrier — no resorption report in the literature had one.
- Avoid 30-35% H2O2 and heat (Goldstein's Ch.12 still uses both: Kwon and Goldstein, 2018). Chamber etching: none (Rotstein, 2019) or 10-15 s phosphoric acid (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007; Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Highest risk: tooth pulpless before 25 years, no or misplaced barrier, Superoxol with heat; lowest: pulpless after 25, correct barrier, perborate with water.
- Recall clinically and radiographically.

**Other methods:** inside-outside (open or closed with a single-tooth tray; 10% CP), synergistic, 6-8 weeks (Sikri, 2020; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); thermocatalytic 30-35% H2O2 with heat — no longer recommended because of cervical resorption (Rotstein, 2019; Ritter et al, 2019).

## 9. Restorative masking: composite, veneers and crowns

### Explanation

Veneers: "A veneer is a layer of tooth-coloured material that is applied to a tooth to restore localized or generalized defects and intrinsic discolourations"; "Partial veneers are indicated for the restoration of localized defects or areas of intrinsic discolouration"; direct composite suits "For cases involving young children or a single discoloured tooth"; intra-enamel preparation helps "To remove the outer, fluoride-rich layer of enamel that may be more resistant to acid etching"; subgingival margins only "if it involves significantly dark discolouration that presents a difficult esthetic problem" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Longevity: "Vital bleaching usually results in tooth lightening for only 1–3 years, whereas an etched porcelain veneer should last 10–15 years or longer" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Masking dark teeth: "Some suggest the use of an opaque resin to mask the discolorations prior to the cementation of the porcelain veneers"; lithium disilicate "masking can be achieved with less amount of tooth structure removal", but "it is still essential to take the stump shade of the prepared teeth" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Stepping stone: "Often, bleaching is the stepping‐stone to veneers" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; "the lighter color of the bleached teeth allows lighter and more translucent veneers" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Darkening under veneers: "Both vital and endodontically treated teeth under veneers may darken over time" — bleach from the lingual via the tray (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Hypoplasia: "Cosmetic contouring, microabrasion, bonding resins, veneering, or crowning can be used to treat hypoplastic defects"; in AI, "Bleaching is difficult because the dentinal layer shows through" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]. Composite colour: "Benzoyl peroxide, which is the chemical initiator in all chemically cured composites, is not color stable" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

Marginal stain on composites: after removing superficial stain "the composite’s margins should be etched for 15 s with 32–37% phosphoric acid" and resealed; deep stain means replacement; veneer cement lines darken "if a dual‐cured or chemically cured composite luting agent was used" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

Tetracycline — intentional devitalization? Cohen's notes walking bleach "will, however, require intentional devitalization and endodontic treatment of the tooth" and lists "power bleaching” and veneers" as current options (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri describes intentional root canal treatment for inside-outside bleaching (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Grossman's opposes it: "We believe that labial veneers with composite resins or even porcelain-veneer full-crown restorations are indicated instead of intentional devitalization" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Walton's, by contrast, calls RCT followed by internal bleaching for tetracycline "a more predictable procedure" than external bleaching (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; Endodontology accepts intentional devitalization of severely stained teeth but says it "should only be undertaken after proper consideration of the possible risks" (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Devitalizing a healthy pulp for colour is hard to justify when veneers exist [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Direct composite:** localized defects, single discoloured tooth, children, limited budget; partial or full (window) veneers; opaquers mask dark areas; repairable (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Indirect veneers (processed composite, feldspathic porcelain, pressed lithium disilicate):** generalized intrinsic stain, tetracycline, severe fluorosis, hypoplasia. Intra-enamel preparation gives space, removes fluoride-rich enamel and a finish line; margins subgingival only for very dark teeth (Ritter et al, 2019). Masking: opaque resin cement, lithium disilicate (masks with less reduction), stump shade to the laboratory (Goldstein et al, 2018a). Porcelain veneers last 10-15 years against 1-3 years for vital bleaching (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Bleach before veneers:** lighter base, thinner and more translucent veneer, less reduction; patient previews the result (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

**Discoloured existing restorations:** repolish; for marginal stain refinish, etch 15 s with 32-37% phosphoric acid and reseal; replace if stain is deep or caries is present; chemically cured (benzoyl peroxide) composites darken in bulk and are replaced; use light-cured resin cement under veneers to avoid a dark cement line (Goldstein et al, 2018a).

**Crowns:** badly broken-down teeth, severe tetracycline, AI and DI, large restorations (Goldstein et al, 2018b; Rotstein, 2019).

**Intentional devitalization for tetracycline:** described (Setzer, 2021; Sikri, 2020) and called more predictable than external bleaching by Walton's (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002), but Grossman's prefers veneers or crowns to devitalizing a healthy pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 10. Choice of treatment by cause

### Explanation

Collated from Sections 2-9. Calcific metamorphosis is where books disagree most:
- Ingle's: "Root canal therapy is often required, followed by intracoronal bleaching" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC].
- Torabinejad's (same first author, later): "If the patient desires color correction, external bleaching should be attempted first", then RCT and internal bleaching, and "The esthetic prognosis for such treatment is not always predictable" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC].
- Cohen's: "Bleaching procedures are not expected to render positive results" because the change is in translucency; veneers or crowns (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].
- Summitt's: "The tooth may remain vital but is not a good candidate for endodontic therapy and internal bleaching" — use tray bleaching (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's: "This condition is extremely difficult to treat" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].
- West: "a walking bleach can be performed without endodontics if the canal has receded far enough from the access cavity" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]; Sikri: "Calcified root canals without any root canal treatment are also candidates for non-vital bleaching" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].
- Andreasen's sides with Cohen's: in obliterated teeth "a successful and long-lasting effect of bleaching cannot be expected" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; Walton's follows Torabinejad's sequence and grades the outcome "Such teeth are bleached with fair esthetic prognosis." (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].
The most conservative reading — external bleaching first, then veneer or crown, with endodontics only for pathosis — is the common ground of Torabinejad's, Summitt's and Sturdevant's [Likely].

Ageing: "Unless pulp pathosis is noted, intracoronal bleaching is not indicated" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. AI/DI: AI and DI discolorations "are impossible to eliminate because they originate from the developmental defects of the enamel and dentin" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; DGI: "Bleaching treatment will have minimal to no effect; comprehensive restorative treatment is indicated"; AI bleaching is "of a temporary nature and often seen as an adjunct to comprehensive restorative treatment" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Metallic materials: "any metallic filling that remains visible through existing tooth structure, removal of the filling and exchange with an esthetic filling material is the preferred choice" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; amalgam-stained dentin is hard "to bleach and tend to re-discolor with time" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; pins — "coverage of the pins with a white cement or removal of the metal and replacement of the composite restoration is indicated" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Severe tetracycline: "severe cases, especially those of dark gray color, should be corrected by restorative means" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Metal ions: bleaching works for many sealer and medicament stains, but "this does not hold true for discolorations caused by metal ions (silver pins, amalgam)" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; with amalgam salts "no amount of bleaching with available products will significantly improve the esthetic quality of these teeth" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

**Conflict — blood-pigment stains (erythroblastosis fetalis, porphyria).** Goldstein's Ch.12: bleaching can "be quite effective for the discoloration caused by infusion of the dentin during development", giving as an example "The characteristically brownish teeth caused by destruction of an excessive number of erythrocytes" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Walton's: of erythroblastosis fetalis, "This discoloration is not correctable by bleaching."; of porphyria, thalassaemia, AI and DI, "These conditions are also not amenable to bleaching and should be corrected restoratively." (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Neither gives outcome data; a trial of bleaching with a guarded prognosis is the reasonable course before restoring [Likely]. Aging/smoking: "bleaching with 20% carbamide peroxide in a custom‐ fitting tray easily treats discolorations due to aging, smoking, or chromogenic foods" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Cause | First choice | If inadequate |
|---|---|---|
| Extrinsic (tea, tobacco, chlorhexidine, bacteria) | Prophylaxis, polishing | Vital bleaching |
| Ageing, smoking, food (generalized yellow) | Nightguard bleaching | Veneers |
| Mild-moderate fluorosis, brown spots | Bleaching, then microabrasion | Resin infiltration, composite, veneers |
| White spots, decalcification | Microabrasion or resin infiltration | Composite |
| Tetracycline, mild-moderate (yellow) | Prolonged nightguard bleaching | Veneers |
| Tetracycline, severe (grey-blue, banded) | Veneers (bleach first to lighten base) | Crowns |
| Hypoplasia, AI, DI | Composite or veneers | Crowns |
| Trauma, necrosis, chamber remnants (non-vital) | Walking bleach | Inside-outside, veneer, crown |
| Calcific metamorphosis (vital) | External (tray) bleaching | Veneer or crown; endodontics only for pathosis |
| Amalgam, pins, posts showing through | Replace or cover the metal | Veneer, crown |
| Discoloured or leaking composite | Repolish, reseal or replace | — |
| Endodontic sealer, MTA, TAP stain | Remove material, walking bleach | Veneer, crown |

(Goldstein et al, 2018a; Rotstein, 2019; Setzer, 2021; Ritter et al, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021.)

Ageing alone is treated externally; intracoronal bleaching is not indicated unless pulp pathosis is present (Rotstein, 2019). Blood-pigment stains: bleachable (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) or not (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) — books differ. Amalgam-stained dentin bleaches poorly and tends to rediscolour, so the amalgam is replaced (Rotstein, 2019; Setzer, 2021).

Calcific metamorphosis is contested: Ingle's advises RCT then internal bleaching (Rotstein, 2019); Torabinejad's says external bleaching first, prognosis unpredictable (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021); Cohen's says bleaching is not expected to work (Setzer, 2021), and Andreasen's that a lasting result cannot be expected (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007); West describes walking bleach without endodontics (West et al, 2018).

Blood-pigment stains (erythroblastosis fetalis, porphyria): bleachable according to Goldstein's (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018), not according to Walton's (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) — books differ. Metal-ion stains do not bleach (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 11. Aesthetic considerations in endodontically treated teeth

### Explanation

Why they darken: "Pulpal degeneration and subsequent endodontic access microleakage are the two main reasons for clinical crown discoloration" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]; Goldstein's Ch.23: "the loss of vitality results in a change in color over time" (Goldstein et al, 2018b) [SRC]. Grossman's figure is small: "Fewer than 5% of treated pulpless teeth become noticeably discolored" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Prevention during endodontics: "pulp horns must always be included in the access cavity to ensure removal of pulpal remnants"; obturation discoloration "can be easily prevented by removing all materials to a level just below the gingival margin" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; avoid staining materials (Section 3); irrigate after excessive haemorrhage — "Discoloration of the tooth may occur if hemorrhage is excessive during pulp extirpation" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; seal the access invisibly — "focus should be made to make access cavity repair as “invisible” as possible by blocking out metal" (West et al, 2018) [SRC].

Restoration choice (Goldstein Ch.23): "endodontically treated anterior teeth do not automatically require restoration with a crown"; intact tooth → composite; "If it has changed color, then bleaching of the tooth would also be indicated"; moderate restorations or incisal edge involved → "then a porcelain veneer could be the appropriate choice for treatment"; "bleaching of the endodontically treated tooth prior to restoration with composite resin or a porcelain veneer will provide a better esthetic result"; "Three major reasons for using crowns are (1) if the tooth is badly broken down", (2) major contour change, (3) abutment; and "The purpose of a post is to retain the core; it does not reinforce the root" (Goldstein et al, 2018b) [SRC]. Grossman's: crowns weakened by access and large restorations "should be restored with a post and core and a full-veneer porcelain crown" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; West: "If larger restorations are present or are required, these teeth need to be treated with a veneer or full crown" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]. Bleach first under ceramics: "Discolored tooth structure may reflect through porcelain crowns and veneers thereby compromising the final esthetic result" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]. Andreasen's chapter on traumatized teeth makes bleaching the first step: it "must be regarded as the treatment of choice, alone or preceding a prosthetic restoration", and "bleaching should be the initial treatment in both young and old patients" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]. Prevention in immature teeth: "the use of MTA should be confined to the apical 3 mm" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

Restoring the bleached access: "Placing white cement beneath the composite access restoration is recommended"; "Light curing from the labial surface, rather than the lingual surface, is recommended" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; a sound seal "will prevent rediscoloration due to access microleakage" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]. Rebleaching: "A better treatment option once the tooth has been bleached internally and the lingual access restored with resin composite is to bleach the tooth externally" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

**Conflict — delay before bonding.** Grossman's: "No loss of bond strength is noted if the composite restorative treatment is delayed at least 1 week after cessation of any bleaching" (also in Sturdevant's), and for in-office vital bleaching "Any bonded restoration on bleached surfaces must be postponed by 7–10 days" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's: "waiting for at least 7 days after bleaching, prior to restoring the tooth with resin composites, has been recommended" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's: closure "is delayed for 2 weeks to allow the shade to stabilize" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein's: "wait 2–3 weeks following termination of bleaching before appointing the patient for the restoration procedure" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC]; Cohen's: "Clinicians are generally advised to wait for 2 to 3 weeks prior to placing a definitive adhesive restoration" and "Optimal bonding to bleached dentin and enamel was shown to be reestablished after 3 weeks" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri: "24 hours to 4 weeks have been quoted in various studies" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Endodontology: "an approximately 3-week interval before the" definitive adhesive restoration (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Andreasen's: "It is recommended to have the final restoration placed 1–3 weeks after completion of the bleaching" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; Goldstein's Ch.12: "Placement of the final restoration is delayed for 2 weeks to allow the oxygen generated during bleaching to dissipate" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Alani and Patel: bond strength falls "by up to 60% if the bonding procedure is carried out immediately after tooth bleaching treatment", so restorations "should be delayed for at least 2–4 weeks after the bleaching treatment to allow for optimal adhesive bonding strength" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Sodium ascorbate is offered as a reversal agent: "Sodium ascorbate is a buffered form of vitamin C" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The 1-week figure is the bond-strength minimum; 2-3 weeks adds shade stabilization, which matters for an aesthetic match [Likely]. Detail: P2.

**Conflict — calcium hydroxide after internal bleaching.** Sturdevant's: the calcium hydroxide paste is left "to remain in the pulp chamber for 2 weeks" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Grossman's: "a paste of calcium hydroxide powder and sterile water is placed in the pulp chamber" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Against: Ingle's "this procedure, however, is unnecessary with walking bleach" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's "it may be unnecessary after a walking bleach technique" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's "however, this procedure is ineffective and unnecessary" (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Walton's "This procedure is ineffective and unnecessary." (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. For it, Endodontology: "Calcium hydroxide paste is placed for a minimum of 7 days onto the dentin for neutralization" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Four books against three (Walton's and Torabinejad's share a lineage); the dispute is about perborate (mild) versus Superoxol regimens [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Why endodontically treated teeth darken:** pulp degeneration and blood products before treatment; pulp remnants in the horns; sealer and gutta-percha left in the chamber; staining medicaments and materials; access microleakage; dehydration and loss of translucency (West et al, 2018; Rotstein, 2019; Goldstein et al, 2018b).

**Prevention during endodontic treatment:**
- Access including pulp horns; remove all tissue and blood; irrigate well (Rotstein, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Remove sealer and gutta-percha to just below the gingival margin; clean the chamber with solvent (Rotstein, 2019).
- Avoid silver sealers, minocycline pastes and NaOCl-chlorhexidine contact; keep MTA below the CEJ (Setzer, 2021).
- Seal the access with a well-bonded, tooth-coloured restoration that blocks out metal (West et al, 2018).

**Choice of restoration (anterior teeth):**
- Intact, discoloured: walking bleach + bonded composite in the access; no crown needed (Goldstein et al, 2018b).
- Moderate loss or incisal involvement: bleach, then porcelain veneer (Goldstein et al, 2018b).
- Badly broken down, contour change or abutment: post and core with an all-ceramic crown; the post retains the core, it does not reinforce the root (Goldstein et al, 2018b; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Bleach before any ceramic restoration, because dark dentin shows through (Rotstein, 2019).
- Posterior teeth: cuspal coverage (Goldstein et al, 2018b).

**Restoring the bleached tooth:** white base over the barrier, light-shade acid-etched composite placed deep enough to seal and support the incisal edge, light-cured from labial (Rotstein, 2019). Delay bonding: at least 1 week (Gopikrishna, 2021; Rotstein, 2019), 2 weeks (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) or 2-3 weeks (Goldstein et al, 2018a; Setzer, 2021); 3 weeks (Baumann and Beer, 2010); at least 2-4 weeks, bonding immediately loses up to 60% (Alani and Patel, 2025); sodium ascorbate may reverse the bond loss (Gopikrishna, 2021). Calcium hydroxide dressing: advised by Sturdevant's, Grossman's and Endodontology but called unnecessary after perborate walking bleach by Ingle's, Cohen's, Torabinejad's and Walton's (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Relapse:** rebleach externally with a single-tooth tray rather than removing the composite (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 12. Advanced methods and materials

### Explanation

From Sikri's "advanced techniques" and laser sections: nitrogen-doped TiO2 — "Titanium dioxide nanoparticle doped with nitrogen with less concentration of hydrogen peroxide"; KTP laser — "this energy aligns particularly well with the wavelength of KTP laser (532 nm)" to photo-oxidize the tetracycline quinone, so "Smartbleach is preferred choice for bleaching tetracycline-stained teeth"; non-thermal plasma — "room temperature plasma could be used for tooth bleaching"; remineralization "using CPP-ACP based substances during and after bleaching"; "Enzymatic dentifrices can be used as bleaching catalyst by generating hydrogen peroxide in situ" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. These are reported in one Indian textbook from early studies; Sturdevant's adds that light adds nothing beyond the gel (Section 7) — evidence for lasers is weak [Likely]. Resin infiltration (Section 6), lithium disilicate veneers with stump shade (Section 9), MTA barrier (Section 8), sodium ascorbate (Section 11) and Summitt's single-tooth tray and inside-outside closed technique are the other recent materials. Digital planning: Goldstein's recommends esthetic computer imaging or a trial smile as "the truest form of informed consent" [paraphrased; no locator].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advanced methods and materials:**
- **Bleaching agents:** carbamide peroxide with carbopol, potassium nitrate and fluoride (less sensitivity); nitrogen-doped TiO2 nanoparticles with low H2O2 (Sikri, 2020).
- **Activation:** KTP laser (532 nm) photo-oxidizes the tetracycline quinone (Smartbleach); diode and argon lasers; non-thermal atmospheric plasma (Sikri, 2020). Light activation adds little beyond the gel (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Remineralization:** CPP-ACP during and after bleaching; enzymatic dentifrices generating peroxide in situ (Sikri, 2020).
- **Microinvasive:** resin infiltration (ICON) for white spots and non-pitted fluorosis (Sikri, 2020).
- **Endodontic:** MTA or RMGI cervical barrier; sodium perborate or 10% CP walking bleach; inside-outside closed technique with a single-tooth tray (Gopikrishna, 2021; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- **Bonding:** antioxidants (sodium ascorbate) to reverse post-bleaching bond loss (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Restorative:** lithium disilicate veneers that mask with less reduction; opaque resin cements; stump shade (Goldstein et al, 2018a); digital imaging and trial smile.

## 13. Prognosis, complications and prevention

### Explanation

Prognosis. Internal: Cohen's — "Discolorations induced by trauma or pulp necrosis appear to have a good prognosis of around 95%"; restorative-material stains have "an uncertain prognosis"; thermocatalytic follow-up "reported an 80% success rate after 1 year and a 45% success rate after 6 years" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Summitt's: internal bleaching can "return teeth to their desired color in 83% to 91% of cases", but "Within 1 to 5 years, only 35% to 50% of the teeth maintain their esthetically pleasing appearance" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Grossman's: "The prognosis for bleaching of a discolored pulpless tooth is good when the discoloration is due to the products of pulp decomposition", and "Bleaching generally has an approximate life span of 1–3 years" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's: "External bleaching may show color regressions after 1 to 3 years" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Andreasen's: "More than 90% immediate success has been reported for intracoronal bleaching of non-vital teeth", but "The need for retreatment increased with the observation time, 10% after 1–2 years", "20–25% after 3–5 years" and "40% in teeth observed up to 8 years" (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007) [SRC]; Endodontology: in studies of 8–16 years "success rates of 45%–93% are reported" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

**Conflict — incidence of cervical resorption after internal bleaching.** Sturdevant's: "a slight potential (i.e. 1%) exists"; Summitt's: "This problem affects about 7% of the teeth that have undergone internal in-office bleaching", animal studies "with a resorption incidence of 18% to 25% when heat was applied"; West: "occurred in 5–10% of the teeth examined"; Sikri: "The incidence of cervical root resorption after non-vital bleaching ranges from 5 to 10%" (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; West et al, 2018; Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The figures come from different techniques (with or without heat, Superoxol, barrier) and are not comparable [Likely]. Risk factors: West's least safe — pulpless before 25, no barrier, Superoxol plus heat; safest — "Tooth became pulpless in a patient older than 25 years" (West et al, 2018) [SRC]; Ingle's: "In none of the clinical reports of postbleaching root resorption was a protective barrier used" (Rotstein, 2019) [SRC]; onset "which generally has its onset in 1–7 years" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Detail: P1, B09.

Goldstein's Ch.12 cites Heithersay's series, in which most invasive cervical resorption followed orthodontics or trauma "and 3.9% by intracoronal bleaching" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

Vital-bleaching complications: Craig's: "peroxides can rapidly (within minutes) traverse the dentin in sufficient concentrations to be cytotoxic", and "a legitimate concern exists about the long-term use of these products on vital teeth" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]; sensitivity "in up to 50% of the teeth" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; gingival burns; enamel surface change; mercury release — "no health implications due to the induced release of mercury by tooth bleaching have been reported" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; bond-strength loss (Section 11). Prevention of amalgam stain: "The use of a copal resin cavity varnish will prevent this discoloration" (Goldstein et al, 2018a) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prognosis:**
- Best: trauma and necrosis stains (about 95%) (Setzer, 2021); yellow-orange, ageing, food stains.
- Poor: metallic and restorative-material stains, dark grey-blue and gingival-third tetracycline, AI, DI, severe fluorosis (Setzer, 2021; Goldstein et al, 2018a; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
- Relapse: vital bleaching lasts about 1-3 years (Gopikrishna, 2021; Setzer, 2021). Internal bleaching succeeds initially in 83-91%, but only 35-50% stay acceptable at 1-5 years (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); thermocatalytic 80% at 1 year, 45% at 6 years (Setzer, 2021). Retreatment after internal bleaching rises with time: 10% at 1-2 years, 20-25% at 3-5 years, 40% by 8 years (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007).

**Complications:**
- *Internal:* external cervical resorption — 1% (Ritter et al, 2019), about 7% (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013), 5-10% (West et al, 2018; Sikri, 2020); risk with Superoxol, heat, no barrier, trauma, pulpless before 25 years; onset 1-7 years, so recall radiographs (Gopikrishna, 2021). Also chemical burns, weakened crown, reduced bond strength.
- *External:* peroxide crosses dentin to the pulp within minutes (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019); sensitivity (up to 50%), gingival irritation, enamel surface change, mercury release from amalgam (no proven health effect), colour mismatch with restorations (Setzer, 2021).
- *Abrasion:* loss of enamel, yellower appearance (Sikri, 2020).
- *Restorative:* overcontouring, gingival inflammation, marginal staining, fracture (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Prevention:** oral hygiene and diet counselling; avoid tetracycline in pregnancy and children under 8 (Setzer, 2021); optimal fluoride; varnish or liner under amalgam (Goldstein et al, 2018a); careful endodontics (Section 11); seal restorations; recall and touch-up bleaching.

**Conclusion.** Discoloured teeth are managed by diagnosing the cause and depth of the stain, then using the most conservative effective step — prophylaxis, abrasion, infiltration, bleaching — and keeping veneers and crowns for stains that do not respond.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1 — Walking bleach with sodium perborate and a cervical barrier is safe.** Textbook: Ingle's, Grossman's and Sturdevant's make perborate the agent of choice and the barrier mandatory (Rotstein, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021; Ritter et al, 2019). Evidence read: Ingle's notes that none of the resorption reports used a barrier (Rotstein, 2019); Summitt's cites animal resorption of 18-25% with heat against 0-6% without, but adds that the barrier "reduces the diffusion of hydrogen but does not prevent it" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). Andreasen's sets the clinical series side by side: 7% resorption in 58 teeth bleached with 30% H2O2 and heat, none in 95 teeth after perborate with water at 3 years or in 250 tetracycline teeth at 4 years (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007); Cohen's adds that perborate with water gave similar results "because it did not cause cervical resorption, even 1 year after bleaching" (Setzer, 2021). Interpretation: refinement — the barrier and avoidance of heat and Superoxol lower the risk; they do not abolish it. Limitation: the incidences (1% to 10%) come from mixed techniques, case series and animal work; no comparison of barrier versus no barrier in patients was available. Conclusion: use perborate, a barrier and no heat, and recall radiographically for years [Likely].

**Claim 2 — Bleaching is the first treatment for intrinsic stains.** Textbook: Sturdevant's and Goldstein's (Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein et al, 2018a). Challenge: for calcific metamorphosis Cohen's expects no benefit (Setzer, 2021); for white spots Cohen's warns of more contrast (Setzer, 2021); for AI and DI the effect is minimal or temporary (Setzer, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021). Interpretation: bleaching first is right where the colour is pigment in dentin (ageing, necrosis, mild tetracycline, brown fluorosis); where the problem is structure or translucency, abrasion, infiltration or restoration comes first. Strength: clinical case series and expert opinion; no comparative trials were available in the books read [Likely].

**Claim 3 — Internal bleaching gives a lasting result.** Textbook: Grossman's calls the prognosis good for necrosis stains (Gopikrishna, 2021). Refinement: Summitt's reports 83-91% initial success but only 35-50% stable at 1-5 years; Cohen's cites 80% at 1 year falling to 45% at 6 years for thermocatalytic bleaching, while "A randomized clinical trial evaluating CIELab parameters of teeth bleached using the walking bleach or an inside-outside technique" found "no significant changes in color and lightness comparing the short-term bleaching results with the 1-year follow-up" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Setzer, 2021). Longer series show the decline: retreatment in 10% at 1-2 years, 20-25% at 3-5 years and 40% by 8 years (Dahl and Pallesen, 2007), and 45-93% success at 8-16 years (Baumann and Beer, 2010). The 1-year trial and the 8-year series do not conflict — relapse appears after the first year. Interpretation: the immediate result is good, long-term stability is moderate, and it depends on the technique and the coronal seal. Clinical implication: tell the patient that touch-up bleaching may be needed, and place a well-sealed restoration [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Classification: extrinsic, intrinsic, internalized (Sikri); Nathoo N1/N2/N3 for extrinsic stain.
- Commonest single-tooth cause: pulp necrosis or decomposition; commonest pre-eruptive: fluorosis.
- Ladder: prophylaxis → micro/macroabrasion → resin infiltration → vital bleaching → non-vital bleaching → composite → veneers → crowns.
- Microabrasion: 18% HCl plus pumice (or about 11% HCl with silicon carbide), limit 0.2-0.3 mm.
- Nightguard: 10-15% CP (10% CP ≈ 3% H2O2); in-office 30-35% H2O2; tetracycline 2-12 months.
- Walking bleach: perborate + water, 2 mm cervical barrier 1 mm incisal to the epithelial attachment, no heat.
- Delay bonding at least 1 week; 2-3 weeks for shade stability.
- Endodontically treated anterior teeth do not automatically need crowns; bleach before veneer or crown.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing only bleaching techniques and omitting diagnosis, the ladder and restorative masking.
- Treating calcific metamorphosis with routine endodontics and internal bleaching as if it were the only option — books disagree; external bleaching first.
- Recommending microabrasion for tetracycline or age-related stains (contraindicated).
- Omitting the cervical barrier, or using Superoxol with heat, in non-vital bleaching.
- Bonding composite immediately after bleaching.

## MNEMONIC
**"PAIR-VNC"** — the ladder: **P**rophylaxis, **A**brasion (micro/macro), **I**nfiltration, b**R**ightening (vital then non-vital bleaching), **V**eneer (direct then indirect), **N**ew restoration (replace stained fillings), **C**rown.
