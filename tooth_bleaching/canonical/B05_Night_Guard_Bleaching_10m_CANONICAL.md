## 1. Definition and history

### Explanation

Nightguard vital bleaching (NGVB, also called dentist-prescribed home-applied, at-home or matrix bleaching) is the dentist-supervised application of a low-concentration peroxide gel in a custom tray worn by the patient. Goldstein: "Matrix bleaching refers to bleaching procedures that the patient uses outside the dental office" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson: "This technique is sometimes also referred to as nightguard bleaching, as it requires construction of a thin bleaching tray out of soft polyvinyl" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

History. Summitt: the home technique "employing a custom-fit tray containing 10% carbamide peroxide solution, was first used by Klusmier" in the late 1960s, but the profession took it up only after "In 1989, Haywood and Heymann wrote the first article on an at-home bleaching technique" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant agrees: home bleaching of vital teeth "began around 1968, although it was not commonly known until the late 1980s" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Anagnostaki dates "night guard vital bleaching in 1989 by Haywood and Heymann" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC], and Powers and Wataha: "Whitening teeth outside the dental office was introduced in 1989" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Regulatory history: "In 1991, the FDA ruled that the use of carbamide peroxide in the form advocated for home bleaching constituted a new drug use", later reconsidered (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Nightguard vital (matrix, home-applied) bleaching: the patient applies a low-concentration peroxide gel in a custom soft tray under the dentist's supervision (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018).

**History.** First used by Klusmier around 1968; popularised by Haywood and Heymann in 1989 (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).

## 2. Bleaching agent

### Explanation

Concentration. Sturdevant recommends "A 10%–15% carbamide peroxide bleaching material" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt says the "best combination of safety, limited side effects, and speed of action is obtained with a 10% solution", that 90% of surveyed dentists used 10%, and "the 15% formulation is also very popular" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson: 10% "provides a balance between optimal results and minimal side effects" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein: "Most matrix bleaching uses 10–15% carbamide peroxide" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Wider ranges: 10% to 22% (Setzer, 2021; Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], "carbamide peroxide in concentrations up to 20%" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC]. Hydrogen peroxide day-wear products "range in concentration from 7.5% to 9.5% hydrogen peroxide. They are only active for 30 to 60 minutes" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Breakdown of 10% carbamide peroxide — nine books, five figures (the same set is shown in B02, B04, B05, B10 and B11): Summitt's "6.5% urea and 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Torabinejad's "approximately 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" with urea, ammonia and carbon dioxide (Rotstein and Silvestrin, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's 10-30% "(equivalent to 3.5% to 8.6% hydrogen peroxide)", although its home-bleaching section gives 10-22% "with an effective yield of 4% to 7.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's "approximately 3.4% hydrogen peroxide and 6.5% urea", although its reaction scheme a page earlier writes "+ 3.5% hydrogen peroxide" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson's "release 3.3% hydrogen peroxide", the one-third rule (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's "3% hydrogen peroxide (active ingredient) and 7% urea" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri's "10% carbamide peroxide is equal to 3.0% hydrogen peroxide" in both his books (Sikri, 2020; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Alani and Patel's "approximately 3% hydrogen peroxide over a period of 4 h" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Reasoning: the crystals "contain H2O2 in a concentration of about 35%" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC], so 10% gives about 3.5%; 3.3% and 3% are rounded one-third figures [Likely]. At higher strengths the books fall below that proportion: Cohen's 30% = 8.6% (proportionally 10.5%) and Summitt's "A 35% carbamide peroxide product is approximately equivalent to a 9.5% hydrogen peroxide product" (proportionally about 12%) (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; neither book explains the shortfall [Likely; the arithmetic is the auditor's]. Anagnostaki's "a carbamide peroxide concentration of 16% is equivalent to 5.76% of hydrogen peroxide" fits the 36% ratio (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2023) [SRC].

Urea: "The urea degrades into ammonia and carbon dioxide" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] and "is primarily responsible for raising the pH in the oral cavity above 8 for a number of hours" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Carbopol: "carbopol thickens the bleaching solution and extends the oxidation process" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; it is "The most significant additive to carbamide peroxide" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

Activity time — books disagree (the same set is shown in B02, B05 and B11): Summitt's "Carbamide peroxide is active for up to 10 hours, with about 50% of the active agent being used up in the first 2 hours", although "the agent has lost most of its potency after 5 hours" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson: "active even after 10 hours of use" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Grossman's: it "can be active up to several hours" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Alani and Patel: 3% hydrogen peroxide released "over a period of 4 h" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; against Cohen's "whereas carbamide peroxide remains active for 40 to 90 minutes" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha's home regimen, in which "The gel is replenished each hour" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], fits the shorter figure. Summitt's Table 16-2 gives "2 to 10 hours due to carbopol amount" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC], so carbopol content probably explains the gap and Cohen's figure may describe unthickened gels [Likely]; Cohen's also notes that the reaction "takes place at a slower rate compared to hydrogen peroxide" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Agent.** 10% carbamide peroxide (10-15% common; up to 20-22%), with carbopol to thicken the gel and prolong oxygen release; urea raises pH (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). 10% CP ≈ 3.5% H₂O₂ + 6.5% urea (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013); 3.4% (Gopikrishna, 2021); 3% + 7% urea (Ritter et al, 2019). Active up to 10 h (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); Cohen's: 40-90 min (Setzer, 2021). Day-wear: 7.5-9.5% H₂O₂.

## 3. Case selection

### Explanation

Goldstein's indications for matrix bleaching: "yellowed or discolored teeth in first degree and moderate second degree", moderate yellow-brown tetracycline and intrinsic stains, and "patients who are not candidates for in‐office bleaching because of hypersensitive teeth, time restrictions, financial considerations" or objection to rubber dam (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Contraindications: extreme hypersensitivity, allergy-type reactions ("Other hypersensitivity reactions, such as burning sensations, sore throat, nausea, irritation, or edema"), "Lack of compliance", severe discoloration and extensive restorations (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; TMD patients "may not be good candidates for at-home bleaching" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. General contraindications are in B04.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications:** generalized yellow stains, mild tetracycline, patients unsuitable for in-office bleaching. **Avoid:** poor compliance, allergy (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018), TMD (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 4. Tray fabrication

### Explanation

Impression and cast. An alginate impression of one arch; Summitt trims the cast unconventionally — "A model trimmer is used to trim the cast from the base rather than from the sides, until the vestibule is eliminated", or it is "trimmed to a horseshoe shape, leaving only the maxillary or mandibular teeth and periodontal tissues remaining" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's figure keeps "a base height of 10–12 mm" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Material. The original nightguard vital bleaching article "proposed use of a thick, semirigid material"; "The newer materials are thinner, softer, easier to shape and trim, and have reduced gingival and occlusal side effects" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Thickness and sag vary by book: Sturdevant "a sheet of 0.75-to1.5-mm (0.020-to-0.040-in) soft vinyl nightguard material" softened "until the material sags approximately by 25 mm (1 in)" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein cites Haywood that "the best ­prosthetic material is a 0.9 mm clear soft material", and his figure says the sheet is heated until it sags 12 mm (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Sikri "Usually 0.040 inch or 0.035 inch ethyl vinyl acetate" which "sags approximately one inch" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. A boil-and-form thermoplastic tray needs no impression (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

**Scalloping — books disagree.** Sturdevant first trims "about 3–5 mm from the most apical portion of the gingival crest", then "trims the facial edges of the nightguard in a scalloped design, following the outline of the free gingival crest"; lingual scalloping is optional; "This scalloped design is preferred because it allows the tray to cover only teeth" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein: "Trim the gingival margins as close as possible to the cervical margin of the teeth" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri trims "until only about 2.0 mm of tissue apical to gingival crest is covered facially and lingually" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt describes "a scalloped tray border, positioned 1 mm incisal/ occlusal to the soft tissue" but says "Scalloping is not necessary when low concentrations of carbamide peroxide (10%) are used", while "the higher the concentration of bleaching material, the more likely it is that there will be tissue irritation" and the greater the need to scallop; "The original tray design involved a nonscalloped, nonreservoir tray" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. So: scalloped short of the gingiva (Sturdevant, Goldstein, Summitt for higher concentrations) versus extending onto 2 mm of tissue (Sikri; Summitt's non-scalloped option for 10%).

**Reservoirs — books disagree on value.** Construction: light-cured resin "placed 0.5 to 1.0 mm thick on the facial surfaces of the teeth of the cast", which "should terminate 1.0 mm short of the gingival area and should not extend into the embrasures", and "Spacers should not be placed in areas of occlusion" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant: reservoir margins "end 1 mm short of the free gingival margin" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri: facial surface painted "1.0 mm from the gingival margin with a thickness of 0.5 mm" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson use LC Block-out resin (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Value: Summitt — "there is little evidence that the creation of reservoirs actually improves bleaching efficacy" and "several studies have shown no clinical difference in efficacy between reservoir and" non-reservoir trays (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson — need depends on gel viscosity and "The presence of reservoirs increasing the efficacy of the treatment" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri lists advantages (space for gel, seating with viscous gels, "Minimizes tooth sensitivity by avoiding the pinching effect of a light tray") and disadvantages (cost, "Decreased tray retention", gel leakage) (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Foam liners: Sikri claims a "50% reduction in bleaching time" whereas Summitt says "research has failed to demonstrate an advantage" (Sikri, 2020; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC] — a direct conflict.

Occlusion: because the tray is too thin to adjust, "the most posterior tooth molds of the tray should be removed" until anterior contact returns (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant similarly trims "to exclude coverage of the terminal posterior teeth" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Tray fabrication.**
1. Alginate impression; cast trimmed to a thin horseshoe, no vestibule or palate.
2. Optional reservoirs: resin 0.5-1.0 mm thick on labial surfaces, 1.0 mm short of the gingiva; benefit unproven (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013), though Bonsor and Pearson (2013) claim greater efficacy.
3. Vacuum-form 0.75-1.5 mm soft vinyl (Ritter et al, 2019), heated until it sags.
4. Trim: scalloped along the free gingival crest (Ritter et al, 2019), or non-scalloped for 10% CP (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).
5. Try-in: fit, no blanching; remove terminal molar molds to restore anterior contact.

## 5. Regimen and duration

### Explanation

Application: "A thin bead of material is extruded into the nightguard" on the facial aspects; "only the anterior six to eight teeth are bleached"; excess is wiped off and the patient should "not to drink liquids or rinse during treatment" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; the tray is "peeled" from the second molar area (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Timing: "Ideally, carbamide peroxide is best worn overnight for maximum benefit of the product and compliance by the patient"; daytime wear means "2 to 4 or more hours" daily (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson: "2 hours every evening or overnight" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Sikri's daytime regimen changes the gel "every 1.5–2 hours" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Goldstein's commonest regimen is "between 1 and 4 hours daily use from 4 weeks to 6 months (for tetracycline stain)" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]; Grossman's: "The tray should be worn for a time period of 4 hours for every session" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha: a tray of gel "for 3 to 4 hours once or several times a day", and "The gel is replenished each hour" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Hourly replenishment fits Cohen's short activity time rather than Summitt's 10 hours (Section 2) [Likely]. Swallowing small amounts "is not harmful" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

**Duration — books differ:** Sturdevant: "Total treatment time using an overnight approach is usually 1–2 weeks" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri: daytime 1-3 weeks but "4 to 6 weeks are required for night- time bleaching" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Cohen: "2 to 6 weeks" (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]; Goldstein: "9 out of 10 patients experiencing a lightening of their teeth in 2–6 weeks" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's Table 16-3 by stain: normal teeth "3 days to 6 weeks (average of 2 weeks)", nicotine 1-3 months, tetracycline "1 to 12 months (average of 3 to 4 months)" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. The figures are compatible once stain type is accounted for. [Likely]

Monitoring: "It is recommended that only one arch be bleached at a time, beginning with the maxillary arch", so the lower arch is a standard and two trays are not worn together (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen recalls daytime users weekly for 3 weeks and night-time users every other week for 6 weeks (Setzer, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri describes a microsensor embedded in the tray to record wear time (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Touch-up: "it may be 3 years before retreatment is desirable" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Regimen.** Thin bead of gel on the labial of each tooth; seat, wipe excess; no drinking. Overnight is best; or 2-4 hours by day (Ritter et al, 2019; Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013). One arch at a time, maxillary first.
**Duration (books differ):** 1-2 weeks overnight (Ritter et al, 2019); 2-6 weeks (Setzer, 2021); average 2 weeks for normal teeth, 1-3 months nicotine, 1-12 months tetracycline (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013).

## 6. Advantages, disadvantages and side effects

### Explanation

Sturdevant's advantages: "The use of a lower concentration of peroxide (generally 10%–15% carbamide peroxide)", ease of application, minimal side effects, and "Lower cost because of the reduced chair time required for treatment"; disadvantages: "Reliance on patient compliance", longer treatment time and "The (unknown) potential for soft tissue changes with excessively extended use" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri adds that the operator cannot monitor wear time (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

Side effects: "the two primary adverse effects" are sensitive teeth and irritated gingiva (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sensitivity occurs in 25-70% in double-blind studies, but "18% report sensitivity just from wearing the bleaching tray without any gel", and "the greatest sensitivity seems to occur in the first 2 weeks of treatment" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Management: passive (shorten or skip sessions) or active — "the application of 3% to 5% potassium nitrate gel in the tray", or potassium nitrate toothpaste in the tray for 10 to 30 minutes (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]; fluoride acts "as a tubular blocker", potassium nitrate "is thought to act to chemically depolarize the nerve" (Kwon and Goldstein, 2018) [SRC]. Gingival irritation "is most likely the result of an ill-fitting tray rather than the agent itself" (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC]. Others: metallic taste (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC], TMJ symptoms from the tray and sore throat from swallowing gel (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Enamel: Bonsor and Pearson report loss of the aprismatic layer after a month of prolonged daily 10% CP exposure (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC], whereas Sikri cites studies in which 10% CP daily for 6 months did not adversely affect enamel texture (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Safety: the FDA recognises 10% to 15% carbamide peroxide as GRAS for oral use (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages:** low concentration, easy, minimal side effects, less chair time and cost, touch-up with the same tray (Ritter et al, 2019).
**Disadvantages:** depends on compliance, slower, unknown effect of prolonged soft-tissue exposure (Ritter et al, 2019).
**Side effects:** transient sensitivity (manage by shorter sessions or 3-5% potassium nitrate in the tray), gingival irritation from ill-fitting trays, TMJ discomfort, sore throat (Haywood, da Costa and Berry, 2013; Powers and Wataha, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Haywood and Heymann, 1989: 10% carbamide peroxide in a custom tray (first used by Klusmier, late 1960s).
- 10% CP ≈ 3.5% H₂O₂ + 6.5% urea (Summitt); Grossman 3.4%; Sturdevant 3% + 7%; active up to 10 h (Cohen: 40-90 min).
- Carbopol thickens and prolongs release; urea raises pH.
- Tray: 0.75-1.5 mm soft vinyl; reservoirs 0.5-1.0 mm, ending 1 mm short of gingiva (benefit unproven).
- Overnight wear, one arch at a time; usually 2-6 weeks; tetracycline months.
- Sensitivity and gingival irritation are the two main side effects.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Saying 10% carbamide peroxide contains 10% hydrogen peroxide (it yields about 3-3.5%).
- Presenting reservoirs as essential; Summitt reports no proven gain in efficacy.
- Bleaching both arches at once without explaining why one arch at a time is preferred.
- Omitting the occlusal check (removing terminal molar molds).
- Giving a single treatment duration without relating it to stain type.

## MNEMONIC
**"CARBO"** for the nightguard agent: **C**arbamide 10%, **A**ctive H₂O₂ about 3.5%, **R**eservoir optional, **B**edtime wear, **O**ne arch at a time.
