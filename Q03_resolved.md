## STUDY MODE

### S1. How to organise "advances"
Every advance answers one of GIC's weaknesses: slow set and early water sensitivity, low strength and wear, poor aesthetics, and a fluoride benefit that is debated. A good answer groups developments by the problem they solve, and says which have clinical evidence and which are laboratory-only [basic science].

### S2. Advances that fix handling and strength
- **Resin-modified GIC** (first product, Vitrebond, 1989 in Sikri's chronology; "late 1980s" in Sakaguchi et al, 2019; "early 1990s" in Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011) has a liquid with a 15-25% resin component, mostly HEMA plus other methacrylates, and a residual HEMA of 4.5-15% in the set cement; the resin polymerises on light activation and protects the cement from water while the acid-base reaction, begun at mixing, continues at a slower rate (Mount et al, 2016); **tri-cure** adds a chemical dark cure for bulk placement (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **High-viscosity (condensable) GIC**: polyacrylic acid added to the powder and a smaller grain size (about 3 micrometres) give amalgam-like handling and better abrasion resistance, though strength is little changed; examples Fuji IX, Ketac Molar, Chemflex (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); used with minimal preparation in ART (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Fast-setting materials** reach a carvable stage in about 90 seconds; developments aim at ceramic nano-fillers or N-vinyl pyrrolidone polyacids for load-bearing use (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Heat-assisted auto-cure**: a dye absorbs heat from the curing light to speed the acid-base reaction without adding resin (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Use as a core (books differ)**: Bhat lists Fuji VII for core build-up (Bhat et al, 2019); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed (adapted from the 7th ed) says reinforced glass ionomers (Equia, Fuji IX) should not be used for core build-ups, only as bases and block-out (Ritter et al, 2019); Goldstein lists conventional glass ionomer only for blocking out undercuts and resin-modified glass ionomer for a partial core where adequate tooth structure remains (Goldstein et al, 2018); Badr et al quote a fatigue test in which all glass ionomer core crowns failed at 20,000 cycles and call cermet unsuitable for posterior cores (Badr et al, 2016).
- **Reinforcement**: Sikri reports that hollow glass fibres (5-10% by weight) showed improved mechanical properties, whereas Mount says glass or resin fibres have been tried with collateral problems such as an inability to keep a smooth surface, and that efforts to raise fracture resistance have so far been without success (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016); hydroxyapatite or fluorapatite nano-fillers raised compressive, tensile and flexural strength in one commercial GIC (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### S3. Advances in aesthetics and surface
- **Nano-ionomer** (RMGIC with nanomers and nanoclusters, from 2007): better polish and optics, fluoride release not compromised (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); van Noort says the data are sparse and wear and fluoride claims come from the laboratory only (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Resin coat on high-viscosity GIC** (glass hybrid): one 5-year trial found survival of 94.5% for moderate-to-large two-surface Class II glass hybrid restorations against 94.4% for nano-hybrid composite, and success scores of 81.9% against 90.7% (Miletic et al, 2024); in 62 first permanent molars with molar-incisor hypomineralisation, 60-month survival was 59.2% for glass hybrid against 75.5% for fibre-reinforced composite (Dokumacigil et al, 2026). Abstract only.
- **Zirconomer**: zirconium oxide nanoparticles for strength and colour stability, with rough texture and cracks at the zirconium-glass interface (Sikri, 2020).
- **Metal-modified**: Sikri says silver in silver cermet acts as a stress absorber and improves toughness and abrasion resistance, while compressive and flexural strength and solubility stayed without improvement, with a grey colour and interface failure (Sikri, 2020); van Noort and Barbour say the clinical performance of silver cermets was such that continued use is contraindicated (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Glass cermet, sintered glass and metal, is listed separately (Sikri, 2020).

### S4. Advances in biological action [treatment of caries]
- **Antibacterial additives**: chlorhexidine (CHX hexametaphosphate gives long release without harm to strength), triclosan, quaternary ammonium salts, nanoparticles of silver, zinc and titanium dioxide, bioactive glass, plant extracts (Sikri, 2020); Mount calls the results equivocal (Mount et al, 2016); Danelon et al (2025) report reduced S. mutans viability across clinical, in situ and in vitro studies and call for further in vivo research, and Guo et al (2024) found enhanced antimicrobial effect with inconclusive mechanical data for nanosilver GIC. Abstract only. Braga adds that glass ionomers show no evidence of intrinsic antimicrobial activity, that the antibacterial effect is seen in freshly mixed cement and is lost with time, and that the clinical evidence for remineralising and antibacterial effects is scant (Braga, 2021).
- **Remineralising and bioactive additives**: hydroxyapatite nanoparticles (8% by weight) (Sikri, 2020); hydroxyapatite-modified GIC is promising in the laboratory (Zhang et al, 2025); CPP-ACP is equivocal (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Bioactive glass in GIC**: an in vitro study quoted in Hill and Gillam found more bioactive glass gave more apatite formation but lower strength (De Caluwe et al, 2017, quoted in Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **Fluoride recharge and low-pH "smart" materials**: a two-part modified GIC has a restorative part and a gel "charge part" that replenishes fluoride by ion exchange without replacing the restoration; low-pH smart materials release fluoride at low pH (Sikri, 2020).
- **Calcium aluminate GIC hybrid** (luting): powder of calcium aluminate, polyacrylic acid, tartaric acid and strontium fluoro-alumino glass in a liquid that is 99.6% water; calcium aluminate gives basic pH during curing, less microleakage and biocompatibility (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Glass carbomer**: acid-washed glass, silicone oil coating and hydroxyapatite; long-term studies did not confirm superior efficacy (Sikri, 2020).
- **Therapeutic ion release**: Pickard expects more use of ionic exchange to release remineralising and antibacterial ions (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S5. What is new but classed outside the glass ionomers by some authors
Mount classes compomers and giomers as resin-based materials outside the glass-ionomer category (Mount et al, 2016); Sikri lists alkasite (Cention N, 2016) as a direct composite with an alkaline filler (Sikri, 2020). Alkasite materials release hydroxide, calcium and fluoride ions and are suited to Class I, II and V cavities (Bonchev et al, 2025).

### S6. Where the evidence stands
A meta-analysis of alkasite studies found performance comparable to composite and "superior" to GIC (Laporte et al, 2026); one small 2-year RCT found no failures in either group for a bioactive resin and a bulk-fill composite, with significant differences in surface luster, wear, form and fracture favouring the bioactive resin (Raghip et al, 2025). An in-vitro study found a bioactive resin had lower bond strength to dentine than a resin-modified GIC (Al-Hasan et al, 2022). For ART with high-viscosity GIC, a systematic review found no significant survival difference in single-surface cavities (Frencken et al, 2021).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

### Claim. Do the newer ion-releasing and bioactive materials outperform GIC?
- **Textbook position.** Mount's Atlas says the essential elements of true GIC are the ionic bond and fluoride release, and that formulas which reduce them are not true GICs in his view (Mount, 1994); Pickard expects more ion release in future (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Retrieved evidence.** An alkasite meta-analysis found performance comparable to composite and superior to GIC, but moderate risk of bias in several studies and no pooled numbers in the abstract (Laporte et al, 2026); a 2-year RCT in 22 patients reported no failures with either a bioactive resin or a bulk-fill composite, with differences in luster, wear, form and fracture favouring the bioactive resin (Raghip et al, 2025).
- **Interpretation and limit.** The sources are short-term, small and abstract-level; the claimed remineralisation is mostly in vitro. The textbooks give different classifications: compomers and giomers resin-based (Mount et al, 2016), alkasite a direct composite (Sikri, 2020). [Likely]
- **Conclusion.** State each source with its author: the meta-analysis reports alkasite as comparable to composite and superior to GIC (Laporte et al, 2026), while Mount's Atlas names adhesion and fluoride release as the essential elements of true GIC (Mount, 1994).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 43 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement, first reported by Wilson and Kent in 1972 (Mount et al, 2016; 1971 in Sikri's Ch.1, Sikri, 2020), first product ASPA 1976 (van Noort and Barbour, 2013), has been modified to overcome brittleness, slow setting, water sensitivity, poor aesthetics and a debated anticaries effect (Sikri, 2020; van Noort and Barbour, 2013). The research aim is to increase fracture resistance without losing the ion-exchange adhesion and fluoride release (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Advances in handling and setting
- **Resin-modified GIC:** liquid has 15-25% resin component (mostly HEMA), residual HEMA 4.5-15% in the set cement; light cure sets the resin while the acid-base reaction begun at mixing continues (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Tri-cure:** adds a chemical (redox) dark cure for bulk placement where light is poor (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Fast-setting:** carvable in about 90 seconds (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Heat-assisted auto-cure:** a dye absorbs heat from the curing light and hastens the reaction, with no added resin (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Capsules and syringes:** standardised dispensing and machine mixing (Mount et al, 2016); fewer air bubbles (Sikri, 2020).

### 3. Advances in strength and wear
- **High-viscosity GIC:** polyacrylic acid in the powder, grain size about 3 micrometres; amalgam-like handling; better abrasion resistance; examples Fuji IX, Ketac Molar (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); used in ART (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Metal-modified:** silver cermet, abrasion resistance up but strength not (Sikri, 2020), use contraindicated (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); glass cermet (Sikri, 2020).
- **Fibre reinforcement:** hollow glass fibres 5-10% by weight improved properties (Sikri, 2020); fibres gave surface problems and no success (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Nano-fillers:** hydroxyapatite or fluorapatite nano-fillers raised strength; titania improved toughness at 3% but higher amounts weakened the cement (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Zirconomer:** zirconia nanoparticles; colour-stable, but rough and prone to interface cracks (Sikri, 2020).

### 3A. High-viscosity GIC (Fuji VIII and IX) in more detail
- **Advantages:** easily packable and condensable, less early moisture sensitivity, non-sticky, immediate finishing, better wear resistance, low solubility (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Indications by product (Bhat calls only Fuji VIII and IX high-viscosity):** Fuji VII for deciduous molars and core build-up; Fuji VIII for anterior aesthetic restorations; Fuji IX mostly posterior restorations (Bhat et al, 2019); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says reinforced glass ionomers should not be used for core build-ups (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Limit:** strength is little changed from conventional GIC, and GIC is still not recommended for Class II in adults (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Fluoride-rich variant:** Fuji VII is a high-fluoride non-resin auto-cure GIC marketed for caries prevention (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Faster hardening** comes from glass particle size and distribution, and one maker coats the powder with polymer for easier mixing (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 4. Advances in aesthetics
- **Nano-ionomer** (since 2007): nanomers and nanoclusters in RMGIC; better polish and optics (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); evidence mostly laboratory (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Resin coat** (glass hybrid): seals and improves wear; moderate Class II 5-year survival 94.5% against 94.4% for composite, success 81.9% against 90.7% (Miletic et al, 2024).

### 5. Advances in biological action
| Approach | Example | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Antibacterial | chlorhexidine, triclosan, quaternary ammonium, silver and zinc nanoparticles | laboratory, equivocal (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016); effect lost with ageing (Braga, 2021) |
| Remineralising | hydroxyapatite nanoparticles, CPP-ACP | promising in vitro (Sikri, 2020; Zhang et al, 2025) |
| Fluoride recharge | two-part recharge material; low-pH "smart" release | concept (Sikri, 2020) |
| Glass carbomer | acid-washed glass, silicone oil, hydroxyapatite | not confirmed long term (Sikri, 2020) |

### 5A. Advances in biocompatibility of resin-modified materials
- HEMA released from RMGIC is linked to cytotoxicity in laboratory tests (Agha et al, 2021).
- Experimental RMGICs gave 89-98% cell viability from their extracts against 4% for a commercial RMGIC liner, with higher fluoride release for the 5 wt% HEMA experimental material (137 against 88 ppm), but they had lower monomer conversion; this is cell-culture evidence on experimental materials (Potiprapanpong et al, 2023).
- True GIC can be told from resin-based materials by setting in the dark, so the "dark-set" test is a way to classify a new product (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Newer materials outside the glass-ionomer category (Mount et al, 2016)
- **Compomer:** resin with glass and carboxylic groups; no ion-exchange bond; outside the GIC category in Mount (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- **Giomer:** pre-reacted glass-ionomer filler in resin (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- **Alkasite:** direct composite with alkaline filler (Sikri, 2020); hydroxide, calcium and fluoride release (Bonchev et al, 2025); comparable to composite, superior to GIC in a meta-analysis at moderate risk of bias (Laporte et al, 2026).
- **Bioactive resin (ACTIVA type):** no failures at 2 years, luster, wear and form favouring it over bulk-fill composite (Raghip et al, 2025); lower dentine bond than RMGIC in vitro (Al-Hasan et al, 2022).

### 6A. Quick comparison of the main modified forms
| Form | What changed | Main gain | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resin-modified (light, dual, tri-cure) | liquid has 15-25% resin component, mostly HEMA (Mount et al, 2016) | early water resistance, command set | HEMA, shrinkage, water uptake (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) |
| High-viscosity | finer glass, polyacid in powder (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | packable, ART | strength little changed |
| Cermet (silver, glass) | metal sintered into glass (Sikri, 2020) | abrasion resistance | silver: grey colour, interface failure (Sikri, 2020), use contraindicated (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Nano-ionomer | nanofillers in RMGIC (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) | polish, optics | sparse data (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Glass carbomer, zirconomer | glass or nanoparticle changes (Sikri, 2020) | strength claimed | not confirmed long term |

### 7. Clinical evidence for the advances
- ART with high-viscosity GIC: no significant difference from conventional in single-surface cavities up to 5 years (Frencken et al, 2021); better conventional survival (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018); evidence conflicting (Panetta et al, 2024).
- Glass hybrid, Class II: survival 94.5% against 94.4% for composite, success 81.9% against 90.7% (Miletic et al, 2024); MIH molars 59.2% against 75.5% (Dokumacigil et al, 2026).
- Antibacterial additives: lower S. mutans viability, in vivo work still needed (Danelon et al, 2025); nanosilver data inconclusive (Guo et al, 2024). Abstract only.

### 8. Future directions
- Fracture resistance without loss of ion exchange (Mount et al, 2016); higher molecular weight polyacids and dispersed-phase reinforcement (Mount, 1994).
- Amino-acid-modified, non-HEMA GICs and ultrasound-assisted hardening (Vaderhobli, 2011).
- Bioactive orthodontic RMGIC needing more in vitro and in vivo work (Zhang et al, 2017).
- Greater use of ion exchange to release remineralising and antibacterial ions (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 9. Conclusion
The main advances are resin modification, high-viscosity and fast-setting materials, nano-fillers, and bioactive or antibacterial additives. Trials exist for high-viscosity GIC in ART and glass hybrid, with results differing between authors; most additive claims are laboratory findings. State each advance with its problem, one example and its level of evidence.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Resin-modified (late 1980s, Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Vitrebond 1989, Sikri, 2020; early 1990s, Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011): liquid 15-25% resin, mostly HEMA; tri-cure adds redox dark cure (Mount et al, 2016; Bhat et al, 2019).
- High-viscosity: grain size about 3 micrometres; Fuji IX, Ketac Molar; ART (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- Nano-ionomer since 2007 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Glass carbomer, zirconomer: long-term studies did not confirm superior efficacy (Sikri, 2020); fibre reinforcement: improved properties (Sikri, 2020) against no success (Mount et al, 2016).
- Compomer, giomer: outside the glass-ionomer category (Mount et al, 2016); alkasite: direct composite with alkaline filler (Sikri, 2020).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing brand names without the problem each solves.
2. Calling compomer or giomer a glass ionomer without saying that Mount places them outside the category (Mount et al, 2016).
3. Presenting laboratory antibacterial results as clinical proof.
4. Forgetting evidence level (ART and glass hybrid have trials; additives mostly do not).
