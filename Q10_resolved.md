## STUDY MODE

### S1. Setting reaction of conventional GIC [basic science]
Mixing starts an acid-base reaction between the polyacid and the glass. (1) **Dissolution:** the acid attacks the glass surface, freeing calcium or strontium, aluminium and fluoride ions; about 20-30% of the glass decomposes, forming a sol (Mount et al, 2016). (2) **Gelation or hardening:** calcium (strontium) ions bind the carboxylate groups first, giving a clinically hard surface in about 4-10 minutes; aluminium ions then cross-link the chains over the next 24 hours (Mount et al, 2016). (3) **Maturation:** progressive hydration of the salts improves properties sharply (Mount et al, 2016). The result is unreacted glass cores in a siliceous gel inside a cross-linked polysalt matrix (Mount et al, 2016). Sikri describes the same two chemical stages, with a secondary aluminium stage needing at least 24 hours and a full reaction up to 7 days (Sikri, 2020). Mount's summary box puts full set at about one month (Mount et al, 2016), and Sturdevant 5th ed has aluminium replacing calcium over 24 to 72 hours with a silicate co-matrix forming slowly over about 30 days (Sturdevant 5th ed, Roberson et al, 2006); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed also has calcium replaced by more slowly reacting aluminium during the next 24-72 h, with a hydrated silicate co-matrix, but gives no 30-day figure (not covered in the 2nd South Asia edition, Ritter et al, 2019). Garg and Garg (2011) give setting within 6-8 minutes of mixing (Type I 5-7, Type II 10 minutes) (Garg and Garg, 2011). Nakabayashi and Pashley (1998) note that polyacrylic acid (pKa 4.25) is a much weaker acid than phosphoric acid (pKa 2.12), so its demineralising ability is potentially lower, though etching also depends on concentration, contact time and buffering (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).

### S2. Water balance [clinical relevance]
About 24% of the set cement is water, either loosely bound (easily lost early) or tightly bound in the hydration shell of the salts (Mount et al, 2016). The cement is most vulnerable to water in stage 2, and remains vulnerable to water loss for some time (Mount et al, 2016). Early calcium polyacrylate chains can wash out in water, and dehydration cracks the surface (Mount et al, 2016). This is why the cement is sealed at once. The protection window differs by book: fast-set types resist water uptake within about 5 minutes of the start of mixing, yet a surface left exposed for 10 minutes visibly cracks (Mount, 1994); Sikri's microleakage chapter asks for isolation from moisture for at least 30 minutes (Sikri, 2020); and Craig advises protecting luting GIC against gain and loss of water for the first 24 hours (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### S3. Resin-modified and tri-cure GIC
Resin-modified GIC sets by acid-base neutralisation plus free-radical polymerisation of methacrylate groups, so it snap-sets with light and then continues to mature (Mount et al, 2016). Cross-linking can be acid-base, light (camphorquinone) or redox (Mount et al, 2016). The set resin acts as a protective "umbrella" while the acid-base reaction continues more slowly (Mount et al, 2016). Sikri adds that tri-cure materials use a chemical dark cure as well, which he calls perhaps important in deeper restorations where light may not penetrate enough (Sikri, 2020). Light only hardens to the depth of penetration, so build up in increments (Mount et al, 2016).

### S4. Properties [treatment relevance]
Physical: working time about 1.5 minutes after a 30 s mix (Bhat et al, 2019) to 2-3 minutes (Sikri, 2020), depending on product and book; opaque; high early solubility (Sikri, 2020). Thermal expansion is described as close to that of dentin in Sikri's GIC chapter, and his microleakage chapter lists Type II GIC at 11.0 against enamel 11.4 and dentine 8.3 x 10-6/C (Sikri, 2020). In distilled water GIC is the most soluble water-based cement, yet in a clinical study of cements in the mouth it ranked least soluble, so laboratory and clinical solubility disagree (Craig, 2002). Mechanical (values depend on product and test): Sikri gives compressive strength 16,000-22,000 psi (about 110-152 MPa), tensile strength 900-960 psi (about 6-7 MPa) and hardness 48 KHN (Sikri, 2020); Mount gives 70-220 MPa compressive and 12-20 MPa diametral tensile for Type II.1 at 7 days (Mount et al, 2016); Phillips gives Type II 196-251 MPa compressive, 18-26 MPa diametral tensile and 87-177 KHN (Anusavice et al, 2013). The material is susceptible to brittle fracture and is weak and lacking in rigidity compared with composite and amalgam (Mount et al, 2016); restorative GIC is more vulnerable to wear than composite in vitro and less tough (fracture toughness about 0.72 MPa.m1/2 for conventional restorative GIC, 1.37 for hybrid GIC, 1.75-1.92 for hybrid composite) (Anusavice et al, 2013). Biological: fresh pH very low but dentine buffers it; mild pulp response that resolves in 10-20 days (Mount et al, 2016) to within about 30 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008); ion exchange adhesion; fluoride release (Sikri, 2020). Fluoride time-course differs: the burst is complete in 24 h with a low-level release afterwards (Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed, Ritter et al, 2019), mostly in the first 2 days with slow release for up to 3 years (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021); Braga (2021) states that clinical evidence for antibacterial and remineralising effects of GIC is scant.

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement hardens by an acid-base reaction between ion-leachable fluoroaluminosilicate glass and aqueous polyalkenoic acid, in which water is the transfer medium (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Setting reaction of conventional GIC
| Stage | What happens | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Dissolution | acid attacks glass, releasing Ca/Sr, Al and F ions; sol forms (Mount et al, 2016) | not timed in sources |
| 2 Gelation | Ca (Sr) then Al ions cross-link polyacid chains; initial hard set (Mount et al, 2016) | about 4-10 minutes |
| 3 Maturation | Al cross-links, hydration, silica gel formation with Na (Mount et al, 2016) | 24 hours (Mount et al, 2016); 24-72 h for Al (Ritter et al, 2019); 7 days (Sikri, 2020); full set about 1 month (Mount et al, 2016) |
- **Set cement:** unreacted glass in siliceous gel inside a polysalt matrix (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Calcium cross-links** form first, are fragile and vulnerable to early water; **aluminium** later gives insoluble tight bonds (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Water:** about 24% of the set cement; early water uptake or loss damages it (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2A. Factors affecting the rate of set
Glass composition, fusion and annealing temperature, particle size, tartaric acid content and powder:liquid ratio; high ambient temperature speeds the set and low temperature slows it; the liquid must never be refrigerated (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3. Resin-modified GIC
Acid-base reaction plus light-cured polymerisation of methacrylate (HEMA); a snap set with light, then maturation; redox cure is in some (Sikri, 2020) or in RMGIC generally (Mount et al, 2016).

### 4. Tri-cure GIC
Acid-base, light cure and chemical redox dark cure; intended to cure where light does not reach (Bhat et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).

### 5. Physical properties
- Working time about 1.5 minutes after a 30 s mix (Bhat et al, 2019) to 2-3 minutes (Sikri, 2020); thermal expansion close to tooth structure (Sikri, 2020).
- Opaque; solubility 0.3-0.5% (traditional); film thickness 22-25 micrometres (Sikri, 2020).
- Setting shrinkage about 3%, developing slowly (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Mechanical properties
- Compressive strength: Sikri 16,000-22,000 psi (about 110-152 MPa); Mount 70-220 MPa; Phillips Type II 196-251 MPa; depends on product and test (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016; Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Tensile 900-960 psi (about 6-7 MPa); hardness 48 KHN (Sikri, 2020); 87-177 KHN in Phillips (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Brittle (Mount et al, 2016); less wear resistant and less tough than composite (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Only resin-modified GIC is clearly stronger in tension; cermet is about equal (Sikri, 2020).

### 7. Biological properties
- Adhesion to enamel and dentine; fluoride release and recharge (Sikri, 2020); burst complete in 24 h (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Pulp: fresh pH about 1-2 but dentine buffers; mild inflammation resolving in 10-20 days (Mount et al, 2016) to about 30 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008); Sikri advises against direct pulp exposure, Mount allows a clean mechanical exposure of healthy pulp (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).
- Plaque does not thrive on it; tissue tolerance is high (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).

### 8. Conclusion
Setting is a three-stage acid-base process whose product depends on water balance; the properties follow from it and decide technique.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Stages: dissolution, gelation (Ca/Sr then Al), maturation (Mount et al, 2016).
- Hard set about 4-10 minutes; maturation 24 hours (Mount et al, 2016) to 7 days (Sikri, 2020), full set about 1 month (Mount et al, 2016).
- About 24% water; protect early (Mount et al, 2016).
- RMGIC: acid-base plus light; tri-cure adds redox (Sikri, 2020; Bhat et al, 2019).
