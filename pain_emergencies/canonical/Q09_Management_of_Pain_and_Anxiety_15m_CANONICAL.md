# Describe management of Pain and anxiety during Endodontic treatment.

**Marks:** 15 · Essay

**Answer blueprint:** Introduction → Recognising and assessing anxiety → Non-drug management → Drug management of anxiety → Control of pain → Conclusion

## Introduction

Fear and pain reinforce each other, hence the dictum “Deal with the fear first, then pain will be a minor problem” (Gopikrishna, 2021). Between 10% and 30% of adults have moderately severe to extreme dental fear (Malamed, 2019). Anxious, tired patients with inflamed tissue have a lower pain threshold (Reader et al, 2021).

## Recognising and assessing anxiety

- **Causes:** fear of the unknown, of pain and, commonly, of the injection (Malamed, 2019).
- **Signs:** the "white-knuckle syndrome", perspiration, over-prompt answers; ask directly what bothers the patient (Malamed, 2019). Sikri (2019) adds dilated pupils, pallor and raised blood pressure.
- **Assessment:** a questionnaire such as the Modified Dental Anxiety Scale plus a careful dental history (Reiser, 2017).
- **Risk:** 54.9% of dental-office medical emergencies occurred during local anaesthetic administration (Malamed, 2019).

## Non-drug management

1. **Iatrosedation:** the behaviour of doctor and staff relaxes the patient; hypnosis, acupuncture, audioanalgesia and biofeedback are examples (Malamed, 2019). Sikri (2019) terms this psychosedation.
2. **Distraction:** music, dark glasses, movies (Malamed, 2019).
3. **Information:** a running commentary lowered anxiety and pain (Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019).
4. **Confidence:** the frightened patient must trust the management (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
5. **Atraumatic injection:** supine position and slow injection (Malamed, 2019); a two-stage injection for apprehensive patients (Reader et al, 2021).

## Drug management of anxiety

Success rates by route (Malamed, 2019):

| Route | Titration | Success |
|---|---|---|
| Inhalation (N2O–O2) | Yes | 80% |
| Oral (adult) | No | 50–60% |
| Intramuscular, intranasal | No | 67% |
| Intravenous | Yes | 90% |

- **Nitrous oxide–oxygen:** onset about 20–30 s, rapid recovery on 100% oxygen (Malamed, 2019). Oxygen 5–6 litres per minute, nitrous oxide begun at one litre (Sikri, 2019). Contraindicated in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and first-trimester pregnancy (Sikri, 2019), or if the patient cannot breathe through the nose (Malamed, 2019).
- **Oral sedation:** the night before and 1 hour before the visit; an escort is needed (Malamed, 2019). Triazolam 0.25–0.5 mg one hour before (Sikri, 2019); sublingual 0.25 mg an hour before, with monitoring (Reiser, 2017). Others as printed: hydroxyzine 50–100 mg (Malamed, 2019); diphenhydramine 50 mg, promethazine 25–50 mg (Sikri, 2019). Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepines, starting at 0.2 mg intravenously (Reiser, 2017). Hargreaves and Baumgartner (2002) give oral diazepam 5 to 15 mg or triazolam 0.25 mg, and find 50% nitrous oxide effective, 40% marginal, which adds nothing when combined with either drug.
- **Intravenous or intramuscular midazolam or diazepam:** need an operator trained in parenteral moderate sedation (Malamed, 2019).
- **General anaesthesia:** for the few not managed by sedation (Malamed, 2019).

Sedation never replaces local anaesthesia (Reader et al, 2021).

## Control of pain

- **Profound local anaesthesia:** the fastest, most effective anxiolytic and analgesic (Sikri, 2019); anaesthetise at each appointment (Reader et al, 2021). Periodontal, intrapulpal or intraosseous supplements are used when pain persists despite apparent anaesthesia (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
- **Nitrous oxide:** 30% is equivalent to 10 to 15 mg of morphine, and 30% to 50% raised inferior alveolar block success in irreversible pulpitis (Reader et al, 2021).
- **Definitive treatment:** pulpotomy and pulpectomy cut pain (Nusstein et al, 2021).
- **Pretreatment analgesic and long-acting anaesthetic:** an NSAID or acetaminophen 30 minutes before treatment (Nusstein et al, 2021); long-acting anaesthetics delay post-treatment pain (Nusstein et al, 2021).

## Conclusion

Identify the fearful patient early, manage behaviour first, add sedation by titratable routes where needed, and secure profound anaesthesia.
