# Endodontic emergencies.

**Marks:** 100 · Single essay

## 1. Definition and scope

An **endodontic emergency** is pain and/or swelling caused by inflammation or infection of the pulp and/or periradicular tissues that needs immediate diagnosis and treatment (Gopikrishna, 2021; Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Sikri (2019) also counts fracture and avulsion; Walton and Ruparel (2021) include severe trauma but manage it elsewhere. The denominators differ between books: endodontic problems are about two-thirds of dental emergencies (Walton and Ruparel, 2021), about 85% (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021) or 30 to 90% (Sikri, 2019), and about 90% of patients seeking relief of toothache have pulpal or periapical disease (Weine, 2004). Pain arises from chemical mediators and from fluid pressure (Gopikrishna, 2021).

A **true emergency** needs an unscheduled visit; an **urgency** can be booked. Three telephone questions separate them (Walton and Ruparel, 2021):
1. Does the problem disturb sleeping, eating or work?
2. How long has it been present (a true emergency has rarely been severe for more than a few hours to 2 days)?
3. Did analgesics fail?

## 2. Classification

| Author | Scheme |
|---|---|
| Gopikrishna (2021) | **Before treatment:** crown-originating fracture, symptomatic reversible pulpitis, symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, primary and secondary symptomatic apical periodontitis (acute exacerbation or Phoenix abscess), symptomatic (acute) alveolar abscess, cellulitis, traumatic injuries. **During:** hot tooth, flare-up. **After:** post-obturation pain, vertical root fracture |
| Teixeira and Cheung (2021) | Five accepted presentations: symptomatic irreversible pulpitis with normal apex; with symptomatic apical periodontitis; necrosis with symptomatic apical periodontitis; necrosis with fluctuant swelling; necrosis with diffuse facial swelling. Plus trauma, previously treated teeth, flare-ups |
| Sikri (2019) | Pretreatment (hyperreactive pulpalgia, acute irreversible pulpitis, acute apical periodontitis, acute periradicular abscess in four forms, cracked tooth, fractures, luxation, avulsion); mid-treatment (incomplete pulp removal, apical periodontitis, Phoenix abscess); post-treatment (over-instrumentation, over-obturation, high restoration, root fracture) |
| Walton and Ruparel (2021) | Pretreatment; interappointment and postobturation ("flare-up") |

Sikri's "hyperreactive pulpalgia" and "acute" labels are older terms; the pulpal and apical diagnoses of Gopikrishna and Teixeira and Cheung are the current ones.

**Non-odontogenic mimics.** Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck (2021) group them as musculoskeletal, neurovascular, neuropathic, pain from a pathologic process, and psychogenic. Pain that does not respond to local anaesthesia suggests a non-odontogenic source. A periodontal abscess can simulate an apical abscess, but its pulp is usually vital (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

## 3. Patient assessment

1. **History.** Review medical history first; the need for antibiotic prophylaxis must be settled before probing, which can cause bacteraemia (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). Sikri (2019) notes immunocompromised, hypertensive, allergic and diabetic patients may need consultation or modified treatment.
2. **Subjective examination** is the most important part. Record location, severity, duration, character and stimuli; spontaneity, intensity and duration indicate significant pathosis. Pain from thermal stimuli or referred pain is pulpal; well-localised pain on biting is apical (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). Pulpal pain is poorly localised because the pulp has little to no proprioception; periradicular pain is easier to localise (Law, Nixdorf and Mattscheck, 2021).
3. **Extraoral examination** looks for swelling, cellulitis, lymphadenopathy, trismus, eye closure and fever (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
4. **Intraoral examination:** swelling, sinus tracts, defective restorations, caries, discoloration, fractures.
5. **Tests.** Cold is the most accurate vitality test, and cold with EPT is better; repeat the stimulus the patient describes (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). For the apex use palpation, pressure, light percussion and selective biting (tooth slooth). Probe the periodontium; a narrow isolated defect suggests a fracture. A test cavity may settle doubtful vitality.
6. **Radiographs.** Periapical and bitewing views show caries, exposure, resorption and periapical change; about half of teeth with periapical pathosis are asymptomatic to periapical tests. CBCT is more accurate for suspected fractures (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). An acute abscess may show no radiolucency (Sikri, 2019).
7. **Diagnosis.** In a true emergency one tooth is the offender; record a pulpal and a periapical diagnosis (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). Weine (2004) reduces it to three determinations: vitality, percussion and radiograph. If no diagnosis emerges, refer.

## 4. Principles of management

- **Manage the patient first.** Past experience and present anxiety alter the pain reaction threshold; control the situation, gain confidence, give attention and sympathy (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). The same chapter says anxiety "decreases" pain perception, which contradicts its own point and is a plain slip. Mild anxiety may need nitrous oxide; oral triazolam is fast-acting, and a sedated patient needs an escort (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
- **Remove the irritant and relieve pressure.** Pressure release is the more effective (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). The patient must be told the alternatives, risks and prognosis; the plan is never forced (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021).
- **Diagnose before treating**; a wrong diagnosis worsens the pain (Weine, 2004).
- **Do not push debris beyond the apex.** Use crown-down shaping; positive-pressure irrigation can extrude debris (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021).
- **Seal the access**, usually with calcium hydroxide in the canal if the case is left for a later visit (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021).
- **Prescribe by diagnosis**, and arrange follow-up.

## 5. Anaesthesia and the "hot tooth"

For maxillary teeth use infiltration or block; for mandibular teeth add a long buccal injection and articaine buccal infiltration to the inferior alveolar block. Retest with cold before entering the pulp; if the patient still reacts, give periodontal, intrapulpal or intraosseous injections (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). Necrotic pulps usually need none, but local infiltration into the swollen, acidic, inflamed area is ineffective and may spread infection; use a block instead (Gopikrishna, 2021; Weine, 2004).

**Hot tooth** has two meanings in the books.

| Author | Meaning and management |
|---|---|
| Gopikrishna (2021) | A tooth that is difficult to anaesthetise (Sikri, 2019, also links the term to anaesthesia), typically the mandibular first molar, because tetrodotoxin-resistant sodium channels resist lidocaine. Supplement with 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (0.9 to 1.2 mL) buccal infiltration, buffered solution, 0.2 mL intraligamentary injection, or intrapulpal injection |
| Weine (2004) | Any tooth with pulpal or periapical disease needing relief at the first visit |

A preoperative NSAID given 1 hour before the local anaesthetic enhances its depth (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 6. Management of each emergency

**Diagnostic features.**

| Diagnosis | Key findings |
|---|---|
| Reversible pulpitis | Reversible sensitivity to cold or osmotic change (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021); a shock sensation that stops when the stimulus is removed, with normal percussion (Sikri, 2019) |
| SIP, normal apex | Intermittent or spontaneous pain; intense, prolonged response to cold that lingers (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021); worse at night or on bending, and may be referred (Sikri, 2019) |
| SIP with symptomatic apical periodontitis | Vital pulp and tender to percussion; heat causes intense pain and cold relieves; radiograph normal, widened ligament or small radiolucency (Weine, 2004; Sikri, 2019) |
| Necrosis with symptomatic apical periodontitis | No response to vitality tests; periapical change from ligament thickening to a large lesion (Weine, 2004) |
| Acute apical abscess | Tender, mobile, non-vital tooth with swelling (Weine, 2004) |

**Reversible pulpitis.** Remove caries or the irritant, protect the dentine and restore; review vitality periodically (Gopikrishna, 2021). Sikri (2019) adds occlusal checking and a sedative dressing.

**Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP), with or without symptomatic apical periodontitis.** Removing the inflamed tissue relieves pain. Complete cleaning and shaping is preferred if time permits; if not, pulpotomy or partial pulpectomy of the largest canal is done (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). The books differ:

| Question | Views |
|---|---|
| Pulpotomy | Effective emergency alternative; more pain relief than root canal treatment in one study (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Greater than 90% success reducing pain to mild or none (Walton and Ruparel, 2021) |
| Partial pulpectomy | Walton and Ruparel (2021) strongly discourage it in severely inflamed teeth; Weine (2004) permits it in the largest canal of a molar and says pulpotomy alone will not relieve SIP with apical periodontitis |
| Chamber medicament | A dry cotton pellet is as effective as a moistened one (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021; Walton and Ruparel, 2021); Weine (2004) places a formocresol-dampened pellet after pulpotomy |

Sikri (2019) adds that with an open apex a pulpotomy with MTA or calcium hydroxide is preferred, and that emergency root canal opening (ERCO) is used when time is short. In the most recent survey cited, most endodontists cleaned to the electronic apex locator length (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Single-visit treatment is not contraindicated in irreversible pulpitis, but time at an emergency visit often prevents it; in necrotic and previously treated teeth it is controversial (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Occlusal reduction helps if the tooth is tender to percussion (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Antibiotics have no effect on pain in irreversible pulpitis (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Trephination is contraindicated in a vital inflamed pulp (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

**Necrosis with symptomatic apical periodontitis, no swelling.** The aim is to reduce canal irritants and encourage drainage. Do complete debridement after determining working length; if time is short, light debridement at the estimated length with passive crown-down. Irrigate with NaOCl (Walton and Ruparel suggest 6 or 8%), finish with 17% EDTA, dry, place calcium hydroxide and seal (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). **Trephination** is perforating the cortical plate to release exudate. Teixeira and Cheung (2021) say recent studies show no benefit, though some still advocate it. Gopikrishna (2021) reserves it for severe pain without swelling when nothing else works, and Sikri (2019) for failure of canal drainage and incision.

**Acute apical (periradicular) abscess.** The tooth is tender, mobile and non-vital, with pain relieved when exudate crosses the cortical plate (Weine, 2004; Sikri, 2019). Sikri (2019) notes it may show only a widened ligament space. Treatment is biphasic: **debridement first, drainage second** (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

Weine (2004) describes the drainage pattern: pus mixed with blood, then mainly blood, then clear serum, taking 15 minutes to over an hour; at that point take a working-length film, enlarge the canal and close with a sterile pellet and zinc oxide-eugenol. In the same chapter he notes that a necrotic pulp without swelling is rarely a true emergency, but the patient should not wait more than a day or two.

*Drainage through the canal* (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Ruparel, 2021):
1. Rubber dam; brace the tooth with finger pressure to reduce pain from bur vibration.
2. Open the chamber, irrigate, and instrument within the canals without forcing debris.
3. If there is no drainage, pass a small file (up to #25) or a patency file about 1 mm beyond the constriction. Weine (2004) enlarges to size 25 or 30 and warns against going to 70 or 80 if this fails.
4. If pus keeps flowing, let the patient wait with the tooth open under the dam, then irrigate (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
5. Dry, place calcium hydroxide, seal, relieve occlusion.

**Incision for drainage (I&D)** (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021; Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019):
1. Anaesthetise by block, or infiltrate around, not into, the swelling.
2. Make a stab incision through the periosteum at the **most dependent** point (Gopikrishna recommends a No. 11 blade); Teixeira and Cheung specify a vertical incision.
3. Dissect bluntly with a hemostat or periosteal elevator to break up pockets of exudate.
4. Compress to evacuate; irrigate with saline or 0.12% chlorhexidine (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
5. Place a drain (iodoform gauze, rubber dam or Penrose); suture it for larger swellings; remove after 1 to 2 days.
6. Prescribe warm saline rinses; slightly disocclude the tooth.

Drainage through the access alone may not significantly reduce pain or swelling (Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019), and drainage of a localised swelling may not relieve pain immediately; pain often falls as soft tissue swells because bone pressure is released (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Needle aspiration with an 18-gauge needle gives less scarring, a sample for culture and no drain removal (Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019).

| Swelling | Views |
|---|---|
| Fluctuant | Incise and drain (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Ruparel, 2021) |
| Indurated, localised | Walton and Ruparel (2021): incise whether fluctuant or not. Sikri (2019): do not incise; drain via the canal and use warm saline. Gopikrishna (2021): warm saline hourly until fluctuant, though some incise hard tissue |
| Cellulitis | Incise whether indurated or fluctuant (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021) |

**Leaving the tooth open** is a further disagreement. Teixeira and Cheung (2021), Gopikrishna (2021) and Walton and Ruparel (2021) advise against it: it admits oral organisms and a needle was reported broken in one open tooth. Weine (2004) found teeth left open needed 5.11 visits with 25 of 81 exacerbating, against 3.31 visits and 11 of 144 when kept closed, yet allows leaving an abscessed tooth open if there is no time to watch the drainage ("If you file, don't close.").

**Phoenix abscess** is an acute exacerbation of a chronic apical periodontitis (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021). Sikri (2019) offers two explanations: oxygen entry favouring facultative bacteria, or a virulent strain multiplying once competitors fall. Treat as for an acute abscess.

**Cellulitis and spreading infection.** Cellulitis is diffuse swelling through connective tissue and fascial planes, with fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy and trismus; the tooth is the focus (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021; Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019). Spread follows the apex's relation to cortex and muscle attachment: a mandibular molar apex above the mylohyoid goes sublingual, below it submandibular. Ludwig angina involves the submental, sublingual and submandibular spaces and can obstruct the airway; midface infection via the canine space can cause cavernous sinus thrombosis (Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019; Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Debride, incise, give antibiotics and review daily. Toxicity, high temperature, lethargy, CNS change, airway compromise, or any need for extraoral drainage mean **referral** to an oral surgeon or hospital for intravenous antibiotics (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021; Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

**Previously treated teeth.** The goal is the same: remove contaminants and establish drainage; posts and filling may need removal, and trephination or apicoectomy may follow (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021).

## 7. Interappointment emergencies: flare-ups

A **flare-up** is an acute exacerbation of periradicular pathosis after starting or continuing root canal treatment (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021); Gopikrishna (2021) and Baumgartner and Rosenberg (2019) say of an asymptomatic pulp or periapical pathosis.

| Item | Teixeira and Cheung | Others |
|---|---|---|
| Incidence | 2 to 20%; 8.4% in a strict meta-analysis (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021) | 1.4 to 19% (Gopikrishna, 2021); about 3% in controlled prospective studies (Walton and Ruparel, 2021); 1 to 25% (Sikri, 2019) |
| Treatment causes | Over-instrumentation, extruded debris, remnants, overfilling, irrigants, high occlusion, fracture | Walton and Ruparel (2021): no definitive treatment risk factor identified; Baumgartner and Rosenberg (2019): multifactorial |

Risk is highest with necrosis, symptomatic apical periodontitis or acute abscess, a radiolucency, preoperative pain and retreatment, and low in vital pulps (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

**Management.** Reassurance ("the Big R") comes first, then comfort and long-acting anaesthesia such as bupivacaine (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
1. *Vital pulp, complete debridement:* reassurance, mild analgesic, check the temporary restoration for occlusion; nothing is gained by opening.
2. *Vital pulp, incomplete debridement:* recheck length, reclean and irrigate, dry pellet.
3. *Necrotic, no swelling:* reopen, reclean, drain if possible, leave under dam until drainage stops, calcium hydroxide, seal.
4. *Necrotic with swelling:* I&D with drain after debridement (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

Antibiotics are not indicated in the necrotic case without swelling (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). Sikri (2019) adds a corticosteroid-antibiotic intracanal paste; Walton and Ruparel (2021) found steroids lower moderate pain but do not prevent flare-ups. Reduce high occlusion (Gopikrishna, 2021). Defer obturation if acute apical periodontitis is present (Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019).

**Sodium hypochlorite accident.** Signs are sudden severe pain, bleeding into the canal and tissue swelling. Allow bleeding, flush with saline, anaesthetise, and give analgesics and antibiotics with follow-up (Gopikrishna, 2021). Sikri (2019) adds aspiration, cold packs and hospitalisation if severe.

## 8. Cracked tooth and fracture emergencies

**Crown-originating fracture** (cracked tooth) typically causes pain on release of biting, and cold sensitivity (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019). Detect by transillumination, dye (methylene blue), tooth slooth bite test and magnification. If the pulp is reversible, stabilise with an orthodontic band for 2 weeks then give full coverage; if irreversible, root canal treatment then coverage (Gopikrishna, 2021). A crack with periodontal breakdown has a poor prognosis; extract (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). **Vertical root fracture** gives a deep narrow probing defect, a J-shaped or halo radiolucency and a sinus tract placed coronally; extraction is the choice (Gopikrishna, 2021), or hemisection for a multirooted tooth (Sikri, 2019).

## 9. Post-obturation pain and trauma

Some discomfort after obturation subsides in 2 to 5 days; persistence needs reassessment (Gopikrishna, 2021). Causes are overinstrumentation, overfilling, missed canal, high restoration, poor coronal seal and fracture. Pain is mostly in the first 24 hours and tracks preoperative pain (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). Reassure, give analgesics, adjust occlusion, retreat or do apical surgery if inadequate, and refer when unresponsive (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

**Trauma** (brief): crown and root fractures, luxations and avulsion need immediate attention; replantation is best within 15 to 20 minutes, with milk or HBSS if transported (Sikri, 2019).

## 10. Analgesics and antibiotics

**Analgesics.** Treating the source matters more than the drug. Ibuprofen is the first choice for acute dental pain, maximum 3.2 g daily; acetaminophen maximum 4 g (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Walton and Ruparel (2021) find 600 mg ibuprofen, with or without 1000 mg acetaminophen, most effective, taken by the clock with the first dose before anaesthesia ends; opioids are second line (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Antibiotics.** Indicated only for systemic involvement (fever above 100°F, malaise, lymphadenopathy, trismus), cellulitis, progressive or persistent swelling, and medically compromised patients (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021; Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019; Sikri, 2019). Not indicated for irreversible pulpitis, symptomatic apical periodontitis, sinus tract, localised swelling after drainage, or to prevent flare-ups (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).

| Drug | Gopikrishna (2021) | Baumgartner and Rosenberg (2019) | Walton and Ruparel (2021) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amoxicillin | 1000 mg then 500 mg every 8 h, 5 to 7 days | 1,000 mg then 500 mg every 8 h; 7-day course usual | 1 g then 500 mg every 6 h, 2 to 3 days |
| Clindamycin (penicillin allergy) | 600 mg then 300 mg every 6 h | Same | 300 to 600 mg then 150 to 300 mg every 6 h |
| Metronidazole | 1000 mg then 500 mg every 6 h with amoxicillin | Same | Not given |

Teixeira and Cheung prefer amoxicillin, add clavulanate or metronidazole in severe cases, and stop antibiotics 1 or 2 days after resolution; Baumgartner and Rosenberg say 2 to 3 days. Oral cephalosporins and macrolides have little value in abscesses with penicillin-resistant strains (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021).

## 11. Medical considerations, referral, prevention and follow-up

- **Medical:** patients on daily cardioprotective aspirin may take occasional ibuprofen but not regular doses (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Acetaminophen suits NSAID sensitivity, ulcers, asthma and hypertension (Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019). Clindamycin carries eight-fold greater risk of *C. difficile* infection, and oral contraceptives need alternative cover (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
- **Referral:** airway risk, systemic toxicity, extraoral drainage, no response to treatment, or complexity beyond the operator (AAE case difficulty form, Teixeira and Cheung, 2021).
- **Prevention:** prepare the patient for expected soreness; give ibuprofen 400 to 600 mg in the chair, then by the clock for 24 to 48 hours (Walton and Ruparel, 2021); use passive irrigation through a side-vented 30-gauge needle 2 to 3 mm short in posterior teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021); maintain length and coronal seal.
- **Follow-up:** telephone next day (Walton and Ruparel, 2021); review cellulitis daily (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021); return for definitive root canal treatment when symptoms subside.

## 12. Conclusion

Correct emergency care rests on one tooth, a pulpal and periapical diagnosis, removal of the irritant, drainage, sensible drugs and follow-up.
