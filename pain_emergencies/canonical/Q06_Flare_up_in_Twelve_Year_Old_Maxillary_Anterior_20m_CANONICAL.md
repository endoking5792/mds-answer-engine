# Discuss the management of an endodontic flare-up in a twelve year old patient pertaining to maxillary anterior tooth.

**Marks:** 20 · Case-based long essay

**Answer blueprint:** Introduction (why the immature incisor differs) → History and diagnosis (trauma, pulp status) → Emergency management (anaesthesia, access, irrigation, drainage, medicament) → Drugs → Definitive management (apexification or regeneration) → Follow-up

## Introduction
A flare-up is an acute exacerbation of a periradicular pathosis after the initiation or continuation of root canal treatment (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). At twelve years a maxillary incisor may still be immature, especially if injured early: root formation continues 2 to 3 years after eruption, and pulp necrosis or trauma arrests it, leaving an open apex and thin walls (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Flare-ups may occur more in females under 20 and in maxillary lateral incisors (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Reassurance, debridement and drainage apply, modified for the open apex and the need to preserve root development.

## History and diagnosis
- **Trauma history.** Take a history of any injury (Andreasen, Andreasen and Andersson, 2007); trauma can disrupt root development (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Test several adjacent and opposing teeth; an initial lack of response is not unusual, so repeat at 4 to 6 weeks (Paranjpe and Noblett, 2021).
- **Immature-tooth testing.** Teeth may not react before root formation is complete; carbon dioxide snow and aerosol cold sprays are reliable even in immature teeth (Andreasen, Andreasen and Andersson, 2007). Periodic films show whether root growth continues (Paranjpe and Noblett, 2021).
- **Diagnosis.** Flare-ups occur mostly in necrotic teeth with symptomatic apical periodontitis or acute apical abscess (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

## Emergency management
1. **Reassure** the child and parent first (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
2. **Anaesthesia.** Infiltrate away from the swollen tissue, since forcing solution into infected swelling may increase pain and spread infection (Gopikrishna, 2021). Intraligamentary injection is painful in maxillary anterior teeth; infiltration is preferred (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021).
3. **Access** under rubber dam, with gentle debridement. Clean wide canals by circumferential filing with larger K-files (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).
4. **Irrigation must be passive.** Extrusion of irrigant is a risk in open apices; use a side-vented, close-ended needle and treat sodium hypochlorite more as a working solution (Gopikrishna, 2021). Strength differs: 0.5% (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021) against 1.5% (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).
5. **Drainage.** For localized swelling, drainage through the canal is preferred (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021); incise a fluctuant swelling (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). Never leave the tooth open (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
6. **Medicament.** Calcium hydroxide where there is swelling or a sinus tract, then a coronal seal (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). In the esthetic zone use calcium hydroxide or omit minocycline from the antibiotic paste, because minocycline stains the crown (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021).
**Close** temporarily; relieve the occlusion (Sikri, 2019).

## Drugs
- **Analgesic:** NSAID (Sikri, 2019), or acetaminophen if NSAIDs are contraindicated (Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019). The antibiotic doses tabulated in Grossman are adult doses (Gopikrishna, 2021); no child dose is taken from these books, so it is worked out separately for the child.
- **Antibiotics:**

| Author | Position |
|---|---|
| (Walton and Ruparel, 2021) | not necessary for localized swelling |
| (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021) | progressive or persistent infection with fever, malaise, cellulitis, trismus |

Diffuse spreading swelling needs referral (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

## Definitive management after the flare-up settles
The emergency treatment of a necrotic traumatised tooth is the same whether or not the apex is closed, but the therapy that follows differs (Weine, 2004).

| | Apexification | Regenerative procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Calcium hydroxide: 3 to 9 months (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), up to 18 months (Paranjpe and Noblett, 2021), 3 to 18 months (Kahler, 2021). Or an MTA plug: at least 5 mm (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), 4 to 5 mm (Kahler, 2021) | Minimal instrumentation, calcium hydroxide or antibiotic paste, then induced bleeding at 2 to 4 weeks (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) or 3 to 4 weeks (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) |
| Gain | Over 90% resolution of apical periodontitis (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) | Thicker walls and longer roots (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) |
| Limit | Root development arrests, with cervical fracture risk (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021); long-term calcium hydroxide may lower fracture resistance (Paranjpe and Noblett, 2021), but Kahler (2021) cites a study linking fracture to root stage instead | Not true regeneration; canal calcification (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Local anaesthetic without vasoconstrictor at the bleeding visit; MTA discolours severely, so Biodentine or ES putty is preferred (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) |

## Follow-up
Contact the child daily until symptoms abate (Walton and Ruparel, 2021). Recall at 6 and 12 months after apexification (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021); after regeneration 6 months then yearly (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) or 12 to 18 months (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021).
