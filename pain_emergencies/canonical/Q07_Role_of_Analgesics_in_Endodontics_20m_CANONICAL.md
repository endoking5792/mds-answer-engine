# Describe the role of Analgesics in endodontics. Add a note on management of hot tooth.

**Marks:** 20 · Long essay

**Answer blueprint:** Role and principle → Non-opioid analgesics → Opioids → Combinations and flexible plan → Pre-emptive and scheduled dosing → Cautions → Note on hot tooth → Conclusion

## Role and principle

Endodontic pain is inflammatory, so NSAIDs are the first choice, but no pain medication replaces thorough cleaning of the canal system, which removes the source (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Pain control rests on three Ds: diagnosis, definitive dental treatment and drugs (Nusstein et al, 2021). Analgesics are adjuncts; antibiotics are not analgesics (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).

## Non-opioid analgesics

- **NSAIDs** block cyclooxygenase and so prostaglandin synthesis; plasma-protein binding delivers them to inflamed tissue (Nusstein et al, 2021). Ibuprofen is the drug of choice for acute dental pain unless contraindicated (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021).
- **Acetaminophen** is preferred when NSAIDs are contraindicated (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). It has little anti-inflammatory effect (Sikri, 2019).

| Drug (dose as printed) | Author |
|---|---|
| Ibuprofen 600 mg every 6 hours; ketorolac 10 mg every 6 hours; piroxicam 20 mg every 12 hours; diclofenac 50 mg every 8–12 hours; acetaminophen 1000 mg every 6–8 hours | (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Aspirin 300–600 mg, 6–8 hourly | (Sikri, 2019) |
| Ibuprofen ceiling 3.2 g in 24 hours | (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021) |

Sikri's table prints an ibuprofen maximum of 320 mg/day, a slip for 3.2 g (Sikri, 2019). Acetaminophen's daily ceiling differs: 3 g for healthy adults (Nusstein et al, 2021); 4 g currently in force (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021; Walton and Ruparel, 2021), though Walton and Ruparel (2021) find 325 to 500 mg with ibuprofen 600 mg safer than 1000 mg.

## Opioids

Opioids have no anti-inflammatory action and so no effect on inflamed periapical tissue; nausea, drowsiness, respiratory depression and constipation limit dosage, so they are almost always given in combination (Nusstein et al, 2021). Codeine 60 mg (two tablets of Tylenol with codeine no. 3) beats placebo, whereas 30 mg does not; tramadol 50 mg equals codeine 60 mg (Nusstein et al, 2021). Gopikrishna (2021) gives codeine 60 mg or tramadol 50 mg every 6 hours as second line; Sikri (2019) prints tramadol up to 400 mg/day. Tramadol is not devoid of addiction potential (Walton and Ruparel, 2021); Reiser (2017) advises avoiding opioids unless NSAIDs cannot be tolerated.

## Combinations and flexible plan

Ibuprofen 600 mg plus acetaminophen 1000 mg gave greater relief than ibuprofen alone for 8 hours after pulpectomy (Nusstein et al, 2021). Flexible plan (Nusstein et al, 2021):

| Pain | NSAID usable | NSAID contraindicated |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Ibuprofen 200–400 mg or aspirin 650 mg | Acetaminophen 650–1000 mg |
| Moderate | Ibuprofen 600 mg plus acetaminophen 1000 mg | Acetaminophen plus opiate equal to codeine 60 mg |
| Severe | Add acetaminophen/opiate combination equal to oxycodone 10 mg | Acetaminophen 1000 mg plus opiate equal to oxycodone 10 mg |

## Pre-emptive and scheduled dosing

Pretreatment blocks hyperalgesia by reducing peripheral nociceptor input (Nusstein et al, 2021). Timing differs:

| Author | Pre-treatment |
|---|---|
| (Nusstein et al, 2021) | 30 minutes before: ibuprofen 400 mg, flurbiprofen 100 mg or acetaminophen 1000 mg |
| (Gopikrishna, 2021) | NSAID 1 hour before the local anaesthetic |
| (Reiser, 2017) | Before endodontic surgery: evidence equivocal, but 1 hour before does no harm |

Afterwards the first dose is taken before the anaesthetic wears off (Walton and Ruparel, 2021) and doses are scheduled "by the clock", not on demand (Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019), for the first 2 to 3 days in patients at risk (Hargreaves and Baumgartner, 2002). Hargreaves and Baumgartner (2002) call ibuprofen 600 mg every 6 hours optimal for most patients, with acetaminophen 1000 mg if NSAIDs are not tolerated.

## Cautions

NSAIDs are contraindicated in ulcers and aspirin hypersensitivity; GI (3% to 11%) and CNS (1% to 9%) effects occur (Nusstein et al, 2021). Acetaminophen suits ulcers, ulcerative colitis, asthma or hypertension (Baumgartner and Rosenberg, 2019). COX-2 inhibitors are not recommended for routine endodontic pain (Nusstein et al, 2021). Patients on daily aspirin should take it 2 hours before ibuprofen (Reiser, 2017). Elderly doses are halved (Sikri, 2019).

## Note on hot tooth

A **hot tooth** is one difficult to anaesthetise, commonly a mandibular first molar: lip is numb, yet access is painful (Gopikrishna, 2021). Inflammation shifts C-fibres to tetrodotoxin-resistant sodium channels, five times more resistant to anaesthetic (Gopikrishna, 2021).

1. Retest with cold before access (Walton and Ruparel, 2021).
2. Supplement the block with 4% articaine, 1:100,000 epinephrine, buccal infiltration 0.9–1.2 mL (Gopikrishna, 2021).
3. Intraligamentary 0.2 mL through a 27-gauge needle; intrapulpal only if the exposure admits a needle, and painful (Gopikrishna, 2021).
4. Gow-Gates or Vazirani-Akinosi block (Malamed, 2019).
5. Buffering: quicker onset (Gopikrishna, 2021), but no improved block success in irreversible pulpitis (Reader et al, 2021).

## Conclusion

Analgesics complement, never replace, debridement: NSAID first, acetaminophen if contraindicated, opioid last.
