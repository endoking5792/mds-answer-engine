# Discuss in detail the methods used to secure analgesia in endodontics

**Marks:** 20 · Long essay

**Answer blueprint:** Introduction → Patient preparation → Solutions → Primary techniques → Supplemental techniques → Delivery systems and adjuncts → Cautions → Conclusion.

## Introduction

Local anaesthetics are the only drugs that prevent nociceptive impulses reaching the brain (Malamed, 2019). Analgesia combines psychological management, topical agent, primary injection, supplemental injection and adjuncts.

## Patient preparation

- Psychological approach of four Cs: control, communication, concern, confidence (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021).
- Topical anaesthetic: its main value is showing concern, not mucosal numbness (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021); EMLA cream is lignocaine with prilocaine (Sikri, 2019).
- Patients cannot tell 25-, 27- and 30-gauge needles apart (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021). A slow injection is more comfortable; computer-controlled delivery, such as the Wand, which controls flow and pressure (Sikri, 2019), reduces but does not abolish injection pain (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021).
- Anaesthetise at every appointment, because vital tissue may persist in the apical few millimetres of a necrotic canal (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021).

## Solutions

- Plain mepivacaine 3% or prilocaine 4% are unlikely to give adequate depth or duration for an infected tooth (Malamed, 2019), whereas 3% mepivacaine gave block pulpal rates similar to lidocaine with epinephrine, for about 50 minutes, and suits patients in whom epinephrine needs caution (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021).
- Articaine is not statistically superior to lidocaine for inferior alveolar blocks (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021). A second cartridge does not increase pulpal anaesthesia (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021). Buffering did not improve block success (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021), whereas Malamed (2019) reports onset in 1 min 51 s against 6 min 37 s and anecdotal benefit in hot mandibular molars.
- Bupivacaine lasts almost twice as long, 4 hours in the mandible (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021); Sikri (2019) says up to seven hours.

## Primary techniques

**Mandible:** inferior alveolar block. Lip numbness does not predict pulpal anaesthesia (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021). Failure in asymptomatic teeth ranges from 17% (second molar) to 58% (central incisor) (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021). Gow-Gates has not been shown superior (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021).

**Maxilla:** infiltration gives pulpal anaesthesia in around 87% to 92% (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021); anterior teeth decline after about 30 minutes (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021), so a further cartridge is given at 30 minutes (anterior) or 45 minutes (posterior) (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021).

Before cavity preparation or access, test with cold refrigerant; a response means supplemental anaesthesia, but no response does not ensure anaesthesia (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021).

## Supplemental techniques

| Technique | Points |
|---|---|
| Buccal articaine infiltration | After a failed block in irreversible pulpitis, success was 42% (first molar), 48% (second molar) and 73% (premolar) (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021); Malamed (2019) supports 0.9 to 1.2 mL at the apex, whereas Nusstein, Drum and Khan (2021) find it unpredictable in endodontically involved teeth |
| Intraosseous | About 90% success (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021); perforator and 27-gauge wire (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021); no backflow, or anaesthesia fails (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021) |
| Intraligamentary | Immediate onset (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021); 50% to 96% success (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021); about 0.2 mL given slowly (Malamed, 2019); less successful than intraosseous (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021); lasts 10 to 20 minutes (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); fails in mandibular anteriors (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021) |
| Intraseptal | Only 29% (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021) |
| Intrapulpal | Last resort, exquisitely painful, short-lived; needs back pressure, e.g. a cotton pellet stopper (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021) |

Intraosseous and intraligamentary injections are preferred supplements, intrapulpal when others fail (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021). Intrapulpal is given only after a block plus intraosseous or ligament injection (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021).

## Delivery systems and adjuncts

- **Needle-free:** intranasal Kovanaze, 3% tetracaine with oxymetazoline, for maxillary anterior and premolar teeth; endodontic pulpal data are awaited (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021).
- **Nitrous oxide** at 30% to 50% raised block success in irreversible pulpitis (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021).
- **Premedication:** preoperative analgesics may not give a significant advantage for block success (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021); Malamed (2019) lists a presurgical NSAID an hour before, ibuprofen 600 mg.

## Cautions

- Intraosseous, ligament and intrapulpal injections are contraindicated in a symptomatic necrotic tooth with apical abscess (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021); ligament injection should avoid infection (Malamed, 2019).
- Epinephrine in intraosseous injection causes transient tachycardia in 46% to 93% (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021), absent with 3% mepivacaine (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021); Sikri (2019) advises against vasoconstrictor, whereas Malamed (2019) recommends 1:200,000.
- Bupivacaine should not be used intraosseously (Nusstein, Drum and Khan, 2021).
- If every attempt fails, place a temporary restoration and refer (Reader, Nusstein and Drum, 2021).

## Conclusion

No single technique is reliable; sequence primary block, test, supplement.
