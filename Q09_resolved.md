## STUDY MODE

### S1. Concept [orientation]
Minimal intervention dentistry (MID) means care that deals with the cause of disease and rests on prevention and control; minimal invasive dentistry is the conservative restorative part of that plan (Sikri, 2020). The old teaching of "extension for prevention" became "prevention for extension" (Sikri, 2020). The core principles are recognising risk factors, minimising them (diet, saliva, pH) and remineralising early lesions with fluoride or CPP-ACP (Sikri, 2020).

### S2. What the restorative part needs from a material [basic science]
When the aim is to keep sound and remineralisable tissue, the material should: bond without needing mechanical retention (Sikri, 2020); seal against bacteria and so stop caries beneath it (Mount, 1994); release fluoride and ideally be rechargeable (Sikri, 2020); be easy to place in a small, awkward cavity with little or no special equipment (Sikri, 2020); tolerate moisture and be biocompatible (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020); and be strong enough for its site (Sikri, 2020). No material meets all of them, and adhesive GIC is the closest for low-load sites, with composite and bonded amalgam where strength matters (Sikri, 2020). [Likely]

### S3. How the types of GIC fit [classification]
- **High-viscosity (Fuji IX type) for ART:** hand excavation then adhesive GIC, with undermined dentine becoming supported and no lining needed (Sikri, 2020). Pickard says the provisional restoration need not be fully replaced as arrested caries-affected dentine is sealed from nutrients (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Type II.2 reinforced:** tunnel and Class II minimal restorations, with a radiopaque capsulated cement (Mount, 1994).
- **Type II.1 and resin-modified:** aesthetic minimal Class III and V; resin-modified GIC for transitional restorations (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Type I luting:** Hall technique crowns on carious primary molars with no excavation (Sikri, 2020).
- **Type III liner or base and sealant:** GIC as dentine sealant and fissure sealant, especially where moisture is hard to control (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

### S4. Mount's minimal cavity designs [treatment]
Because GIC bonds, cavities can be minimal: remove infected dentine only, leave affected dentine on the axial floor if the margin is sealed, and let demineralised enamel remineralise with fluoride (Mount, 1994). The tunnel is entered from the occlusal fossa medial to the marginal ridge, without retention, and filled with a radiopaque cement in at least two increments (Mount, 1994). Tunnel restorations showed apparently remineralised enamel at 3 years in the author's cases (Mount, 1994). The minimal concept applies only to new lesions in early dentine involvement and is a first stage that precedes conventional design (Mount, 1994).

### S5. Evidence and limits [prognosis]
A systematic review of 14 RCTs found ART with high-viscosity GIC did not differ significantly from conventional restorations in single-surface cavities and primary teeth, with only 2 of 14 trials at low risk of bias (Frencken et al, 2021). Sikri notes single-surface ART restorations succeed better than multisurface ones and that operator effects are marked (Sikri, 2020). ART needs maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013).

## EXAM MODE

### 1. Introduction
Minimal intervention dentistry (MID) is a patient-care philosophy that treats the cause of disease and rests on prevention and control; minimal invasive dentistry is its conservative restorative component (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Principles
1. Recognise risk factors (caries risk assessment) (Sikri, 2020).
2. Minimise them: reduce cariogenic diet, manage saliva and pH (Sikri, 2020).
3. Remineralise early lesions with fluoride or CPP-ACP (Sikri, 2020).
4. Remove only infected tissue; keep remineralisable tissue (Sikri, 2020).
5. Restore minimally with adhesive materials; repair rather than replace (Sikri, 2020).

### 2A. Traditional against minimal intervention approach
| Feature | Traditional | Minimal intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Philosophy | extension for prevention | prevention for extension (Sikri, 2020) |
| Focus | filling teeth | cause and risk of disease (Sikri, 2020) |
| Caries removal | extend to self-cleansing areas | infected dentine only; keep affected, remineralisable tissue (Mount, 1994) |
| Cavity | mechanical retention, resistance form | no retention needed for adhesive materials (Sikri, 2020) |
| Follow-up | replace when failed | repair, maintain and recall (Sikri, 2020) |

### 3. Techniques of minimal invasive removal
Hand instruments (ART), air abrasion, ultrasonics, chemo-mechanical agents, lasers, and magnification (Sikri, 2020).

### 4. Ideal restorative material for MID
| Requirement | Why |
|---|---|
| Chemical adhesion | no mechanical retention cuts (Sikri, 2020) |
| Bacterial seal | arrests caries beneath (Mount, 1994) |
| Fluoride release and recharge | remineralisation (Sikri, 2020) |
| Easy placement, moisture tolerance | small cavities, little equipment (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016) |
| Biocompatibility | placed near pulp (Sikri, 2020) |
| Adequate strength | site-dependent (Sikri, 2020) |
Examples of adhesive materials: GIC, resin-modified GIC, composite with bonding agents, bonded amalgam (Sikri, 2020). This list is a synthesis from several sources. [Likely]

### 5. Types of GIC in MID
| Type | Use in MID |
|---|---|
| High-viscosity, Type II.2 | ART, tunnel and Class II minimal restorations (Sikri, 2020; Mount, 1994) |
| Type II.1 and resin-modified | aesthetic Class III and V, transitional restorations (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Type I luting | Hall technique crowns (Sikri, 2020) |
| Type III lining, base | dentine sealant under restorations (Mount, 1994) |
| GIC sealant | pit and fissure sealing, especially when moisture control is difficult (Mount et al, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |

### 6. Atraumatic restorative treatment
1. Isolate with cotton rolls; access with hatchet or sharp excavator.
2. Remove undermined enamel and excavate soft dentine.
3. Clean; condition with weak polyacrylic acid.
4. Fill with GIC, including confluent fissures; press with a petroleum-jelly-coated finger.
5. Cover with varnish or petroleum jelly (Sikri, 2020).
**Advantages:** no equipment, no electricity, low cost, minimal discomfort, fluoride effect (Sikri, 2020). **Evidence:** no significant difference from conventional restorations in single-surface cavities (Frencken et al, 2021).

### 6A. Single-step and stepwise caries removal
Pickard describes single-step ART with chemically adhesive high-viscosity GIC after hand excavation: a better adaptive seal, and arrested caries-affected dentine stays sealed from nutrients (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Systematic reviews are cited that one-stage partial caries removal gives significantly fewer pulp exposures than two-step stepwise excavation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 6B. GIC sealants in the minimal intervention plan
GIC sealants are recommended for caries-active individuals (long-term fluoride release and a persistent ion-exchange layer after bulk loss), partially erupted teeth, difficult moisture control and when no light is available (Mount et al, 2016). A weak low-powder mix is contraindicated because of wear (Mount et al, 2016).

### 7. Mount's minimal cavity designs
- Tunnel (occlusal approach) for Class II: enter medial to the marginal ridge; no retention; radiopaque cement (Mount, 1994).
- Proximal approach only when the lesion becomes accessible during adjacent preparation (Mount, 1994).
- Buccal or lingual tunnel in older patients (Mount, 1994).
- Remove infected dentine; leave affected dentine if sealed (Mount, 1994).

### 8. Transitional and stabilising restorations
Rampant caries: GIC or resin-modified GIC strengthens remaining tooth and removes the bacterial reservoir (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); interim therapeutic restoration uses conventional or resin-modified GIC with maintenance (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 8A. Preventive restorations (Pickard)
- **Preventive resin restoration:** minimal excavation of occlusal caries to the enamel-dentine junction, restoration with composite or GIC, then an overlying fissure sealant (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- **GIC fissure sealant protocol:** debride, wash and dry; condition enamel with 10% polyacrylic acid for 15 seconds; wash and dry for 10 seconds; apply GIC or RM-GIC; auto-cure or light-cure (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- **Moisture-tolerant interim cover:** partially erupted molars can be protected with a light-cured glass-ionomer varnish (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 9. Limitations
Single-surface better than multisurface; operator-dependent; not for heavy load; needs maintenance and recall (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013).

### 10. Conclusion
MID shifts the focus from filling teeth to controlling disease; adhesive, fluoride-releasing GIC fits it well in low-load sites, and its types are matched to ART, tunnels, transitional restorations, luting and sealing. Success depends on case selection, moisture control, protection of the cement and recall care.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- MID = cause-based care; minimal invasive = conservative restorative part (Sikri, 2020).
- ART: hand excavation plus high-viscosity GIC; first tried in the mid 1980s (Sikri, 2020).
- Hall technique: GIC-luted metal crown, no excavation (Sikri, 2020).
- Tunnel: enter medial to the marginal ridge (Mount, 1994).
- Ideal material: adhesive, fluoride-releasing, moisture-tolerant (Sikri, 2020).
