## STUDY MODE

### S1. Concept [orientation]
Minimal intervention dentistry (MID) means care that deals with the cause of disease and rests on prevention and control; minimal invasive dentistry is the conservative restorative part of that plan (Sikri, 2020). The old teaching of "extension for prevention" became "prevention for extension" (Sikri, 2020). The core principles are recognising risk factors, minimising them (diet, saliva, pH) and remineralising early lesions with fluoride or CPP-ACP (Sikri, 2020).

### S2. What the restorative part needs from a material [basic science]
When the aim is to keep sound and remineralisable tissue, the material should: bond without needing mechanical retention (Sikri, 2020); seal against bacteria and so stop caries beneath it (Mount, 1994); release fluoride and ideally be rechargeable (Sikri, 2020; Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says the burst is complete in 24 h and protection depends on reloading about three times a day, Ritter et al, 2019); be easy to place in a small, awkward cavity with little or no special equipment (Sikri, 2020); be biocompatible (Sikri, 2020); and be strong enough for its site (Sikri, 2020). Pickard adds that an MI restoration should support the remaining tooth, promote remineralisation and seal residual bacteria from their nutrient supply (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Moisture tolerance is documented for GIC sealants (Mount et al, 2016), but Sikri lists technique sensitivity and opacity as GIC disadvantages, which resin modification eases (Sikri, 2020). No material meets all of them, and adhesive GIC is the closest for low-load sites, with composite where strength matters; Sikri lists bonded amalgam among adhesive materials but notes that proper strength may not be achieved (Sikri, 2020). [Likely]

### S3. How the types of GIC fit [classification]
- **High-viscosity GIC for ART:** also called condensable, with Fuji IX among the commercial examples (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); hand excavation then adhesive GIC, with undermined dentine becoming supported and no lining needed (Sikri, 2020). Pickard says the provisional restoration need not be fully replaced as arrested caries-affected dentine is sealed from nutrients (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Type II.2 reinforced:** tunnel and Class II minimal restorations, with a radiopaque capsulated cement (Mount, 1994).
- **Type II.1 and resin-modified:** aesthetic restorations with adequate translucency where the restoration is supported by tooth structure and not under undue occlusal load (Mount et al, 2016); resin-modified or high-viscosity GIC for transitional restorations (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Type I luting:** Hall technique crowns on carious primary molars with no excavation (Sikri, 2020).
- **Type III liner or base:** GIC as the dentine sealant under restorations (Mount, 1994).
- **Fissure sealant:** Mount specifies a fast-setting Type II.2 auto-cure cement at a high powder:liquid ratio (Mount et al, 2016); Mount et al (2016) report that most publications recommend a resin-based material first and advise GIC where moisture control is difficult. Kidd and Fejerskov 4th ed say clinical trials show high-viscosity GIC and resin sealants equally effective, with GIC preferred in erupting teeth and where moisture control is a problem (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed calls the efficacy of GI for pit-and-fissure sealants questionable (Ritter et al, 2019).

### S4. Mount's minimal cavity designs [treatment]
Because GIC bonds, cavities can be minimal: remove infected dentine only, leave affected dentine on the axial floor if the margin is sealed, and let demineralised enamel remineralise with fluoride (Mount, 1994). The tunnel is entered from the occlusal fossa medial to the marginal ridge, without retention, and filled with a radiopaque cement in at least two increments (Mount, 1994). One illustrated case, a tunnel restoration placed about 3 years earlier, showed apparent stability of the enamel after remineralisation (Mount, 1994); this is a single illustrated case (Mount, 1994). Sikri cautions that studies of tunnel restorations have not shown promising results, that the strength of the marginal ridge is not maintained and that leakage and secondary caries are more likely, so tunnels are indicated in small lesions, especially in deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed does not support the tunnel technique and reports glass ionomer tunnel restorations inadequate as definitive restorations (Ritter et al, 2019); Goldstein 3rd ed calls tunnel preparations ideal for composite or glass ionomer (Goldstein et al, 2018). The minimal concept applies only to new lesions in early dentine involvement and is a first stage that precedes conventional design (Mount, 1994).

### S5. Evidence and limits [prognosis]
A systematic review of 14 RCTs found ART with high-viscosity GIC did not differ significantly from conventional restorations in single-surface cavities and primary teeth, with only 2 of 14 trials at low risk of bias (Frencken et al, 2021). Sikri notes single-surface ART restorations succeed better than multisurface ones and that operator effects are marked (Sikri, 2020). Craig 14th gives two-year survival of 93% (single-surface) and 62% (multi-surface) in primary teeth and 80% five-year survival for single-surface restorations in permanent teeth (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Reviews do not fully agree: Frencken found no significant difference at most time points, with significant differences favouring ART at years 4.3 and 6.3 and no equivalence shown for multi-surface permanent-tooth restorations (Frencken et al, 2021), whereas Ruengrungsom found conventional technique survived better than ART for single-surface occlusal and for multi-surface GIC restorations (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018) [both abstract only]. Pickard notes the occlusal GIC may need resurfacing after 2 to 3 years because of wear and partial dissolution (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). ART needs maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013). Kidd and Fejerskov 4th ed find occlusal ART restorations usually successful but approximal ones prone to loss or fracture, loss not being failure if the cavity stays cleansable (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says ART is not recommended for routine clinical use and is restricted to areas with limited access to dental treatment (Ritter et al, 2019).

### S6. Caries removal, ITR, root caries and SDF [treatment]
Selective removal follows the lesion's histology: caries-infected dentine is never retained at the enamel-dentine junction because it is necrotic, cannot be bonded to and is too soft to support a restoration, but where a peripheral seal can be achieved and there are no pulp symptoms, caries-affected dentine may be left over the pulp, and clinical evidence now shows it protects the pulp better than any artificial restorative material (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). The original two-stage stepwise excavation used a calcium hydroxide lining and a temporary zinc polycarboxylate restoration with re-entry at 6 to 9 months, whereas single-step ART seals the arrested dentine with GIC and, in the reviews cited, gave significantly fewer pulp exposures (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). In ART the occlusal GIC may need resurfacing after 2 to 3 years because of wear and partial dissolution (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). An interim therapeutic restoration (ITR) is used when caries control is needed before definitive restoration: in early childhood or rampant caries, very high caries risk, uncooperative or special-needs patients, when treatment must be postponed or when stepwise excavation is needed; caries is removed with hand or low-speed instruments without exposing the pulp, the cavity is restored with conventional or resin-modified GIC, it works best in single-surface or small two-surface cavities, and it must be accompanied by maintenance care including oral hygiene instruction and topical fluoride (Hilton et al, 2013). For root caries Sikri cites Billings' (1984) four grades: grade I white or light brown and not penetrable (topical fluoride, remineralising agents), grade II light brown, 0.5-1.0 mm (excavate, reshape margins, fluoride), grade III dark brown, over 1.0 mm but not reaching the pulp (restore with GIC), grade IV penetrating the pulp (root canal treatment or extraction), and GIC is the preferred restorative material compared with composite (Sikri, 2020). Sikri also reports that 38% silver diamine fluoride on cavitated lesions has shown promising results and that twice-yearly application might arrest caries (Sikri, 2020); Craig 14th describes about a 30% solution that stains tooth structure black and needs an esthetic covering material (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed also names SDF 38%, applied by microbrush for 3 minutes (Ritter et al, 2019). On stepwise excavation, Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says growing evidence suggests temporising and re-entry does not improve outcomes, so a definitive restoration is supported (Ritter et al, 2019); Kidd and Fejerskov 4th ed say the temporary material seems less relevant than the permanence of the filling (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Garg and Garg (2011) and Singh (2020) say varnish is not to be placed beneath glass ionomer. Silver diamine fluoride and remineralisation are covered fully in the P10 answer.

### 1. Introduction
Minimal intervention dentistry (MID) is a patient-care philosophy that treats the cause of disease and rests on prevention and control; minimal invasive dentistry is its conservative restorative component (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Principles
1. Recognise risk factors (caries risk assessment) (Sikri, 2020).
2. Minimise them: reduce cariogenic diet, manage saliva and pH (Sikri, 2020).
3. Remineralise early lesions with fluoride or CPP-ACP (Sikri, 2020).
4. Remove only infected tissue; keep remineralisable tissue (Sikri, 2020).
5. Restore minimally with adhesive materials; repair rather than replace (Sikri, 2020).

### 2A. Traditional against minimal intervention approach
| Feature | Traditional | Minimal intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Philosophy | extension for prevention | prevention for extension (Sikri, 2020) |
| Focus | filling teeth | cause and risk of disease (Sikri, 2020) |
| Caries removal | extend to self-cleansing areas | infected dentine only; keep affected, remineralisable tissue (Mount, 1994) |
| Cavity | mechanical retention, resistance form | no retention needed for adhesive materials (Sikri, 2020) |
| Follow-up | replace when failed | repair, maintain and recall (Sikri, 2020) |

### 3. Techniques of minimal invasive removal
Hand instruments (ART), air abrasion, chemo-mechanical agents, lasers, magnification; ultrasonics cannot remove soft carious dentine (Sikri, 2020).

### 4. Ideal restorative material for MID
| Requirement | Why |
|---|---|
| Chemical adhesion | no mechanical retention cuts (Sikri, 2020) |
| Bacterial seal | arrests caries beneath (Mount, 1994) |
| Fluoride release and recharge | remineralisation (Sikri, 2020) |
| Easy placement | small cavities; GIC technique-sensitive, resin-modified easier (Sikri, 2020) |
| Moisture tolerance | in GIC sealants (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Biocompatibility | placed near pulp (Sikri, 2020) |
| Adequate strength | site-dependent (Sikri, 2020) |
Adhesive materials: GIC, resin-modified GIC, composite, bonded amalgam (strength may be inadequate) (Sikri, 2020). This list is a synthesis from several sources. [Likely]

### 5. Types of GIC in MID
| Type | Use in MID |
|---|---|
| High-viscosity, Type II.2 | ART (Sikri, 2020); tunnel, Class II (Mount, 1994) |
| Type II.1 | aesthetic restorations with low occlusal load (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Resin-modified, high-viscosity | transitional restorations (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Type I luting | Hall technique crowns (Sikri, 2020) |
| Type III lining, base | dentine sealant under restorations (Mount, 1994) |
| Type II.2 as sealant | fissure sealing; resin first in most publications (Mount et al, 2016) |

### 6. Atraumatic restorative treatment
1. Isolate with cotton rolls; access with hatchet or sharp excavator.
2. Remove undermined enamel and excavate soft dentine.
3. Clean; condition with weak polyacrylic acid.
4. Fill with GIC, including confluent fissures; press with a petroleum-jelly-coated finger.
5. Cover with varnish or petroleum jelly (Sikri, 2020); no varnish beneath GIC (Garg and Garg, 2011).
**Advantages:** no equipment, no electricity, low cost, minimal discomfort, fluoride effect (Sikri, 2020). **Evidence:** no significant difference from conventional restorations in single-surface cavities (Frencken et al, 2021); reviews disagree for multi-surface GIC (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018); approximal ART is prone to loss or fracture (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).

### 6A. Selective, single-step and stepwise caries removal
- **Selective:** never leave infected dentine at the enamel-dentine junction; with a peripheral seal and no pulp symptoms, affected dentine may stay over the pulp (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Original stepwise:** re-entry at 6 to 9 months (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Single-step ART:** adhesive high-viscosity GIC seals arrested dentine from nutrients, with significantly fewer pulp exposures than two-step stepwise (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 6B. GIC sealants in the minimal intervention plan
Mount et al (2016): most publications favour resin first; GIC for caries-active patients, partially erupted teeth, poor moisture control. Banerjee and Watson (2015): GIC is more soluble but leaches fluoride; reviews find no clinically significant advantage, resin showing longer longevity (Mount et al, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Kidd and Fejerskov (2016): equally effective in trials.

### 7. Mount's minimal cavity designs
- Tunnel (occlusal approach) for Class II: enter medial to the marginal ridge; no retention; radiopaque cement (Mount, 1994). Sikri (2020) says tunnel studies did not show promising results, with more leakage (Sikri, 2020); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed does not support tunnels (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Proximal approach only when the lesion becomes accessible during adjacent preparation (Mount, 1994).
- Buccal or lingual tunnel in older patients (Mount, 1994).
- Remove infected dentine; leave affected dentine if sealed (Mount, 1994).

### 8. Transitional and stabilising restorations
Rampant caries: GIC or resin-modified GIC strengthens remaining tooth and removes the bacterial reservoir (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). Interim therapeutic restoration (ITR): conventional or resin-modified GIC after hand removal, for early childhood or rampant caries, special-needs patients or stepwise excavation; mainly single-surface or small two-surface cavities; needs maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 8A. Preventive restorations (Pickard)
- **Preventive resin restoration:** minimal excavation of occlusal caries to the enamel-dentine junction, restoration with composite or GIC, then an overlying fissure sealant (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- **GIC fissure sealant protocol:** debride, wash and dry; condition enamel with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 s (Mount et al, 2016) to 15 s (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); wash and dry for 10 seconds; apply GIC or RM-GIC; auto-cure or light-cure (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- **Moisture-tolerant interim cover:** partially erupted molars can be protected with a light-cured glass-ionomer varnish (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 8B. Root caries and silver diamine fluoride
- **Billings root caries:** grade I fluoride; II excavate and fluoride; III (over 1 mm, pulp spared) restore with GIC; IV root canal treatment or extraction (Sikri, 2020).
- **SDF:** 38% twice yearly may arrest cavitated caries (Sikri, 2020); it stains black (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); see the P10 answer.

### 9. Limitations
Single-surface better than multisurface; operator-dependent; needs maintenance and recall; Banerjee and Watson (2015) report longer longevity for resin sealants (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
### 10. Conclusion
MID shifts the focus from filling teeth to controlling disease; adhesive, fluoride-releasing GIC fits it well in low-load sites, and its types are matched to ART, tunnels, transitional restorations, luting and sealing. Success depends on case selection, moisture control, protection of the cement and recall care.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- MID = cause-based care; minimal invasive = conservative restorative part (Sikri, 2020).
- ART: hand excavation plus high-viscosity GIC; first tried in the mid 1980s (Sikri, 2020).
- Hall technique: GIC-luted metal crown, no excavation (Sikri, 2020).
- Tunnel: enter medial to the marginal ridge (Mount, 1994); Sikri (2020) says studies did not show promising results (Sikri, 2020).
- Ideal material: adhesive, fluoride-releasing, biocompatible and strong enough for its site (Sikri, 2020).
- Fissure sealant: Mount et al (2016) say most publications favour resin first and advise GIC where moisture control is difficult (Mount et al, 2016).
- Billings grade III root caries (over 1 mm, pulp spared) is restored with GIC (Sikri, 2020).
