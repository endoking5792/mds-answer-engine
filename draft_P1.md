## STUDY MODE

### S1. The core contrast [basic science]
Composite bonds to enamel superbly but not reliably to dentine (shrinkage and hydrolysis), whereas GIC bonds to dentine reliably and is water-based [SRC:A484]. McCabe sums up the table: composite is strong, tough, insoluble, radiopaque and quick-setting but has no inherent adhesion, shrinks and releases no fluoride; GIC has inherent adhesion, little shrinkage, fluoride release and biocompatibility but is brittle, soluble, not radiopaque, wears, is water-sensitive and slow-setting [SRC:B56].

### S2. Which to use where [clinical decision-making]
Composite is stronger than GIC under occlusal or incisal load [SRC:A490] [SRC:A488]. In molars composite has little enamel for etching and suffers shrinkage, fatigue and water uptake, while GIC needs support from remaining tooth [SRC:A492]. Composite lacks an intrinsic defence against caries, so once a gap forms microleakage and recurrent caries spread quickly [SRC:B301]. Measured wear for GIC was 73 micrometres in a year against under 10 for composite [SRC:C125].

### S3. Evidence [prognosis]
A meta-analysis of RCTs found less secondary caries with GIC than amalgam, but no significant difference from composite (primary teeth RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.77-1.10; permanent teeth RR 0.77, 0.39-1.51) [LIT:Ge2023]. The Pickard Manual gives average replacement ages of about 3-5 years for GIC and 5-8 for composite [SRC:C2133].

### S4. The combination
The sandwich technique places GIC as dentine replacement under a composite enamel replacement, and uses the strengths of both (see Q7 and Q14) [SRC:A488].

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement and composite resin are the two principal tooth-coloured direct restoratives; they differ in setting chemistry, adhesion, strength and biological action [SRC:B56].

### 2. Comparison table
| Feature | Glass ionomer cement | Composite resin |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | acid-base reaction, water-based [SRC:A78] | polymerisation of resin |
| Adhesion | chemical to enamel and dentine, no etching [SRC:A484] | micromechanical; excellent to enamel, uncertain to dentine [SRC:A484] |
| Shrinkage | little, develops slowly [SRC:A93] | 1-5% [SRC:A386] |
| Fluoride | release and recharge [SRC:B56] | none [SRC:B56] |
| Strength | weaker, lacks rigidity, brittle [SRC:A486] | stronger under load [SRC:A490] |
| Wear | 73 micrometres a year in one measure [SRC:C125] | under 10 [SRC:C125] |
| Aesthetics | opaque, dulls [SRC:G29] | best |
| Radiopacity | restorative GIC radiolucent unless designed otherwise [SRC:B51] | radiopaque |
| Moisture | must be protected early [SRC:G19] | very technique-sensitive |
| Biocompatibility | mild; not for pulp exposure [SRC:G18] | HEMA and monomer issues |
| Replacement age | about 3-5 years [SRC:C2133] | about 5-8 years [SRC:C2133] |

### 2A. Bond strength and the interface
Conventional GIC gives macroshear bond strengths of 6-12 MPa, against 22-35 MPa for modern dentine bonding agents, but GIC fails inside the cement and wets tooth well [SRC:C1213] [SRC:A116]. Composite shrinkage pulls the material from the wall, and when the stress exceeds the bond the adhesion is lost, which compromises the seal [SRC:C116].

### 2B. Cervical lesions
- A resin-modified GIC was retained in 96.4% of restorations at 5 years against 51.5% for bonded composite [SRC:C1106].
- Flowable composites give better aesthetics than GIC in non-carious cervical lesions [SRC:B399].
- Resin-modified GIC can discolour from water uptake after 6 years [SRC:C2120].

### 2C. Class II restorations
GIC is not recommended for Class II in adults [SRC:B262]. A high-viscosity GIC with a resin coat (glass hybrid) survived as well as nano-hybrid composite over 5 years in moderate two-surface Class II cavities (94.5% against 94.4%), although composite had the higher success score [LIT:Miletic2024].

### 2D. Clinical handling
- **GIC:** isolate, condition 10-20 seconds, mix, place under a matrix, seal at once, finish after 24 hours [SRC:G41] [SRC:G43] [SRC:G46] [SRC:G48] [SRC:G47].
- **Technique tolerance:** GIC is easier than composite at subgingival margins, and a GIC base is preferred in Class II with subgingival extension [SRC:G54].
- **Marginal behaviour:** composite has no defence against caries once a gap forms; GIC bonds and releases fluoride at the margin [SRC:B301] [SRC:G23].

### 3. Indications
- **GIC:** Class V and root caries, high caries risk, primary teeth, transitional restorations, ART, subgingival margins, liners and bases [SRC:G11] [SRC:C1118].
- **Composite:** anterior and posterior load-bearing restorations where aesthetics and wear resistance matter [SRC:A490] [SRC:C163].

### 4. Evidence
A meta-analysis of RCTs found less secondary caries with GIC than amalgam but no difference from composite [LIT:Ge2023].

### 5. Combination: sandwich technique
GIC as dentine replacement under composite as enamel replacement; used with margins on dentine [SRC:A488] [SRC:A393].

### 5A. Where the intermediate materials sit
Powers describes a hierarchy: composites release little or no fluoride; compomers are less wear-resistant than composite but aesthetic and release fluoride; resin-modified GIC releases more fluoride than compomers but wears more than they do [SRC:B429]. They are therefore compromises between the two ends of the table.

### 6. Conclusion
Composite is stronger, aesthetic and wear-resistant; GIC is adhesive, fluoride-releasing and moisture-tolerant. The choice depends on load, caries risk, moisture control and aesthetics, and the sandwich restoration combines both.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- GIC: chemical adhesion, fluoride, little shrinkage, brittle and wears [SRC:B56].
- Composite: strong, no fluoride, shrinks, dentine bond uncertain [SRC:A484] [SRC:B56].
- Secondary caries: GIC better than amalgam, not better than composite [LIT:Ge2023].
- Load decides: composite for heavy load [SRC:A490].
