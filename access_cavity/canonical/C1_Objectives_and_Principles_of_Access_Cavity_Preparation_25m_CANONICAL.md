## 1. Definition and objectives

### Explanation

Sikri describes the access cavity as a space cut from the occlusal table to the canal orifices so that instrumentation, irrigation and obturation can follow, and says it is so significant that it is described as "gateway to success" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer add that difficulties met during instrumentation can usually be traced to insufficient access and lack of straight-line access (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

The books list the objectives differently, and neither list is ranked here. Sikri gives four: gain direct access to the apical foramen (not merely to the canal orifices); remove pulp tissue from the coronal and radicular spaces; permit instrumentation, preparation and obturation; and maintain structural integrity of the tooth by conserving as much tooth structure as possible (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's chapter gives six: remove all caries when present, conserve sound tooth structure, unroof the pulp chamber completely, remove all coronal pulp tissue, locate all canal orifices, and achieve straight-line access to the apical foramen or the first curvature (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman presents the same six as Vertucci's objectives (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The practical difference is that Sikri aims at the apical foramen while Cohen and Grossman stop at "the initial curvature" when the canal is curved.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** The access cavity is the channel cut from the occlusal or lingual surface to the canal orifices so that the canals can be cleaned, shaped and filled. It is called the "gateway to success" (Sikri, 2019).

**Objectives** (Vertucci, in Gopikrishna, 2021; Gutmann and Fan, 2021):
1. Remove all caries and defective restorations.
2. Conserve sound tooth structure.
3. Unroof the pulp chamber completely.
4. Remove all coronal pulp, vital or necrotic.
5. Locate all canal orifices.
6. Achieve straight-line access to the apical foramen, or to the first canal curvature (Sikri names the apical foramen).

## 2. Principles of entry: pre-operative, entry surface, caries, cusps

### Explanation

Sikri's rules for entry are: radiographs from at least two angles are evaluated before starting; endodontic entry is made through the occlusal or lingual surface and never through a proximal surface; unsupported cusps are reduced to give clearance in occlusal and lateral movement; and the cavity needs some resistance form so the tooth does not fracture during treatment and retention form for the interim and permanent seal (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen agrees that anterior teeth are entered lingually and posterior teeth occlusally because this gives the best straight-line access with the least aesthetic and restorative cost (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's chapter notes that some authors move the access of mandibular incisors from lingual to incisal in selected cases (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC], and Baumann and Beer note that these teeth may need the opening almost at the incisal edge (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

Caries and defective restorations are removed before the pulp chamber is entered. Cohen says this is essential because it exposes hidden caries or fractures and lets the clinician judge restorability, and sometimes the right decision is to stop in favour of crown lengthening or a bonded build-up (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch add that the chamber should be opened only after decay is removed, so the canal system is not contaminated (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. Where the books differ is on intact restorations. Baumann and Beer say a restoration that looks intact on radiographs can be left, but only complete removal shows the extent of caries and poorly sealed areas (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri says if canals cannot be found by cutting through a crown, the crown should be removed (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Where little tooth remains, Sikri describes building up the walls around the planned access with adhesive composite (the "donut" technique) so that the dam and interim restoration can be retained (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch warn that in older patients the drop into the chamber may not be evident because of mineralisation or tertiary dentin (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

Sikri also states that rubber dam isolation is mandatory for a successful outcome (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principles of entry**
- Rubber dam isolation is mandatory (Sikri, 2019).
- Study two or more radiographs from different angles for crown–root angle, chamber size and depth, canal number and curvature (Sikri, 2019).
- Enter through the lingual surface in anteriors and the occlusal surface in posteriors; never through a proximal surface (Sikri, 2019; Gutmann and Fan, 2021). Mandibular incisors may need an incisal start (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).
- Remove all caries and defective restorations first. This reveals cracks, allows restorability to be judged, and avoids contaminating the canals (Gutmann and Fan, 2021; Setzer and Walsch, 2025). Intact radiographically sound restorations may be left (Baumann and Beer, 2010); if canals cannot be found through a crown, remove it (Sikri, 2019).
- Reduce unsupported cusps (Sikri, 2019).
- If too little tooth remains to hold the dam or an interim seal, build up the walls first with adhesive composite around the planned access (the "donut" technique) (Sikri, 2019).
- Penetrate the roof with a round diamond; the "drop" into the chamber may not be felt in older patients because of mineralisation or tertiary dentin, so take extra care near the floor (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
- Provide resistance form and retention form for the interim and final restoration (Sikri, 2019).

## 3. Laws governing the chamber floor and orifices

### Explanation

Krasner and Rankow examined 500 pulp chambers and found the cementoenamel junction the most important landmark for the position of the chamber and orifices (Gopikrishna, 2021; Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen warns that relying on occlusal or lingual anatomy alone is dangerous because morphology changes as the crown is destroyed and restored, which explains perforations along the cervical line or into the furcation (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC].

Grossman lists nine laws: centrality, concentricity, the CEJ law, symmetry 1 and 2, colour change, and orifice location 1, 2 and 3 (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri lists seven of these (no separate CEJ law and no orifice location 3) and states the centrality and concentricity laws in the same terms (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The colour-change law is used as an end-point check: if the operator can see the wall–floor junction, the outline is complete (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Both books state that symmetry 1 and 2 do not apply to maxillary molars (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Orifice location 3 (orifices lie at the ends of the developmental fusion lines) appears in Grossman and is matched by Setzer and Walsch's statement that developmental lines connect all canal orifices (Gopikrishna, 2021; Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Krasner and Rankow laws** (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019)
| Law | Statement |
|---|---|
| Centrality | Chamber floor lies in the centre of the tooth at the level of the CEJ |
| Concentricity | Chamber walls are concentric with the external surface at the CEJ |
| CEJ | Distance from the crown surface to the chamber wall is the same all round the CEJ; the CEJ is the most consistent landmark |
| Symmetry 1 | Orifices are equidistant from a mesiodistal line through the floor centre (not maxillary molars) |
| Symmetry 2 | Orifices lie on a line perpendicular to that mesiodistal line (not maxillary molars) |
| Colour change | Chamber floor is always darker than the walls |
| Orifice location 1 | Orifices lie at the wall–floor junction |
| Orifice location 2 | Orifices lie at the angles of the wall–floor junction |
| Orifice location 3 | Orifices lie at the ends of the root developmental fusion lines |

Clinical use: do not rely on occlusal anatomy alone, because it changes with caries and crowns (Gutmann and Fan, 2021). If the wall–floor junction is visible, the outline is complete (Sikri, 2019).

## 4. Outline form, convenience form and phases

### Explanation

Sikri applies Black's headings: outline form, convenience form and cleaning the cavity. Because the outline is dictated by the internal anatomy, endodontic preparations are made "from inside of the tooth to the outside". The size of the chamber matters (preparations must be more extensive in young patients than in older ones), as do its shape (a three-canal tooth gives a triangular outline) and the configuration of the canals (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Convenience form allows instruments to be introduced freely. If the axial walls are under-extended, the orifice appears partly in the wall as a "mouse hole" effect, and dentinal overhangs between the operator's fingers and the instrument tip cause binding, which leads to perforation, ledging, breakage or transportation (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri sets out the phases as pre-access analysis, preparation of the tooth, removal of the roof and coronal pulp, identification of orifices and preparation of the radicular portion, and divides roof removal into penetration, enlargement and finishing/flaring steps (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Baumann and Beer give the usual outlines: triangular for maxillary incisors, oval (buccolingual) for premolars, a central-fossa start with the bur towards the mesiopalatal cusp in maxillary molars, and trapezoidal in mandibular molars with the bur directed somewhat distally (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri adds that an ideal access needs magnification and illumination, that a slow-speed handpiece suits calcified chambers, and that ultrasonic tips trough obstructions (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Setzer and Walsch describe the ideal molar cavity as one that gives good visualisation and lets all orifices be identified without excessively weakening the tooth, and give a rule of thumb: remove as little as possible, but only as much as necessary (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Outline form.** Dictated by the internal anatomy, so cut from inside to outside (Sikri, 2019). Three factors: chamber size (larger preparation in the young), chamber shape (three canals give a triangle, four a square or rhomboid) and canal configuration (Sikri, 2019).

**Convenience form.** Direct view of the floor and all orifices, free entry of instruments into each orifice, and straight-line access to the foramen or first curvature (Sikri, 2019; Gutmann and Fan, 2021). Under-extension gives a "mouse hole" orifice; overhangs bind instruments and cause ledges, perforation, breakage and transportation (Sikri, 2019). Design depends on the whole canal curvature as well as on orifice position (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).

**Usual outlines** (Baumann and Beer, 2010): maxillary incisors triangular, centred on the palatal surface; premolars oval, long axis buccolingual; maxillary molars opened in the central fossa with the bur inclined towards the mesiopalatal cusp; mandibular molars trapezoidal, with the bur directed somewhat distally.

**Aids** (Sikri, 2019): magnification and illumination are required for an ideal access; a slow-speed handpiece is used where increased tactile awareness is needed, as in calcified chambers; ultrasonic tips can trough away obstructions.

**Phases** (Sikri, 2019): pre-access analysis; preparation of the tooth; removal of roof and coronal pulp (penetration, enlargement, finishing/flaring); orifice identification; radicular preparation. Finally, clean the cavity with irrigant; do not direct air into the orifices (Sikri, 2019).

## 5. Balance between access and tooth preservation

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer state that the access must never be so small that the orifices cannot be seen, while excessive preparation reduces fracture resistance, and that structural weakening during access is the most important factor in the fracture susceptibility of root-filled teeth (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. A lack of straight-line access from the chamber leads to ledging in curved canals and lateral perforation, and rotary NiTi instruments need straight-line access to the mid-root to avoid coronal curving and cyclic fatigue (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Final length measurement should be taken only after straight-line access is achieved (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

Sikri also describes a conservative preparation, which "disregards the traditional requirement" of full unroofing and straight-line access, yet states that no conservative approach should be followed at the cost of non-negotiation of the root canal, that conservative preparation should not take precedence over traditional preparation, and that a wider cavity to find extra canals is better than ignoring them (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The minimally invasive evidence is discussed in C4.

Typical errors listed by Sikri include gouging the labial wall in maxillary anterior teeth when the linguoaxial inclination is not recognised, a white roof indicating an under-extended cavity, perforation in the maxillary first premolar (the most susceptible tooth), and furcation perforation with a long-shank bur in mandibular molars (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer add the risk of cervical perforation on the labial side if the chamber is opened at a steep angle in anterior teeth (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Balance.** The cavity must give a full view of the chamber without needless loss of dentin (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Excessive cutting lowers fracture resistance; too little causes ledges, perforation and missed canals (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Straight-line access is also needed for rotary NiTi to avoid cyclic fatigue, and working length should be taken only after it is achieved (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Setzer and Walsch, 2025). Rule: remove as little as possible but as much as necessary (Setzer and Walsch, 2025). Do not let conservatism prevent canal negotiation (Sikri, 2019).

**Common errors** (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010): gouging of labial wall from missed axial inclination; under-extension (white roof); cervical or furcation perforation; failure to find extra canals; leaving pulp horns, causing discolouration.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim:** every canal needs full unroofing and straight-line access. Sikri and Setzer and Walsch support this as traditional teaching; Cohen notes the minimally invasive approach lacks definitive outcome evidence (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC], and Baumann and Beer name access-related weakening as the most important factor in fracture susceptibility. The two positions are therefore not reconciled by the books: traditional access protects canal finding and cleaning, conservative access protects pericervical dentin. [Likely] that the practical answer is case-based access, adequate to find and shape every canal. This is argued further in C4.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Objectives: six (Vertucci / Cohen); Sikri names four and aims at the apical foramen.
- Entry: lingual in anteriors, occlusal in posteriors, never proximal.
- Nine laws of Krasner and Rankow; symmetry laws do not apply to maxillary molars.
- Colour change: dark floor, light walls; visible junction means the outline is complete.
- Outline is dictated by internal anatomy, cut inside to outside.
- Straight-line access is required before length measurement.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Listing only "to locate canal orifices" (Sikri says access to the apical foramen, not merely to the orifices).
- Writing the laws without saying that symmetry 1 and 2 exclude maxillary molars.
- Describing entry through a proximal surface.
- Calling Black's "outline form" the same as an operative cavity; endodontic outline is cut from inside to outside.
- Omitting rubber dam isolation and caries removal as pre-conditions.
