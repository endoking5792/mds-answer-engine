## 1. Concept and rationale

### Explanation

Sikri defines conservative access ("minimal invasive endodontics" or "microendodontics") as preparing smaller access cavities and using smaller tapered files to conserve more dentin around the pulp spaces, credits the idea to Clarke and Khademi, and rests the rationale on preserving pericervical dentin (4.0 mm above and 6.0 mm below the CEJ), which he considers a key factor in reducing fracture of root-filled teeth (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri also states that conservatively designed molars have been observed to be 2.5 times stronger than traditionally designed ones (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; the figure is a single textbook statement, and the primary study was not opened here [Likely].

Cohen's objective is the same: preserve as much tooth structure as possible while still locating, debriding, enlarging, shaping and obturating the canals, with particular care to avoid gouging cervically, laterally or into the chamber floor (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch describe the central idea as keeping the chamber roof as intact as possible and minimising dentin removal in the pericervical area to reduce crown and root fractures (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer point the same way when they say that weakening during access is the most important factor in the fracture susceptibility of root-filled teeth, though they do not discuss minimally invasive designs (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Access designs that remove less tooth structure, particularly pericervical dentin (4 mm above to 6 mm below the CEJ), than traditional full unroofing, using smaller instruments (Sikri, 2019). Also called conservative access, microendodontics, contracted endodontic cavity (CEC).

**Rationale.** Pericervical dentin is considered key to the strength of root-filled teeth (Sikri, 2019; Setzer and Walsch, 2025); access-related weakening is a major cause of fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Objective.** Preserve tooth structure while still locating, debriding, shaping and obturating the canals (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).

**Features of the conservative preparation** (Sikri, 2019): less widening of the chamber; no extra convenience form; smaller-flute instruments at the coronal canal; the traditional requirement of complete unroofing and straight-line access is set aside. Clarke and Khademi are credited with the idea and use endoguide burs for access and orifice enlargement with orifice openers, and V-taper variable-taper NiTi files (Sikri, 2019). Smaller tapers are also preferred in calcified and severely curved canals (Sikri, 2019).

**Why it matters.** No restorative material or technique can replace lost dentin, so conservation is directed at key areas (Sikri, 2019). The goal is not simply a small cavity but a design that conserves dentin while fulfilling the treatment objectives (Sikri, 2019).

## 2. Designs and terminology

### Explanation

Terminology differs between books. Cohen lists the guided endodontic technique, microguided technique, contracted cavities, truss access openings and orifice-directed dentin conservation access as names for the approach (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch group the variants under "contracted cavity design": ultraconservative or "ninja" access (one file at a time), truss access (a dentin truss kept over the chamber in molars), incisal access in anteriors, caries access through the space left after caries removal, and restorative-driven access through existing restorations (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. The Guidebook uses the classification of Shabbir et al as its guiding reference (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. Cohen lists "guided endodontic technique" as one of the names, whereas Setzer and Walsch treat guided techniques as a separate, targeted-access method; the two uses are not the same (see C5).

Sikri notes that conservative preparation reduces excessive widening of the chamber, drops the convenience form, uses smaller flute diameters at the coronal canal, and disregards the traditional requirement of straight-line access and complete unroofing (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch add that modern NiTi rotary instruments are more flexible, which is what allowed access size to be reduced (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Design | Meaning (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) |
|---|---|
| Traditional | Complete unroofing, all pulp horns removed |
| Conservative | Smaller, but chamber roof largely removed [RECALL] |
| Ultraconservative / "ninja" | Opening admits one file at a time |
| Truss access | Dentin truss kept over the chamber; separate access to each canal |
| Incisal access | Entry through the incisal edge in anteriors |
| Caries / restorative-driven | Access follows the caries space or an existing restoration |

Other names: microguided technique, orifice-directed dentin conservation access (Gutmann and Fan, 2021). Instrument flexibility of modern NiTi made smaller access possible (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).

## 3. Evidence: benefits and risks

### Explanation

Cohen states that definitive evidence-based outcomes have yet to be seen, that loss of cervical tooth structure may weaken the tooth, although this concept has been challenged, and that overly restricted access may or may not greatly affect later procedures (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch are more critical. They report that experimental studies are inconclusive on whether contracted cavities improve fracture resistance; that contracted designs left more unprepared canal wall after instrumentation; and that MB2 was detected less often with ultraconservative access (31.6%) than with traditional (60%) or conservative (53.3%) access in one study (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. They state that no clinical study has shown a higher or equal success rate for extreme minimally invasive approaches and that there is still a burden of proof (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. Proponents suggest mitigating the risk of missed canals by identifying all canals with cone-beam CT beforehand (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. Sikri, writing from the traditional side, says conservative preparation should not take precedence over traditional preparation and that a wider cavity to find extra canals is better than ignoring them (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

The Shabbir et al review, as seen in a web-search summary of the abstract, covered 49 articles examining 11 treatment parameters, found that most did not show clear benefit of minimally invasive designs, raised concern about disinfection, filling and restoration, and advised reserving them for selected cases with suitable equipment (Shabbir et al, 2021) [LIT, abstract summary only; full text not opened]. The books and the review agree in direction; none of the sources supplies clinical outcome data.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Possible benefit.** Higher preservation of pericervical dentin and so possibly greater fracture resistance (Sikri, 2019). Evidence: experimental studies are inconclusive (Setzer and Walsch, 2025); Cohen notes the weakening concept "has been challenged" (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).

**Risks**
- Incomplete inspection of the chamber floor, so pulp remnants and extra canals may be missed (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
- More unprepared canal wall after instrumentation (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
- Lower MB2 detection with ultraconservative access: 31.6% against 60% traditional and 53.3% conservative in one study (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
- Difficult cleaning and filling through restricted access (Shabbir et al, 2021, abstract level).

- Poor access to the far buccal and far lingual portions of the distal canals of mandibular molars, and difficulty finding supernumerary canals (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
- Missed canals raise the risk of persistent periapical lesions (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
- No verification that all tissue remnants and debris were removed from grooves and pulp horns (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).

| Feature | Traditional | Minimally invasive |
|---|---|---|
| Chamber roof | Fully removed (Sikri, 2019) | Kept as intact as possible (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) |
| Straight-line access | Required | Set aside (Sikri, 2019) |
| Pericervical dentin | Reduced | Preserved |
| Visual check of floor | Complete | Limited (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) |
| Evidence of better outcome | Long-standing practice | None clinical (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) |

**Status.** No clinical study shows equal or higher success for extreme designs; the burden of proof remains (Setzer and Walsch, 2025). Not recommended for routine use; reserve for selected cases with adequate equipment, ideally with CBCT identification of all canals (Shabbir et al, 2021; Setzer and Walsch, 2025).

## 4. Modifications of access in special situations

### Explanation

The "modifications in access cavity preparation" wording is answered here as the clinical alternatives to the standard outline. Cohen mentions moving the mandibular incisor access to the incisal surface in selected cases, and says that crowded or rotated teeth may need a buccal or incisal access chosen on straight-line and conservation principles (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri recommends a buccal access for rotated teeth (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], a shortened bur or mouth prop in limited mouth opening (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], removal of a crown when canals cannot be found through it (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and pre-endodontic composite build-up ("donut") when little tooth remains (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen advocates guided access for partially or completely calcified chambers (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC], and Setzer and Walsch consider guided techniques a valid alternative in severe calcification and dens evaginatus (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Modified access, by situation**
- *Mandibular incisors*: incisal rather than lingual entry in selected cases (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).
- *Rotated or crowded teeth*: buccal or incisal access based on straight-line and conservation principles (Gutmann and Fan, 2021; Sikri, 2019).
- *Limited opening*: mouth prop, short bur (Sikri, 2019).
- *Crowns and restorations*: remove the crown if canals cannot be found (Sikri, 2019).
- *Lost structure*: build-up before access (Sikri, 2019; Setzer and Walsch, 2025). An adhesive resin composite build-up is often the interim restoration of choice, and the build-up can often serve as the definitive post-endodontic restoration or as a secure base for a prosthetic one (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- *Pulp stones and calcifications*: loupes, the microscope and ultrasonic tips protect the floor and reduce the risk of perforation (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- *Principle for every modification*: the choice rests on straight-line access and conservation of structure (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).
- *Calcified or anomalous teeth*: guided targeted access (Gutmann and Fan, 2021; Setzer and Walsch, 2025).

## 5. Conditions for using a reduced access

### Explanation

Cohen says that the approach is enhanced by, and with a minimal cavity would essentially require, magnification when searching for calcified orifices (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch suggest identifying all canals beforehand with cone-beam CT (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. Sikri warns that conservative designs must not be allowed to prevent location, negotiation and preparation of the canals (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Based on these statements, the conditions below are a synthesis rather than a quoted list [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conditions for a reduced access (synthesis of the books)**
- Magnification is essential (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).
- Pre-operative CBCT or equivalent to confirm the canals (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
- Convert to a wider access whenever a canal cannot be found, cleaned or negotiated (Sikri, 2019).
- Avoid in teeth with suspected extra canals, severely curved canals or inadequate chamber visibility [RECALL].
- Guided targeted access is reserved for severe calcification or anomalous teeth (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim:** conservative access strengthens the tooth. The textbooks differ. Sikri reports molars 2.5 times stronger (Sikri, 2019); Cohen calls the weakening concept "challenged" (Gutmann and Fan, 2021); Setzer and Walsch say experimental fracture-resistance results are inconclusive and clinical outcome data are absent (Setzer and Walsch, 2025). The Shabbir review summary agrees that most studies show no clear benefit (Shabbir et al, 2021; abstract level). Most of the evidence is laboratory; it cannot show that a tooth survives longer. **Conclusion:** use as much access as is needed to find, clean and shape every canal, and preserve pericervical dentin where that is possible without compromising that aim. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Pericervical dentin: 4 mm above to 6 mm below the CEJ (Sikri).
- Variants: ultraconservative ("ninja"), truss, incisal, caries, restorative-driven.
- Guiding review: Shabbir et al 2021 (49 articles, 11 parameters).
- No clinical study shows equal success for extreme designs.
- MB2 detection falls with ultraconservative access (one study).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Presenting minimally invasive access as proven better; the books say the evidence is laboratory based.
- Confusing "guided endodontics" (a navigation method) with minimal access.
- Forgetting the risk of missed canals and uncleaned chamber.
- Quoting the 2.5× figure without saying it comes from one textbook statement.
