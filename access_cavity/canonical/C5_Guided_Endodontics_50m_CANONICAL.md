## 1. Definition, rationale and place in endodontics

### Explanation

Nair et al call 3D-guided surgical and non-surgical endodontic treatment an emerging application that uses guides manufactured with the aid of CBCT images and intraoral scanning of the area of interest, and state that studies with a high level of evidence are lacking, with several in vitro studies and case reports published (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. Gutmann and Fan advocate a guided access technique for the coronal entry into teeth with partially or completely calcified pulp chambers and/or obstructed canals (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC], and, in their minimally invasive section, list the "guided endodontic technique" and "microguided technique" among the names for minimally invasive access (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch treat guided techniques as a targeted, minimised access and report them as a valid alternative to conventional techniques in severe pulp canal calcification, dens evaginatus and other anomalous teeth (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

Why it exists is a [RECALL] summary: a canal that has narrowed or disappeared is the hardest access problem in endodontics, freehand searching removes dentin and risks perforation, and the position of the canal can be planned beforehand from a three-dimensional image. The books' two uses of the term are different. In Cohen Ch.7 it is one name for conservative access, whereas in Cohen Ch.2 and in Setzer and Walsch it is a navigation technique for difficult teeth; both should be stated so that the examiner sees the distinction.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Guided endodontics is the use of a three-dimensional plan, made from CBCT data usually combined with an intraoral or model surface scan, to direct a bur along a pre-planned path to a canal, so that access to a calcified or anomalous canal is made with minimal dentin loss. The plan is transferred to the patient by a printed template (static guidance) or by a real-time tracking system (dynamic navigation) (Nair et al, 2021 for the template; static and dynamic types [RECALL]).

**Status.** An emerging technology. High-level evidence is lacking; in vitro studies and case reports dominate (Nair et al, 2021).

**Two uses of the term, to be kept apart**
- A *navigation method* for difficult teeth: advocated for partially or completely calcified chambers (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) and described as a valid alternative in severe calcification and dens evaginatus (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
- A *name* for conservative access in Cohen's access chapter, alongside microguided technique, contracted cavities and truss access (Gutmann and Fan, 2021). See C4.

**Rationale** [RECALL]: obliterated canals are hard to find freehand; each extra millimetre of searching removes dentin and risks perforation; a pre-planned path reduces both.

## 2. Types of guided access

### Explanation

None of the three textbook sources separates static from dynamic guidance; that division is [RECALL]. A web-search summary of a 2025 narrative review reports that static navigation is very accurate for straight canals but limited in posterior teeth and curved canals, while dynamic navigation is more flexible during the procedure but depends on training and costly equipment (Guided2025, summary level) [LIT]. Another search summary reports intracoronal and sleeveless guides introduced to deal with limited mouth opening in posterior teeth; this was not verified against a primary paper [LIT, summary]. Static guidance can be pictured as "drilling through a tube", dynamic navigation as "following a GPS" [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Feature | Static (template) guidance | Dynamic navigation |
|---|---|---|
| Principle | 3D-printed template with a sleeve fixes the bur path (Nair et al, 2021) | Real-time optical tracking of the handpiece against the CBCT plan [RECALL] |
| Needs | CBCT, surface scan, planning software, printer (Nair et al, 2021) | CBCT, tracking system, registration marker, training [RECALL] |
| Strength | High accuracy in straight canals (Guided2025, summary level) | Can adjust the path during the procedure (Guided2025, summary level) |
| Weakness | Limited in curved canals and posterior teeth; mouth opening (Guided2025, summary level) | Cost and learning curve (Guided2025, summary level) |
| Variants | Intracoronal or sleeveless guides for posterior teeth [LIT, summary only] | Used also for fibre-post removal and surgery [LIT, summary only] |

## 3. Indications and contraindications

### Explanation

Nair et al say the technology is used for teeth with calcified canals and teeth with anatomical variations of the root canal system (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch name severe pulp canal calcification, dens evaginatus and other anomalous teeth (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. Search summaries of case reports add dens invaginatus and micro-endodontic surgical procedures, and one source says that treating an obliterated canal is indicated only when there is a periapical lesion or symptoms of irreversible pulpitis [LIT, summary only]. Sikri, writing on conventional access, reminds the reader that calcification is a problem of locating canals and that tertiary dentin from caries or leakage reduces the chamber volume (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Contraindications are not stated in the textbooks; those below are [RECALL] and follow from the method.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications**
1. Pulp canal obliteration or calcification of the chamber or canals, commonly after trauma, with a pulp or periapical indication for treatment (Nair et al, 2021; Gutmann and Fan, 2021; the indication for treatment [LIT, summary only]).
2. Anatomical anomalies: dens evaginatus (Setzer and Walsch, 2025); dens invaginatus [LIT, summary only].
3. Access in a normal tooth where tooth structure must be preserved (a minimally invasive use) (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).
4. Guided endodontic surgery (see section 8).

**Contraindications and cautions** [RECALL]
- Severely curved canals beyond the straight, accessible portion (see Guided2025, summary level).
- Limited mouth opening, where the guide and a long bur cannot be seated.
- Very little remaining tooth structure to seat the guide.
- No pulpal or periapical indication for treatment of an asymptomatic obliterated canal.
- Patient or operator unable to take CBCT or lacking the equipment and training.

## 4. Procedure: static guided access, step by step

### Explanation

The books give only the core of the workflow. Nair et al describe a template created by superimposing CBCT images on intraoral scanned images, with sleeves placed in the virtual plan to create a 3D-printed guide with a directed sleeve, usually made with implant planning software (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. Their figure legend shows the sequence for a block of two molars: 2D rendering from the CBCT, volumetric rendering of the teeth, a lateral view with virtual files in position, the printed guide, and an occlusal view of the accessed tooth (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch note that proponents of contracted access suggest identifying all canals before the procedure with cone-beam CT (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

Web-search summaries of two case series give more detail. A 2025 series of 11 calcified-canal cases describes a high-resolution CBCT and an intraoral scan used for virtual cavity planning, the two superimposed, and virtual sleeves placed to limit drilling deviation, and reports the approach as safe and accurate with less tooth removal and shorter working time (Case2025, summary level) [LIT]. A 2023 series of calcified anterior teeth, as summarised, superimposed STL files from an intraoral scan on CBCT DICOM data in planning software, used 0.75 mm burs, and set the drilling depth from the CBCT so as to give straight-line access (DentJ2023, summary level) [LIT]. The success figures of these series were not retrieved. The remaining steps are standard practice, [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Static guided access: steps**
1. **Diagnosis and case selection.** Clinical and radiographic examination; confirm the indication and the tooth's restorability [RECALL].
2. **Imaging.** A small-field-of-view, high-resolution CBCT of the tooth, and an intraoral scan (or scan of a model) of the arch (Nair et al, 2021; Case2025, summary level).
3. **Data merging.** Export the CBCT data (DICOM) and the surface scan (STL) and superimpose them in planning software; implant planning software is most often used (Nair et al, 2021; DentJ2023, summary level).
4. **Virtual planning.** Plan a virtual bur path to the canal: entry point, angulation, depth and sleeve position, aiming for straight-line access; virtual files may be placed to check the path (Nair et al, 2021; DentJ2023, summary level; Cohen figure legend).
5. **Guide fabrication.** The template, with a metal sleeve of the planned diameter, is 3D-printed (Nair et al, 2021). Narrow burs are used (0.75 mm in one series, summary level).
6. **Try-in.** Check seating, stability and clearance for the handpiece and bur on the tooth or model [RECALL].
7. **Clinical access.** Anaesthesia and isolation as far as the guide allows; seat the guide; drill through the sleeve with the guide-specific long bur to the planned depth, with irrigation to cool the bur [RECALL].
8. **Canal location.** Remove the guide; explore the canal with a small K-file coated with chelating agent to confirm patency, using short strokes (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).
9. **Completion.** Conventional glide path, cleaning, shaping, disinfection and obturation, with an interim and final coronal seal [RECALL].
10. **Review.** Postoperative periapical image and follow-up [RECALL].

**Sequence shown in Cohen's figure** (Nair et al, 2021): CBCT slice, volumetric rendering, virtual files, printed guide, accessed tooth.

## 5. Procedure: dynamic navigation, in outline

### Explanation

The textbooks do not describe dynamic navigation for access. A search summary of a systematic review reports six studies, five of them on dynamic navigation for access cavities and one on fibre-post removal, concluding that navigation showed increased accuracy compared with the freehand technique (Jonaityte2022, summary level) [LIT]. A thesis summary reports that in a systematic review of teeth with pulp canal obliteration, dynamic navigation improved the accuracy of canal location in all three included studies compared with freehand access, with no study comparing dynamic with static navigation (Bradley2025, summary level) [LIT]. An integrative review summarised in the same search stated that dynamic navigation outperformed static navigation, but this is a lower-quality source and is not accepted here [LIT, summary only]. The workflow is [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Dynamic navigation: outline** [RECALL]
1. CBCT with a registration marker (fiducial) or marker-free registration.
2. Virtual plan in the navigation software.
3. Calibration of the handpiece and tracking device; registration of the patient to the scan.
4. Real-time drilling guided on screen, with the path adjustable during the procedure.
5. Verification, then conventional treatment as in section 4.

**Evidence.** Reviews summarised in the literature report increased accuracy compared with freehand access, including in pulp canal obliteration (Jonaityte2022; Bradley2025; summary level). No study in the thesis review compared dynamic with static navigation (Bradley2025, summary level).

## 6. Accuracy and outcomes

### Explanation

Nair et al quote a study of calcified teeth in which the guided approach detected more canals (91.7% against 41.7%) and lost less tooth substance (49.9 against 9.8, printed with the unit "mm", probably a printing error for a volume) (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. The book's own reference for this study is a comparison of substance loss using 3D-printed teeth, so this is laboratory evidence (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch say guided techniques have been shown to be a valid alternative to conventional techniques in severe calcification and anomalous teeth (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

From search summaries: a small in vitro study reported angular deviations of 1.40° to 10.60°, largest in deeper canal systems, and another a mean tip deviation of about 0.30 mm with a mean angular deviation of 3.62° [LIT, summary only; the studies were not identified]. A 2026 prospective study of 60 teeth with pulp canal obliteration, as summarised, reported canal location of 96.7% with guidance against 73.3% conventionally, with less negotiation time, less dentin removal and fewer procedural complications, while noting that clinical effectiveness has yet to be demonstrated and that the evidence is largely case based (Prospective2026, summary level) [LIT]. A search summary also reported that a conventional microscope-based series achieved full working length in 90% of cases with an 80% success rate at 3 years; this is a comparator, not a guided result [LIT, summary only]. Dynamic navigation deviations of about 1.2 mm and 2.3° come from a systematic review of endodontic surgery and should not be applied to coronal access (DiSpirito2025, summary level) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Reported results**
- Canal detection in calcified teeth: 91.7% guided against 41.7% conventional; tooth substance loss lower with guidance (Nair et al, 2021); laboratory study on printed teeth.
- A 2026 prospective study of 60 teeth with obliteration: canal location 96.7% against 73.3% (Prospective2026, summary level; verify).
- Deviation in in vitro studies: roughly 0.3 mm at the tip and 3.6° in one study; 1.4° to 10.6° in another, larger in deep canals [LIT, summary only; verify].
- Case series of calcified canals report safe, accurate access with less tooth removal (Case2025, summary level).

**Interpretation.** Results are favourable, but the study types are laboratory, case series and small prospective studies; high-level evidence is lacking (Nair et al, 2021).

## 7. Advantages and limitations

### Explanation

The advantages follow from the evidence above: more canals found, less dentin lost, shorter working time in series, and a lower perforation risk when the path is correct [LIT, summary] and [RECALL]. The limitations are in the search summaries: static navigation is limited in posterior teeth and curved canals (Guided2025, summary level); limited mouth opening can prevent the guide and a long bur fitting; thin posterior dentin walls, poor visibility and root curvature raise the perforation risk; and one case report linked a perforation to inaccurate guide fixation and manual merging of the scans [LIT, summary only]. Cost, radiation from CBCT, and time are inherent [RECALL]. The textbook states only that high-level evidence is lacking (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages**
- Higher canal detection in calcified teeth (Nair et al, 2021).
- Less dentin removed, so the tooth is stronger (Nair et al, 2021; pericervical dentin argument, see C4).
- Reduced perforation risk and chair time when correctly planned [RECALL; case series summary].
- Useful in anomalous teeth such as dens evaginatus (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).

**Limitations**
- Evidence is mainly in vitro and case based (Nair et al, 2021).
- Limited in curved canals and posterior teeth (Guided2025, summary level).
- Limited mouth opening prevents seating the guide and a long bur [LIT, summary only].
- Errors from guide fixation, scan merging and planning can cause perforation [LIT, summary only].
- Needs CBCT (radiation), software, printing, cost and training [RECALL].
- Not a substitute for knowledge of canal anatomy and conventional skills.

## 8. Related uses and the conventional comparator

### Explanation

Nair et al mention 3D-printed guides in endodontic surgery, where a study reported a deviation from the target of 1.743 mm with guides against 2.638 mm freehand (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]; this is guided endodontic surgery, a separate use. The conventional alternative for calcified canals, described by Gutmann and Fan, is a magnified search after the chamber is completed and its floor cleaned and dried, using floor colour change, a fibre-optic light, 1% methylene blue and bleeding points, then a small K-file with chelating agent for patency (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri adds the champagne-bubble test, long-shank burs and ultrasonic tips (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Guided endodontic surgery.** Templates guide the osteotomy and apical localisation; one study reported deviation of 1.743 mm with guides against 2.638 mm freehand (Nair et al, 2021).

**Conventional alternative to guided access** (Gutmann and Fan, 2021; Sikri, 2019): complete and dry the chamber floor; use colour change, transillumination, methylene blue, bleeding points and the champagne-bubble test; ultrasonic tips or long-shank burs to remove dentin; a small K-file with chelating agent to confirm patency. Guided access is for cases where this fails, or where the dentin cost of freehand searching is too high.

## 9. Relation to access principles

### Explanation

The principles of access in C1 (straight-line access, conservation of tooth structure, locating all orifices) are the same goals guided endodontics tries to reach in difficult teeth. Setzer and Walsch caution that the evidence for extremely minimised access in general is experimental and that the burden of proof remains (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC], whereas guided techniques in severe calcification are described as a valid alternative (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. The two statements are compatible: guidance justifies a minimal access in a specific indication, not a general policy.

#### ✎ Exam core

Guided access serves the objectives of access: it conserves sound structure, locates the orifice and gives a direct path (Gutmann and Fan, 2021). It is justified for severe calcification and anomalous teeth (Setzer and Walsch, 2025), not as a routine access for every tooth, where the evidence for minimal access remains experimental (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).

## 9A. Factors that affect accuracy, and checking it

### Explanation

The textbooks give no list of error sources for guided endodontics. The factors below are standard in guided-surgery practice and are [RECALL]; the search summaries support only the general points that guide fixation and manual merging of scans can cause error and that deeper canals show larger deviations [LIT, summary only]. Nair et al note that guided surgery brought precision (deviation of 1.743 mm with guides against 2.638 mm freehand) and that CBCT planning helps identify anatomical structures (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sources of error** [RECALL]
- *Imaging*: large voxel size or large field of view, patient movement, metal artefact.
- *Merging*: poor superimposition of DICOM and STL data, or manual alignment errors [LIT, summary only].
- *Design and printing*: printer accuracy, sleeve position, play between bur and sleeve, material distortion.
- *Seating*: an unstable or incompletely seated guide, or movement of the patient or guide during drilling [LIT, summary only].
- *Operator*: bur not fully seated in the sleeve, excessive pressure, overheating, inadequate irrigation.
- *Anatomy*: curved or deep canals show larger deviations [LIT, summary only].

**Checking accuracy** [RECALL]: superimpose a post-operative CBCT on the plan and measure the deviation at the entry point, at the end point and in angulation. Clinically, confirm with a file and a periapical image before conventional shaping.

## 9B. Pitfalls and their management

### Explanation

None of the textbooks lists complications of guided access. A search summary mentions root perforation linked to inaccurate guide fixation and scan merging, and notes that perforation risk stays high near the furcation even with a microscope [LIT, summary only]. The management below is [RECALL] and follows the general principles of perforation and procedural-error management.

#### ✎ Exam core

| Problem | Cause | Management [RECALL] |
|---|---|---|
| Guide does not seat | Undercuts, scan or print error, interference from adjacent teeth | Do not force; relieve the guide or remake it |
| Bur binds in the sleeve | Heat, debris, bur-sleeve mismatch | Stop, cool, clean; resume only if the bur is free |
| Canal not reached at planned depth | Planning or merging error, guide movement | Stop; take a periapical image; re-plan or revert to a magnified freehand search (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) |
| Perforation | Wrong trajectory, inaccurate fixation or merging [LIT, summary only] | Seal at once with a repair material, document, and review |
| Overheating of the root | No cooling or irrigation through a long bur | Irrigate and use intermittent drilling |
| Missed canal after the first canal is found | Anatomy not fully assessed | Re-examine the CBCT; remember that access is only the first step |

## 9C. Practical and ethical considerations

### Explanation

These points are [RECALL] and follow from the method.

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Radiation**: a limited-volume CBCT is justified only when the benefit outweighs the dose, according to the ALARA principle; explain this to the patient [RECALL].
- **Consent**: explain that guided access is still an emerging technique with limited long-term evidence (Nair et al, 2021), and that failure to reach the canal may require conversion to another method.
- **Cost and time**: scanning, planning and printing add cost and a pre-treatment visit; this suits elective rather than emergency care [RECALL].
- **Training**: the operator must still be able to complete access, shaping and obturation conventionally (Gutmann and Fan, 2021 for the conventional search).
- **Documentation**: keep the plan, guide design and post-operative images [RECALL].

**Case-selection checklist** [RECALL]
1. Is there a pulpal or periapical indication for treatment?
2. Has freehand access under magnification failed, or is its dentin cost too high?
3. Is the canal straight enough in the accessed portion for a rigid bur path?
4. Is mouth opening adequate for the guide and a long bur?
5. Is there enough tooth structure to seat a stable guide?
6. Are CBCT, planning software, printing and operator training available?

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: guided access finds calcified canals more reliably than freehand access.** *Textbook:* 91.7% against 41.7% in the study quoted by Nair et al (2021), whose reference is a laboratory study on printed teeth; Setzer and Walsch call it a valid alternative (2025). *Literature (summary level):* a 2026 prospective study of 60 teeth reports 96.7% against 73.3%; a thesis review found improved location in three dynamic-navigation studies. *Assessment:* the direction is consistent; the studies are laboratory, case-series or small prospective studies, and none of the primary papers was read here. *Limitation:* these data do not show survival of the treated teeth. [Likely]

**Claim 2: guidance preserves more dentin.** *Textbook:* lower substance loss quoted by Nair et al (2021), with the unit problem noted. *Literature:* a systematic review summary reports that dynamic navigation preserved the most dentin but that the evidence is thin. *Assessment:* plausible and consistent, mainly laboratory. It supports the pericervical-dentin argument of C4 only indirectly, because the books also report that the benefit of minimal access for fracture resistance is unproven (Setzer and Walsch, 2025). [Likely]

**Claim 3: dynamic navigation is more accurate than static guidance.** One integrative review summary says so, whereas the thesis review found no study comparing the two, and the 2025 narrative review describes different strengths and limits for each. *Conclusion:* not established; I cannot rank them from these sources. [Guessing]

**Overall.** Guided access is a reasonable, selective option for severe calcification and anomalous teeth when equipment and training exist; its effect on long-term outcome is unproven.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Inputs: CBCT plus intraoral scan; software merges them; printed guide with a sleeve (static) or tracked handpiece (dynamic).
- Indications: obliterated canals, dens evaginatus and invaginatus, selected conservative access, guided surgery.
- Cohen's quoted study: 91.7% against 41.7% canal detection; laboratory.
- Limitations: curved canals, posterior teeth, mouth opening, cost, CBCT dose.
- Evidence: in vitro, case series, small prospective studies; high-level evidence lacking.
- Cohen Ch.7 also uses "guided endodontic technique" as a name for minimal access.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Describing it as routine; the books call it emerging with evidence lacking.
- Omitting the merging of CBCT and intraoral scan.
- Confusing guided access with guided endodontic surgery.
- Quoting accuracy figures without saying they come from laboratory or small studies.
- Confusing "guided endodontics" (navigation) with "minimally invasive access" (Cohen uses the term for both).
