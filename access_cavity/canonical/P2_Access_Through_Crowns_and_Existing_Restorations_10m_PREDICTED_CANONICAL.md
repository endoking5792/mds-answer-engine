## 1. Remove or retain?

### Explanation

Cohen says removing defective restorations and caries before entering the chamber is essential because hidden caries or fractures are found and restorability can be judged, and sometimes treatment stops in favour of crown lengthening or a bonded build-up (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. The books differ on intact restorations. Baumann and Beer say that restorations that look intact on radiographs can be left, but that only complete removal shows the extent of caries and poorly sealed areas (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch's checklist says "if possible, do not leave existing restorations" (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC], and Sikri says a crown should be removed if the canals cannot be found through it (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Setzer and Walsch list situations in which removal may be prohibited: irreversible pulpitis after a newly cemented crown or fixed partial denture; a crown anchoring a removable partial denture; large restorations that would need replacement; and teeth of questionable restorability once the restoration is removed (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**General rule.** Remove defective restorations and caries first (Gutmann and Fan, 2021); open the chamber only after decay is removed (Setzer and Walsch, 2025). Removal reveals hidden caries and cracks and allows restorability to be judged (Gutmann and Fan, 2021). A radiographically sound restoration may be left (Baumann and Beer, 2010); the checklist says avoid leaving restorations where possible (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).

**Reasons not to remove** (Setzer and Walsch, 2025): irreversible pulpitis after a newly cemented crown or bridge; crown anchoring a removable partial denture; large restoration costly to replace; questionable restorability.

## 2. Technique through a retained crown

### Explanation

Setzer and Walsch say any existing restoration may not allow a complete inspection of the hard tissue underneath, so margins must be checked on bitewings and clinically, and if existing decay cannot be removed, the restoration must be removed (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. A full-coverage restoration removes the cusps, grooves and ridges that guide the bur, so radiographs and the crown margins are inspected for rotation or inclination to avoid perforation; the dental microscope has made this access easier (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. Sikri notes that crowns mask the orientation of the tooth and that removing the crown reduces the chance of cutting sound dentin and of perforation (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer say metal or ceramic restorations must often be drilled through, which may need special burs (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Sikri names transmetal burs for amalgam, cast metal or metal copings (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Through a retained crown**
1. Check margins with bitewings and clinically (Setzer and Walsch, 2025); a bitewing is also advised for teeth with large restorations (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
2. Study the radiograph and the root at the crown margin for rotation or inclination (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
3. Use magnification and illumination (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
4. Use burs designed for metal or ceramic (Baumann and Beer, 2010); transmetal burs for amalgam, cast metal or metal copings (Sikri, 2019).
5. Remember that cusps, grooves and ridges are lost, and the crown can mask the tooth's orientation (Setzer and Walsch, 2025; Sikri, 2019).
6. If the canals cannot be found, remove the crown (Sikri, 2019); this reduces sound dentin loss and perforation risk (Sikri, 2019).

## 3. After access: build-up and seal

### Explanation

Setzer and Walsch say a build-up may be needed before access when isolation of a tooth with little structure is not possible (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]. Sikri describes the "donut" technique of building walls around the access with adhesive composite to retain the dam (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer note that a composite build-up is often the interim restoration of choice and can serve as the definitive post-endodontic restoration (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Restorability and build-up**
- If isolation is not possible, build up the walls before access (Setzer and Walsch, 2025); the "donut" technique uses adhesive composite around the access (Sikri, 2019).
- Adhesive composite is often the interim restoration of choice and may serve as the definitive restoration (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Once the old restoration is removed, an interim restoration gives a stable coronal reference point for working length and protects against fracture and salivary contamination between visits (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Setzer and Walsch illustrate a conservative access through an existing coronal restoration in a mandibular first molar (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
- If restorability is doubtful after removal, consider crown lengthening before proceeding (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Remove defective restorations and caries before opening the chamber.
- Intact restorations: book opinion differs (leave vs avoid leaving).
- Crowns remove landmarks and mask orientation; use radiographs and magnification.
- Special burs for metal and ceramic.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
- Decision flowchart: crown present, decay and margins assessed, restorable, remove or access through, build-up. Earns marks for logic.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Saying all restorations must always be removed; list the exceptions.
- Forgetting the loss of landmarks and the perforation risk.
- Ignoring restorability.
