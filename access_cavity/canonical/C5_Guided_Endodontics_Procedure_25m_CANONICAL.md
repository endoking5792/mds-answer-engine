## 1. Definition and types

### Explanation

Nair et al describe 3D-guided surgical and non-surgical endodontic treatment as an emerging application that uses guides made with the aid of CBCT images and intraoral scanning of the area of interest (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. Gutmann and Fan, in the access chapter, advocate a guided access technique for the coronal entry into teeth with partially or completely calcified chambers or obstructed canals (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC], and list "the guided endodontic technique" and "microguided technique" among the names given to minimally invasive access (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch describe guided techniques as a targeted, minimised access (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

The split into static (printed guide) and dynamic (real-time navigation) systems is not described in the three textbook sources here; it is [RECALL]. A web-search summary of a 2025 narrative review reports the same split, with static guides very accurate in straight canals but limited in posterior teeth and curved canals, and dynamic navigation more flexible but dependent on training and costly equipment (Guided2025, summary level) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Guided endodontics uses a CBCT scan, often combined with an intraoral surface scan, to plan the path of a bur to a canal in a virtual model, and then transfers that path to the mouth with a printed guide (static) or with a real-time tracking system (dynamic) (Nair et al, 2021 for the guide; static/dynamic types [RECALL]).

**Main use.** Teeth with calcified canals and teeth with anatomical variations of the root canal system (Nair et al, 2021); partially or completely calcified chambers (Gutmann and Fan, 2021); severe pulp canal calcification and dens evaginatus (Setzer and Walsch, 2025).

## 2. The procedure, step by step

### Explanation

Nair et al describe the template as created by superimposing CBCT images on intraoral scanned images, with sleeves placed in the virtual plan to create a 3D-printed guide with a directed sleeve, usually made with implant planning software (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. The figure legend in the same chapter shows the sequence for a block of two molars: 2D rendering of the CBCT, volumetric rendering of the teeth, lateral view of the teeth with virtual files in position, the printed guide, and an occlusal view of the accessed tooth (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch say that identifying all canals before the procedure with CBCT is the way proponents of contracted access mitigate missed canals (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC].

The rest of a clinical protocol is not given in these books. The sequence below follows common practice and is [RECALL]: diagnosis and case selection; CBCT with a small field of view; intraoral or model scan; superimposition (DICOM and STL files) in planning software; virtual planning of the bur trajectory, depth and sleeve position; printing and try-in of the guide for fit and stability; local anaesthesia and rubber dam where the guide allows; drilling through the sleeve with a long, guide-specific bur to the planned depth; removal of the guide; confirmation of canal patency; conventional cleaning, shaping and obturation; and a follow-up image.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Procedure**
1. **Case selection**: calcified chamber or canals, or an anomalous tooth (Nair et al, 2021; Setzer and Walsch, 2025).
2. **Imaging**: a CBCT scan of the area, and an intraoral scan of the teeth (Nair et al, 2021).
3. **Virtual planning**: superimpose the CBCT and intraoral scan; plan the bur path to the canal in the software, with sleeve position and depth (Nair et al, 2021; planning software usually implant software). Bur depth and trajectory details [RECALL].
4. **Guide fabrication**: a 3D-printed template with a directed sleeve (Nair et al, 2021). Try-in for fit [RECALL].
5. **Clinical access**: the guide seated on the teeth, a long bur used through the sleeve to the planned depth [RECALL], exposing the canal.
6. **Completion**: remove the guide, check patency with a small file, then conventional cleaning, shaping and obturation (Gutmann and Fan, 2021 for canal negotiation; protocol [RECALL]).
7. **Review** with a periapical image [RECALL].

The legend in Cohen shows the order: CBCT slice, volumetric rendering, virtual files, printed guide, accessed tooth (Nair et al, 2021).

## 3. Advantages and limitations

### Explanation

Nair et al quote a study of calcified teeth in which the guided approach detected more canals (91.7% against 41.7%) and cut tooth substance loss (49.9 against 9.8, reported with the unit "mm" in the book) (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. The unit is probably a printing error because a volume loss would be in mm³ [Likely]; the book's own reference list gives the study as a comparison of substance loss using 3D-printed teeth, so this is laboratory evidence (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. Nair et al also state that studies with a high level of evidence are lacking, with several in vitro studies and case reports published (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and Walsch say guided techniques have been shown to be a valid alternative to conventional techniques in severe calcification, dens evaginatus and other anomalous teeth (Setzer and Walsch, 2025) [SRC]; they cite studies (their references 39–42, not opened here) for this.

Literature summaries seen in the web search report that evidence is mostly in vitro or case based, with an umbrella review of systematic reviews, and that static guidance is limited by curved canals and by mouth opening in posterior teeth, though the details were not verified (Guided2025; Umbrella2024; summary level) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages**
- Higher canal detection in calcified teeth: 91.7% against 41.7% in the study quoted by Nair et al (2021).
- Less tooth substance lost (reported 9.8 against 49.9) (Nair et al, 2021).
- Advocated for coronal entry into partially or completely calcified chambers (Gutmann and Fan, 2021).

**Limitations**
- High-level evidence is lacking; in vitro studies and case reports dominate (Nair et al, 2021).
- Requires CBCT (radiation), scanning, planning software and printing [RECALL]; cost and training [LIT, summary].
- Rigid guides suit straight canals better than curved ones, and may be limited by mouth opening in posterior teeth [LIT, summary only].
- Not a substitute for knowledge of anatomy; navigation errors are possible [RECALL].

**Position.** A valid, selective option in severe calcification and anomalous teeth (Setzer and Walsch, 2025), not a routine method (Nair et al, 2021).

## 4. The conventional comparator and the surgical use

### Explanation

Guided access is chosen against the conventional, magnified freehand search for calcified canals. Gutmann and Fan describe that approach: complete the chamber preparation first and clean and dry the floor, then search for orifices (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]; use the chamber floor colour (darker than the walls, with lighter developmental grooves), a fibre-optic light through the CEJ, staining with 1% methylene blue and searching for bleeding points, all enhanced by magnification; and once a canal is found, a small K-file coated with a chelating agent is introduced to determine patency (Gutmann and Fan, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri adds that careful use of magnification and the champagne-bubble test helps, and that ultrasonic tips trough obstructions (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Nair et al also mention 3D-printed guides in endodontic surgery, where a study reported deviations from the target location of 1.743 mm with guides against 2.638 mm freehand (Nair et al, 2021) [SRC]; this is a separate use from guided access.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Comparator: freehand access under magnification** (Gutmann and Fan, 2021)
- Complete and dry the chamber floor before the search.
- Use colour change: floor darker than walls, grooves lighter.
- Fibre-optic transillumination, 1% methylene blue and bleeding points.
- A small K-file with chelating agent to confirm patency.
- Ultrasonic tips to trough dentin (Sikri, 2019).

| | Freehand | Guided |
|---|---|---|
| Needs CBCT and printing | No | Yes (Nair et al, 2021) |
| Canal detection in calcified teeth | 41.7% in the quoted study | 91.7% (Nair et al, 2021) |
| Operator skill | High | Planning skill and equipment |
| Evidence | Established practice | Lacking high-level studies (Nair et al, 2021) |

**Related use.** Guided templates are also used in endodontic surgery to place the osteotomy; one study reported deviation of 1.743 mm with guides and 2.638 mm freehand (Nair et al, 2021). This is guided endodontic surgery, not guided access.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim:** guided access is more accurate and conservative than freehand access in calcified teeth. The textbook quotes one study with a higher detection rate and less substance loss, and its own reference title indicates a laboratory study on printed teeth (Nair et al, 2021). Setzer and Walsch say guided techniques are a valid alternative (Setzer and Walsch, 2025). A web-search summary of the umbrella review points the same way but also describes the evidence as mostly in vitro or case based (Umbrella2024; summary only). **Strength:** weak to moderate, largely laboratory. **Conclusion:** guided access is reasonable for severe calcification when equipment and training exist; it cannot yet be said to improve tooth survival. [Likely]

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Describing the procedure without the CBCT and intraoral scan merging step.
- Presenting it as routine; the books call it emerging with evidence lacking.
- Confusing guided endodontics with guided endodontic surgery (osteotomy guides).
- Giving accuracy figures without saying they come from laboratory studies.
