## STUDY MODE

### S1. Terms [orientation]
- MID is used here for minimally invasive / minimal intervention dentistry, the two wordings of the question. Sikri says the two terms describe different concepts: minimal intervention rests on prevention and control of the cause, and minimal invasive dentistry, in his words, "is merely a component" of that care plan (Sikri, 2020).
- Pickard's Guide defines both terms separately: minimum intervention care is team-based, patient-centred care, and minimally invasive operative dentistry is the repair part (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Sikri heads his four core principles and seven components "minimal invasive", while the FDI list that follows is headed "principles of minimal intervention" (Sikri, 2020).
- "Extension for prevention" has changed to "prevention for extension" (Sikri, 2020). Black's classification described the final restoration location, not the lesion (Mount et al, 2016). Singh calls Black's "extension for prevention" the "golden statement" (Singh, 2020).

### S2. Principle [basic science]
- The basis is to move the clinical focus from restoring teeth damaged by caries to preventing disease at its early stages (Sikri, 2020).
- "Restoration of a lesion is not caries treatment: it is defect repair" (Mount et al, 2016); "drilling and filling teeth does not cure caries" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- A restoration may start a restorative cycle of repeated replacement (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant describes a medical model of caries management (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Sikri's four core principles: recognise risk factors; minimise them; remineralise incipient lesions; repair, using the least tooth structure and repairing old restorations rather than replacing them (Sikri, 2020).

### S3. "Golden triangle" [terminology]
The term is in the question but is not defined in the textbooks read, and no source names three corners for it. As a synthesis: Pickard's Guide shows a four-phase care cycle of identify, control and prevent, restore minimally invasively, and recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sikri lists seven components from early diagnosis to repair rather than replacement (Sikri, 2020).

### S4. Detection and risk [diagnosis]
- Sikri scores ICDAS II from 0 to 6 (Sikri, 2020). Sikri grades risk low, moderate or high; his high-risk list includes two or more carious lesions in the last year, among other features (Sikri, 2020). Pickard's Guide defines high risk as two or more new, progressing or restored lesions in the previous 2 years, and says a simpler low or high division is usually more than sufficient (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- A sharp probe is contraindicated because it can cavitate a non-cavitated lesion (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S5. Non-operative and microinvasive techniques [treatment]
- Fluoride varnish is 22 600 ppm F (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Silver diamine fluoride: Sikri gives 38%, applied twice yearly, which "might be able to arrest" caries (Sikri, 2020); Craig's describes a solution of about 30% and says there is growing evidence that caries is arrested, with staining of tooth structure (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); Sturdevant reports that up to 50% of arrested lesions were reactivated by 24 months after one application (Ritter et al, 2019). Resin infiltration is a microinvasive treatment that arrests early lesions without drilling (Sikri, 2020).

### S6. Operative techniques [treatment]
- Site and size: size 0 is the earliest identifiable lesion, to be recorded and, if possible, the cause eliminated and the lesion remineralised and healed; size 1 is minimal surface cavitation with dentine involvement (Mount et al, 2016).
- Selective removal: where a peripheral seal can be achieved and pulp symptoms are not present, caries-affected dentine may stay over the pulp; chronic pulp symptoms need complete excavation; caries-infected dentine is never left at the enamel-dentine junction (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sturdevant leaves soft dentine within about 1 mm of the pulp (Ritter et al, 2019).
- ART: hand instruments, then a glass ionomer restoration, first tried in the mid 1980s (Sikri, 2020).
- Hall technique: a preformed crown cemented with glass ionomer over a carious primary molar, with no excavation (Sikri, 2020).
- Rotary designs: fissurotomy, minibox and tunnel (Sikri, 2020). Air abrasion, chemo-mechanical gels such as Carisolv, and lasers are the non-rotary and other options (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020).

### S7. Repair [treatment]
Pickard's Guide gives the 5 Rs: review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace, with replacement indicated when more than half of the restoration has failed or the tooth is compromised (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri also says repair should be preferred to replacement, as each replacement loses more tooth (Sikri, 2020).

### S8. Critical evidence note: tunnel preparation [prognosis]
The books differ, and the statements are given side by side, not ranked.
- Sikri: studies "could not show promising results", marginal ridge strength is not maintained, and tunnels are indicated in small lesions, especially in deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020).
- Mount et al: a tunnel (internal occlusal fossa) design for Site 2, Size 1, with one glass ionomer case satisfactory at 12 years (Mount et al, 2016).
- Summitt: 21% of tunnel restorations failed in 7 years against none of the slot restorations; 46% against 76% survival in another study; tunnel restorations "are not indicated and should be avoided" (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Sturdevant says the tunnel remains controversial and is not supported in that textbook (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Pickard's Guide says tunnelling beneath the marginal ridge is only successful clinically if the lesion is sufficiently small, with a high risk of fracture of the weakened ridge (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S9. Web check, abstract level [verification]
Tyas et al (2000), abstract only, review minimal intervention dentistry and question extension for prevention. Schwendicke et al (2016), abstract only, recommend selective removal of carious tissue to firm or soft dentine depending on lesion depth.

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 42 minutes.)*

### 1. Definition and terminology
- MID here means minimally invasive / minimal intervention dentistry, the two wordings of the question. Sikri says they describe different concepts: minimal intervention "deals with the cause of dental disease" and rests on prevention and control, while minimal invasive dentistry is in his words "merely a component" of that care plan (Sikri, 2020).
- Pickard's Guide defines minimum intervention care (team-delivered, individualised, patient-centred) and minimally invasive operative dentistry (repair to maintain pulp vitality, function and aesthetics) separately (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- "Extension for prevention" has changed to "prevention for extension" (Sikri, 2020). Black's classification described the final restoration location, not the lesion (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Principle
- Shift the focus from restoring teeth damaged by caries to preventing disease at its early stages (Sikri, 2020).
- Restoration of a lesion is not caries treatment; it is defect repair (Mount et al, 2016). "Drilling and filling teeth does not cure caries" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- A restoration may start a restorative cycle of repeated replacement (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Four core principles (Sikri heads them "minimal invasive"): recognise risk factors; minimise them (diet, fluid balance, pH); remineralise incipient lesions with fluoride or CPP-ACP; repair with minimum tooth structure, repairing old restorations rather than replacing them (Sikri, 2020).
- FDI principles, as Sikri lists them under "minimal intervention": patient education and motivation; modification of oral micro-organisms; remineralisation of early lesions; minimal preparation of cavitated lesions; repair of defective restorations (Sikri, 2020).

### 3. "Golden triangle"
The term appears in the question but is not defined in the textbooks read, and no three-cornered figure for MID is given in them. Singh uses "golden statement" for a different idea, Black's "extension for prevention" (Singh, 2020). The framework below is a synthesis from the cited books, not a quoted definition.

| Phase of care | Content | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify | detection, risk assessment, diagnosis, care planning | (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| 2. Control and prevent | home care and team-applied active care | (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| 3. Restore | minimally invasive repair | (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| 4. Recall | review according to caries susceptibility | (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |

Sikri lists seven components: early diagnosis; caries risk assessment; arresting existing lesions; remineralisation; minimal invasive restoration; resin infiltration; repair rather than replacement (Sikri, 2020).

### 4. Techniques

Further points:
- **Early approximal lesion:** with a demineralised subsurface and intact surface, surgical treatment is neither necessary nor ethical (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Selective removal:** affected dentine may stay over the pulp where a peripheral seal is achieved and pulp symptoms are not present; chronic pulp symptoms need complete excavation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sturdevant removes caries to within about 1 mm of the pulp in deep lesions (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 5. Repair rather than replacement
- Each replacement loses more tooth structure (Sikri, 2020).
- Replace when more than 50% of the restoration has failed or the tooth is compromised; otherwise repair is more conservative (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- The decision rests on caries risk (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Materials
Adhesive materials need no mechanical retention; set glass ionomer can be recharged with fluoride (Sikri, 2020). See Q09.

### 7. Advantages and limitations
- Smaller cavities: easier void-free placement, better moisture control and seal, stronger crown, simpler maintenance (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Infection control with remineralising agents can reduce restoration placement to approximately 50% (Sikri, 2020; no study cited in the text).
- Operative MID alone does not cure disease and needs patient responsibility (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- MID can become "just a new buzzword" (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- Tunnel, books side by side: Sikri, marginal ridge strength not maintained, indicated in small lesions especially in deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020); Mount et al, a tunnel design for Site 2 Size 1, one glass ionomer case satisfactory at 12 years (Mount et al, 2016); Summitt, 21% failed in 7 years against none of the slots, "not indicated and should be avoided" (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant, not supported (Ritter et al, 2019); Pickard, works only if the lesion is small, high fracture risk (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 8. Conclusion
MID, as Sikri frames it, treats the disease first (minimal intervention) and restores minimally (minimal invasive), with repair in preference to replacement (Sikri, 2020; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- MID covers both wordings; Sikri calls minimal invasive dentistry "merely a component" of minimal intervention care (Sikri, 2020).
- Four core principles: recognise, minimise, remineralise, repair (Sikri, 2020).
- Golden triangle: not defined in the books read; give the four-phase care cycle as a synthesis (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Infected dentine never left at the junction; affected dentine may stay over the pulp only with a peripheral seal and no pulp symptoms (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Hall technique: no excavation, glass ionomer luting (Sikri, 2020).
- 5 Rs of repair (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
