## STUDY MODE

### S1. Definition and terminology [definition]
- **Operative component:** Pickard's Guide defines minimally invasive operative dentistry as the part of restorative dentistry that repairs and/or restores damaged and defective tooth structure directly, to maintain pulp vitality, function and aesthetics (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Care philosophy:** the same book defines minimum intervention oral healthcare separately, as a team approach to individualised patient-centred care built around detection and risk assessment, non-operative control, minimally invasive repair, and review and recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). It adds that drilling and filling teeth does not cure caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Sikri's wording:** the terms minimal intervention and minimal invasive are used interchangeably although they describe different clinical concepts; minimal intervention deals with the cause of the disease, and minimal invasive dentistry is merely a component of the minimal intervention care plan (Sikri, 2020). Sikri adds that "ultraconservative" and "microdentistry" are synonymous with minimal invasive dentistry (Sikri, 2020).
- **Kidd and Fejerskov's wording:** minimal intervention dentistry stresses a preventive, caries control philosophy, with the aim of healthy teeth for life and minimal need for operative intervention (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- **FDI review (abstract only):** Tyas and colleagues state that the approach arose from a better understanding of caries and from adhesive materials, and that demineralised, non-cavitated enamel and dentine can be remineralised (Tyas et al, 2000).
- **Origin of the term:** the books read do not state who coined it (not stated in the books read).

### S2. Why the approach changed [background]
- **Black era:** Black's 1908 classification described the final restoration location, not the lesion, and became tied to cavity forms larger than necessary (Mount et al, 2016). In the first half of the twentieth century restorations were large and, when they failed and were replaced, became larger still (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Reversal of the rule:** Sikri writes that "extension for prevention" has changed to "prevention for extension" (Sikri, 2020). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia edition says the extension for prevention principle has virtually been eliminated because of fluoride, hygiene and diet education (Ritter et al, 2019). Modern Operative Dentistry says it has been largely abandoned (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020).
- **Restorative cycle:** Summitt notes that a restoration may be the start of a cycle of repeated replacement (Hilton et al, 2013). Kidd and Fejerskov say cutting a tooth weakens it and starts a restorative cycle (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- **Surgical to medical model:** Sturdevant says dentistry historically waited for cavitation and then restored, which failed to control the disease, and a medical model with individual risk assessment followed (Ritter et al, 2019).

### S3. Frameworks the books give [framework]
The books give different lists. They are shown side by side and are not ranked.
| Source | What it lists |
|---|---|
| Banerjee and Watson (2015) | four phases: identify; non-operative prevention and control; minimally invasive restoration; recall, review, maintenance (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Sikri (2020) | four core principles: recognise risk factors; minimise risk factors; remineralise incipient lesions; repair with minimum tooth sacrifice (Sikri, 2020) |
| Sikri (2020), as adopted by FDI | patient education and motivation; modification of oral micro-organisms; remineralisation of early lesions; minimal tooth preparation for cavitated lesions; repair of defective restorations (Sikri, 2020) |
| Summitt, 4th ed (2013) | patient-centred caries management: preventive and noninvasive options; operative and minimally invasive options; maintenance care with recall (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| Zeller et al (2019), CAMBRA | assess risk; reduce pathological factors; enhance protective factors; minimally invasive restorative care as indicated (Zeller et al, 2019) |
Sikri also lists seven components, from early diagnosis to repair rather than replacement (Sikri, 2020).

### S4. Detection, risk and activity [diagnosis]
- **Probe:** the Guide says sharp probes are contraindicated for caries detection because they can cavitate a non-cavitated lesion (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).- **Scoring systems side by side:** the Guide uses a simplified modified ICDAS of 0 to 4 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sikri and Summitt give ICDAS II with a caries score of 0 to 6 (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013). Kidd and Fejerskov write that ICDAS seems difficult to chart in the clinic and that they prefer the Nyvad criteria (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- **ICCMS:** Mount states that ICCMS merges the seven ICDAS codes into four (Mount et al, 2016); Pitts and Ekstrand describe ICCMS as integrating tooth and patient information including risk (Pitts and Ekstrand, 2013; abstract only).
- **High risk, as each book states it:** the Guide gives two or more new, progressing and/or restored carious lesions in the previous 2 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sikri gives two or more carious lesions in the last year among the high-risk features (Sikri, 2020).
- **Activity and recall:** active lesions demand management and inactive lesions need intervention only to re-establish aesthetics or function (Ekstrand and Braga, 2019); arrested lesions need no caries intervention except for aesthetics, function or risk for pulp death (Fontana and Huysmans, 2019). The Guide says recall should not be standardised at 6 months or any other period (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S5. Non-operative care [prevention]
- **Fluoride:** the Guide gives fluoride toothpaste of 1350 to 1500 ppm F and varnish of 22 600 ppm F (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Summitt also gives 22,600 ppm, and Sturdevant's table gives 22,500 ppm (Hilton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Sealants and infiltration:** Summitt says sealed lesions do not progress as long as the fissure remains sealed, and that sealants fail at 5% to 10% per year (Hilton et al, 2013). Sikri calls resin infiltration a microinvasive regime (Sikri, 2020).
- **Silver diamine fluoride:** Sikri and Sturdevant give a 38% solution (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019); Craig gives about 30% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Sturdevant reports that up to 50% of arrested lesions were reactivated by 24 months after one application (Ritter et al, 2019). See P10 for the full treatment.

### S6. Operative minimal intervention [technique]
- **Guide's definition of the operative approach:** excavation of unrepairable, diseased enamel and dentine only, modification of the walls, and restoration with adhesive materials (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Peripheral seal:** caries-infected dentine should never be retained at the enamel-dentine junction (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Endpoints, side by side:**
| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| Banerjee and Watson (2015) | no absolute correct or incorrect amount of caries to excavate; decision pragmatic and documented |
| Torres and Schwendicke (2020) | selective removal to firm dentine for shallow and moderately deep lesions; to soft dentine for deep lesions; non-selective removal to hard dentine not recommended any longer |
| Ritter et al (2019) | selective removal to a caries-free junction peripherally and soft dentine pulpally for deep lesions; growing evidence suggests temporising with re-entry does not contribute to improved outcomes |
| Kidd and Fejerskov (2016) | selective removal leaving soft dentine preferable if the tooth is symptomless and vital; complete removal with risk of pulp exposure "should be considered unethical"; the authors would not re-enter |
| Schwendicke et al (2016), abstract only | selective removal to firm dentine for shallow or moderately deep lesions; to soft dentine for deep lesions; stepwise an option in permanent teeth |
- **Stepwise re-entry intervals:** the Guide gives 6 to 9 months (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Modern Operative Dentistry gives 6 to 12 months (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020); Mount gives a transitional restoration of at least three weeks and no longer than six months (Mount et al, 2016).
- **ART, ITR, Hall:** ART removes caries with hand instruments and restores with glass ionomer (Sikri, 2020). Summitt describes interim therapeutic restorations as part of comprehensive care with glass ionomer (Hilton et al, 2013). Sikri describes Hall technique as preformed metal crowns cemented over carious primary molars without preparation or excavation (Sikri, 2020); Kidd and Fejerskov add no local anaesthetic and no caries removal, and state that clinical trials have shown excellent results (a textbook statement, evidence level: one textbook) (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Sturdevant's ART section ends that ART as a technique and material is not recommended in routine clinical use and is restricted to areas with limited access to dental treatment (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Cavity designs:** Mount's site-and-size description separates lesion description from cavity form (Mount et al, 2016); Sikri calls it the basis for minimal cavity preparation but subjective (Sikri, 2020). Tunnel preparation is treated differently by each book (see S10).

### S7. Repair rather than replacement [maintenance]
- **5Rs:** review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Rule:** when more than 50% of a restoration has failed or the tooth is compromised, replacement is indicated (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri notes that replacement causes more tooth loss each time (Sikri, 2020). Summitt reports that the attachment strength of new amalgam to old is only 30% to 60% of unrepaired amalgam (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S8. Materials [materials]
Adhesive materials need no retention features cut into sound tooth (Sikri, 2020). The Guide notes that amalgam retention requires undercuts and slots which remove tooth and is "not particularly minimally invasive" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Glass ionomer and composite details are in Q09 and Composite Q28.

### S9. Merits and limits [evaluation]
Merits: smaller cavities give easier void-free placement, better moisture control and seal and a stronger crown (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Limits: operative care alone does not cure disease and depends on patient responsibility (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); the approach can become "just a new buzzword" and the authors note no realistic fees for caries control in the health service at press time (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).

### S10. Critical evidence synthesis [evidence]
- **Statements the books report on caries removal (not graded):** partial caries removal reduced the risk of pulp exposure by 98% against complete excavation in deep lesions (two systematic reviews quoted in Hilton et al, 2013); Kidd and Fejerskov report that complete removal exposed the pulp in up to 53% (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); the Guide reports that one-stage partial removal gave significantly fewer pulp exposures than two-step stepwise (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Against this, only 17% of surveyed practitioners said they would stop and leave carious dentine (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Where books differ:** on calcium hydroxide, the Guide cannot suggest positive indications beneath adhesive restorations (Banerjee and Watson, 2015), Summitt's protocol uses calcium hydroxide then resin-modified glass ionomer (Hilton et al, 2013), and Sturdevant says use of calcium hydroxide after caries removal does not appear to improve outcomes (Ritter et al, 2019). On tunnels, each book as written: Mount calls the occlusal fossa (tunnel) approach the simplest and most conservative for an enamel lesion at least 3.00 mm apical to the marginal ridge crest, a most conservative approach, with glass ionomer, once a proximal lesion has reached dentine, and a desirable cavity design (Mount et al, 2016); Pickard's Guide says the tunnel beneath the marginal ridge is successful only if the lesion is sufficiently small, with a high risk of fracture of the weakened ridge (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sikri says tunnels are indicated in small lesions, especially in deciduous teeth, and that studies could not show promising results (Sikri, 2020); Summitt gives 21% failures at 7 years against none for slot restorations in one evaluation and concludes that tunnel restorations are not indicated and should be avoided (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant says the design remains controversial and is not supported in that textbook (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **ART survival, two books:** Phillips gives 99% at 1 year and 88% at 3 years for single-surface permanent restorations, and, in a group of children followed for 6.3 years, cumulative survival of 66.1% for ART against 57.0% for amalgam (Anusavice et al, 2013); Craig gives 93% against 62% for single- against multi-surface in primary teeth at 2 years, and 80% at 5 years for single-surface restorations in permanent teeth (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). These come from different studies and are not reconciled in the books.
- **Remineralisers and SDF:** Mount reports no long-term clinical evidence for CPP-ACP caries control (Mount et al, 2016); Slayton et al (2018), abstract only, recommend against 10% CPP-ACP (Slayton et al, 2018). Hill and Gillam quote reviews on SDF side by side: one says SDF is effective for caries arrest on cavitated lesions in primary teeth and root caries in the elderly, and a root-caries review reports no severe adverse effects; a single meta-analysis (Oliveira et al, 2019; primary dentition, four trials) reports at least one domain of unclear or high risk of bias in all studies and glass ionomer more effective than SDF at 12 months without a significant difference (Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **Risk assessment:** Summitt calls risk assessment inevitably a best guess (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant says past caries experience is the best predictor of future caries (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Sikri's 50% figure:** Sikri states that restoration placement can be reduced to approximately 50% with infection control and remineralising agents and cites no study for it in the text (Sikri, 2020).
- **Gap:** the books read give no trial of the whole minimal intervention pathway against conventional care (not stated in the books read).

### S11. Recent and future [advances]
Frencken et al (2012), abstract only, review detection, prevention, restorative and non-restorative treatment, and repair (Frencken et al, 2012). Resin infiltration is described as an emerging alternative (Hilton et al, 2013). Guide: an ideal detection device would detect biochemical activity before tissue damage (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 1. Definition, terminology and origin
- Minimally invasive operative dentistry: the part of restorative dentistry that repairs and/or restores damaged and defective tooth structure directly, to maintain pulp vitality, function and aesthetics (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Minimum intervention oral healthcare: a team approach to individualised patient-centred care, built on detection and risk assessment, non-operative control, minimally invasive repair, and review and recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Sikri: the two terms are used interchangeably but describe different concepts; minimal intervention deals with the cause of disease, and minimal invasive dentistry is a component of the minimal intervention plan (Sikri, 2020).
- Kidd and Fejerskov: a preventive, caries control philosophy aiming at healthy teeth for life and minimal need for operative intervention (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- Tyas et al (2000), abstract only: the approach arose from a better understanding of caries and from adhesive materials, and non-cavitated demineralised tissue can be remineralised (Tyas et al, 2000).
- Synonyms in Sikri: ultraconservative dentistry and microdentistry (Sikri, 2020). Who coined the term: not stated in the books read.
- Origin: Black's classification described the final restoration location and led to large cavities; in the first half of the twentieth century restorations became larger each time they failed and were replaced (Mount et al, 2016). "Extension for prevention" has changed to "prevention for extension" (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Philosophy: from the surgical to the medical model
- Dentistry waited for cavitation and then restored; this failed to control disease, so a medical model with individual risk assessment developed (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Restoration of a lesion is not caries treatment; it is defect repair (Mount et al, 2016). Drilling and filling does not cure caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Surgery on an intact surface over a demineralised subsurface is neither necessary nor ethical (Mount et al, 2016).
- A restoration may start a restorative cycle of repeated replacement (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Dental professionals can now primarily be physicians, and surgery can be minimally invasive if done with precision (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3. Framework and components (as each book lists them)
| Source | Components |
|---|---|
| Banerjee and Watson, 2015 | identify; non-operative control; minimally invasive restoration; recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Sikri, 2020 | recognise risk factors; minimise them; remineralise; repair (Sikri, 2020) |
| FDI principles as given by Sikri, 2020 | education and motivation; modify micro-organisms; remineralise early lesions; minimal preparation; repair defective restorations (Sikri, 2020) |
| Hilton et al, 2013 | preventive and noninvasive; operative and minimally invasive; maintenance with recall (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| Zeller et al, 2019 (CAMBRA) | assess risk; reduce pathological factors; enhance protective factors; minimally invasive restoration if needed (Zeller et al, 2019) |
- Sikri also lists seven components: early diagnosis; risk assessment; arrest of existing lesions; remineralisation; minimally invasive restoration; resin infiltration; repair rather than replacement (Sikri, 2020).

### 4. Detection, risk assessment and activity
- Detection: clean, dry teeth, good light, magnification and a ball-ended probe; sharp probes are contraindicated because they can cavitate a lesion (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Radiographs: bitewings help follow progress but are not useful for early caries (Sikri, 2020). Radiographic appearance is often described as up to 6 months behind histological spread (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
| Scoring | Range |
|---|---|
| Pickard's Guide mICDAS (simplified) | 0 to 4 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| ICDAS II in Sikri and Summitt | caries codes 0 to 6; 0 is sound after 5 s drying (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013) |
| ICCMS per Mount | seven ICDAS codes merged to four (Mount et al, 2016) |
- Kidd and Fejerskov prefer activity-based Nyvad criteria because they link to treatment decisions (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- Risk categories: Sikri lists low, moderate and high; high risk includes poor hygiene, two or more lesions in the last year, deep fissures and inadequate saliva (Sikri, 2020). The Guide defines high risk as two or more new, progressing and/or restored lesions in the previous 2 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Activity: active lesions demand management (Ekstrand and Braga, 2019); arrested lesions need no caries intervention except for aesthetics, function or risk for pulp death (Fontana and Huysmans, 2019).
- Recall is based on risk and not standardised at 6 months (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Bitewing intervals, as each book gives them: every 6 to 12 months for high risk in the Guide (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); every 3 to 6 months for high risk and every 12 to 18 months for moderate risk in Summitt (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Magnification: the Guide's authors regard it as an essential prerequisite for high-quality minimally invasive operative dentistry (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 5. Non-operative management
- Biofilm control with fluoride toothpaste of 1350 to 1500 ppm F (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Fluoride in toothpaste delays lesion progression primarily by inhibiting demineralisation and encouraging remineralisation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Fissure sealants: the Guide notes no clinically significant advantage of resin over glass-ionomer sealants in systematic reviews, with resin showing better longevity and wear (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Therapeutic sealing of early damaged fissures is done without physically excavating the lesion (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Fluoride varnish: 22 600 ppm F in the Guide and in Summitt; 22,500 ppm in Sturdevant's table (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Hilton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
- Sealants: sealed lesions do not progress while the fissure stays sealed; sealants fail at 5% to 10% per year, and annual recall with repair of lost sealant improves effectiveness (Hilton et al, 2013).
- CPP-ACP and bioactive glass for remineralisation (Sikri, 2020); Mount reports no long-term clinical evidence for CPP-ACP caries control (Mount et al, 2016); the Slayton et al (2018) abstract recommends against 10% CPP-ACP (abstract only).
- Silver diamine fluoride: 38% in Sikri and Sturdevant, about 30% in Craig; black staining; up to 50% of arrested lesions reactivated by 24 months in Sturdevant (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019; Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Full treatment in P10.
- Resin infiltration: a microinvasive treatment that arrests early lesions without drilling; the kit has a 15% hydrochloric acid etch gel, an ethanol drying agent and a TEGDMA-based light-cured infiltrant (Sikri, 2020). Sikri lists white spot lesions, fluorosis, amelogenesis imperfecta and molar-incisor hypomineralisation as indications (Sikri, 2020). Summitt places it between radiographic inner enamel and the outer third of dentine and notes that the infiltrant is radiolucent (Hilton et al, 2013). Modern Operative Dentistry limits it to non-cavitated lesions (Borges et al, 2020).
- Sikri: restoration is avoided for non-cavitated white spots, secondary caries at margins and root lesions, which get topical remineralising agents, but inaccessible lesions do not fall under this category, and cavitation makes plaque control difficult so a surgical approach is more reliable (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Minimally invasive operative techniques
**6.1 Principles of cavity preparation**
- Reasons to intervene operatively: to relieve pain and protect the pulp; to restore form, function and appearance; to aid plaque control; and caries risk, since small lesions in a high-risk patient whose prevention has failed may need operative care (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Summitt adds that the most important reason for placing a restoration is to aid plaque control (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Excavate unrepairable, diseased enamel and dentine only, modify walls, restore with adhesive materials (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Caries-infected dentine must not remain at the enamel-dentine junction (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Adhesive materials need no retention features cut into sound tooth (Sikri, 2020).
- Dentine zones: caries-infected dentine (outer, irreparable) is removed; caries-affected dentine (inner, harder, "sticky and scratchy") is repairable and can be retained (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). The Guide states that, where a peripheral seal can be achieved (enamel margins intact) and pulp symptoms are not present, clinical evidence now shows affected dentine gives better indirect pulp protection than any artificial restorative material (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Mount calls retention and healing of the affected layer one of the cornerstones of minimal intervention dentistry (Mount et al, 2016).
- Caries-detector dyes, side by side: the Guide says dyes stain deeper collagen and lead to over-preparation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sturdevant says their use is no longer recommended (Ritter et al, 2019); Mount says they disclose bacteria rather than demineralised dentine (Mount et al, 2016).
- Hand excavators give tactile discrimination between dentine zones, and burs are not self-limiting (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Fissure lesions (Mount, site 1): with adhesive materials only the pits involved need be removed, and the floor is left untouched as it remineralises once sealed (Mount et al, 2016).

**6.2 Caries removal endpoints (books side by side)**
| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| Banerjee and Watson, 2015 | no absolute correct or incorrect amount; pragmatic, documented decision |
| Torres and Schwendicke, 2020 | selective removal to firm dentine (shallow, moderate) or soft dentine (deep); non-selective removal to hard dentine not recommended any longer |
| Ritter et al, 2019 | deep lesions: sound junction peripherally, soft dentine left pulpally; growing evidence suggests re-entry does not contribute to improved outcomes |
| Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016 | selective removal preferable if tooth symptomless and vital; complete removal with risk of pulp exposure "should be considered unethical"; authors would not re-enter |
| Schwendicke et al, 2016 (abstract only) | firm dentine for shallow or moderate lesions, soft dentine for deep ones, stepwise an option in permanent teeth |
- Stepwise re-entry: 6 to 9 months in the Guide; 6 to 12 months in Modern Operative Dentistry; Mount advises a transitional restoration for at least three weeks and no more than six months (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Torres and Schwendicke, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).

**6.3 ART, ITR and Hall technique**
- ART: hand-instrument excavation, conditioning with weak polyacrylic acid, glass ionomer placed also in confluent pits and fissures, finger pressure with petroleum jelly; first tried in the mid 1980s; single-surface restorations did better than multisurface (Sikri, 2020).
- ART advantages in Sikri: no sophisticated equipment, no dependence on electricity, minimal discomfort, low cost and fluoride release from the cement; if it fails, a more radical approach can follow (Sikri, 2020). Sturdevant: ART as a technique and material is not recommended in routine clinical use and is restricted to areas with limited access to dental treatment (Ritter et al, 2019).
- ITR: caries control with conventional or resin-modified glass ionomer before definitive care (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Hall technique: preformed metal crown cemented with glass ionomer on a carious primary molar without preparation or caries excavation (Sikri, 2020); no local anaesthetic and no caries removal; clinical trials have shown excellent results (textbook statement, evidence level: one textbook) (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Innes et al (2017), abstract only, report randomised trials favouring the Hall technique over comparators.

**6.4 Conservative cavity designs**
- Site-and-size description of Mount and Hume separates the lesion from the cavity form (Mount et al, 2016); Sikri calls it the basis of minimal cavity preparation but subjective (Sikri, 2020). Mount's three sites are fissures and pits, approximal surfaces, and the cervical third or root; Size 1 is minimal surface cavitation with dentine involvement just beyond treatment by remineralisation alone (Mount et al, 2016).
- Mount's system aims to break the conceptual connection between detecting a lesion and preparing a cavity (Mount et al, 2016).
- Preventive resin restoration: preparation limited to carious pits and fissures (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Minibox keeps occlusal fissures if caries removal is unnecessary there (Sikri, 2020). Fissurotomy: bur tip ten times smaller than a #556 bur (Sikri, 2020).
- Tunnel, each book as written (not ranked): Mount: the simplest and most conservative approach for an enamel lesion at least 3.00 mm apical to the ridge crest, and a desirable design (Mount et al, 2016); Pickard's Guide: successful only if the lesion is sufficiently small, with a high risk of ridge fracture (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sikri: indicated in small lesions, especially in deciduous teeth, but studies could not show promising results (Sikri, 2020); Summitt: 21% failures at 7 years against none for slot restorations in one evaluation, and tunnel restorations are not indicated and should be avoided (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant: controversial and not supported in that textbook (Ritter et al, 2019).

| Laser | explosive subsurface expansion of water; drawbacks of time and impaired adhesion (Sikri, 2020) |

### 7. Materials
- Adhesive restorative materials conserve tooth structure because no mechanical retention is cut (Sikri, 2020). Amalgam retention requires undercuts and slots and is not particularly minimally invasive (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Glass ionomer is used for ART in Sikri (Sikri, 2020); conventional or resin-modified glass ionomer for ITR in Summitt, who also describes resin-modified glass ionomer with composite in tunnels (Hilton et al, 2013); Mount describes glass ionomer in tunnel cavities (Mount et al, 2016). Details in Q09, P5 and P10.
- Pulp protection: indirect pulp capping when the chamber is not breached; direct capping only if the exposure is no wider than the tip of a Williams periodontal probe, and success is not guaranteed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Sikri notes that flowable composites have low modulus and higher polymerisation shrinkage and are not used in routine (Sikri, 2020).

### 8. Repair rather than replacement: the 5Rs
- Review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Replacement is indicated when more than 50% of the restoration has failed or the tooth is compromised; otherwise repair is the more conservative option (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Replacement loses more tooth structure each time (Sikri, 2020). Summitt calls replacing only the failed portion the most conservative and simplest option for a failed amalgam, and gives the attachment strength of new amalgam to old as only 30% to 60% of unrepaired amalgam (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 9. Advantages and limitations
| Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|
| smaller cavities: easier void-free placement, better seal, stronger crown (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) | operative care alone does not cure disease; depends on patient responsibility (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| selective removal cuts pulp exposure (Hilton et al, 2013) | residual caries needs a good seal and review (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| removes only unrepairable, diseased enamel and dentine (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) | may become "just a new buzzword"; no realistic fees noted in the health service of the authors (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) |

### 10. Critical evidence
- Statements the books report (not graded): partial removal reduced pulp exposure by 98% in two reviews quoted by Summitt (Hilton et al, 2013); complete removal exposed the pulp in up to 53% (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- Books differ on calcium hydroxide: Guide, no positive indications under adhesive restorations; Summitt, used then resin-modified glass ionomer; Sturdevant, does not appear to improve outcomes (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Hilton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
- Remineralisers: no long-term clinical evidence for CPP-ACP (Mount et al, 2016). SDF, as quoted by Hill and Gillam: reviews report effectiveness for arrest on cavitated primary lesions and root caries in the elderly; one primary-dentition meta-analysis (Oliveira et al, 2019, four trials) reports unclear or high risk of bias in all studies (Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- Sikri's figure that restorations fall to about 50% cites no study (Sikri, 2020).
- Risk assessment is inevitably a best guess (Hilton et al, 2013).
- No trial of the whole pathway is given in the books read (not stated in the books read).

### 11. Recent advances and future
- Frencken et al (2012), abstract only, reviewed detection, prevention, restorative and non-restorative treatment and repair.
- Resin infiltration and ICCMS are described as newer tools (Hilton et al, 2013; Mount et al, 2016). An ideal future detection device would detect biochemical activity before tissue damage (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 12. Clinical relevance
- A formal recorded risk assessment is a dento-legal requirement, and the Guide warns that over-standardised systems miss patient nuance (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- The patient must understand that caries is controlled and not cured, like diabetes (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Where caries-affected dentine is retained as an indirect pulp cap under a long-term adhesive restoration, pulp status is reviewed at planned recall appointments (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- ART needs no electricity or sophisticated equipment and suits rural areas (Sikri, 2020).

### 13. Conclusion
Minimal intervention dentistry treats the disease first and the cavity last: risk-based prevention, remineralisation, selective removal and repair, with restoration only when needed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Minimum intervention is the care philosophy; minimally invasive is the operative component (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020).
- Extension for prevention became prevention for extension (Sikri, 2020).
- Sharp probes are contraindicated for caries detection (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- mICDAS 0 to 4 (Guide) against ICDAS II 0 to 6 (Sikri) (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020).
- High risk: two or more new, progressing and/or restored lesions in 2 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Caries-infected dentine is never left at the junction (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- 5Rs: review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace; 50% rule (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Partial removal: 98% fewer pulp exposures (Hilton et al, 2013).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Treating minimal intervention and minimally invasive as the same without mentioning that Sikri notes they are used interchangeably yet describe different concepts and that the Guide defines them separately.
2. Giving one ICDAS range, one SDF concentration or one varnish figure when books differ.
3. Writing that all caries must be removed from the floor of the cavity.
4. Calling tunnel preparation an accepted standard design.
5. Using "golden triangle" as if a textbook defines it.
