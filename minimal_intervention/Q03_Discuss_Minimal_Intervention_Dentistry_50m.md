## STUDY MODE
Shared background (definitions, Black era, framework lists, restorative cycle, 5Rs) is in the Study Mode of Q02, sections S1 to S3 and S7. Only the "current concepts" material is repeated here.

### S1. What "current" means [framing]
- **Shift from restoration to disease control:** Mount states that dental professionals can now primarily be physicians, preventing, diagnosing and treating disease non-surgically, and that surgery can be minimally invasive if performed with precision (Mount et al, 2016). Sturdevant describes the move from a surgical model to a medical model with individual risk assessment (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Terminology:** the Guide separates minimum intervention care from minimally invasive operative dentistry (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sikri treats the two terms as used interchangeably though different in concept (Sikri, 2020).

### S2. FDI principles and the FDI-linked reviews [guideline]
- Sikri lists five FDI principles: patient education and motivation; modification of oral micro-organisms; remineralisation of early lesions; minimal tooth preparation for cavitated lesions; repair of defective restorations (Sikri, 2020).- Tyas et al (2000), abstract only, state that the approach questions the surgical model and Black's extension for prevention (Tyas et al, 2000). Frencken et al (2012), abstract only, follow it and review detection, prevention, restorative and non-restorative treatment, and repair (Frencken et al, 2012).

### S3. Selective caries removal, the consensus position [technique]
- **Consensus abstract:** Schwendicke et al (2016), abstract only, recommend biofilm control first, restoration only when lesions cannot be cleaned or sealed, selective removal to firm dentine in shallow or moderately deep lesions, selective removal to soft dentine in deep lesions, with stepwise removal an option in permanent teeth (Schwendicke et al, 2016).
- **Textbooks alongside:** Modern Operative Dentistry gives the same depth-based terms and states that non-selective removal to hard dentine is not recommended any longer (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020). Sturdevant says there is overwhelming scientific evidence for selective removal, but that it remains a controversial procedure in certain countries with liability and board-examination implications (Ritter et al, 2019). Kidd and Fejerskov say selective removal is preferable provided the tooth is symptomless and vital, and that complete removal with the risk of pulp exposure should be considered unethical (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- **Re-entry:** Sturdevant reports that growing evidence suggests temporising followed by re-entry does not contribute to improved outcomes (Ritter et al, 2019); Kidd and Fejerskov say the authors would not re-enter (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); Modern Operative Dentistry describes stage 2 at 6 to 12 months (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020).

### S4. Newer non-operative agents and their evidence [evidence]
- **SDF:** concentrations are given as 38% (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019) and about 30% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Sturdevant reports that up to 50% of arrested lesions were reactivated by 24 months after one application (Ritter et al, 2019). Hill and Gillam quote reviews side by side: one says SDF is effective for caries arrest on cavitated lesions in primary teeth and root caries in the elderly, and a root-caries review reports no severe adverse effects; a single meta-analysis (Oliveira et al, 2019; primary dentition, four trials) reports SDF reduced new dentine caries at 24 months, at least one domain of unclear or high risk of bias in all studies, and glass ionomer more effective than SDF at 12 months without a significant difference (Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **P11-4:** a meta-analysis quoted by Hill and Gillam gives a relative risk of 1.82 for caries arrest and notes that trials had elevated risks of bias (Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **CPP-ACP:** Mount reports no long-term clinical evidence for caries control yet (Mount et al, 2016); a meta-analysis quoted by Hill and Gillam found CPP-ACP plus fluoride did not overcome fluoride alone (Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **Guideline (abstract only):** Slayton et al (2018) give 11 recommendations by lesion type, surface and dentition, using GRADE; the abstract lists 38% SDF, sealants, 5% NaF varnish, 1.23% APF gel and 5000 ppm F toothpaste or gel among effective interventions and recommends against 10% CPP-ACP (Slayton et al, 2018).

### S5. Detection and recall, current tools [diagnosis]
- **ICCMS:** Mount states that ICCMS merges the seven ICDAS codes into four and that ICDAS alone gives no guidance on subsequent clinical decisions (Mount et al, 2016). Pitts and Ekstrand describe ICCMS as integrating tooth and patient information including risk (Pitts and Ekstrand, 2013; abstract only).
- **Activity:** expert recommendations quoted by Fontana and Huysmans state that arrested lesions need no caries intervention except for aesthetics, function or risk for pulp death, and that non-cavitated active lesions get non-restorative methods (Fontana and Huysmans, 2019).
- **Near-infrared transillumination:** Detection and Assessment places it in a ladder from preventive care to minimally invasive or operative care by lesion depth (Kuhnisch, 2019).

### S6. Repair [maintenance]
- The Guide's 5Rs, the 50% rule and Sikri's point that each replacement loses more tooth are in Q02 S7 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020).

### S7. Critical evidence synthesis [evidence]
- **Statements the books report on selective removal (not graded):** Summitt quotes two systematic reviews showing a 98% reduction in exposure risk against complete excavation (Hilton et al, 2013), and Kidd and Fejerskov report exposure in up to 53% with complete removal (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- **SDF and P11-4 (second-hand reviews):** the P11-4 and SDF figures come from reviews quoted by Hill and Gillam, the SDF risk-of-bias statement from one primary-dentition meta-analysis (Hill and Gillam, 2024). Hill and Gillam describe the consensus from reviews of remineralisation products as somewhat inconclusive (printed "inclusive") (Hill and Gillam, 2024).
- **Remineralisers:** Mount reports no long-term clinical evidence for CPP-ACP caries control (Mount et al, 2016); the Guide says long-term trials are needed for CPP-ACP and bioactive glass (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); the Slayton abstract recommends against 10% CPP-ACP (Slayton et al, 2018; abstract only).
- **Books differ:** tunnel preparations (Mount: simplest and most conservative approach for an enamel lesion at least 3.00 mm apical to the ridge crest, a most conservative approach with glass ionomer once the lesion is in dentine, and a desirable design; Pickard's Guide: successful only if the lesion is sufficiently small, with a high risk of ridge fracture; Sikri: indicated in small lesions, especially in deciduous teeth, but studies could not show promising results; Summitt: 21% failures at 7 years against none for slot restorations in one evaluation, tunnel restorations not indicated and should be avoided; Sturdevant: controversial and not supported in that textbook), and calcium hydroxide under restorations (Guide: no positive indications; Summitt: used; Sturdevant: does not appear to improve outcomes) (Mount et al, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Hall technique (evidence level: one textbook statement plus one abstract):** Kidd and Fejerskov state that clinical trials have shown excellent results and that the crowns take the teeth symptomless to exfoliation (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); Sikri describes the method without outcome figures (Sikri, 2020); Innes et al (2017), abstract only, report randomised trials favouring it over comparators (Innes et al, 2017).
- **Whole-pathway evidence:** the books read give no trial of the whole minimal intervention pathway against conventional care (not stated in the books read).

### S8. Future [advances]
- The Guide says the ideal detection device would detect biochemical activity before tissue damage (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Lasers in Dentistry notes carbon dioxide and erbium lasers are cleared by the FDA for caries removal but remain handheld and visually guided, and image-guided selective removal has not yet been realised (Muruppel and Fried, 2023).
- Bioactive glass composites are proposed in Dentine Hypersensitivity as a response to the Minamata Convention and for remineralisation (Hill and Gillam, 2024).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 90 minutes.)*

### 1. Definition and scope
- Minimally invasive operative dentistry repairs and/or restores damaged and defective tooth structure directly, to maintain pulp vitality, function and aesthetics (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Minimum intervention oral healthcare is the wider team approach built on detection and risk assessment, non-operative control, minimally invasive repair, and review and recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Sikri: the two terms are used interchangeably but differ in concept; minimal intervention deals with the cause of disease, and minimal invasive dentistry is a component of it (Sikri, 2020).
- Kidd and Fejerskov: a preventive, caries control philosophy aiming at minimal need for operative intervention (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- Who coined the term: not stated in the books read.

### 2. How the concept has changed
| Earlier practice | Current concept |
|---|---|
| Black's classification described the restoration location and gave large cavity forms (Mount et al, 2016) | "extension for prevention" has changed to "prevention for extension" (Sikri, 2020); the principle has virtually been eliminated (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| Surgical model: wait for cavitation, then restore (Ritter et al, 2019) | Medical model with individual risk assessment (Ritter et al, 2019); restoring a lesion is defect repair and not caries treatment (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Removal of all softened dentine from the cavity floor, called fraught with danger by Mount (Mount et al, 2016) | Selective removal to firm or soft dentine; non-selective removal to hard dentine not recommended any longer (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020) |

- Current practice in Pickard's Guide: recall is set by risk and not standardised at 6 months; failing restorations are managed by the 5Rs (review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace); sharp probes are contraindicated for caries detection (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 3. Framework today
- FDI principles as given by Sikri: patient education and motivation; modification of micro-organisms; remineralisation of early lesions; minimal preparation of cavitated lesions; repair of defective restorations (Sikri, 2020).
- Pickard's Guide: identify, control/prevent, minimally invasive restoration, recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- CAMBRA: assess risk, reduce pathological factors, enhance protective factors, minimally invasive restoration if needed (Zeller et al, 2019).

### 4. Risk- and activity-based diagnosis
- Scoring: the Guide uses a simplified mICDAS of 0 to 4 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); ICDAS II has caries codes 0 to 6 (Sikri, 2020); ICCMS merges the seven ICDAS codes into four (Mount et al, 2016). Mount notes that ICDAS gives no guidance on subsequent clinical decisions (Mount et al, 2016); Pitts and Ekstrand describe ICCMS as integrating tooth and patient information including risk (Pitts and Ekstrand, 2013; abstract only).
- Kidd and Fejerskov find ICDAS difficult to chart in the clinic and prefer the activity-based Nyvad criteria (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- High risk: two or more new, progressing and/or restored lesions in the previous 2 years in the Guide (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); two or more carious lesions in the last year in Sikri (Sikri, 2020).
- Activity decides treatment: arrested lesions need no caries intervention except for aesthetics, function or pulp risk; non-cavitated active lesions get non-restorative methods (Fontana and Huysmans, 2019).
- Risk assessment is a best guess based on available evidence (Hilton et al, 2013); a recorded risk assessment is an essential dento-legal requirement (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 5. Current non-operative tools
| Agent | What the books say | Evidence note |
|---|---|---|
| High-fluoride toothpaste | 2800/5000 ppm F for high-risk patients (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) | up to 75% reduction in caries incidence with 5,000 ppm topical treatments (Hilton et al, 2013); rehardened 76% of root lesions against 35% with 1100 ppm (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) |
| Fluoride varnish | 22 600 ppm F (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Hilton et al, 2013); 22,500 ppm in Sturdevant's table (Ritter et al, 2019) | figures differ between books |
| SDF | 38% (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019); about 30% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) | reviews quoted by Hill and Gillam: effective for arrest on cavitated primary lesions and root caries in the elderly; one primary-dentition meta-analysis: fewer new dentine lesions at 24 months, unclear or high risk of bias, glass ionomer better than SDF at 12 months, not significant (Hill and Gillam, 2024); up to 50% reactivation by 24 months in Sturdevant (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| CPP-ACP | remineralising adjunct (Sikri, 2020) | no long-term clinical evidence (Mount et al, 2016); plus fluoride did not overcome fluoride alone (Hill and Gillam, 2024); Slayton abstract recommends against 10% CPP-ACP (Slayton et al, 2018; abstract only) |
| P11-4 | self-assembling peptide (Hill and Gillam, 2024) | relative risk 1.82 for arrest; trials had elevated risks of bias (Hill and Gillam, 2024) |
| Resin infiltration | microinvasive; arrests early lesions without drilling (Sikri, 2020) | non-cavitated lesions only (Borges et al, 2020) |
| Sealants | sealed lesions do not progress while sealed (Hilton et al, 2013) | fail at 5% to 10% per year (Hilton et al, 2013) |
- Slayton et al (2018), abstract only: a GRADE-based guideline with 11 recommendations on non-restorative treatment; effective interventions include 38% SDF, sealants, 5% NaF varnish, 1.23% APF gel and 5000 ppm F toothpaste or gel, and it recommends against 10% CPP-ACP (Slayton et al, 2018).
- Sealed restorations placed directly over frankly cavitated lesions can arrest lesion progression (Hilton et al, 2013). Sealants with preventive resin restorations tend to be lost at 5% to 10% per year, so they must be monitored and repaired or replaced as needed (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Sikri: restoration is avoided for non-cavitated white spots, marginal secondary caries and root surface lesions (topical remineralising agents); inaccessible lesions are excluded, and cavitation makes plaque control difficult so surgery is more reliable (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Current operative practice
**6.1 Caries removal**
| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| Schwendicke et al, 2016 (abstract only) | firm dentine for shallow or moderate lesions; soft dentine for deep lesions; stepwise an option in permanent teeth |
| Torres and Schwendicke, 2020 | same depth-based terms; non-selective removal to hard dentine not recommended any longer; stage 2 of stepwise at 6 to 12 months |
| Ritter et al, 2019 | selective removal supported by overwhelming evidence but controversial in certain countries; growing evidence suggests re-entry does not contribute to improved outcomes |
| Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016 | selective removal preferable if symptomless and vital; complete removal with risk of pulp exposure should be considered unethical; authors would not re-enter |
| Banerjee and Watson, 2015 | no absolute correct amount of caries to excavate; infected dentine never left at the junction |
- The Guide states that, where a peripheral seal can be achieved (enamel margins intact) and pulp symptoms are not present, clinical evidence now shows affected dentine gives better indirect pulp protection than any artificial restorative material (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Detection dyes: Sturdevant says their use is no longer recommended, and Modern Operative Dentistry does not recommend them in pulpal areas (Ritter et al, 2019; Torres and Schwendicke, 2020).

**6.2 ART, ITR and Hall technique**
- ART: hand-instrument excavation and glass ionomer, first tried in the mid 1980s (Sikri, 2020).
- ITR: caries control with glass ionomer before definitive care (Hilton et al, 2013).
- ART advantages: no sophisticated equipment, no dependence on electricity, minimal discomfort, low cost, fluoride release (Sikri, 2020). Sturdevant: ART as a technique and material is not recommended in routine clinical use and is restricted to areas with limited access to dental treatment (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Hall technique: preformed metal crown on a carious primary molar without preparation or caries excavation (Sikri, 2020); no local anaesthetic, and clinical trials are stated to have shown excellent results (textbook statement) (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); randomised trials favoured it over comparators (Innes et al, 2017; abstract only). Evidence level: one textbook plus one abstract.

**6.3 Conservative cavity designs**
- Preventive resin restoration limits preparation to carious pits and fissures (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Mount's site-and-size description separates lesion description from cavity form (Mount et al, 2016).
- Tunnel preparation, each book as written (not ranked): Mount: simplest and most conservative approach for an enamel lesion at least 3.00 mm apical to the ridge crest, and a desirable design (Mount et al, 2016); Pickard's Guide: successful only if the lesion is small enough, high risk of ridge fracture (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sikri: indicated in small lesions, especially deciduous teeth, but studies could not show promising results (Sikri, 2020); Summitt: 21% failures against none for slot restorations at 7 years, not indicated and should be avoided (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant: not supported (Ritter et al, 2019).

**6.4 Instruments**
- Air abrasion: no heat or vibration (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); cannot make well-defined margins (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Carisolv: 0.1% hypochlorite-based gel of pH 11 in the Guide (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); a liquid component containing 0.5% sodium hypochlorite in Sikri (Sikri, 2020).
- Lasers: carbon dioxide and erbium lasers are cleared by the FDA for caries removal but are handheld and visually guided (Muruppel and Fried, 2023); Sikri lists time and impaired adhesion as drawbacks (Sikri, 2020).

### 7. Repair rather than replacement
- Replacement is indicated when more than 50% of a restoration has failed or the tooth is compromised; otherwise repair is the more conservative option (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Each replacement loses more tooth structure (Sikri, 2020).
- The 5Rs are review, refurbish, reseal, repair and replace (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Repairing failing portions is the more conservative and minimally invasive option (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- For amalgam, the attachment strength of new amalgam to old is only 30% to 60% of unrepaired amalgam (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 8. Critical appraisal of the evidence
| Claim | Support | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Selective removal lowers pulp exposure | 98% lower exposure risk in two reviews (Hilton et al, 2013); complete removal exposed pulp in up to 53% (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) | controversial in some countries (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| SDF in caries management | reviews quoted by Hill and Gillam report effectiveness for arrest on cavitated primary lesions and root caries in the elderly; one primary-dentition meta-analysis reports fewer new dentine lesions at 24 months (Hill and Gillam, 2024) | that meta-analysis: unclear or high risk of bias in all studies; reactivation up to 50% in Sturdevant (Hill and Gillam, 2024; Ritter et al, 2019) |
| P11-4 arrests lesions | relative risk 1.82 (Hill and Gillam, 2024) | elevated risks of bias (Hill and Gillam, 2024) |
| CPP-ACP remineralises | favourable short-term effect (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) | long-term trials needed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount et al, 2016); Slayton abstract recommends against 10% CPP-ACP (Slayton et al, 2018; abstract only) |
| Whole pathway improves outcomes | not stated in the books read | no trial of the whole pathway given |

### 9. Practical challenges
- The approach can become "just a new buzzword" (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- Operative care alone does not cure disease; the patient must take responsibility (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Retained soft dentine can be mistaken radiographically for residual caries (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 10. Future directions
- Detection of biochemical activity before tissue damage (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Image-guided selective laser removal has not yet been realised (Muruppel and Fried, 2023).
- Bioactive glass composites for remineralisation (Hill and Gillam, 2024).

### 11. Conclusion
Current minimal intervention dentistry is risk- and activity-based: control the disease, remineralise where possible, remove caries selectively and repair rather than replace, with the evidence statements set out by source in section 8 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Hill and Gillam, 2024).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

- ICCMS merges seven ICDAS codes into four (Mount et al, 2016).
- SDF 38% in Sikri and Sturdevant, about 30% in Craig (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Writing only a definition and no current concepts.
2. Giving one SDF concentration or one ICDAS range when books differ.
3. Presenting SDF, P11-4 or CPP-ACP as proven without stating the quality of evidence.
4. Citing the FDI list without saying which document it comes from.
5. Using "golden triangle" as if a textbook defines it.
