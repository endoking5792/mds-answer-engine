## STUDY MODE

### S1. The problem [definition]
- Each replacement loses more tooth structure, more with tooth-coloured materials, and repeated replacement leads to complex or indirect restorations (Sikri, 2020). Removal and replacement lead to the cycle of re-restoration with increasingly larger preparations (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Mount: each time a restoration is replaced there is inevitably further loss of tooth structure, and failure should never be judged on external appearance alone (Mount et al, 2016).

### S2. The 5 Rs [classification]
Review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace; they are not mutually exclusive and using them is not a failure of care (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S3. The decision rule, side by side [technique]
- 10th edition: when more than 50% has failed or the surrounding tooth is structurally or functionally compromised, partial or complete replacement is indicated; otherwise careful minimally invasive repair may be appropriate (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- 9th edition: the same threshold, with replacement as the indicated option and repair of deficient sections otherwise (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
- Sikri: repair should rest on caries risk, the judgment of benefit against risk and conservative principles (Sikri, 2020).

### S4. By material [treatment relevance]
- Amalgam, favouring repair or resealing: ditching does not by itself dictate replacement; repair of noncarious gaps with flowable composite or sealant enhances longevity (Hilton et al, 2013); localized defects may be repaired (Ritter et al, 2019); Pickard says it is more usual to refurbish, reseal or repair adhesive restorations, and that removing old corroded amalgam can accelerate cusp failure (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Amalgam, Mount: repair of the margins is not normally indicated, ditching of low-copper amalgam is normal, and repolishing the occlusal surface is strictly contraindicated; limited repair may be a proposition because amalgam seals its margins by corrosion (Mount et al, 2016).
- Amalgam repair caveats: attachment of new amalgam to old is only 30% to 60% of unrepaired amalgam (Hilton et al, 2013); there is limited evidence for the long-term success of bonded amalgam repair (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Composite: repair, sealing or replacement of defective margins improves longevity (Hilton et al, 2013). Mount: extensive replacement is necessary in the majority of marginal failures, but there will be occasions where the bulk is sound and one section can be rebuilt, minor ditching can be treated by limited opening and glass ionomer, and resealing is occasionally possible in a relatively new restoration (Mount et al, 2016). Pickard's Guide: it is more usual to refurbish, reseal or repair adhesive restorations (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Glass ionomer: Mount says complete replacement is probably the best solution, with partial removal and lamination as an alternate method of repair (Mount et al, 2016); Hilton et al say a marginal gap is less critical for anticariogenic materials (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S5. Repair technique [technique]
Repair needs independent retention and resistance form (Sikri, 2020). Glass ionomer repair uses polyacrylic acid conditioning (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S6. Evidence and limits [prognosis]
Abstract only: a 2012 FDI task group report reviewing repair among other topics (Frencken et al, 2012). The books cite retrospective and short-term clinical studies of repair, such as a long-term retrospective study of amalgam repair (Hilton et al, 2013), and say repair has been shown to be effective (Ritter et al, 2019); no randomised comparison of repair against replacement is described in the books read.

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 18 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Each replacement removes more tooth, so repair is preferred where possible (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. The 5 Rs (Banerjee and Watson, 2015)
| R | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Review | monitor minor defects (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Refurbish | reshape, remove excess, polish (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Reseal | sealant into non-carious margin gap (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Repair | add material to existing restoration (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Replace | remove whole restoration; multiple or severe problems (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |

### 3. Decision
More than 50% failed, or tooth structurally or functionally compromised: partial or complete replacement (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Banerjee and Watson, 2011). Otherwise careful repair (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Review when operative treatment gives no net clinical advantage (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Fracture lines dictate replacement (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 4. By material
- **Amalgam:** ditching alone is not a reason to replace; seal gaps with flowable composite (Hilton et al, 2013); Mount: margin repair not normally indicated (Mount et al, 2016); new-to-old amalgam attachment only 30% to 60% (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Composite:** repair, sealing or replacement of defective margins improves longevity (Hilton et al, 2013); Mount says replacement is usually necessary for marginal failure, though one section may be rebuilt (Mount et al, 2016); old-to-new composite bond falls with age of the restoration (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Glass ionomer:** Mount: complete replacement probably best; partial removal with lamination is an alternate repair (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Indications for replacement
Include unrepairable marginal voids, an overhang contributing to periodontal breakdown, and caries not treatable by repair (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 6. Repair steps
Remove the defective part, gain retention, refresh margins, condition, apply adhesive and add material (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri: composite repair is partial replacement; roughening, organosilane and bonding agent improve the bond (Sikri, 2020). Air abrasion might help when old adhesive restorations are cut (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 7. Limits
The books cite retrospective and short-term studies of repair (Hilton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019); no randomised comparison is described.

### 8. Conclusion
Review, refurbish, reseal and repair come before replacement (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- 5 Rs: review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Partial or complete replacement when more than 50% has failed or the tooth is compromised (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Amalgam ditching does not dictate replacement (Hilton et al, 2013).
- The 9th edition has the rule but not the 5 Rs (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Writing replacement as the default for any defect (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
2. Treating marginal staining as proof of caries; Pickard's Guide states margin stain is not an indicator of recurrent caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
3. Giving the 5 Rs without the 50% decision rule (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
