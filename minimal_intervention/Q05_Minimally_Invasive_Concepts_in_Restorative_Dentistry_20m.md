## STUDY MODE

### S1. Definition [orientation]
- Minimally invasive operative dentistry repairs or restores damaged and defective tooth structure directly, to maintain pulp vitality, function and aesthetics, and is part of the wider minimum intervention philosophy (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Sikri says the focus of minimal invasive dentistry is maximum conservation of demineralised, non-cavitated lesions of enamel and dentine and that it "is merely a component" of the minimal intervention care plan (Sikri, 2020). MID is used here for minimally invasive / minimal intervention dentistry.

### S2. Why the concepts changed [basic science]
- Black's classification described the final restoration location, not the lesion, and linked lesions to cavity forms larger than necessary (Mount et al, 2016). The alternatives now: earlier diagnosis of activity, disease control, remineralisation, adhesive and bioactive materials, magnification (Mount et al, 2016). Extension for prevention has virtually been eliminated (Ritter et al, 2019); it has been largely abandoned (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020).
- A restoration may start a restorative cycle of repeated replacement (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S3. Disease-based care [treatment]
- Sturdevant describes a medical model of caries management (Ritter et al, 2019). Surgical treatment is neither necessary nor ethical where the enamel subsurface is demineralised but the surface is intact (Mount et al, 2016).

### S4. Cavity size follows lesion size [treatment]
- Restoration size should follow lesion size, not detection (Mount et al, 2016).
- In the Site 2, Size 1 tunnel-cavity technique Mount et al say undermined enamel is of no consequence because adhesion supports it and the cavity should not be extended to remove plaque retention because the remaining enamel will remineralise (Mount et al, 2016).
- Site 1, Size 1: only the pits involved, and that section of the fissure obviously demineralised or cavitated, are removed surgically (Mount et al, 2016).
- The adhesive Class 2 preparation has no "extension for prevention"; preventive resin restoration prepares only carious pits and fissures (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S5. Caries removal and repair [treatment]
- Infected dentine is never left at the enamel-dentine junction; affected dentine may stay over the pulp only with a peripheral seal and no pulp symptoms (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Torres and Schwendicke: selective removal to soft dentine is recommended for deep lesions in teeth with vital painless pulps; the firm dentine endpoint is for shallow or moderately deep lesions, "but not deep lesions"; nonselective removal to hard dentine is not recommended any longer for deep lesions (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020). Sturdevant: still controversial in certain countries (Ritter et al, 2019).
- The 5 Rs are review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Repair of defective restorations instead of their replacement is listed among contemporary preventive strategies (Borges et al, 2020).

### S6. Critical evidence: is a second visit needed after partial caries removal? [prognosis]
| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| Pickard's Guide | One-stage partial removal gave fewer pulp exposures than two-step stepwise excavation; the original stepwise protocol re-entered between 6 and 9 months later (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). |
| Sturdevant | Growing evidence suggests that temporising followed by re-entry does not contribute to improved outcomes, so a definitive restoration is supported (Ritter et al, 2019). |
| Kidd and Fejerskov | Complete removal exposed the pulp in up to 53% of control cases; later excavation exposed it in 17%; the authors would not re-enter (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). |
| Summitt | Partial removal reduced pulp exposure risk by 98% compared with complete removal; the protocol delays the final indirect restoration 4 to 8 months; studies of partial removal re-entered the tooth after 4 to 12 months (Hilton et al, 2013). |
| Torres and Schwendicke | Stepwise means selective removal to soft dentine, then to firm dentine 6 to 12 months later (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020). |
The designs and wording differ; the statements are not reconciled here.

### S7. Limits [prognosis]
- Tunnel, side by side: Sikri, ridge strength not maintained, indicated in small lesions especially in deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020); Mount et al, a tunnel (internal occlusal fossa) design for Site 2, Size 1, one glass ionomer case satisfactory at 12 years (Mount et al, 2016); Summitt, 21% failed in 7 years against none of the slot restorations, "are not indicated and should be avoided" (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant, not supported (Ritter et al, 2019); Pickard, only if the lesion is sufficiently small, with a high risk of ridge fracture (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Risk assessment is "inevitably a best guess" (Hilton et al, 2013). Operative care alone does not cure disease (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 36 minutes.)*

### 1. Definition
- Minimally invasive operative dentistry repairs or restores damaged tooth structure directly, to maintain pulp vitality, function and aesthetics (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- It is part of the wider minimum intervention philosophy: excavate only unrepairable diseased enamel and dentine, modify the walls, restore with adhesive materials (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- MID is used here for minimally invasive / minimal intervention dentistry. Sikri: the focus is maximum conservation of demineralised, non-cavitated enamel and dentine, and minimal invasive dentistry "is merely a component" of minimal intervention care (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Change from Black's concepts
| Point | Black's era | Minimally invasive concept |
|---|---|---|
| Cavity size | prescribed forms larger than necessary (Mount et al, 2016) | cavity follows lesion size (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Extension | extension for prevention (Mount et al, 2016) | virtually eliminated (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| Retention | mechanical interlocks (Mount et al, 2016) | adhesive and bioactive materials (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Demineralised tissue | all removed (Mount et al, 2016) | remineralised and healed (Mount et al, 2016) |

### 3. Concepts
1. **Disease-based care:** a medical model with risk assessment (Ritter et al, 2019); restoration is defect repair, not caries treatment (Mount et al, 2016).
2. **Early lesions:** intact-surface lesions are not operated on (Mount et al, 2016).
3. **Site and size:** restoration size follows lesion size (Mount et al, 2016).
4. **Selective removal:** infected dentine leaves the junction; affected dentine may stay over the pulp if a peripheral seal is achieved and pulp symptoms are absent (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
5. **Adhesive materials:** no mechanical retention needed (Sikri, 2020).
6. **Conservative designs and repair:** see sections 6 and 8.

### 4. Detection and risk
- A sharp probe is contraindicated for caries detection (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Sikri grades risk low, moderate or high; two or more carious lesions in the last year is among the high-risk features (Sikri, 2020).
- Pickard's Guide defines high risk as two or more new, progressing or restored lesions in the previous 2 years and says a low or high division is usually more than sufficient (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 5. Non-operative care
- Fluoride varnish is 22 600 ppm F (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Remineralising agents include fluorides and CPP-ACP (Sikri, 2020).
- An intact-surface lesion with a demineralised subsurface can be fully reversed by biochemical means only: diet change, salivary flow, plaque control and fluoride (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Conservative cavity designs
- Preventive resin restoration: only carious pits and fissures are prepared (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Conservative composite restoration: treatment of choice for a primary occlusal lesion (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Site 1, Size 1: remove only the pits involved (Mount et al, 2016).
- Tunnel, side by side: Sikri, ridge strength not maintained, indicated in small lesions especially in deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020); Mount et al, a design for Site 2, Size 1, one case satisfactory at 12 years (Mount et al, 2016); Summitt, 21% failed in 7 years against none of the slots, "not indicated and should be avoided" (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant, not supported (Ritter et al, 2019); Pickard, only for small lesions, ridge fracture risk (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 7. Caries removal
- Deep lesions: Torres and Schwendicke recommend selective removal to soft dentine in vital painless teeth; the firm dentine endpoint is for shallow or moderately deep lesions, not deep lesions; nonselective removal is no longer recommended for deep lesions (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020).
- The peripheral seal is best against sound enamel, then sound dentine, then caries-affected dentine; caries-infected dentine or carious enamel is not recommended (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Re-entry: Pickard's original stepwise protocol re-entered at 6 to 9 months and one-stage partial removal gave fewer pulp exposures (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Torres and Schwendicke give 6 to 12 months (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020); Sturdevant: growing evidence that re-entry does not improve outcomes (Ritter et al, 2019); Kidd and Fejerskov would not re-enter (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).

### 8. Materials and repair
- Adhesive materials need no mechanical retention: glass ionomer cement, resin-modified glass ionomer, composite with bonding agent, and bonded amalgam (Sikri, 2020).
- The 5 Rs: review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace; replace when over 50% of the restoration has failed or the tooth is compromised (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Repair instead of replacement is a contemporary strategy (Borges et al, 2020).

### 9. Advantages and limitations
- Smaller cavities: easier placement, better seal, stronger crown, simpler maintenance (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Limits: operative care alone does not cure disease (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); risk assessment is a best guess (Hilton et al, 2013); selective removal is controversial in some countries (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 10. Conclusion
The concepts move care from extension and replacement to disease control, lesion-sized cavities and repair (Mount et al, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Black described restoration location, not the lesion (Mount et al, 2016).
- Extension for prevention virtually eliminated (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Infected dentine never left at the junction (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Restoration size follows lesion size (Mount et al, 2016).
- 5 Rs; replace when over 50% has failed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing only materials; the concepts include disease control and lesion-sized cavities.
2. Saying all demineralised dentine must be removed; the Guide retains affected dentine over the pulp where a peripheral seal is achieved and pulp symptoms are absent (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
3. Presenting tunnel preparation as accepted or rejected; the books differ (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013; Mount et al, 2016).
