## STUDY MODE

### S1. Definition and terminology [definition]
Pickard's Guide defines minimally invasive operative dentistry as the part of restorative dentistry that repairs and/or restores damaged and defective tooth structure directly, to maintain pulp vitality, function and aesthetics, with the primary goal of respecting tooth structure (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Minimum intervention oral healthcare is the wider approach in which the whole team, led by the dentist, gives individualised patient-centred care and advice (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri says the two terms are used interchangeably but describe different concepts: minimal intervention deals with the cause of disease and rests on prevention and control, and minimal invasive dentistry is merely a component of that plan (Sikri, 2020). Sikri gives "ultraconservative" and "microdentistry" as synonyms (Sikri, 2020). Pickard's preface calls the biologically based approach the gold standard (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S2. Origin and the shift from extension for prevention [concept]
Mount writes that for the first half of the century after Black's work restorations tended to have a short life span; they were large initially, became larger when replaced, and the end point was most often extraction (Mount et al, 2016). Black's classification described the final restoration location, not the lesion, and Mount says its continued use is no longer appropriate (Mount et al, 2016). Mount lists why Black's designs were large: no ion exchange or adhesion, mechanical interlocks for retention, and extension to self-cleansing areas to avoid recurrent caries (Mount et al, 2016). Mount adds that professionals can now primarily be physicians, treating disease non-surgically, with minimally invasive surgery when needed (Mount et al, 2016). Sikri says "extension for prevention" has changed to "prevention for extension" (Sikri, 2020); Sturdevant says the practice has virtually been eliminated (Ritter et al, 2019); Modern Operative Dentistry says it has been largely abandoned (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020). The foreword of the Manual, paraphrasing Black, says operative dentistry is now more about prevention and preservation of tooth tissue (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).
The FDI route: the 2000 FDI Commission review (Tyas, Anusavice, Frencken, Mount) says in its abstract that the concept arose from better understanding of caries and adhesive materials (Tyas et al, 2000, abstract only); the 2012 task group review followed it (Frencken et al, 2012, abstract only). Sikri lists the five principles of minimal intervention "as adopted by FDI": patient education and motivation, modification of oral micro-organisms, remineralisation of early lesions, minimal tooth preparation for cavitated lesions, and repair of defective restorations (Sikri, 2020). Who first coined the term is not stated in the books read.
From surgical to medical model: Sturdevant says dentistry historically waited for cavitation and restored, and that treating only the end result without addressing aetiology did not control the disease (Ritter et al, 2019). Summitt says restoration may be the start of a restorative cycle of repeated replacement (Hilton et al, 2013). Pickard's slogan is that drilling and filling does not cure caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S3. Component 1: risk assessment and detection [diagnosis]
Pickard's care cycle is identify, control and prevent, restore, recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri's seven components start with early diagnosis and risk assessment (Sikri, 2020).
- **Who is high risk.** Pickard: two or more new, progressing and/or restored lesions in the previous 2 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri: his high-risk list includes two or more carious lesions in the last year (Sikri, 2020). Sturdevant: past caries experience is the best predictor of future caries activity (Ritter et al, 2019). Both Pickard and Mount give 0.7 ml/min as a stimulated saliva flow figure for risk (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Tools.** Mount describes the Cariogram as using nine weighted parameters (Mount et al, 2016). Summitt's acronym is PTPM (Hilton et al, 2013). CAMBRA is a disease-based approach (Ferreira Zandona and Longbottom, 2019).
- **Recall and bitewings.** Pickard: recall should not be standardised at 6 months or any other period; its Table 9.3 gives recall ranges from 2 to 6 months up to 12 to 18 months according to lesions and risk, "purely a guide"; repeated bitewings every 6 to 12 months for high-risk or caries-active patients (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Summitt: recall every 3 months and bitewings every 3 to 6 months in its high-risk guideline (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Detection.** Sharp probes are contraindicated for lesion detection (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount et al, 2016). Radiographs are often described as up to 6 months behind histological spread (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) and considerably underestimate lesion size (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Indices.** Pickard: simplified 0-4 scale (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Summitt: ICDAS detection codes include a seven-point staging of caries (Hilton et al, 2013); Sikri describes ICDAS II as separating sound surface, white spot, distinct enamel change, microcavitation and cavitation of different depths (Sikri, 2020). Pickard says the Foundation was convened in 2004; Mount says the system was developed in 2002 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount et al, 2016). Mount notes ICDAS gives no guidance on later clinical decisions and that ICCMS merges seven codes to four (Mount et al, 2016). Kidd and Fejerskov prefer Nyvad's criteria (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Pitts and Ekstrand describe ICCMS as integrating tooth and patient information (Pitts and Ekstrand, 2013, abstract only). A "detect-decide-do" pathway is not stated in the books and could not be confirmed on the web.

### S4. Component 2: prevention and remineralisation [prevention]
Pickard separates standard care, which is patient-led and non-operative, from dentist-led active care (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Summitt calls oral self care, with twice-daily careful cleaning using a toothbrush and an effective fluoride toothpaste, the cornerstone of any preventive strategy (Hilton et al, 2013), and Pickard says fluoride delays lesion progression primarily by inhibiting demineralisation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Summitt cites as much as a 75% reduction in caries incidence with 5,000 ppm topical treatments (Hilton et al, 2013). Varnish is given as 22 600 ppm F (2.2% F) by Pickard (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) and 22,500 ppm for 5% sodium fluoride varnish in Sturdevant's table (Ritter et al, 2019); these are the same strength to rounding. Sealants: Summitt says they tend to fail at 5% to 10% per year (Hilton et al, 2013); Pickard says reviews find no clinically significant advantage of one type over another, although resin-based sealants appear to show increased longevity and better wear resistance, while GIC sealants are more soluble but leach fluoride and may still be recommended where moisture control is difficult (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri describes resin infiltration as microinvasive (Sikri, 2020); Modern Operative Dentistry limits it to non-cavitated lesions (Borges et al, 2020); Summitt calls it an emerging alternative (Hilton et al, 2013).
SDF: Sikri reports 38% SDF used in cavitated carious dentine (Sikri, 2020); Sturdevant's table of caries-reduction estimates lists SDF (38%) at about 96.1% for caries arrest and the text reports up to 50% reactivation by 24 months (Ritter et al, 2019); Craig describes a solution of about 30% SDF, of which approximately 25% is silver and 5% fluoride (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). The books differ on the strength: 38% (Sikri; Sturdevant) and about 30% (Craig). The Slayton guideline abstract lists 38% SDF among effective interventions and recommends against 10% CPP-ACP (Slayton et al, 2018, abstract only). CPP-ACP: Pickard says current research tends towards a favourable short-term remineralising effect and long-term trials are needed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sturdevant says mounting evidence indicates that regular use is effective in enamel remineralisation, but most studies did not separate its effect from fluoride (Ritter et al, 2019). Sikri states that infection control plus remineralisers could reduce restoration placement to approximately 50% (Sikri, 2020). Frencken's 2012 abstract names key behaviours as sugar intake not more than five times daily and twice-daily fluoridated toothpaste (Frencken et al, 2012, abstract only).

### S5. Component 3: minimally invasive operative care [treatment]
Pickard's definition: excavate only unrepairable diseased enamel and dentine, optimise the remaining walls, and restore with adhesive materials that support the tooth, promote remineralisation and potentially have antibacterial activity (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Infected dentine is never left at the enamel-dentine junction, while affected dentine over the pulp provides better indirect pulp protection than any artificial restorative material (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Mount says the affected layer can be remineralised until less than 10% of mineral remains attached to collagen (Mount et al, 2016). Disclosing solutions disclose bacteria rather than demineralised dentine (Mount et al, 2016); Sturdevant no longer recommends dyes (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Selective, partial and stepwise removal.** Pickard: one-stage removal gives significantly fewer pulp exposures than two-step stepwise (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Summitt: partial removal reduced the risk of pulp exposure by 98%, with indirect restorations delayed 4 to 8 months in the protocol (Hilton et al, 2013). Kidd and Fejerskov: complete removal exposed the pulp in up to 53%, re-entry exposed it in 17%, and the authors would not re-enter (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Sturdevant: remove to within about 1 mm of the pulp; re-entry gives no benefit (Ritter et al, 2019). Mount advises caries control in an extensive lesion in two stages, with a minimum of three weeks of GIC seal before reassessment (Mount et al, 2016). Modern Operative Dentistry: selective removal to firm or soft dentine; stepwise excavation is selective removal to soft dentine at stage 1 and to firm dentine 6 to 12 months later (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020). The 2016 consensus abstract gives the same depth-based terms (Schwendicke et al, 2016, abstract only).
- **Cavity designs.** Mount's site-and-size system divides lesions by site and size (Mount et al, 2016); Sikri calls it the basis for minimal cavity preparation, though advanced and extensive lesions do not fall under it, the classification is subjective and lesion size is not well defined (Sikri, 2020). With adhesive materials only the involved pits need removal, and a sealed demineralised floor may be left (Mount et al, 2016). Tunnel: Mount calls the tunnel approach the simplest and most conservative when the enamel lesion is at least 3.00 mm apical to the ridge crest, and the slot approach suits a lesion closer than 3.00 mm (Mount et al, 2016). Summitt reports 21% tunnel failures in 7 years against none of the slot amalgams in the study cited, and survival of 46% against 76% for Class 2 composite in another, and says tunnels are not indicated and should be avoided (Hilton et al, 2013); Sikri says studies have not shown good results (Sikri, 2020); Sturdevant does not support the technique (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Small adhesive restorations.** PRR limits preparation to carious pits and fissures (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant calls it a conservative composite restoration (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **ART.** Hand excavation and GIC, first tried in the mid-1980s (Sikri, 2020); occlusal ART restorations are usually successful but approximal ones prone to loss (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); Sturdevant says ART is "not recommended in routine clinical use" and is restricted to areas with limited access to dental treatment (Ritter et al, 2019), while Summitt recommends it as a definitive restoration for children and fearful adults (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Hall technique (thin sources).** Crown cemented with GIC over a cavitated symptomless primary molar with no anaesthetic, caries removal or reduction (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016; Sikri, 2020). Pickard mentions it in passing (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). The Innes review abstract reports 99% (UK) and 100% (Germany) success at 1 year (Innes et al, 2017, abstract only).
- **ITR (one textbook).** Hand or low-speed removal, GIC restoration and maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Tools.** Air abrasion was developed in 1945 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) but cannot produce well-defined margins (Ritter et al, 2019). Chemo-mechanical gels: Pickard 0.1% hypochlorite (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sikri 0.5% sodium hypochlorite for Carisolv (Sikri, 2020). Several newer technologies lack clinical research (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S6. Component 4: repair and maintenance [maintenance]
The 5Rs are review, refurbish, reseal, repair and replace (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Replacement is indicated when over 50% has failed or the tooth is compromised, and repairing failing portions is the more conservative option (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Refurbishment can take only a few minutes (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Summitt gives amalgam repair strength as only 30% to 60% of unrepaired amalgam (Hilton et al, 2013). Sikri says each replacement loses more tooth structure (Sikri, 2020); Sturdevant names the cycle of re-restoration (Ritter et al, 2019). Pickard gives "often quoted" average ages at replacement of 10 to 15 years (amalgam), 5 to 8 (composite) and 3 to 5 (GIC), says obtaining a precise figure is impossible and arguably irrelevant, and says longevity matters less than preserving tooth and pulp (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S7. Materials [materials]
GIC is a fluoride reservoir with release high initially and falling after 8 to 10 weeks, though Pickard notes contrary evidence that the release is of subclinical value after initial placement (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Mount says release is still present over at least seven years, calls it bioactive and ideal in a high caries rate (Mount et al, 2016); it is not universally recommended for long-term stress-bearing sites (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Composite shrinkage: 1.5-3.5% by volume in Pickard (Banerjee and Watson, 2015), 1-2% in Mount with careful incremental placement (Mount et al, 2016). Sturdevant sees likely no indications for compomers (Ritter et al, 2019). Amalgam: Pickard restricts it to large load-bearing restorations and says its retention is not particularly minimally invasive (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Mount describes it as sealing its own margins by corrosion and as able to last for most if not all of a patient's life (Mount et al, 2016). Pickard says Biodentine can be termed bioactive (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Full treatment is in GIC Q09, P5 and P10.

### S8. Merits and limitations [evaluation]
Merits: easier void-free placement, better moisture control, a strengthened crown and increased longevity of the tooth (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); ART needs no sophisticated equipment (Sikri, 2020). Limitations: operative MID alone does not provide the actual cure (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); even the best operative dentistry fails without patient responsibility (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); risk assessment is inevitably a best guess (Hilton et al, 2013); Kidd and Fejerskov warn that minimal intervention can become a new buzzword, with no realistic fees for caries control in England and Wales at press time (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).

### S10. The future [recent advances]
Pickard: perhaps the ideal detector would sense biochemical changes before damage; fluorescence lifetime imaging for dentine zones; new air-abrasion powders for self-limiting removal; chair-side scanners, now being developed to measure tooth-wear surface change over time, which may prove useful in practice (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Lasers, enzymes, photoactive disinfection and ozone lack clinical research (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri asks for on-the-spot activity assessment (Sikri, 2020). Resin infiltration is an emerging alternative (Hilton et al, 2013). The Guide's foreword, written by a guest writer, hopes that teachers will revise curricula and funders will review their systems (Wilson, 2015).

### 1. Introduction and definition
Minimally invasive operative dentistry is the part of restorative dentistry that repairs and/or restores damaged and defective tooth structure directly, to maintain pulp vitality, function and aesthetics, and its primary goal is to respect tooth structure (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Minimum intervention oral healthcare is the wider approach in which the dental team, led by the dentist, gives individualised patient-centred care and advice (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri says the two terms are used interchangeably but describe different concepts: minimal intervention dentistry deals with the cause of dental disease and rests on prevention and control, and minimal invasive dentistry is merely a component of that plan (Sikri, 2020). Sikri also gives "ultraconservative" and "microdentistry" as synonyms of minimal invasive dentistry (Sikri, 2020). Pickard's Guide calls the biologically based approach the gold standard (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). This answer uses "minimally invasive dentistry (MID)" for the whole subject.

### 2. Origin of the concept
1. Mount: despite Black's work, for the first half of that century restorations tended to have a short life span; they were large initially, became larger when replaced, and the end point was most often extraction (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Black's classification described the final restoration location, not the lesion, and Mount states its continued use is no longer appropriate (Mount et al, 2016).
3. Mount: dental professionals can now primarily be physicians, treating disease non-surgically, and extraction need no longer be contemplated as part of the treatment of caries (Mount et al, 2016).
4. The 2000 FDI Commission review by Tyas, Anusavice, Frencken and Mount says in its abstract that the concept arose from better understanding of caries and adhesive materials, and questions "extension for prevention" (Tyas et al, 2000, abstract only); a 2012 FDI task group review followed it (Frencken et al, 2012, abstract only).
5. Sikri lists the principles of minimal intervention "as adopted by FDI": patient education and motivation; modification of oral micro-organisms; remineralisation of early lesions; minimal tooth preparation for cavitated lesions; repair of defective restorations (Sikri, 2020).
6. Who first used the term "minimal intervention" is not stated in the books read.

### 3. Philosophy: traditional against minimal intervention
| Feature | Traditional (surgical) | Minimal intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Model | wait for cavitation, then restore (Ritter et al, 2019) | medical model: assess risk, treat the disease (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| Guiding rule | extension for prevention (Mount et al, 2016) | prevention for extension (Sikri, 2020) |
| Role of the filling | restoration once the lesion cavitated (Ritter et al, 2019) | restoration of a lesion is not caries treatment (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Caries removal | remove all demineralised tissue (Mount et al, 2016) | remove infected tissue; keep remineralisable affected dentine (Mount et al, 2016) |
| After failure | replace; cycle of re-restoration (Ritter et al, 2019) | review, refurbish, reseal, repair (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
Sturdevant says extension for prevention has virtually been eliminated (Ritter et al, 2019); Modern Operative Dentistry says it has been largely abandoned (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020). Pickard says drilling and filling teeth does not cure caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2015), and Sturdevant says caries is only controlled and not cured (Ritter et al, 2019). Summitt quotes Kutsch on treating the cause of disease, and the chapter authors liken restoration without removal of the causative factors to placing a new roof on a burning house (Hilton et al, 2013). Sikri's four core principles are recognising risk factors, minimising them, remineralising incipient lesions and repair with minimum sacrifice of tooth (Sikri, 2020). Summitt's new paradigm is patient-centered caries management, a personalised plan of preventive and noninvasive options, operative and minimally invasive options, and maintenance care with recall (Hilton et al, 2013). Summitt's guideline acronym PTPM stands for plaque control, treatment of existing caries lesions, protection of surfaces at risk, and maintenance for prevention (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 5. Risk assessment and detection
1. Risk assessment relates the patient's risk to lesion activity, and without it caries management cannot succeed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
2. Pickard defines high risk as two or more new, progressing and/or restored lesions in the previous 2 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri's high-risk list includes two or more carious lesions in the last year, poor hygiene and inadequate salivary flow (Sikri, 2020). Sturdevant calls past caries experience the best predictor of future activity (Ritter et al, 2019).
3. Recall is risk-based and should not be standardised at 6 months or any other period; Pickard's Table 9.3 ranges run from 2 to 6 up to 12 to 18 months and are purely a guide, with bitewings every 6 to 12 months at high risk (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Summitt gives recall every 3 months and bitewings every 3 to 6 months in its high-risk guideline (Hilton et al, 2013).
4. Visual detection needs clean teeth and good light; sharp probes are contraindicated because they can cavitate a lesion (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Mount says the sharp-explorer technique cannot now be justified as part of an examination (Mount et al, 2016).
5. Radiographic lesions are often described as up to 6 months behind histological spread (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Summitt says radiographs considerably underestimate lesion size (Hilton et al, 2013). Sikri says bitewings are not useful in detecting early caries (Sikri, 2020).
6. Summitt says ICDAS detection codes include a seven-point staging of caries (Hilton et al, 2013); Pickard uses a simplified 0 to 4 scale (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Mount notes ICDAS gives no guidance on subsequent clinical decisions and reports that ICCMS merges the seven codes into four (Mount et al, 2016). Kidd and Fejerskov find ICDAS difficult to chart in the clinic and prefer Nyvad's activity-based criteria (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). The books differ on origin: Pickard says the ICDAS Foundation was convened in 2004 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015), while Mount says the system was developed in 2002 (Mount et al, 2016). The Pitts and Ekstrand abstract says ICCMS integrates tooth and patient information including risk (Pitts and Ekstrand, 2013, abstract only).
7. Mount describes the Cariogram as using nine weighted parameters (Mount et al, 2016). CAMBRA is a disease-based approach that treats patient-specific risk factors (Ferreira Zandona and Longbottom, 2019).

### 6. Prevention and remineralisation
1. Summitt: the cornerstone of any preventive strategy is oral self care, twice-daily careful cleaning with a toothbrush and an effective fluoride toothpaste (Hilton et al, 2013); Pickard says fluoride delays lesion progression primarily by inhibiting demineralisation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
2. Summitt: as much as a 75% reduction in caries incidence with 5,000 ppm fluoride topical treatments (Hilton et al, 2013). Pickard recommends high-concentration prescription toothpastes (2800/5000 ppm fluoride) for high-risk patients with multiple lesions or dry mouth (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
3. Varnish: about 22,500 to 22,600 ppm F (2.2% F), the two books differing only by rounding (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Ritter et al, 2019).
4. Sealants: Summitt says they tend to fail at 5% to 10% per year (Hilton et al, 2013); Pickard cites reviews finding no clinically significant advantage of one type over another, with resin sealants appearing longer lasting and more wear resistant, and GIC sealants still recommended where moisture control is poor (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
5. Resin infiltration: Sikri calls it a microinvasive regime (Sikri, 2020); Modern Operative Dentistry limits it to non-cavitated lesions (Borges et al, 2020); Summitt calls it an emerging alternative (Hilton et al, 2013).
6. SDF: Sikri names 38% SDF for cavitated carious dentine (Sikri, 2020); Sturdevant's table lists 38% SDF at about 96.1% caries arrest and reports up to 50% of arrested lesions reactivated by 24 months (Ritter et al, 2019). Craig describes about 30% SDF, of which approximately 25% is silver and 5% fluoride (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). The Slayton guideline abstract lists 38% SDF among effective interventions (Slayton et al, 2018, abstract only).
7. CPP-ACP: Pickard says research tends towards a favourable short-term remineralising effect and long-term trials are needed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sturdevant says regular use is effective in enamel remineralisation, but most studies did not separate its effect from fluoride (Ritter et al, 2019); the Slayton abstract recommends against 10% CPP-ACP (Slayton et al, 2018, abstract only).
8. Sikri states that infection control plus remineralising agents could reduce restoration placement to approximately 50% (Sikri, 2020). Pickard separates standard care (patient-led, non-operative) from active care (dentist-led) (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 7. Minimally invasive operative techniques
**Principle.** Excavate only unrepairable diseased enamel and dentine, optimise the walls, and restore with adhesive materials (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Infected dentine is never retained at the enamel-dentine junction (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Affected dentine over the pulp gives better indirect pulp protection than any artificial restorative material (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Mount calls retention of the affected layer a cornerstone of minimal intervention dentistry (Mount et al, 2016), and states that this layer can be remineralised up to the point where less than 10% of mineral remains attached to the collagen (Mount et al, 2016). Mount warns that caries-disclosing solutions disclose bacteria rather than demineralised dentine (Mount et al, 2016), and Sturdevant no longer recommends detector dyes (Ritter et al, 2019).
**Caries removal: positions side by side**
| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| Mount | caries control of an extensive lesion in two stages, with a minimum of three weeks' seal (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Torres | stepwise: soft dentine removed at stage 1, firm dentine removed 6 to 12 months later (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020) |
| Pickard | one-stage removal with no re-entry gives significantly fewer pulp exposures than two-step stepwise (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Summitt | partial removal reduced the risk of pulp exposure by 98% (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| Kidd and Fejerskov | complete removal exposed the pulp in up to 53%; later excavation at re-entry exposed it in 17%; they would not re-enter (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) |
| Summitt | indirect restorations delayed 4 to 8 months in the partial-removal protocol (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| Sturdevant | remove to within about 1 mm of the pulp; re-entry gives no benefit (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| Modern Operative Dentistry | selective removal to firm or soft dentine; non-selective removal not recommended (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020) |
| 2016 consensus (abstract) | biofilm control first; shallow or moderately deep lesions, selective removal to firm dentine; deep lesions, selective removal to soft dentine (Schwendicke et al, 2016, abstract only) |
**Cavity designs**
1. Site-and-size: Mount separates lesion description from cavity form, and its Size 1 is minimal surface cavitation with dentine involvement (Mount et al, 2016); Sikri calls it the basis for minimal cavity preparation, though advanced and extensive lesions do not fall under it, the classification is subjective and lesion size is not well defined (Sikri, 2020).
2. Pits only: with adhesive materials only the involved pits are removed and the rest sealed (Mount et al, 2016); the floor may be left untouched, since once sealed it will remineralise (Mount et al, 2016).
3. Preventive resin restoration limits preparation to carious pits and fissures, an average PRR occupying 5% of the occlusal surface against 25% for an average occlusal amalgam (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant now terms it a conservative composite restoration (Ritter et al, 2019).
4. Tunnel: Mount calls it the simplest and most conservative approach when the enamel lesion is at least 3.00 mm apical to the ridge crest, and illustrates a 12-year case (Mount et al, 2016); Sikri says studies of tunnels have not shown good results (Sikri, 2020); Summitt says it remains largely unused, 21% of tunnel restorations failing in 7 years against none of the slot amalgams in the study it cites, 46% survival against 76% for Class 2 composite in another, and tunnels not indicated (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant does not support it (Ritter et al, 2019).
**Techniques**
1. ART: hand excavation and GIC, first tried in the mid-1980s (Sikri, 2020); the steps are isolation, access with hand instruments, excavation of soft dentine, conditioning, GIC filling and finger pressure (Sikri, 2020); it needs no sophisticated equipment and, in routine, no lining or base material (Sikri, 2020); Pickard describes single-step ART with occlusal resurfacing of the GIC possibly needed after 2 to 3 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Summitt calls it minimally invasive and highly acceptable, recommended as a definitive restoration for children and fearful adults (Hilton et al, 2013); Sturdevant says it is not recommended in routine clinical use and is restricted to areas with limited access to dental treatment (Ritter et al, 2019).
2. Hall technique (thin sources; Pickard mentions it only in passing, Banerjee and Watson, 2015): a crown cemented with GIC over a carious primary molar, with no anaesthetic, caries removal or tooth reduction (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016; Sikri, 2020). The 10-year review abstract reports success of 99% (UK) and 100% (Germany) at 1 year (Innes et al, 2017, abstract only).
3. ITR (one textbook): caries control before definitive restoration, with GIC and maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013).
4. Air abrasion was developed in 1945 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sturdevant says it cannot produce well-defined margins (Ritter et al, 2019).
5. Chemo-mechanical: Pickard describes a 0.1% hypochlorite-based gel (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Sikri describes Carisolv with 0.5% sodium hypochlorite (Sikri, 2020).
6. Lasers: Sikri notes explosive subsurface expansion of water as the mechanism (Sikri, 2020).
7. Pickard states that no perfect instrument exists (Banerjee and Watson, 2015), and that magnification is an essential prerequisite for high-quality minimally invasive operative dentistry (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 8. Repair, refurbishment and maintenance
1. The 5Rs are review, refurbish, reseal, repair and replace (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
2. Replacement is indicated when over 50% of a restoration has failed or the tooth is compromised; repairing failing portions is the more conservative option (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
3. Margin stain is not an indicator of recurrent caries, so staining alone does not justify replacement (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Refurbishment can take only a few minutes (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Resealing is application of sealant to a non-carious defective margin gap (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
4. Amalgam repair strength is only 30% to 60% of unrepaired amalgam (Hilton et al, 2013).
5. Each replacement loses tooth structure (Sikri, 2020), and Sturdevant calls the result the cycle of re-restoration (Ritter et al, 2019).
6. Pickard gives "often quoted" average ages at replacement of 10 to 15 years for amalgam, 5 to 8 for composite and 3 to 5 for GIC, adds that a precise figure is impossible and arguably irrelevant (Banerjee and Watson, 2015), and says how long restorations last matters less than preserving the tooth and pulp (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 9. Materials
| Material | Statement |
|---|---|
| GIC | fluoride reservoir, though contrary evidence argues the release is of subclinical value after initial placement (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Mount: release present over at least seven years, ideal in a high caries rate (Mount et al, 2016); not universally recommended for long-term stress-bearing sites (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Composite | polymerisation shrinkage 1.5-3.5% by volume (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); 1-2% with careful incremental placement (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Compomer | Sturdevant sees likely no indications (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| Amalgam | restricted to large load-bearing restorations (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); Mount: seals its own margins by corrosion and can last for most if not all of a patient's life (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Calcium silicate | Biodentine can be termed bioactive (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
| Retention features | amalgam retention is not particularly minimally invasive (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); adhesive materials need none (Sikri, 2020) |
Pickard adds that GIC fluoride release is high initially and falls away 8 to 10 weeks after placement (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). GIC types and ART materials are covered in GIC Q09 and P5.

### 10. Merits and limitations
**Merits:** easier void-free placement, better moisture control, a strengthened crown and increased longevity of the tooth (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). ART needs no sophisticated equipment (Sikri, 2020). Mount describes glass-ionomers as bioactive and very conservative of tooth structure (Mount et al, 2016).
**Limitations:** operative MID alone does not provide the actual cure (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); success depends on patient responsibility (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); caries risk assessment is inevitably a best guess (Hilton et al, 2013); minimal intervention can become a new buzzword, with no realistic fees for caries control in England and Wales at press time (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); tunnel and Hall evidence is thin, as above.

| Issue | One source | Another source |
|---|---|---|

| Stepwise removal and re-entry | two stages (Mount et al, 2016); soft then firm dentine 6 to 12 months later (Torres and Schwendicke, 2020) | one stage, no re-entry, fewer exposures (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); no benefit of re-entry (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| CPP-ACP | favourable short-term effect, trials needed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); effective with regular use, fluoride not separated (Ritter et al, 2019) | recommended against at 10% (Slayton et al, 2018, abstract only) |
| Tunnel | simplest, most conservative at 3.00 mm or more from the ridge crest (Mount et al, 2016) | not supported (Ritter et al, 2019); not indicated (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| SDF strength | about 30% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) | 38% (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019) |
| ART | restricted to limited-access areas, not for routine use (Ritter et al, 2019) | definitive restoration for children and fearful adults (Hilton et al, 2013) |

### 12. Future
1. Detection: perhaps the ideal device would detect biochemical activity changes before damage (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); near-infrared transillumination images are used to classify proximal lesion stages and link them to treatment (Ferreira Zandona and Longbottom, 2019); Sikri asks for on-the-spot activity assessment (Sikri, 2020).
2. Air abrasion: new powders are being investigated for self-limiting dentine removal (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
3. Chair-side scanners, being developed to measure tooth-wear surface change over time, may prove useful in practice (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
4. Technologies such as lasers, enzymes, photoactive disinfection and ozone suffer from a lack of clinical research (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Fluorescence lifetime imaging for dentine zones is a research direction (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
5. Resin infiltration is an emerging alternative (Hilton et al, 2013).
6. The Guide's foreword, written by a guest writer, hopes teachers will revise curricula and funders will review their systems (Wilson, 2015).
7. The 2012 FDI task group abstract names key behaviours as sugar intake not more than five times daily and twice-daily fluoridated toothpaste (Frencken et al, 2012, abstract only).

### 13. Clinical relevance and conclusion
In practice the sequence is identify, control, restore and recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). A patient with two or more new, progressing or restored lesions in 2 years is high risk and is given active care (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). A cavitated lesion is restored with the smallest adhesive restoration that removes infected tissue and leaves a sound peripheral seal (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). A failing restoration is first reviewed, refurbished, resealed or repaired before replacement is considered (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Recall is based on risk and is not standardised at 6 months (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
**Conclusion:** Minimal invasive dentistry shifts care from restoring teeth to controlling disease, keeping the operative part small and repairable (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Minimally invasive operative dentistry repairs and/or restores damaged tooth structure directly to maintain pulp vitality, function and aesthetics (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Minimal intervention dentistry deals with the cause of disease; minimal invasive dentistry is merely a component of it (Sikri, 2020).
- "Extension for prevention" has changed to "prevention for extension" (Sikri, 2020).
- Pickard's four phases are identify, control and prevent, restore minimally invasively, recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Sikri's seven components run from early diagnosis to repair rather than replacement (Sikri, 2020).
- Pickard: high risk is two or more new, progressing and/or restored lesions in 2 years; Sikri: two or more in the last year (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020).
- Sharp probes are contraindicated for lesion detection (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Infected dentine is never retained at the enamel-dentine junction; affected dentine over the pulp may stay (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- The 5Rs: review, refurbish, reseal, repair, replace; replace when over 50% has failed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Tunnel restorations: four textbook positions, none ranked here (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
- Hall technique and ITR rest on thin sources (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013).
- The origin of the term is not stated in the books read; the FDI review is cited from its abstract only (Tyas et al, 2000, abstract only).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Defining minimal invasive dentistry as only small cavities and leaving out risk assessment, prevention and recall (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020).
2. Using the same word for minimal intervention and minimal invasive dentistry without saying how Sikri separates them (Sikri, 2020).
3. Stating one figure for high-risk definition, SDF strength or composite shrinkage when the books give different figures (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020; Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016).
4. Writing that the term was coined by a named author, when the books read do not state who first used it.
5. Presenting tunnels, Hall technique or ITR as settled when the sources are thin or disagree (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019).
6. Leaving out the future section: detection, remineralisation, new materials and repair (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
