## STUDY MODE

### S1. Interim therapeutic restoration (evidence: one textbook) [technique]
- Hilton et al: used as part of caries management when caries control is needed before definitive restorations, for early childhood caries, rampant caries, uncooperative patients and patients with special health care needs (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Caries is removed with hand or low-speed rotary instruments without exposing the pulp, then restored with conventional or resin-modified glass ionomer (Hilton et al, 2013). It is applicable mainly to single-surface or small two-surface restorations and must be accompanied by maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S2. Hall technique (evidence: two books and an abstract) [technique]
- Sikri: preformed metallic crowns cemented with glass-ionomer luting cement over carious primary molars; tooth preparation and caries excavation are not required (Sikri, 2020).
- Kidd and Fejerskov: stainless steel crown on a cavitated, symptomless, vital deciduous tooth, no local anaesthetic, no caries removal, no tooth reduction; the bite is high for 2 to 4 weeks, then re-establishes (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- Kidd and Fejerskov state that trials showed excellent results and that children preferred it (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Abstract only: a 2017 paper reports randomised trials finding it superior to the comparators (Innes et al, 2017).

### S3. Atraumatic restorative treatment (several books) [technique]
- Hand instruments alone remove carious tissue, avoiding anaesthesia and expensive equipment; the cavity is restored with glass ionomer (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Kidd and Fejerskov: occlusal restorations are usually successful but approximal ones tend to be lost or fracture; restoration loss is not necessarily a failure provided the remaining hole is kept clean (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Banerjee and Watson: occlusal resurfacing may be needed after 2 to 3 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S4. Survival figures, side by side [prognosis]
Anusavice et al (Phillips): 99% at 1 year and 88% at 3 years for partially and fully retained single-surface restorations in permanent teeth; 66.1% for ART against 57.0% for amalgam at 6.3 years (Anusavice et al, 2013). Sakaguchi et al (Craig): 93% single-surface and 62% multi-surface in primary teeth at 2 years; 80% at 5 years for single-surface in permanent teeth (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). These come from different studies and are not reconciled.

### S5. Where the books differ [prognosis]
Hilton et al say ART has a place in the industrialized world and recommend it as a definitive restoration for children and fearful adults (Hilton et al, 2013); Kidd and Fejerskov say it has invaluable applications in high income countries (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); Ritter et al say it is not recommended for routine use and is restricted to areas with limited access (Ritter et al, 2019).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 18 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Each option is described with its source; ITR rests on one textbook and the Hall technique on two (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020; Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).

### 2. Interim therapeutic restoration (one textbook)
- **Use:** caries control before definitive restoration in early childhood caries, rampant caries, uncooperative patients and special needs (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Steps:** remove caries with hand or low-speed rotary instruments without exposing the pulp; restore with conventional or resin-modified glass ionomer (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Limits:** mainly single-surface or small two-surface; maintenance care is needed (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 3. Hall technique (two books)
- Preformed metallic crown cemented with glass-ionomer luting cement over a carious primary molar (Sikri, 2020).
- No local anaesthetic, caries removal or tooth reduction; occlusion re-establishes in 2 to 4 weeks (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- Trials are said to show excellent results (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016); a 2017 paper (abstract only) reports superiority over comparators (Innes et al, 2017).

### 4. Atraumatic restorative treatment
- Hand excavation, weak acid conditioning, high-viscosity glass ionomer pressed with a finger (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Advantages: no electricity, minimal discomfort, low cost (Sikri, 2020).
- Occlusal ones usually succeed; approximal ones tend to fail, but loss is not a failure if the hole is kept clean (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).

### 5. Survival figures (different studies)
| Source | Figure |
|---|---|
| Anusavice et al, 2013 | 99% at 1 year, 88% at 3 years, partially and fully retained single-surface, permanent teeth |
| Sakaguchi et al, 2019 | 93% single-surface, 62% multi-surface, primary teeth, 2 years |

### 6. Choice and limits
Techniques can be combined: ART for occlusal lesions; for larger approximal lesions, opening for cleaning or a Hall crown (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016). Hilton et al accept ART in industrialized countries and as a definitive restoration for children and fearful adults; Kidd and Fejerskov see invaluable uses in high income countries; Ritter et al restrict it to limited-access areas (Hilton et al, 2013; Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016; Ritter et al, 2019).

### 7. Conclusion
ITR and ART restore with glass ionomer; the Hall technique seals without excavation; evidence levels differ (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020).
