## STUDY MODE

### Frame
"Justify the role" = state each category, then give the clinical circumstances in which one is rationally preferred.

### Direct tooth-coloured restorations (composite, glass ionomer, compomer)
Single visit, conservative, repairable, lower cost; limited by polymerisation shrinkage and stress at the interface, operator-dependent contacts and contour, and wear. Anterior composite Class I restorations average 5-8 years with occlusal wear limiting; well-executed ones have lasted over 15 years; marginal staining makes failures visible early (Goldstein et al, 2018, via digest).

### Indirect tooth-coloured restorations (ceramic, indirect composite, resin-matrix)
Fabricated outside the mouth; indicated when more than half of tooth structure (especially dentin support) is lost or a wide proximal area is involved; when strength, accurate cusp planes and contacts are needed; when a directly placed restoration repeatedly fractured; patient-driven amalgam replacement; protection of root-filled posterior teeth with onlays; patients who cannot sit long (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest).

### Comparison
| Feature | Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|---|
| Visits | One | Two (or one with CAD/CAM) |
| Polymerisation shrinkage | In the cavity | Mostly on the die (laboratory composite) or none (ceramic); only the luting cement shrinks |
| Degree of conversion | Lower | Higher with heat and pressure (e.g. 140 °C, 0.6 MPa, 10 min) |
| Contact and anatomy | Operator-dependent | Controlled on a model |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |
| Tooth removal | Minimal | Slightly more (taper, no undercuts) |
| Repair | Easy | Easier for composite; difficult for ceramic |
| Wear | Mostly within enamel range (30 μm/yr molar, 15 μm/yr premolar) | Product-dependent; ceramic may abrade antagonists |
| Survival | Composite Class I 5-8 years average | Ceramic inlay about 90-92% at 8 years; indirect composite 5% failure at 4-6 years |
(Anusavice et al, 2013; Hilton et al, 2013; Goldstein et al, 2018, via digest; Guess et al, 2011; Zhang and Kelly, 2017.)

### Important honesty point
Indirect composite is not categorically better than direct composite: two clinical studies found no significant difference between composite inlays and direct composites at 5 and 11 years (Hilton et al, 2013, via digest), and in minimal cavities direct composite is more appropriate (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest).

### Biological and esthetic considerations
Ceramic inlays strengthen tooth through bonding; indirect restorations give better proximal contact; direct composite has lower cost and is reparable; both need adhesive technique. Esthetic zone: ceramic veneers/crowns versus direct composite layering.

### Decision
Size of loss, load, esthetics, isolation, patient factors, cost and operator skill.

### Mount's decision framework (pp.217-221), confirmed directly this session — a concise, directly quotable comparison
Mount frames the choice as a conservatism-versus-reversibility distinction that is easy to miss: **a laminate veneer is conservative of remaining tooth structure but is NOT reversible** — placing a veneer removes at least half the labial enamel thickness, an irreversible commitment even though the absolute amount of tissue removed is small. Mount's broader framework: **composite resin** is indicated for minor, single-tooth or few-tooth restorations; **porcelain** (veneer, inlay/onlay, crown) is indicated once the restoration becomes multiple-teeth or more extensive in scope; and a **partial-coverage crown or onlay is the most conservative extracoronal option** when full coverage is not yet required — i.e. the conservatism ranking, from least to most tissue removed for a given clinical problem, generally runs composite → veneer/partial coverage → full crown, but the veneer's "conservative" status is about tissue volume, not about reversibility [SRC, MOUNT].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim — indirect restorations are justified by cavity size and load, not by a general superiority.** More than half of tooth structure lost, wide proximal areas and cuspal protection favour indirect (Sikri, 2017, via digest); in small and moderate cavities, indirect composite showed no significant advantage over direct at 5 and 11 years (Hilton et al, 2013, via digest). INTERPRETATION: choose indirect when mechanics and contacts cannot be reliably achieved directly. LIMITATION: studies are small and heterogeneous and generally not randomised head-to-head with ceramic. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 760; about 35 min)**

### Introduction
Tooth-coloured restorations may be placed directly (composite, glass ionomer) or made indirectly (ceramic, laboratory composite, resin-matrix). Each has a role.

### Direct restorations
- **Merits:** conservative, one visit, cheaper, repairable, easy to modify.
- **Demerits:** polymerisation shrinkage stress, operator-dependent contacts and contours, wear and staining, technique-sensitive in large cavities.
- **Indications:** small to moderate lesions, class III/IV/V, cases where cost or time limits.

### Indirect restorations
- **Merits:** better contact, contour and occlusal anatomy; higher strength and wear resistance; polymerisation shrinkage outside the mouth; high degree of conversion (composite) or none (ceramic); bonding strengthens the tooth (ceramic); durability (ceramic inlays about 90% at 8 years).
- **Demerits:** more visits, cost, laboratory dependence, more tooth reduction (taper), brittle (ceramic), technique-sensitive cementation, repair difficult (ceramic).
- **Indications:** loss of more than half the tooth structure; wide proximal area; cusp protection (onlay), root-filled molars; failed direct restorations; amalgam replacement; high esthetic and anatomic demands.

### Comparison table
| Criterion | Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|---|
| Visits | 1 | 2 (1 with CAD/CAM) |
| Shrinkage | In tooth | On die/none |
| Anatomy and contacts | Variable | Predictable |
| Strength | Moderate | Higher |
| Cost | Low | High |
| Repair | Easy | Composite easy; ceramic hard |
| Tooth reduction | Minimal | Taper required |

### Selection
Small to moderate: direct. Large, cuspal replacement, or poor-access contacts: indirect. Esthetic zone with discolouration: ceramic veneers or crowns; localised: direct composite. Economic or paediatric: direct.

### Mount's decision framework (MOUNT pp.217-221)
Composite resin: minor, single/few-tooth restorations. Porcelain (veneer, inlay/onlay, crown): multiple-teeth or more extensive restoration. Partial-coverage crown/onlay: the most conservative extracoronal option when full coverage is not yet needed. Important distinction: a laminate veneer is conservative of tooth structure but NOT reversible — it removes at least half the labial enamel thickness.

### Conclusion
Neither is superior in all situations; indirect restorations are justified when direct methods cannot restore strength, contacts and anatomy predictably, and direct restorations are preferred for conservative, localised lesions.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Indirect when more than half of the tooth is lost or proximal area wide.
- Shrinkage on the die; higher conversion.
- No proven categorical superiority of indirect composite over direct.
- Ceramic inlay about 90% at 8 years.
- Mount: veneer is conservative of tissue but NOT reversible (removes ≥half labial enamel thickness); composite for minor/few-tooth; porcelain for multiple-teeth/extensive; partial-coverage crown/onlay is the most conservative extracoronal option.
