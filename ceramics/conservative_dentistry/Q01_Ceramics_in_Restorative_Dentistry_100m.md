## STUDY MODE

### 1. Why this is a 100-mark essay
The examiner expects a full account: definition and history; classification; microstructure and strengthening; each system with properties; fabrication; clinical properties and performance; failure and design principles; indications and selection; preparation, bonding and cementation; recent advances; and a critical conclusion. The unifying idea is the **strength-versus-optics-versus-bonding trade-off** governed by microstructure.

### 2. Definition and history
A ceramic is a non-metallic inorganic material processed by firing at high temperature to achieve its properties (Moshaverinia, 2020). Porcelain is the narrower kaolin-quartz-feldspar family; zirconia and lithium disilicate are ceramics but not porcelains (study-material `SubModule_2.2`, via Craig's 14th ed., p.209).
Landmarks: porcelain dentures by Duchateau and de Chemant (1774); porcelain inlays, onlays and crowns by Charles Land in 1886; the original porcelain was extremely brittle (about 60 MPa); vacuum firing (1949); in 1962 Weinstein and colleagues solved the CTE mismatch between porcelain and metal with a leucite-containing porcelain, creating the metal-ceramic system; pressing and CAD/CAM arrived in the 1980s (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). By 2011 an estimated 70-80% of fixed prostheses in the United States were still metal-ceramic (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).

### 3. Classification
**By composition** (Nejat, 2025; Moshaverinia, 2020): glass-matrix (feldspathic, leucite-reinforced, lithium disilicate, lithium silicate, zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate), polycrystalline (alumina, zirconia), resin-matrix (PICN, resin nanoceramics). Glass-ceramics, glass-infiltrated ceramics (ceramic-glass interpenetrating network) and ceramic-resin interpenetrating network are further microstructural classes used by Zhang and Kelly (2017).
**By fabrication** (Nejat, 2025; Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest): layering/sintering, slip-casting and glass infiltration, casting, heat-pressing, subtractive CAD/CAM and additive manufacturing.
**By application:** veneering ceramic for metal or ceramic cores, all-ceramic cores, monolithic restorations.
**By crystallinity** (Powers and Wataha, 2013, via digest): glassy → glass-dominated → crystalline-dominated (glass-infused) → crystalline.
**By clinical indication:** ISO 6872 classifies by indication, because core fracture toughness tracks clinical success (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).

### 4. Microstructure and strengthening
- **Dispersion strengthening:** crystals in glass deflect cracks. Fracture toughness rises from 0.75 (soda-lime glass) to 1.3 (about 34 vol% leucite, IPS Empress) to 3.3 MPa·m^0.5 (about 70 vol% interlocked lithium disilicate) (Anusavice et al, 2013, via digest).
- **Ceraming:** controlled heat treatment nucleates and grows crystals in a glass (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).
- **Interpenetrating networks:** a porous alumina scaffold infiltrated with lanthanum glass (In-Ceram), about 70 vol% alumina, about 550 MPa (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).
- **Transformation toughening:** in 3Y-TZP, stress at a crack tip transforms tetragonal to monoclinic grains with a volume increase of about 4%, placing the crack under compression (Guess et al, 2011; Anusavice et al, 2013, via digest).
- **Resin-ceramic networks:** Enamic is 86 wt% (75 vol%) feldspathic network with infiltrated dimethacrylate polymer, giving a modulus nearer dentin (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).

### 6. Fabrication
- **Powder-slurry layering and sintering:** condensation, vibration, firing; sintering shrinkage up to 10% linear for stacked ceramics (Powers and Wataha, 2013, via digest).
- **Slip-casting and glass infiltration:** alumina slip (>90% alumina, 0.5-3.5 μm) on a refractory die, fired 4 h at 1100 °C, infiltrated with lanthanum glass at 1150 °C for 4 h; about 450 MPa (Craig, 2002, via digest); In-Ceram Spinell (translucent, weaker) and In-Ceram Zirconia (stronger, more opaque).
- **Casting:** Dicor, obsolete.
- **Heat-pressing:** lost-wax; IPS e.max Press ingots are pressed at 920 °C for 20 min (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).
- **CAD/CAM milling:** e.max CAD precrystallised (lithium metasilicate) blocks are crystallised at 840 °C for 7 min (Zhang and Kelly, 2017; Nejat, 2025 states above 750 °C); zirconia is milled presintered and sintered with about 25% shrinkage compensated by enlargement (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest).
- **Additive:** stereolithography/DLP of zirconia and lithium disilicate; promising but inconsistent (Nejat, 2025; Bacchi and Cesar, 2022).

### 7. Optical and biological properties
Glassy ceramics best mimic enamel optics; translucency falls as crystal content and grain birefringence rise; tetragonal zirconia is birefringent so scatters light; sub-100 nm grains would be needed for translucency at full strength, which is hard to achieve, so cubic zirconia is introduced, weakening the material (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). Underlying stump colour shows through feldspathic ceramics most and zirconia least (Goldstein et al, 2018, via digest). Ceramics are inert, with low plaque affinity when polished and low thermal conductivity (Moshaverinia, 2020; Nejat, 2025).

### 8. Wear
Rough glazed ceramic abrades enamel; polished zirconia produced less enamel wear than glass-ceramics, and surface roughness predicts antagonist wear; clinical enamel wear against zirconia was similar to or greater than against natural teeth but lower than against glass-ceramic (Bacchi and Cesar, 2022).

### 9. Clinical performance
- Feldspathic: inlays/onlays 92% at 8 years, veneers 94% at 12, crowns 95% at 11 (Zhang and Kelly, 2017); feldspathic veneers 95.5% at 10 years (Nejat, 2025; Blatz et al, 2022); inlays/onlays 88.7% at up to 17 years (Blatz et al, 2022).
- Empress inlays/onlays 90% at 8 years; veneers 94.4% at 12; crowns 95.2% at 11; Empress 2 crowns 95.5% at 10; 3-unit Empress 2 FDPs 70% at 5 years because of connector fracture (Guess et al, 2011). Empress 2 FDPs failed in 9-50% at 24-60 months (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).
- Lithium disilicate: long-term data support single restorations anywhere in the mouth and short-span anterior FDPs (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). Cautious for FDPs (Nejat, 2025).
- Dicor molar crowns failed at 4-6% a year (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).
- Veneered zirconia: chipping and delamination 6-10% at 3-5 years in single crowns and 20-32% at 5-10 years in FDPs versus 2.7-6% up to 15 years for metal-framework prostheses (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). Core fractures 7% single crowns at 2 years (Guess et al, 2011).
- Monolithic zirconia crowns on natural teeth 91-100% survival; chipping 0% versus 7.61% for metal-ceramic implant crowns (Nejat, 2025).
- ZLS partial coverage 80% at 5 years; molars 69% (Nejat, 2025).

### 10. Failure modes and design principles (Zhang and Kelly, 2017)
Fracture is governed by toughness and strength (crack propagation vs initiation), and to a lesser extent by modulus and hardness. Principles: (i) **monolithic** structures avoid veneer chipping; (ii) **thickness**: thicker layers reduce flexure (thickness-squared relationship); (iii) **residual stress** from CTE mismatch and rapid cooling, especially over low-conductivity zirconia cores; (iv) **contact conditions**: avoid sharp cusps near edges; (v) **tooth size and shape**; (vi) **substrate modulus**: dentin about one fifth of enamel; cement 2-5 times lower; high-modulus build-ups and cements help; (vii) **surface state**: aggressive sandblasting or coarse-bur adjustment introduces flaws.

### 11. Indications and selection (Moshaverinia, 2020)
Position, design, strength demand, substrate and translucency. More than 50% enamel and no discolouration favours feldspathic; less than 50% or discolouration favours lithium disilicate; posterior and high stress favour zirconia or bonded lithium disilicate. Zirconia is bonded only when retention is compromised (under 3 mm height or over 20° convergence). Highly translucent monolithic ceramics need opaque luting agent for discoloured substrates (Bacchi and Cesar, 2022).

### 12. Preparation (Sikri, 2017, via digest)
Axial reduction 1.0-1.5 mm, incisal/occlusal 1.0-2.0 mm, interocclusal clearance 1.5-2.0 mm, chamfer rather than rounded shoulder (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest); taper about 10° for resin-luted restorations; 1.0 mm uniform shoulder for all-ceramic; rounded line angles. Minimum thickness by material (manufacturer data): lithium disilicate crown 1.0-1.5 mm, 3Y zirconia 0.4-0.5 mm, 4Y/5Y 0.8-1.0 mm (Nejat, 2025).

### 13. Bonding and cementation
Ideal bond is dual: micromechanical and chemical (Blatz et al, 2022).
| Ceramic | Treatment (Blatz et al, 2022, Table 1) |
|---|---|
| Feldspathic | 9.8% HF 2 min; rinse; ultrasonic alcohol 5 min; silane 20 s |
| Leucite | 9.8% HF 1 min; silane |
| Lithium silicate/disilicate | 4.6% HF 20 s; silane |
| PICN | 9.8% HF 1 min; silane |
| Y-TZP | Air abrasion 50 μm alumina at 2 bar; phosphate-monomer (MDP) primer; no HF |
Sikri (2017, via digest) quotes 8-12% HF for 1-3 min (feldspathic longer); Nejat (2025) tabulates 5% HF for 20-60 s by manufacturer; the concentration and time depend on the ceramic's glass and crystal content and the manufacturer's instructions (Blatz et al, 2022). Dual-cure resin cement is preferred for posterior and thick restorations; light-cure for veneers for colour stability and working time (Blatz et al, 2022). Glass ionomer cementation of ceramics gives weaker restorations than resin (Sikri, 2017, via digest). Zirconia full-coverage crowns with adequate retention can be cemented conventionally (Blatz et al, 2022).

### 14. Limitations and unsettled issues
Brittleness, bonding technique sensitivity, repair difficulty, low-temperature degradation of zirconia, short follow-up for newer materials, and the unproven integrity of multilayer zirconia structures (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1 — "all-ceramic" is not one material class: failure mode depends on the system.** Veneered zirconia fails by veneer chipping (6-10% single crowns, 20-32% FDPs at 5-10 years), glass-ceramic FDPs by connector fracture (Empress 2: 9-50% at 2-5 years), and bonded feldspathic and lithium disilicate single units perform at 90-95% at about a decade (Zhang and Kelly, 2017; Guess et al, 2011; Nejat, 2025). INTERPRETATION: the clinical question is not "ceramic or metal" but "which structure, which thickness, which support". LIMITATION: the cited trials differ in definition of failure, operator, ceramic generation and follow-up, so pooled comparison is not strictly valid. [Likely]

**Claim 2 — translucency and strength trade against each other, and new zirconias move the trade-off rather than remove it.** Cubic-rich zirconia is more translucent but about half as strong and tough as 3Y-TZP (609 MPa and 2.4 vs 1200 MPa and 4.0), and mostly remains opaque unless under 0.5 mm (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). Gradient discs are mechanically unproven (Zhang and Kelly, 2017); one fatigue study found strength-graded zirconia better than 4Y (Bacchi and Cesar, 2022). INTERPRETATION: monolithic high-translucency zirconia suits low-load and anterior use; 3Y zirconia remains the choice where strength dominates. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 3,300; about 150 min)**

### Introduction
A dental ceramic is a non-metallic inorganic material processed by firing at high temperature. Ceramics are the principal tooth-coloured indirect restorative materials because of their esthetics, biocompatibility and chemical durability, but they are brittle. This essay covers history, classification, microstructure, systems, fabrication, properties, performance, design, selection, bonding and advances.

### History
- 1774 Duchateau and de Chemant: first porcelain dentures.
- 1886 Land: porcelain inlays, onlays and crowns (about 60 MPa, brittle).
- 1949 vacuum firing; 1962 Weinstein: leucite porcelain matched to alloy CTE, giving the metal-ceramic crown.
- 1960s alumina reinforcement; 1980s pressing and CAD/CAM; 1990s In-Ceram, Procera, Empress 2; 2000s zirconia; 2010s translucent zirconia, resin-matrix and printed ceramics.

### Classification
1. **Composition:** glass-matrix (feldspathic, leucite, lithium disilicate, ZLS), polycrystalline (alumina, zirconia), resin-matrix (PICN, nanoceramic).
2. **Fabrication:** layering/sintering, slip-casting with infiltration, casting, pressing, milling, printing.
3. **Application:** veneering, core, monolithic.
4. **Crystallinity spectrum:** glassy to fully crystalline.

### Strengthening mechanisms
| Mechanism | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Dispersion strengthening | Leucite, lithium disilicate | Crack deflection; KIc 0.75 → 1.3 → 3.3 |
| Ceraming | Dicor, e.max | Controlled crystal growth |
| Interpenetrating network | In-Ceram | Alumina scaffold plus glass; about 550 MPa |
| Transformation toughening | 3Y-TZP | Tetragonal to monoclinic, about 4% expansion |
| Resin-ceramic network | Enamic | Dentin-like modulus |

### Systems and properties
| Material | Strength (MPa) | Toughness (MPa·m^0.5) | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feldspathic | 60-122 | 0.8-1.2 | Veneers, inlays |
| Leucite | 120-140 | 1.2-1.3 | Anterior crowns, veneers |
| Lithium disilicate | 306-480 | 2.3-3.2 | Inlays to molar crowns, short anterior FDP |
| In-Ceram alumina | 548 | 3.6 | Copings |
| 3Y zirconia | 900-1200 | 4-6 | Crowns, FDPs |
| Cubic zirconia | 485-609 | 2.4 | Monolithic anterior |
| PICN | 159 | 1.3 | Single units, chairside |
(Values from Zhang and Kelly 2017 and Sakaguchi et al 2019; sources differ by method.)

### Fabrication
Sintering with condensation; slip-casting and glass infiltration (alumina slip fired 4 h at 1100 °C, glass at 1150 °C); casting (Dicor); heat-pressing (e.max Press at 920 °C); CAD/CAM (precrystallised lithium disilicate crystallised at about 840 °C; presintered zirconia sinters with about 25% shrinkage); additive manufacturing.

### Properties
- **Optical:** best natural mimicry from glassy ceramics; translucency falls with crystal content; zirconia masks stump best.
- **Mechanical:** high compressive strength, low tensile strength and toughness; flaw-sensitive.
- **Biological:** inert; low plaque affinity; low thermal conductivity.
- **Wear:** polish; rough ceramic abrades enamel.

### Clinical performance
| System | Result |
|---|---|
| Feldspathic inlay/onlay, veneer, crown | 92% at 8 years, 94% at 12, 95% at 11 |
| Empress 2 crown | 95.5% at 10 years |
| 3-unit Empress 2 FDP | 70% at 5 years (connector) |
| Veneered zirconia | Chipping 6-10% single, 20-32% FDP |
| Monolithic zirconia crown | 91-100% |
| Metal-ceramic | 2.7-6% fracture up to 15 years |

### Design principles for survival
Monolithic where possible; adequate thickness; supported veneer; slow cooling and framework design; blunt cusps away from margins; high-modulus build-up and cement; avoid aggressive sandblasting and coarse-bur adjustment; polish after adjustment.

### Indications and selection
| Situation | Material |
|---|---|
| Anterior, more than 50% enamel, no discolouration | Feldspathic or leucite |
| Less than 50% enamel or discoloured | Lithium disilicate |
| Posterior single, high load | Bonded lithium disilicate or zirconia |
| FDP | Zirconia |
| Highly discoloured stump | Veneered zirconia or opaque cement |
| Chairside single visit | CAD/CAM block, PICN |

### Preparation
1.0-1.5 mm axial, 1.0-2.0 mm incisal/occlusal, 1.5-2.0 mm clearance; chamfer or 1 mm shoulder; about 10° taper; rounded angles; uniform reduction; minimum thickness by material.

### Bonding and cementation
| Ceramic | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Feldspathic / leucite | 9.8% HF 2 / 1 min → ultrasonic alcohol → silane 20 s |
| Lithium disilicate | 4.6-5% HF 20 s → silane |
| Zirconia | 50 μm alumina at 2 bar → MDP primer (no HF) |
| PICN | HF 1 min → silane |
Tooth: 30-37% phosphoric acid → adhesive. Resin cement: dual-cure for posterior, light-cure for veneers.

### Recent advances
Translucent 4Y/5Y and graded zirconia; zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate; resin-matrix blocks; CAD-on; printed ceramics; digital shade analysis.

### Limitations
Brittleness, technique sensitivity, repair, wear, low-temperature degradation, unproven long-term data for newer materials.

### Conclusion
Ceramics have progressed from brittle feldspathic porcelain to a spectrum of engineered materials that cover most indications. Success depends on matching material microstructure to substrate, load and optics, designing for crack resistance, and following material-specific bonding.
