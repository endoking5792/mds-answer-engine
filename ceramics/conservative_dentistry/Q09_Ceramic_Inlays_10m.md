## STUDY MODE

### Definition
A ceramic inlay is an intracoronal indirect restoration of glass-ceramic or porcelain, adhesively bonded; an onlay covers cusps (Sikri, 2017, via digest).

### Materials and fabrication
Feldspathic (sintered on refractory die or CAD/CAM), leucite-reinforced (pressed or CAD), lithium disilicate (pressed or CAD). Refractory-die technique depends on technician skill; the pressed technique follows lost-wax steps and is more standardised (Hilton et al, 2013, via digest).

### Indications
Moderate to large cavities where direct composite is unreliable but cusps need not all be covered; esthetic demand; replacement of amalgam; accurate contact and anatomy (Sikri, 2017, via digest).

### Advantages
Esthetics; wear resistance; colour stability; biocompatibility; low plaque; adhesive bonding strengthens remaining tooth; no polymerisation shrinkage in the restoration; survival 90% at 8 years (Empress; Guess et al, 2011) and 92% at 8 years (Zhang and Kelly, 2017); feldspathic inlays/onlays 88.7% at up to 17 years (Blatz et al, 2022).

### Disadvantages
Brittle; technique-sensitive bonding and isolation; two visits (unless CAD/CAM); cost; can abrade antagonists if rough; marginal fracture on removal of excess cement; repair difficult.

### Preparation
Occlusal convergence about 10° (resin-luted), 10-20° posterior; wall height at least 3 mm (4 mm in molars); no undercuts; rounded internal angles (Sikri, 2017, via digest). All-ceramic depth 1.0-1.5 mm; minimum inlay/onlay thickness 1.0 mm (lithium disilicate) to 1.5 mm (feldspathic, leucite, ZLS) (Nejat, 2025).

**Cavity-design specifics, confirmed directly from SIKRI/STURD this session**: a ceramic inlay cavity needs a **uniform 2.0 mm thickness** of ceramic throughout (not a tapered/variable depth as for a metal inlay), walls with **10-12° divergence** from the axial wall for a single path of withdrawal, and explicitly **no bevels** on the cavosurface margin (confirming, not just assuming, the earlier "[Likely, not in sources read]" flag) — a bevel in brittle ceramic creates a thin, unsupported, fracture-prone edge. Internal line angles must be **rounded**, not sharp, for the same stress-concentration reason. Where a base is needed under the ceramic, **resin composite is recommended, not glass ionomer** — SIKRI explicitly states GIC is not preferred here because of its lower compressive strength and lower elastic modulus relative to resin composite [SRC, SIKRI/STURD].

### Cementation — full 12-step clinical sequence (SIKRI, confirmed directly this session)
1. Try-in (check fit, contacts, margins).
2. Rubber-dam isolation.
3. HF-etch the ceramic intaglio (feldspathic 9.8% 2 min; lithium disilicate 4.6% 20 s — Blatz et al, 2022; or SIKRI's own 8-12% HF 1-3 min, older protocol — present both, attributed).
4. Silane coupling agent.
5. Phosphoric-acid etch of the tooth (enamel and dentin per the adhesive system).
6. Apply dentin/enamel adhesive.
7. Load dual-cure (or light-cure for shallow inlays) resin cement.
8. Seat the restoration under firm, sustained occlusal closure/finger pressure.
9. Clean up gross excess cement before full cure.
10. Light-cure from multiple directions (facial, lingual, occlusal) for full depth of cure.
11. Apply a glycerin/oxygen-blocking gel over the margins before the final cure increment, to prevent an oxygen-inhibited uncured surface layer.
12. Minimal occlusal/marginal polish only — **avoid further intraoral adjustment of the fired ceramic surface after cementation** where possible, since grinding removes the glazed/polished surface and can introduce new flaws [SRC, SIKRI].

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 330; about 15 min)**

### Definition
Intracoronal indirect restoration of porcelain or glass-ceramic bonded to tooth; onlay if cusps are covered.

### Materials
Feldspathic, leucite-reinforced, lithium disilicate; sintered, pressed or CAD/CAM.

### Indications
Moderate to large cavities, esthetic need, amalgam replacement, need for good contact and occlusal anatomy.

### Advantages
- Esthetic, colour-stable, wear-resistant.
- Bonding strengthens the tooth.
- Good contact and anatomy.
- Survival about 90% at 8 years.

### Disadvantages
- Brittle; needs adequate thickness (1.0-1.5 mm).
- Technique and moisture sensitive.
- Costly; may abrade antagonists.
- Repair difficult.

### Preparation
Occlusal convergence 10-12° divergence of walls (SIKRI/STURD); walls at least 3 mm high; no undercuts; rounded internal angles; no bevel (confirmed, SIKRI/STURD); uniform depth — 2.0 mm uniform ceramic thickness (SIKRI/STURD), minimum material thickness 1.0-1.5 mm (Nejat); margins away from heavy occlusal contacts; resin-composite base, NOT glass ionomer, if a base is needed (SIKRI: GIC's lower compressive strength/modulus is a specific contraindication here).

### Cementation (12-step sequence, SIKRI)
Try-in → rubber dam → HF etch intaglio (feldspathic 9.8%/2min or older 8-12%/1-3min; lithium disilicate 4.6%/20s) → silane → phosphoric-acid etch tooth → adhesive → dual-cure (or light-cure if shallow) resin cement → seat under occlusal closure → clean up excess → multi-direction light-cure → glycerin/oxygen-block gel → minimal polish, avoid further intraoral adjustment of the ceramic.

### Conclusion
Ceramic inlays offer durable, esthetic, tooth-strengthening restorations when bonded under good isolation with sufficient thickness.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Must be bonded; resin cement, dual-cure.
- Uniform 2.0 mm ceramic thickness; 10-12° wall divergence; no bevels; rounded internal angles (SIKRI/STURD).
- Resin-composite base, not GIC, under a ceramic inlay (SIKRI: GIC compressive strength/modulus too low).
- 12-step cementation sequence (SIKRI): try-in → rubber dam → HF etch → silane → tooth etch → adhesive → cement → seat → cleanup → multi-direction cure → oxygen-block gel → minimal polish.
- Survival about 90% at 8 years.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Bevelling margins.
2. Cementing with zinc phosphate or GIC.
3. Not stating thickness or isolation.
