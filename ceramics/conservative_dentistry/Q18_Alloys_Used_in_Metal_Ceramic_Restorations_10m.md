## STUDY MODE

### S1. Requirements [requirements]
Anusavice et al: the alloy must bond to porcelain, have compatible thermal contraction, and a solidus high enough to resist softening during firing (Anusavice et al, 2013). Craig and Powers add a fusing temperature more than 100 C above the ceramic and high sag resistance (Craig and Powers, 2002); Bhat gives a solidus at least about 100 C above that of ceramics (Bhat et al, 2019).

### S2. Bonding [mechanism]
Gold does not form an oxide, so small amounts of oxide-forming elements are added (van Noort, 2013). Less than 1% of iron, indium or tin improved bond strength by a factor of three (Anusavice et al, 2013). Craig and Powers describe the bond as chemisorption between surface oxides and the ceramic (Craig and Powers, 2002). Powers and Wataha say "degassing" is a misnomer because no gas is evolved (Powers and Wataha, 2013). A slight contraction mismatch with the metal higher is recommended (Anusavice et al, 2013).
Thermal match, side by side: Craig and Powers print porcelain coefficients of 13.0 to 14.0 and metal 13.5 to 14.5, a difference of 0.5 (units not legible in the text copy) (Craig and Powers, 2002). Anusavice et al state that a coefficient difference of 1.7 x 10-6/K can produce a shear stress of 280 MPa in porcelain next to the interface on cooling from 954 C, and that systems differing by 0.5 x 10-6/K or less are thermally compatible (Anusavice et al, 2013).
Base-metal oxides: Craig and Powers state that NiO-rich oxides tend to be dark gray and Cr2O3-rich oxides greenish, and that alloys forming Cr2O3-rich layers typically need a bonding agent (Craig and Powers, 2002). A ceramic-metal bond may fail in any of three locations (Craig and Powers, 2002). The ceramic-metal joint should be as far as possible from contact with opposing teeth (Craig and Powers, 2002).
Silver "greening": Anusavice et al attribute it to colloidal silver entering the porcelain by vapor transport or surface diffusion, most severe with higher-silver alloys (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S3. Alloy families [classification]
Anusavice et al print three ADA categories (Table 16-1): high noble, at least 40% Au and at least 60% noble metal elements; noble, at least 25% noble metal elements; predominantly base metal, under 25% (Anusavice et al, 2013). Au-Pt-Pd alloys were the first successful, Pd-Ag the first gold-free noble alloy, and Pd-Cu-Ga gives a dark oxide hard to mask (Anusavice et al, 2013). Silver discoloration is most severe in Pd-Ag alloys (Anusavice et al, 2013). Base metal alloys allow thin copings (Anusavice et al, 2013); Phillips cautions that reducing a base metal coping from 0.3 to 0.1 mm may increase the risk of porcelain fracture (Anusavice et al, 2013). McCabe and Walls give a minimum coping thickness of around 0.5 mm for high-gold alloys (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
Captek: Anusavice et al print copings of 0.25 mm for anterior and 0.35 mm for posterior crowns (Anusavice et al, 2013). McCabe and Walls describe a sintered high-palladium network infiltrated with 97% pure gold, bonded to porcelain mechanically (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### S4. Hazards [safety]
Beryllium, below 0.9% in some Ni-Cr alloys, is released during grinding at 5 to 6 times the bulk composition; nickel is a recognised allergen (van Noort, 2013). Beryllium content differs by book: Craig and Powers print 1.6% to 2.0% Be, about 11 atomic percent (Craig and Powers, 2002); Phillips gives an example alloy of 1.8 wt% Be equal to 10.7 atomic percent (Anusavice et al, 2013).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 17 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Metal-ceramic alloys carry fired porcelain, so they need properties beyond those of all-metal casting alloys (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 2. Requirements
- Bond to porcelain through an oxide; thermal contraction compatible; solidus above the firing temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Margin of solidus: more than 100 C (Craig and Powers, 2002); at least about 100 C (Bhat et al, 2019).
- High modulus and low creep (van Noort, 2013).
- Biocompatible, corrosion resistant, castable, low cost (van Noort, 2013).

### 3. Classification and alloys
| Class | Alloys and points |
|---|---|
| High noble (at least 40% Au, 60% noble) | Au-Pt-Pd (first successful; low sag resistance), Au-Pd-Ag, Au-Pd (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Noble (at least 25% noble) | Pd-Ag (silver discoloration most severe), Pd-Cu-Ga (dark oxide), Pd-Ga-Ag (silver below 5%) (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Predominantly base metal (under 25% noble) | Ni-Cr, Co-Cr, commercially pure titanium (Anusavice et al, 2013) |

### 4. Coping thickness (books differ)
| Book | Statement |
|---|---|
| Anusavice et al, 2013 | base metal 0.1 to 0.2 mm; 0.3 to 0.1 mm may raise porcelain fracture risk; Captek 0.25 mm anterior, 0.35 mm posterior |
| van Noort, 2013 | high gold 0.5 mm; Ni-Cr-Mo 0.3 mm |
| McCabe and Walls, 2008 | high gold minimum around 0.5 mm |

### 5. Bonding and thermal match
- Oxide-formers iron, indium, tin added to noble alloys (Anusavice et al, 2013); van Noort names none (van Noort, 2013).
- 1.7 x 10-6/K mismatch can give 280 MPa shear stress in porcelain (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- "Degassing" forms oxide; no gas is removed (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- Metal contraction slightly above porcelain (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Ni-Cr fractures occur through the oxide layer (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### 6. Special points
- Base metals: greater solidification shrinkage; type V die stone and more investment expansion (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Titanium: poor bond above 800 C; coefficient 9.6 ppm per C needs special ceramics (van Noort, 2013).
- Beryllium and nickel hazards; Be below 0.9% (van Noort, 2013), 1.6% to 2.0% (Craig and Powers, 2002).

### 7. Conclusion
Choice balances oxide bond, thermal match, cost and safety (Anusavice et al, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Less than 1% Fe, In or Sn trebled bond strength (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Metal contraction slightly higher than porcelain (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Be below 0.9%, release 5 to 6 times bulk (van Noort, 2013).
- Ti: 9.6 ppm per C, poor bond above 800 C (van Noort, 2013).
