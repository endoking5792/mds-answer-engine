## STUDY MODE

### Requirements of an alloy for metal-ceramic (Bhat et al, 2019, via digest)
Biocompatible, corrosion resistant, oxide-forming for chemical bonding, high strength and modulus, low creep (sag resistance), fusion temperature above porcelain, CTE about 13.5-14.0 ppm/°C slightly above that of porcelain (13.0-13.5), and no copper or silver that would discolour porcelain.

### Classification (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest)
ADA compositional groups: **high noble** (noble metal at least 60 wt%, gold at least 40 wt%), **noble** (noble at least 25 wt%), **predominantly base metal** (noble under 25 wt%).

| Group | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| High noble | Au-Pt-Pd, Au-Pd | Best esthetics and biocompatibility; low modulus gives little veneer support and a tendency to chip (Zhang and Kelly, 2017) |
| Noble | Pd-Ag, Pd-Cu, Pd-Au | Palladium's high melting point (1170-1190 °C) allows firing at 950-1000 °C; both Pd and Ag absorb oxygen when molten and can give porous castings (Sikri, 2017, via digest) |
| Base metal | Ni-Cr, Co-Cr, Ti | Ni and/or Co with about 15-20% Cr, passivating Cr2O3; cheap; high modulus; Be in some (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Sikri, 2017, via digest) |

Gold-based alloys often carry 1-5% palladium for tarnish resistance (Sikri, 2017, via digest).

### Properties and concerns
- Gold alloys: ductile, easy to cast, excellent bonding by oxide of trace base metals; expensive.
- **Palladium-silver/silver-containing noble alloys: silver causes porcelain "greening," now confirmed directly this session (CR) rather than left as [RECALL]** — the mechanism is **ionic silver diffusion** into the porcelain during firing, not simple surface contamination, which is why it specifically implicates silver-containing alloys and not gold-only compositions.
- Base metals: high modulus and hardness, difficult casting; oxide layer thickness critical; nickel allergy 5-8% to 10-20% of females (Craig's two passages), palladium allergy usually co-exists with nickel allergy. **Beryllium — an occupational-safety fact previously missing, now added (VN)**: Ni-Cr alloys sometimes contain **<0.9% beryllium**, added for castability and bond quality, but beryllium is released during grinding/polishing of the casting at a rate **5-6× the bulk composition** — i.e. the dust the technician is exposed to is far more concentrated in beryllium than the alloy itself, and beryllium is a known carcinogen; this is a genuine occupational-health risk to the dental technician, not the patient, and is worth stating explicitly in any alloy-selection answer.
- Alloy choice influences porcelain veneer: low-modulus high-gold frameworks support veneer poorly (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).
- **Titanium — a specific bonding limitation, previously missing (VN)**: titanium-ceramic bonding is **poor above about 800°C** because of excessive oxidation of the titanium surface at that temperature. Titanium's own CTE is only **9.6 ppm/°C**, much lower than conventional porcelain, so titanium-ceramic systems require **specially formulated low-expansion ceramics** fired at lower temperatures — titanium cannot simply be veneered with standard metal-ceramic porcelain.

### Bonding of porcelain to metal — mechanism, classification and design rule, substantially expanded this session (CR/PHIL/MCC/VN)

**Three controlling factors for the bond** (CR/PHIL/MCC all agree, a clean three-part answer): (1) **mechanical interlocking** — porcelain flows into the microscopic irregularities of the metal surface; (2) **interatomic/chemical bonding** across the oxide-porcelain interface; (3) **residual stress** magnitude, governed by the CTE match between metal and porcelain.

**"Degassing" is a misnomer, not a minor terminology point (PHIL)**: the pre-oxidation firing cycle commonly called "degassing" does not actually remove gas from the alloy — it **forms the surface oxide layer** that the porcelain will bond to. The name describes what the step looks like, not what it does.

**Oxide colour indicates alloy composition, and predicts bond quality (CR)**: a **NiO-rich oxide is dark grey**; a **Cr2O3-rich oxide is greenish** — and Cr2O3-rich oxides **may bond poorly**, sometimes needing a separate bonding agent applied before porcelain application. This gives a simple visual/clinical check on what kind of oxide has formed.

**CTE-mismatch stress calculation, a specific worked figure (PHIL)**: a CTE difference of **1.7×10⁻⁶/K generates approximately 280 MPa of shear stress** on cooling from a firing temperature of 954°C. "Thermally compatible" is formally defined as **Δα ≤ 0.5×10⁻⁶/K** — i.e. a genuinely small, specific numeric tolerance, not just "close CTE."

**Bond-failure classification, a clean three-type scheme (CR, Fig. 11.10)**: (1) **adhesive failure** at the metal/oxide interface (weakest, worst outcome); (2) **cohesive-in-oxide** failure (within the oxide layer itself); (3) **cohesive-in-ceramic** failure — failure within the porcelain itself, away from the interface — which is the **strongest** bond outcome, since it means the metal-ceramic interface held and the porcelain failed before the bond did.

**Captek — a contrasting, non-oxide bonding system (PHIL/MCC)**: Captek uses a **capillary-casting** process with two component metals (a Au-Pt-Pd "P-metal" and a Au-Ag "G-metal"), producing a coping as thin as 0.25mm (vs 0.50mm for conventional casting). Crucially, Captek bonds to porcelain via a **mechanical "bonder" layer**, not via an oxide layer — a genuine mechanistic contrast worth citing when a question asks specifically about bonding mechanisms, to show that oxide bonding is not the only route used clinically.

**Design rule for the metal-ceramic junction (CR)**: the junction between metal and ceramic should be placed **at least 1.5mm from centric occlusal contacts**, and the **shoulder depth should be at least 1.2mm** — both specific, examinable numeric design parameters for crown preparation under a metal-ceramic restoration.

Classical bonding principle, retained: oxygen bonding through surface oxides; mechanical interlocking; compressive stress from CTE difference (Bhat et al, 2019, via digest) — now folded into the three-factor/classification framework above.

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 340; about 16 min)**

### Introduction
Alloys for metal-ceramic restorations must withstand porcelain firing temperatures, bond to porcelain and be biocompatible.

### Requirements
Fusion temperature above porcelain; CTE slightly higher than porcelain (13.5-14.0 vs 13.0-13.5 ppm/°C); oxide formation; high modulus and creep resistance; biocompatible and corrosion resistant; no Cu or Ag.

### Classification
| Class | Alloys |
|---|---|
| High noble | Au-Pt-Pd, Au-Pd |
| Noble | Pd-Ag, Pd-Cu, Pd-Au |
| Base metal | Ni-Cr, Co-Cr, Ti |

### Features
| Alloy | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| High noble | Biocompatible, castable, esthetic | Cost; low modulus (veneer chipping) |
| Palladium-based | Cheaper; high melting point; good sag resistance | Oxygen absorption; porosity; silver-containing variants cause porcelain "greening" via ionic Ag diffusion |
| Ni-Cr, Co-Cr | Strong, rigid, cheap | Difficult casting; allergy (Ni); <0.9% Be for castability/bond, released during grinding/polishing at 5-6× bulk concentration — carcinogen risk to technician |
| Ti | Biocompatible; passivating TiO2 | Bonding poor above 800°C (oxidation); CTE only 9.6ppm/°C needs specially formulated low-expansion ceramics |

### Bonding mechanism, classification and design rule
Three controlling factors (CR/PHIL/MCC): mechanical interlocking, interatomic/chemical bonding across the oxide-porcelain interface, residual stress magnitude. "Degassing" is a misnomer — it forms oxide, not removes gas (PHIL). Oxide colour indicates composition: NiO-rich = dark grey; Cr2O3-rich = greenish, may bond poorly, needing a bonding agent (CR). CTE-mismatch stress: a 1.7×10⁻⁶/K difference generates ~280 MPa shear stress cooling from 954°C; "thermally compatible" = Δα≤0.5×10⁻⁶/K (PHIL). Bond-failure classification (CR, Fig.11.10): adhesive (metal/oxide interface, weakest) → cohesive-in-oxide → cohesive-in-ceramic (strongest — failure within the porcelain itself). Captek: capillary-cast two-metal system (Au-Pt-Pd + Au-Ag), 0.25mm coping, bonds via a mechanical "bonder" layer, NOT an oxide layer (PHIL/MCC) — a contrasting non-oxide bonding example. Design rule: metal-ceramic junction ≥1.5mm from centric occlusal contacts, shoulder depth ≥1.2mm (CR).

### Precautions
Avoid nickel in allergic patients (Co-Cr), protect technician from beryllium dust during grinding/polishing, control oxidation (avoid excessive Cr2O3 oxide without a bonding agent), do not fire titanium above 800°C.

### Conclusion
The alloy is chosen for compatibility with porcelain (CTE, fusion, oxide), mechanical needs, biocompatibility and cost.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- High noble ≥60% noble and ≥40% gold.
- Pd melting point 1170-1190 °C.
- CTE: metal slightly higher; mismatch of 1.7×10⁻⁶/K → ~280 MPa shear stress; "thermally compatible" = Δα≤0.5×10⁻⁶/K (Phillips).
- Co-Cr for nickel-allergic patients; Be release during grinding is 5-6× bulk concentration — technician carcinogen risk.
- Three bond factors: mechanical interlock, chemical/oxide bonding, residual stress. "Degassing" is a misnomer — forms oxide.
- Bond-failure classification: adhesive (weakest) → cohesive-in-oxide → cohesive-in-ceramic (strongest).
- Silver causes porcelain "greening" via ionic diffusion; Ti bonding poor above 800°C, CTE 9.6ppm/°C.
- Captek: mechanical "bonder" layer, not oxide bonding.
- Design rule: metal-ceramic junction ≥1.5mm from occlusal contacts, shoulder ≥1.2mm.
