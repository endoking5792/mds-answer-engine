## STUDY MODE (builds on Dental Materials Q9)

### Definition and rationale
CAD/CAM is the computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacture of restorations from a digital model. It aims at accuracy, standard industrial materials, fewer laboratory steps and, chairside, single-visit treatment (Roberson et al, 2006, via digest; Zhang and Kelly, 2017).

### History
Copy milling (stylus tracing a replica; can machine titanium) preceded CAD/CAM milling, which digitises the preparation directly. Five stages: surface digitisation, CAD, CAM, computer-aided esthetics, computer-aided finishing, the last two least automated (Roberson et al, 2006, via digest). Systems in the sources: Cerec, Lava, Procera, Cercon, Etkon (Guess et al, 2011). Pressing and CAD/CAM both arrived in the 1980s (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).

### System components
1. **Data acquisition:** intraoral scanner or laboratory scan of impression or die.
2. **Design software:** margin, contacts, occlusion, connectors, thickness; library or biogeneric tooth.
3. **Manufacturing:** chairside mill (single-visit), laboratory mill or centralised milling centre, or additive printer.
4. **Post-processing:** crystallisation or sintering, staining, glazing, finishing.

### Types of workflow
Chairside (same-day inlays, onlays, veneers, crowns); laboratory (scanned model or impression, frameworks); centralised milling.

### Materials
| Class | Examples | Post-milling step |
|---|---|---|
| Feldspathic | Vita Mark II (122 MPa) | Glaze or polish |
| Leucite | Empress CAD, ProCAD (140-160 MPa) | Glaze |
| Lithium disilicate | e.max CAD (precrystallised about 130 MPa → 400-530 MPa after crystallisation) | Crystallise above 750 °C (840 °C for 7 min in Zhang and Kelly) |
| ZLS | Celtra Duo, Suprinity | Crystallise |
| Fully crystallised LDS | GC Initial LiSi, n!ce, CEREC Tessera | Glaze or none |
| Zirconia | Lava, Cercon, Katana (presintered) | Sinter (about 25% shrinkage) |
| Alumina | In-Ceram, Procera | Infiltration or sinter |
| Resin-matrix | Vita Enamic, Lava Ultimate, Cerasmart | None, polish |
| Printed | Zirconia, lithium disilicate, resin ceramics | Debind, sinter or post-cure |
Range of strength among milled materials from 122 MPa to 1625 MPa (Roberson et al, 2006, via digest), so CAD/CAM compatibility says nothing about strength.

### Zirconia processing (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013, via digest)
Presintered ("green") blocks are easy to mill, then sinter with about 25% shrinkage compensated by software enlargement; HIP blocks are fully dense, hard to mill, less variable.

### Accuracy
- Marginal and internal fit similar for lithium disilicate and zirconia crowns; PICN and lithium disilicate inlays under 100 μm internal gap.
- Pressed lithium disilicate gave better marginal adaptation but milled gave better internal fit in one study; leucite and ZLS crowns fitted better by CAD/CAM than by pressing.
- 3D-printed zirconia gave larger internal and marginal discrepancies than milled, all within clinically acceptable range (Bacchi and Cesar, 2022).

### Clinical performance
Monolithic zirconia crowns 91-100% survival (Nejat, 2025); lithium disilicate single units high survival, cautious for FDPs (Nejat, 2025); ZLS partial coverage 80% at 5 years; milled lithium disilicate has lower fatigue failure load (1200 N) than pressed (1460 N) in one simplified-crown study (Bacchi and Cesar, 2022).

### Advantages
Same-day restoration; no conventional impression or provisional; standardised block quality (fewer porosities); reproducible; digital archive and communication; reduced laboratory steps; usable with implants; waste reduction by additive methods (Nejat, 2025).

### Disadvantages
Cost; learning curve; scanning limits for deep subgingival margins; milling damage and bur wear; marginal chipping of thin ceramics (Zhang and Kelly, 2017); limited characterisation; block size limits; post-milling firing for some materials; short follow-up for printed ceramics (Nejat, 2025).

### Additive manufacturing
Vat polymerisation (DLP, SLA) and 3D gel deposition for zirconia; issues: slurry viscosity and bubbles, powder agglomerates, interlayer defects, nesting angle, debinding and sintering; higher flexural strength, similar grain size and fit, lower surface hardness and higher roughness and porosity reported (Nejat, 2025).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim — CAD/CAM restorations are as accurate as conventionally made ones, and the material, not the manufacturing route, determines clinical success.** Marginal and internal gaps under about 100 μm across materials (Bacchi and Cesar, 2022) and the 13-fold strength spread among milled materials (Roberson et al, 2006, via digest) support this. INTERPRETATION: choosing the block matters more than choosing milling over pressing. LIMITATION: the fit data are in vitro; clinical outcome studies for printed ceramics and gradient zirconia are absent in the sources read. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 1,100; about 50 min)**

### Introduction
CAD/CAM uses digital data to design and manufacture restorations. It has moved ceramic dentistry from manual layering to standardised, repeatable production.

### History
Copy milling → CAD/CAM milling (1980s) → chairside systems (Cerec) → zirconia frameworks (Lava, Procera, Cercon) → printing.

### Components
| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| Scanner | Digitises preparation (intraoral or laboratory) |
| CAD software | Designs restoration: margin, contacts, occlusion |
| CAM unit | Mills (subtractive) or prints (additive) |
| Furnace | Crystallises or sinters |

### Classification
1. Chairside; 2. Laboratory; 3. Centralised milling; 4. Subtractive versus additive.

### Materials
Feldspathic, leucite, lithium disilicate, ZLS, zirconia, alumina, resin-matrix (PICN), composite blocks, PMMA (provisional), printed ceramics.

### Procedure (chairside)
Prepare → scan (optical) → design → mill block → crystallise/glaze → try-in → bond (HF and silane; zirconia MDP) → finish.

### Properties
Accuracy; internal gap under 100 μm; strength of blocks 122-1625 MPa; zirconia shrinks about 25% on sintering (compensated).

### Applications
Inlays, onlays, veneers, crowns, FDPs, implant abutments and frameworks, provisional restorations, dentures and surgical guides.

### Advantages
Single visit; no impression or provisional; precise; high-quality industrial blocks; digital archive; fewer lab steps.

### Disadvantages
Expensive; steep learning curve; subgingival margin scanning; milling damage and bur wear; limited characterisation; block limits.

### Additive manufacturing
Promising: complex geometries, less waste; problems: porosity, interlayer defects, lower hardness; evidence limited.

### Clinical outcome
Monolithic zirconia 91-100%; lithium disilicate singles high; ZLS partial coverage 80% at 5 years.

### Conclusion
CAD/CAM has made precise, esthetic and fast restorations routine; correct material choice and bonding remain the determinants of success.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Five stages: digitisation, CAD, CAM, esthetics, finishing.
- Zirconia sinters with about 25% shrinkage.
- Precrystallised lithium disilicate is milled at about 130 MPa and crystallised afterwards.
- Printed zirconia: inconsistent strength; more porosity.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Defining CAD/CAM as a material.
2. Forgetting post-milling crystallisation or sintering.
3. Listing advantages only.
4. Ignoring additive manufacturing.
