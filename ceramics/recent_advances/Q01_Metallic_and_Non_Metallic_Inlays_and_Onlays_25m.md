## STUDY MODE

### Definitions
An **inlay** is an intracoronal indirect restoration without cusp coverage; an **onlay** extends over one or more cusps, giving cuspal protection (Sikri, 2017, via digest). The distinction is not rigid (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest).

### Why indirect
Direct restorations become unreliable when more than half the tooth structure, especially dentin support, is lost, or the proximal area is wide; indirect restorations give better strength and longevity, anatomical detail and contacts, and shift fabrication out of the chair (Sikri, 2017, via digest). Onlays protect weakened cusps without further reduction, especially in root-filled posterior teeth (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest).

### Metallic
**Cast gold alloys.** High-noble alloys (≥60 wt% noble metal, ≥40% gold); Types I-IV by yield strength: Type I under 140 MPa (low-stress inlays), Type II 140-200 (inlays and onlays), Type III 201-340 (crowns and short-span), Type IV over 340 (thin veneer, RPD) (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest). Type 1 suits simple inlays; type 2 when cusps are covered (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest). Advantages: excellent marginal fit and burnishability, longevity, strength with minimal bulk, kind to antagonists, low wear. Disadvantages: colour, cost, two-visit, thermal conduction, need for bevels and tooth reduction, and galvanic effects with amalgam. Gold can be adhesively bonded after sandblasting or oxidation (400 °C, 10 min) (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest).
**Base-metal** (Ni-Cr, Co-Cr) are rarely used for inlays.

### Non-metallic
**Ceramic (feldspathic, leucite, lithium disilicate; CAD/CAM or pressed).** Esthetic, wear-resistant, biocompatible, bonded; but brittle, technique-sensitive, need minimum thickness (about 1.0-1.5 mm for inlays/onlays per manufacturer data, Nejat, 2024), abrasive to antagonist if rough. Empress inlays/onlays 90% at 8 years; Zhang and Kelly cite 92% at 8 years (Guess et al, 2011; Zhang and Kelly, 2017). Ceramic restorations should be adhesively luted; resin cements are the routine choice, with dual-cure preferred (Sikri, 2017, via digest).

**Indirect composite (laboratory).** Heat/pressure cure (e.g. 140 °C, 0.6 MPa for 10 min) gives a higher degree of conversion than direct or chairside post-cure; shrinkage occurs on the die, reducing stress at the tooth interface; repairable; less abrasive to antagonists than ceramic (Anusavice et al, 2013, via digest). Wear is product-dependent; wear similar to gold but more abrasive to enamel; in prospective studies 5% failure at 48-75 months and 12% interfacial staining at 5 years; no significant difference from direct composite at 5 and 11 years (Hilton et al, 2013, via digest). Bonding requires a surface activator or sandblasting because few unreacted double bonds remain (Roberson et al, 2006, via digest).

### Preparation differences (Sikri, 2017, via digest)
General taper is 6-10° anterior and 10-20° posterior, with about 10° now the target for resin-luted indirect restorations; ceramic and composite use greater convergence to ease the path of insertion (Sikri, 2017, via digest). Margin bevels: none for most cast gold, a short bevel for softer Type 1-2 alloys, a long bevel where greater marginal adaptation is needed (Marzouk, via digest). Tooth-coloured materials are prepared without a bevel with rounded internal angles and butt-joint margins [Likely, not in the sources read]. Occlusal reduction: 1.0-1.5 mm on working cusps and 0.5-1.0 mm on non-working cusps for metal; 1.0-1.5 mm depth is ideal for all-ceramic (Sikri, 2017, via digest).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim — gold remains the benchmark for longevity, while ceramic and indirect composite trade durability for esthetics.** Cast gold's long record, burnishability and low antagonist wear are textbook claims (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest), while ceramic inlay survival of 90-92% at 8 years (Guess et al, 2011; Zhang and Kelly, 2017) and indirect composite 5% failure at about 5 years (Hilton et al, 2013, via digest) are comparable at medium term. INTERPRETATION: patient esthetics, cost and biological conservation, not survival, decide the material in most cases. LIMITATION: the study samples, definitions of failure and operators differ; no head-to-head trial is cited in the sources read. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 900; about 40 min)**

### Introduction
Inlays (intracoronal) and onlays (cuspal coverage) are indirect restorations used when direct materials cannot give adequate strength, contour, contact or longevity. They may be metallic (cast gold) or non-metallic (ceramic, indirect composite).

### Indications
Extensive loss (over half the tooth), wide proximal area, need for accurate contacts and cusp planes, previous fractured direct restoration, patients who cannot tolerate long chairside time, protection of root-filled teeth (onlay).

### Metallic inlays and onlays
- **Material:** high noble gold Types I-IV (Type I-II inlays/onlays; III-IV crowns).
- **Advantages:** longevity, excellent marginal adaptation (bevel, burnishing), strength with thin sections, kind to antagonists, low wear.
- **Disadvantages:** unesthetic, expensive, two visits, thermal conduction, bevel needs more sound tooth, cannot be directly bonded without treatment, galvanic risks.

### Non-metallic inlays and onlays
| Feature | Ceramic | Indirect composite |
|---|---|---|
| Esthetics | Excellent | Good |
| Wear of antagonist | Higher if rough | Lower |
| Strength | Brittle | Moderate |
| Repair | Difficult | Intraoral possible |
| Polymerisation shrinkage | Nil | On the die, so less stress |
| Survival | Empress 90-92% at 8 years | About 95% at about 5 years |
| Luting | Resin cement, HF + silane | Resin cement, sandblast/activator |

### Comparative table (metal versus non-metal)
| Criterion | Gold | Ceramic | Indirect composite |
|---|---|---|---|
| Esthetics | Poor | Best | Good |
| Longevity | Best | High | Moderate |
| Bonding | Optional | Essential | Essential |
| Preparation | Short/long bevel on soft alloys; 6-10° / 10-20° taper | Butt joint, rounded angles, more taper | Butt joint, rounded angles, more taper |
| Antagonist wear | Least | Can be high | Moderate |
| Cost | High (metal) | High | Moderate |

### Selection
Esthetic zone or patient demand: ceramic or composite; high load or parafunction with a single posterior tooth: gold; low-cost or reparable: indirect composite; limited enamel: composite or gold.

### Conclusion
Indirect inlays and onlays are decided by esthetics, load, cost and tooth substrate; gold remains a durable benchmark, ceramic gives the best esthetics, and indirect composite offers repairable economy.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Inlay = no cusp coverage; onlay = cusp coverage.
- Gold Type I-II for inlays/onlays.
- Ceramic must be bonded; gold may be cemented.
- Indirect composite: shrinkage occurs on die; wear product-dependent.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Evaluating only one material.
2. Saying all ceramic inlays are bevelled.
3. No selection criteria or conclusion.
4. Claiming indirect composite is categorically better than direct composite.
