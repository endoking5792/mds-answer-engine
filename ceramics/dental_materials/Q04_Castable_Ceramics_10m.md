## STUDY MODE

### Definition
Castable ceramics are glass-based ceramics formed by melting and casting a glass into a mould (lost-wax technique), followed by a controlled heat treatment ("ceramming") that nucleates and grows crystals within the glass, converting it to a glass-ceramic.

### Representative material
**Dicor** was the first glass-ceramic used for dental restorations; it consisted of fluormica crystals as plates or sheets embedded in a glass matrix (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). The casting method with Dicor was applied to anterior crowns and the product is no longer available (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011, via digest). A machinable variant, Dicor MGC, contains about 70 vol% fluormica crystals (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).

### Process [Likely]
Wax pattern, investment, burnout, casting of molten glass, divestment, then ceramming (heat treatment to crystallise), followed by staining or veneering and adhesive or conventional cementation. The cast glass is transparent; ceramming makes it opaque-translucent glass-ceramic of higher strength.

### Properties
Flexural strength of Dicor about 229 MPa (Zhang and Kelly, 2017), above feldspathic porcelain (60-80 MPa, Sakaguchi et al, 2019 via digest) but below lithium disilicate; good marginal fit from casting; translucent.

### Why it declined
Poor mechanical performance in clinical use: failure rates of 4-6% per year for Dicor molar crowns (Zhang and Kelly, 2017); Dicor was withdrawn from the market (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). It was superseded by pressable and CAD/CAM glass-ceramics.

### Significance
It proved the principle of **controlled crystallisation** and led to Empress and e.max.

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 300; about 14 min)**

### Definition
Glass ceramics cast by the lost-wax technique and then crystallised by controlled heat treatment (ceramming).

### Example
Dicor (Corning/Dentsply): fluormica crystals in a glass matrix; flexural strength about 229 MPa.

### Steps
Wax pattern → investment → burnout → cast molten glass → divest → ceram → stain/glaze → cement.

### Advantages
- Good marginal fit (casting accuracy).
- Better strength than feldspathic porcelain.
- Esthetic, translucent; chameleon effect.

### Disadvantages
- Low strength: 4-6% per year failure for molar crowns.
- Technique-sensitive, multiple steps, special equipment.
- Needs staining for shade; limited indications (anterior crowns).
- Withdrawn from market.

### Present status
Obsolete; replaced by heat-pressed (Empress, e.max Press) and CAD/CAM glass-ceramics. Historical value: first application of nucleation and crystal growth to dental ceramics.

### Conclusion
Castable ceramics established glass-ceramic technology but were limited by strength; their legacy is the controlled-crystallisation concept used in all modern glass-ceramics.
