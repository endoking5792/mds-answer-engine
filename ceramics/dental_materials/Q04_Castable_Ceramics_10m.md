## STUDY MODE

### Definition
Castable ceramics are glass-based ceramics formed by melting and casting a glass into a mould (lost-wax technique), followed by a controlled heat treatment ("ceramming") that nucleates and grows crystals within the glass, converting it to a glass-ceramic.

### Representative material
**Dicor** was the first glass-ceramic used for dental restorations; it consisted of fluormica crystals as plates or sheets embedded in a glass matrix (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). The primary-textbook composition is specific: a **fluormica glass-ceramic**, SiO2·K2O·MgO·MgF2·Al2O3·ZrO2, centrifugally cast at approximately **1380°C** (Bonsor) [SRC, BON]. The casting method with Dicor was applied to anterior crowns and the product is **now discontinued** — Bonsor gives the specific reason: **inadequate strength combined with excessive translucency**, which caused a visible grey discolouration at the incisal tips of anterior crowns [SRC, BON]. A machinable variant, Dicor MGC, contains about 70 vol% fluormica crystals (Zhang and Kelly, 2017).

**Do not conflate Dicor with an earlier, different material**: MacCulloch (1968) is credited with the first dental glass-ceramic *concept*, used for denture teeth, but that material was a different Li2O-ZnO-SiO2 (lithium-zinc-silicate) system — a distinct, earlier material from Dicor's fluormica system, despite both being "glass-ceramics" (per the audit's history notes, preserved in the five readers' session transcripts rather than the summary ledger file itself — flag for page-level verification if cited with a page number) [SRC].

### Process [Likely]
Wax pattern, investment, burnout, casting of molten glass, divestment, then ceramming (heat treatment to crystallise), followed by staining or veneering and adhesive or conventional cementation. The cast glass is transparent; ceramming makes it opaque-translucent glass-ceramic of higher strength.

### Properties
Flexural strength of Dicor about 229 MPa (Zhang and Kelly, 2017), above feldspathic porcelain (60-80 MPa, Sakaguchi et al, 2019 via digest) but below lithium disilicate; good marginal fit from casting; translucent.

### Clinical survival data (Phillips, direct textbook read)
A specific long-term figure strengthens the "why it declined" case: Dicor's **14-year survival was 76% when etched and resin-bonded versus only 50% when non-etched/conventionally cemented** (P<0.001) — a clear, citable demonstration that even a mechanically weak ceramic benefits substantially from adhesive bonding, and conversely that non-bonded Dicor performed poorly enough to justify its withdrawal [SRC, PHIL].

### Why it declined
Poor mechanical performance in clinical use: failure rates of 4-6% per year for Dicor molar crowns (Zhang and Kelly, 2017); Dicor was withdrawn from the market, with Bonsor specifically attributing this to the combination of inadequate strength and excessive translucency causing grey incisal discolouration [SRC, BON]. It was superseded by pressable and CAD/CAM glass-ceramics.

### Significance
It proved the principle of **controlled crystallisation** and led to Empress and e.max.

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 300; about 14 min)**

### Definition
Glass ceramics cast by the lost-wax technique and then crystallised by controlled heat treatment (ceramming).

### Example
Dicor (Corning/Dentsply): fluormica glass-ceramic (SiO2·K2O·MgO·MgF2·Al2O3·ZrO2), centrifugally cast at ~1380°C; flexural strength about 229 MPa. Now discontinued (Bonsor: inadequate strength + excessive translucency causing grey incisal tips). 14-year survival: 76% etched/resin-bonded vs 50% non-etched (P<0.001, Phillips). Distinct from MacCulloch's 1968 Li2O-ZnO-SiO2 denture-tooth glass-ceramic concept, an earlier, different material.

### Steps
Wax pattern → investment → burnout → cast molten glass → divest → ceram → stain/glaze → cement.

### Advantages
- Good marginal fit (casting accuracy).
- Better strength than feldspathic porcelain.
- Esthetic, translucent; chameleon effect.

### Disadvantages
- Low strength: 4-6% per year failure for molar crowns; non-etched/conventionally-cemented 14-year survival only 50% vs 76% etched/resin-bonded (P<0.001, Phillips).
- Technique-sensitive, multiple steps, special equipment.
- Needs staining for shade; limited indications (anterior crowns).
- Discontinued: Bonsor attributes this to inadequate strength combined with excessive translucency, which caused grey discolouration at incisal tips.

### Present status
Obsolete; replaced by heat-pressed (Empress, e.max Press) and CAD/CAM glass-ceramics. Historical value: first application of nucleation and crystal growth to dental ceramics.

### Conclusion
Castable ceramics established glass-ceramic technology but were limited by strength; their legacy is the controlled-crystallisation concept used in all modern glass-ceramics.
