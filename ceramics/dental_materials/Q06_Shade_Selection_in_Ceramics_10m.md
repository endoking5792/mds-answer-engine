## STUDY MODE

### What is special about ceramics
Ceramics are layered, translucent, and the final colour is the sum of the ceramic, its thickness, the luting cement and the underlying substrate. Shade selection is therefore a **system** decision, not a tab number.

### Colour dimensions — the Munsell system (confirmed directly from Phillips/Sikri)
The formal basis for hue/value/chroma is the **Munsell colour system** (hue, value, chroma as its three defined axes) (Phillips) [SRC, PHIL]. Hue (A-D in VITA Classic), value (lightness), chroma (saturation). **Value is confirmed as the single most critical parameter for a successful shade match — not hue or chroma** — because the human eye is far more sensitive to lightness differences than to hue/chroma differences (Sikri; Phillips both state this independently) [SRC, SIKRI/PHIL]. Powers & Wataha describe the practical consequence directly: the **"squinting technique"** — partially closing the eyes reduces chroma/hue discrimination and isolates the eye's perception of value, letting the clinician judge value in isolation [SRC, PW]. VITA A1 has a higher value than B1 but looks darker because it is more chromatic, so the standard advice is an overall best match (Hilton et al, 2013, via digest).

### Metamerism — formal definition (Phillips)
Phillips gives metamerism a formal definition, not just "looks different under different light": **two objects that match under one illuminant can appear to mismatch under another** because their spectral reflectance curves differ even though their perceived colour coincided under the first light source [SRC, PHIL]. Clinically this is why shade must be checked under **multiple illuminants** — sunlight, tungsten (incandescent), and fluorescent light — since a restoration validated under only one light source risks a visible mismatch under another [SRC, PHIL/SIKRI].

### The energy-balance equation (Phillips)
Phillips gives a formal optical energy-balance equation for a ceramic restoration: **incident light = scattered + reflected + absorbed + transmitted + fluoresced** light. This equation is the physical basis for why ceramic thickness, translucency and the underlying substrate all affect final perceived shade — each term in the balance is altered by material choice and layering [SRC, PHIL].

### Shade-tab systematic-mismatch problem (Phillips/Sikri)
A specific, examinable pitfall: commercial shade tabs are a **systematic mismatch** for the actual restoration — tabs are typically **thicker and more translucent** than the eventual thin ceramic restoration, and tab **necks are deliberately made higher-chroma** than the body shade to assist visual matching at the gingival third. Both effects mean that matching a tab exactly does not guarantee the finished restoration will match — the clinician must mentally correct for tab thickness/translucency and the artificially saturated cervical third [SRC, PHIL/SIKRI].

### Optical phenomena
Translucency, opacity, gloss, opalescence (blue in reflected, orange in transmitted light), fluorescence (dentin; 340-410 nm; invisible under normal light but dull porcelain looks lifeless under UV), metamerism (match under one light fails under another) (Sikri, 2017, via digest).

### Conditions
Daylight-simulating light 1,000-1,500 lux, CRI 90 or more, 25-35 cm distance, viewing at 0° and light at about 45° (Hilton et al, 2013, via digest). Take the shade at the start of the visit and before preparation; remove lipstick; limit each glance to about 5 seconds; check the middle third; confirm under two light sources (Sikri, 2017, via digest). Light quality was the most influential factor in one study (Dagg et al, 2004, via digest).

### SIKRI's full clinical protocol (Ch.7, read directly this session — confirms and extends the digest above)
Sikri gives a specific, structured sequence that should replace a generic "take the shade carefully" answer:
1. **Shade BEFORE preparation** — never after the tooth is cut, since dehydration and preparation change the optical baseline.
2. Have the patient **remove lipstick and bright/saturated clothing** before shade-taking, since reflected colour from clothing and lips biases the eye's colour perception.
3. **Assess at the patient's eye level** (not looking down), to match the natural viewing angle others will use.
4. Limit each assessment to a **5-second window**, since the eye's colour receptors fatigue and re-adapt with prolonged staring, distorting judgement.
5. Compare under **multiple lighting conditions in sequence, culminating in natural daylight** as the final, most reliable check (addressing metamerism directly).
6. Use **5 structured methods of communicating the shade to the laboratory**: (i) a written shade designation (e.g. VITA tab number), (ii) a digital photograph with the shade tab held against the tooth, (iii) a diagram marking internal characterisations (craze lines, incisal translucency, hypocalcification spots), (iv) a verbal/written description of surface texture and gloss, and (v) direct lab communication (phone/video) for complex or unusual cases [SRC, SIKRI].

### Ceramic-specific factors
- **Substrate effect (stump effect):** feldspathic and leucite ceramics transmit light highly and show the stump colour; lithium disilicate is medium-low; zirconia has the least stump effect (Goldstein et al, 2018, via digest). Monolithic translucent ceramics cannot mask severe discolouration without an opaque luting agent or opaque flowable composite (Bacchi and Cesar, 2022).
- **Thickness and layering:** the translucent veneer porcelain can influence the final colour more than the opaque core; thickness ratio of core and veneer correlates with colour (Moshaverinia, 2020).
- **Cement shade:** try-in pastes and cement colour change veneer shade, especially thin veneers [Likely].
- **Characterisation:** internal staining incorporated before glaze; surface stain reserved for the final touch (Goldstein et al, 2018, via digest).

### Aids
Shade guides (VITA Classical, 3D-Master), colorimeters (Easyshade), spectrophotometers (a 33% increase in accuracy over less sophisticated methods per Sikri, 2017, via digest), digital photography with a grey card. Using visual and instrumental methods together compensates for the weakness of each.

### Communication
Send the lab a photograph with shade tabs, a diagram of characterisation (craze lines, incisal translucency), and the stump shade.

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 350; about 16 min)**

### Introduction
Shade selection in ceramics is matching hue, value and chroma of the restoration to the natural teeth, accounting for translucency, substrate and thickness.

### Principles
1. Colour dimensions (Munsell system — hue, value, chroma): **value first, then chroma, then hue**; value is the single most critical parameter (Sikri/Phillips) — use the "squinting technique" (Powers & Wataha) to isolate value judgement.
2. Optical properties: translucency, opalescence, fluorescence, metamerism (formally defined by Phillips: matching spectral curves can diverge under a different illuminant even after matching under the first).
3. Conditions: daylight-simulating light 1,000-1,500 lux, CRI of 90 or more, grey neutral background, 25-35 cm, early in the visit, before preparation.
4. SIKRI's full protocol: shade BEFORE prep → remove lipstick/bright clothing → assess at eye level → 5-second windows → multiple lights culminating in daylight → 5 structured methods of lab communication (written designation, photo with tab, characterisation diagram, texture/gloss description, direct lab communication).
5. Shade-tab pitfall: tabs are thicker/more translucent than the real restoration, and necks are deliberately higher-chroma — correct for both when matching.

### Methods
| Method | Notes |
|---|---|
| Visual (shade guide) | Quick; subjective; fatigue, metamerism |
| Colorimeter | Three filters; fast |
| Spectrophotometer | Full spectrum; most accurate (about 33% better) |
| Digital photography | Documentation for lab |

### Ceramic-specific factors
Stump shade (zirconia masks best, feldspathic least), ceramic thickness, core-to-veneer thickness ratio, cement shade and opacity, glaze and characterisation.

### Common errors
Poor light, tired eye, dehydrated teeth, single-method reliance, no photograph, cement not considered, colour-vision deficiency of the observer.

### Conclusion
Predictable ceramic colour needs controlled conditions, combined visual and instrumental methods, awareness of substrate and cement, and clear laboratory communication.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Munsell system: hue, value, chroma. Value is the most critical dimension; determined first (Sikri/Phillips, confirmed independently).
- "Squinting technique" (Powers & Wataha) isolates value judgement by suppressing hue/chroma discrimination.
- Metamerism formally defined (Phillips): spectral-curve mismatch surfaces under a different illuminant even after a match under the first — check under sunlight, tungsten, and fluorescent light.
- Shade tabs are a systematic mismatch: thicker/more translucent than the real restoration, necks deliberately higher-chroma.
- Energy-balance equation: incident = scattered + reflected + absorbed + transmitted + fluoresced.
- SIKRI protocol: shade before prep, remove lipstick/bright clothing, eye-level assessment, 5-second windows, multiple lights ending in daylight, 5 structured lab-communication methods.
- 1,000-1,500 lux, CRI 90 or more, D65 or D55.
- Zirconia masks stump colour best; feldspathic least.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Starting with hue instead of value.
2. Ignoring metamerism and cement.
3. Taking the shade after preparation or at the end of the day.
4. Quoting instruments as a replacement for the eye.
