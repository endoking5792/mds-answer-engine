## STUDY MODE

### Definition and rationale
A metal-ceramic (porcelain-fused-to-metal, PFM) restoration is a cast metal framework veneered with at least two layers of ceramic (opaque and body/incisal), designed to bond durably, remain colour-stable and be tissue-friendly. The metal compensates for the low fracture toughness of the ceramic (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest).

### Requirements (Bhat et al, 2019, via digest)
**Metal:** biocompatible, corrosion resistant, able to form an oxide that bonds to ceramic, high yield strength and modulus (resists sag), low creep, fusion temperature above porcelain, CTE of about 13.5-14.0 ppm/°C, slightly above the porcelain's, and no copper or silver that would discolour the porcelain.
**Porcelain:** biocompatible, CTE about 13.0-13.5 ppm/°C, wets and bonds to the oxide, fusion temperature below the metal, colour stable.

### CTE matching
Matching the CTE between veneer and framework prevents harmful stresses on cooling from firing temperature (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). A slightly higher metal CTE leaves the porcelain in residual compression, which resists tensile failure (Bhat et al, 2019, via digest).

### Bonding mechanisms (confirmed from primary textbooks, replaces the earlier [Likely] tag)
CR/PHIL/MCC all agree on **three controlling factors** for the metal-ceramic bond: (1) **mechanical interlocking** at the metal-oxide-porcelain interface, (2) **interatomic/chemical bonding** across that interface (ionic/covalent bonds between the oxide layer and the porcelain's glass phase), and (3) **residual stress** magnitude set up on cooling [SRC, CR/PHIL/MCC]. "Degassing" is explicitly a **misnomer**: the step does not remove gas, it deliberately forms the oxide layer that bonding depends on (Phillips) [SRC, PHIL]. Oxide colour indicates alloy composition and bond quality: **NiO-rich oxide is dark grey; Cr2O3-rich oxide is greenish** and may bond poorly, sometimes needing a separate bonding agent (Craig's) [SRC, CR]. Silver-containing noble alloys can cause porcelain **"greening"** via ionic silver diffusion into the porcelain (Craig's) [SRC, CR].

**CTE-mismatch stress, quantified:** a CTE difference of 1.7×10⁻⁶/K between metal and porcelain generates approximately **280 MPa shear stress** on cooling from a firing temperature of 954°C; "thermally compatible" is formally defined as a CTE difference **Δα ≤ 0.5×10⁻⁶/K** (Phillips) [SRC, PHIL].

**Bond-failure classification** (Craig's, Fig. 11.10) — three failure-location types, a clean examinable scheme: (i) **adhesive failure** at the metal/oxide interface (weakest type); (ii) **cohesive-in-oxide** failure within the oxide layer itself; (iii) **cohesive-in-ceramic** failure within the porcelain itself — this is the **strongest** bond type, since the porcelain fails before the bond does [SRC, CR].

### Layers and fabrication
Cast coping → degas/oxidise (forms the bonding oxide layer, not a gas-removal step) → opaque (masks metal) → body (dentin) → incisal → glaze. Typical alloy families: high-noble gold-palladium, palladium-silver, nickel-chromium and cobalt-chromium. Beryllium improves castability and the porcelain-metal bond but is hazardous (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via study-material `SubModule_2.1`; see Q8 of this file for the occupational-safety detail on Be release).

**Captek contrast** (Phillips/McCabe): the Captek capillary-casting system bonds via a mechanical "bonder" layer rather than an oxide layer — a genuine mechanistic contrast to conventional oxide-adherent metal-ceramic bonding, useful to cite when the examiner asks "is oxide bonding the only mechanism?" [SRC, PHIL/MCC].

### Occlusal load context and design rule (Phillips/Craig's)
Real bite-force data worth citing directly: **890 N** typical maximum anterior bite force, **2224 N** typical maximum posterior bite force, and a recorded maximum clench of **4337 N** (Phillips) [SRC, PHIL]. Design rule to keep the metal-ceramic junction out of load-bearing contact: the junction should be **≥1.5 mm from centric occlusal contacts**, with a minimum **shoulder depth of 1.2 mm** (Craig's) [SRC, CR].

### Strengths and failures
PFM remains the benchmark: 70-80% of fixed prostheses in the US were PFM in 2011 (Zhang and Kelly, 2017) — though Phillips' 12th ed. gives a declining-over-time trend rather than one fixed figure (~90% in a 1994 survey vs "about 60-70%" more recently) [SRC, PHIL]. Chip-off fractures occurred in 19.4% of metal-ceramics (25% of zirconia-supported prostheses) in one series (Guess et al, 2011). Problems: opacity, grey gingival line, chipping, biocompatibility issues (nickel), greater tooth reduction (1.75-2.0 mm shoulder at the margin for PFM, Sikri, 2017 via digest). Long-term clinical data (Craig's) are favourable overall: **10-year success approximately 95%**, and most retreatments are **biological** (tooth fracture, periodontal disease, secondary caries) rather than material failure — prosthesis/esthetic failure accounts for only about 20% of single-unit retreatments; long-span FDPs (≥5 units) show roughly double the failure rate of short-span prostheses [SRC, CR]. Phillips separately gives a 7.5-year fracture rate as low as 2.3% for high-noble alloy, with 7.5-year survival of 97.7% [SRC, PHIL].

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 340; about 15 min)**

### Definition
Metal-ceramic restoration: a cast metal substructure fused to dental porcelain.

### Rationale
Metal gives strength and fit; ceramic gives esthetics.

### Requirements
| Metal | Ceramic |
|---|---|
| Biocompatible, corrosion resistant | Biocompatible, colour stable |
| Fusion temperature above ceramic | Fusion temperature below metal |
| CTE 13.5-14.0 | CTE 13.0-13.5 |
| Forms oxide for bonding | Wets oxide |
| High modulus, low creep | Adequate strength |
| No Cu/Ag staining | Good optical properties |

### Bonding
Three controlling factors (CR/PHIL/MCC): mechanical interlocking, interatomic/chemical bonding across the oxide-porcelain interface, residual stress magnitude. "Degassing" forms the oxide, it does not remove gas. Oxide colour indicates composition: NiO-rich = dark grey; Cr2O3-rich = greenish, may bond poorly. Silver alloys risk porcelain "greening." Bond-failure classification: adhesive (metal/oxide) < cohesive-in-oxide < cohesive-in-ceramic (strongest, failure in the porcelain itself).

### Layers
Metal → oxide (bonding layer, not degassing) → opaque → body → incisal → glaze. Captek contrasts by bonding via a mechanical "bonder" layer, not an oxide layer.

### CTE and design
1.7×10⁻⁶/K CTE mismatch → ~280 MPa shear stress cooling from 954°C; "thermally compatible" = Δα ≤0.5×10⁻⁶/K (Phillips). Junction ≥1.5mm from occlusal contacts; shoulder ≥1.2mm (Craig's). Real bite forces: 890N anterior, 2224N posterior typical max, 4337N record.

### Advantages
Long clinical record (10-yr success ≈95%, most retreatments biological not material — Craig's), strong, suitable for long spans and posterior teeth, accurate fit.

### Disadvantages
Reduced translucency, grey cervical line, porcelain chipping, metal allergy, more tooth reduction, wear of antagonist by porcelain; long-span FDPs (≥5 units) ≈2x failure rate of short-span (Craig's).

### Conclusion
A materials-compatibility system: alloy, oxide, CTE, firing schedule and framework design must be matched; it remains the benchmark for survival, with most failures being biological rather than material in origin.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Metal CTE slightly higher than porcelain (about 0.5 ppm/°C); formal "thermally compatible" definition Δα ≤0.5×10⁻⁶/K (Phillips).
- Metal fusion temperature above porcelain.
- Avoid Cu and Ag in alloy (Ag causes porcelain "greening").
- PFM was 70-80% of US fixed prostheses (2011) — but Phillips frames this as a declining trend (~90% in 1994 vs ~60-70% "recently").
- Three bond-controlling factors: mechanical interlocking, chemical/interatomic bonding, residual stress.
- "Degassing" is a misnomer — it forms oxide.
- Bond-failure classification: adhesive < cohesive-in-oxide < cohesive-in-ceramic (strongest).
- 10-yr success ≈95%; most retreatments are biological, not material failure.
