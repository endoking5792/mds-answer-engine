## STUDY MODE

### Definition and rationale
A metal-ceramic (porcelain-fused-to-metal, PFM) restoration is a cast metal framework veneered with at least two layers of ceramic (opaque and body/incisal), designed to bond durably, remain colour-stable and be tissue-friendly. The metal compensates for the low fracture toughness of the ceramic (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest).

### Requirements (Bhat et al, 2019, via digest)
**Metal:** biocompatible, corrosion resistant, able to form an oxide that bonds to ceramic, high yield strength and modulus (resists sag), low creep, fusion temperature above porcelain, CTE of about 13.5-14.0 ppm/°C, slightly above the porcelain's, and no copper or silver that would discolour the porcelain.
**Porcelain:** biocompatible, CTE about 13.0-13.5 ppm/°C, wets and bonds to the oxide, fusion temperature below the metal, colour stable.

### CTE matching
Matching the CTE between veneer and framework prevents harmful stresses on cooling from firing temperature (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). A slightly higher metal CTE leaves the porcelain in residual compression, which resists tensile failure (Bhat et al, 2019, via digest).

### Bonding mechanisms (standard teaching) [Likely]
Chemical bonding through the oxide layer, mechanical interlocking with the surface, compressive residual stress, van der Waals forces.

### Layers and fabrication [Likely]
Cast coping → degas/oxidise → opaque (masks metal) → body (dentin) → incisal → glaze. Typical alloy families: high-noble gold-palladium, palladium-silver, nickel-chromium and cobalt-chromium. Beryllium improves castability and the porcelain-metal bond but is hazardous (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via study-material `SubModule_2.1`).

### Strengths and failures
PFM remains the benchmark: 70-80% of fixed prostheses in the US were PFM in 2011 (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). Chip-off fractures occurred in 19.4% of metal-ceramics (25% of zirconia-supported prostheses) in one series (Guess et al, 2011). Problems: opacity, grey gingival line, chipping, biocompatibility issues (nickel), greater tooth reduction (1.75-2.0 mm shoulder at the margin for PFM, Sikri, 2017 via digest).

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 340; about 15 min)**

### Definition
Metal-ceramic restoration: a cast metal substructure fused to dental porcelain.

### Rationale
Metal gives strength and fit; ceramic gives esthetics.

### Requirements
| Metal | Ceramic |
|---|---|
| Biocompatible, corrosion resistant | Biocompatible, colour stable |
| Fusion temperature above ceramic | Fusion temperature below metal |
| CTE 13.5-14.0 | CTE 13.0-13.5 |
| Forms oxide for bonding | Wets oxide |
| High modulus, low creep | Adequate strength |
| No Cu/Ag staining | Good optical properties |

### Bonding
Chemical (oxide), mechanical, compressive stress from CTE difference, van der Waals.

### Layers
Metal → oxide → opaque → body → incisal → glaze.

### Advantages
Long clinical record, strong, suitable for long spans and posterior teeth, accurate fit.

### Disadvantages
Reduced translucency, grey cervical line, porcelain chipping, metal allergy, more tooth reduction, wear of antagonist by porcelain.

### Conclusion
A materials-compatibility system: alloy, oxide, CTE, firing schedule and framework design must be matched; it remains the benchmark for survival.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Metal CTE slightly higher than porcelain (about 0.5 ppm/°C).
- Metal fusion temperature above porcelain.
- Avoid Cu and Ag in alloy.
- PFM was 70-80% of US fixed prostheses (2011).
