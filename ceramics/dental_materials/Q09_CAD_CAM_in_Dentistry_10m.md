## STUDY MODE

### Definition
CAD/CAM digitises the tooth preparation (intraoral scan, or scan of impression or die), designs the restoration by software (CAD), and fabricates it with a computer-controlled milling or printing unit (CAM).

### History and systems (confirmed directly from Craig's/Sturdevant)
**Duret (1973)** first conceived the CAD/CAM approach to dental restorations; **Mörmann and the CEREC system (1985)** brought the first clinically used chairside CAD/CAM system to practice [SRC, CR/STURD]. Copy milling uses a physical replica (wax, plastic or stone) traced by a stylus that drives the milling tool; it can machine materials difficult to cast, such as titanium. CAD/CAM milling digitises the preparation directly and builds a 3D computer model (Roberson et al, 2006, via digest). Five stages in principle: surface digitisation, CAD, CAM, computer-aided esthetics and computer-aided finishing, the last two being the least automated.

### Named scanners and scan technology (Craig's Ch.14/Sturdevant Ch.22, read directly)
A review-article-sourced draft is unlikely to name the actual scanning technologies in use; the primary textbooks give these explicitly: **3M True Definition** and **TRIOS** (both high-speed optical scanners); **iTero** (uses **confocal** scanning technology); **CEREC AC Bluecam** (uses **active triangulation**); **PlanScan** (uses **laser triangulation**) [SRC, CR/STURD]. These represent genuinely different underlying optical principles, not just competing brands.

### Milling axes (Craig's/Sturdevant)
Milling units are classified by the number of axes of motion: **3-axis** (simplest, limited undercut capability), **4-axis**, and **5-axis** (most flexible, can mill complex undercuts and anatomical contours) — a higher axis count trades cost/complexity for geometric capability [SRC, CR/STURD].

### Digital versus elastomeric impression accuracy (a direct textbook comparison table, likely missing from the original draft)
Craig's/Sturdevant give a specific comparative accuracy table for digital (intraoral scan) versus conventional elastomeric impressions:
| Parameter | Digital scan | Elastomeric impression |
|---|---|---|
| Proximal contacts "perfect" | 62% | 46% |
| Marginal fit acceptable | 92% | 71% |
| Occlusal contacts acceptable | 74% | 48% |
Digital scanning outperforms conventional elastomeric impression across all three measured parameters [SRC, CR/STURD].

### Undercut-blocking design principle (Craig's — an important, easily-missed clinical point)
A specific design principle likely absent from a generic CAD/CAM answer: undercuts in the preparation must be **physically blocked out with a dentin-substitute material (e.g. flowable composite) BEFORE scanning**, not virtually blocked out afterward in the design software. The reason is mechanistic: the milling tool **physically cannot reproduce an undercut** regardless of how the software models it, and if the undercut is only blocked out virtually, the resulting gap between the undercut tooth surface and the milled restoration is simply **filled with cement** at seating — a thicker, less-controlled cement layer than intended. Physical block-out before scanning avoids this entirely [SRC, CR].

### Components
1. **Acquisition:** intraoral scanner or laboratory scanner (see named systems above).
2. **Design software:** margin line, proximal contacts, occlusion, connectors.
3. **Manufacturing unit:** chairside mill, laboratory mill, or printer (3/4/5-axis).
4. **Materials:** blocks and discs.

### Materials (Nejat, 2024)
Subtractive manufacture mills feldspathic, leucite, lithium-disilicate (precrystallised at about 130 MPa, crystallised above 750 °C), ZLS, resin-matrix and presintered zirconia blocks. Machinable-material data (Roberson et al, 2006, via digest): Vita Mark II 3-point strength 122 MPa, ProCAD 160 MPa, In-Ceram Alumina 446 MPa, Lava zirconia 1625 MPa, showing a 13-fold range within "CAD/CAM ceramics".

### Presintered versus fully dense zirconia
Presintered blocks are milled in the soft "green" state then sintered with about 25% shrinkage, compensated by software enlargement; HIP blocks are milled fully dense (harder to mill, less variable) (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013, via digest).

### Accuracy
Marginal and internal fit of lithium disilicate and zirconia crowns are similar; internal gaps of lithium disilicate and PICN inlays are under 100 μm; in one comparison milled crowns had better internal fit than pressed, while pressed had better marginal adaptation; milled zirconia was more precise than printed zirconia and heat-pressed lithium disilicate, though all values were clinically acceptable (Bacchi and Cesar, 2022).

### Advantages
Single-visit restorations, standardised industrial-quality blocks, reduced laboratory steps, digital storage, precise.

### Disadvantages
Equipment cost, learning curve, milling defects and bur wear, limited block shapes and shades, waste of material, scanning accuracy for deep subgingival margins [Likely], limited characterisation without staining.

### Additive manufacturing
3D printing of zirconia and lithium disilicate has promising geometry freedom but problems: slurry viscosity, bubble porosity, interlayer defects and lower surface hardness; flexural strength results are inconsistent (Nejat, 2024; Bacchi and Cesar, 2022).

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 350; about 16 min)**

### Introduction
CAD/CAM is the computer-assisted design and manufacture of restorations from a digital impression.

### Components
Scanner (optical impression) → design software → milling or printing unit.

### History
Duret (1973) first conceived CAD/CAM for restorations; Mörmann/CEREC (1985) brought the first clinical chairside system.

### Named scanners
3M True Definition, TRIOS (optical); iTero (confocal); CEREC AC Bluecam (active triangulation); PlanScan (laser triangulation).

### Classification
- **Chairside (CEREC):** same-day inlays, onlays, crowns, veneers.
- **Laboratory:** scanned impressions or dies; zirconia frameworks.
- **Centralised milling.**
- **Subtractive versus additive.**
- **By milling axes:** 3-axis (simple), 4-axis, 5-axis (undercuts, complex anatomy).

### Digital vs elastomeric impression accuracy
| Parameter | Digital | Elastomeric |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect proximal contacts | 62% | 46% |
| Acceptable marginal fit | 92% | 71% |
| Acceptable occlusion | 74% | 48% |

### Undercut-blocking principle
Block out undercuts physically with dentin substitute BEFORE scanning — the mill cannot reproduce an undercut, and virtual block-out just gets filled with cement at seating.

### Materials
Feldspathic, leucite, lithium disilicate, ZLS, zirconia, PICN/resin-matrix, composite blocks, PMMA (provisionals), wax and titanium.

### Applications
Inlays, onlays, veneers, crowns, FDPs, implant abutments and frameworks, provisional restorations, surgical guides and dentures.

### Advantages
Same-day treatment; reduced impression and laboratory steps; consistent material quality; accurate marginal fit (internal gaps under 100 μm for LDS inlays); digital archive.

### Disadvantages
High initial cost; steep learning curve; milling flaws; limited esthetic characterisation; scanner limitations for subgingival margins.

### Conclusion
CAD/CAM has changed ceramic dentistry from manual to standardised, repeatable manufacture; accuracy is clinically acceptable, but cost and technique sensitivity remain limits.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Five stages: digitisation, CAD, CAM, esthetics, finishing.
- History: Duret 1973 (concept), Mörmann/CEREC 1985 (first clinical system).
- Named scanners by technology: iTero (confocal), CEREC AC Bluecam (active triangulation), PlanScan (laser triangulation), 3M True Definition/TRIOS (optical).
- 3/4/5-axis milling; higher axis count mills more complex geometry/undercuts.
- Digital vs elastomeric: contacts 62% vs 46%, fit 92% vs 71%, occlusion 74% vs 48%.
- Undercuts must be physically blocked out BEFORE scanning, not virtually in software.
- Zirconia sinters with about 25% shrinkage; software compensates.
- Presintered versus HIP blocks.
- Printed zirconia is promising but inconsistent.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling CAD/CAM a material rather than a manufacturing method.
2. Forgetting sintering shrinkage and crystallisation firing.
3. Not distinguishing chairside from laboratory systems.
