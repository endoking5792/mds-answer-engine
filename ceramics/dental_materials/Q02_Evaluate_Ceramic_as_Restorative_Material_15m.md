## STUDY MODE

### Frame of an "evaluate" answer
An evaluation needs criteria. Use the ideal-requirement frame: biological, optical, mechanical, handling and clinical-evidence criteria, then reach a verdict.

**Biological.** Ceramics are chemically inert and biocompatible, with low plaque affinity when polished, good gingival response and low thermal conductivity, which protects the pulp (Moshaverinia, 2020; Nejat, 2024). A more specific biocompatibility nuance from the textbook audit: lithium-disilicate ceramics show measurable **initial** in-vitro cytotoxicity that **diminishes over several weeks** as "surface corrosion" subsides (Powers & Wataha, citing Giordano et al.) — the material is not acutely inert from the first contact, the cytotoxic response resolves over time [SRC, PW]. Occupational note (applicable where metal-ceramic alloys are discussed alongside ceramic restorations): some Ni-Cr base-metal alloys used under porcelain contain <0.9% beryllium for castability/bond, and Be release during grinding/polishing of such alloys is a known carcinogen risk to the dental technician, at a release rate 5-6× the bulk alloy composition (van Noort) [SRC, VN].

**Optical.** Translucency, fluorescence and shade stability allow the best esthetic match of any restorative material. Translucency falls as crystalline content rises; opaque substrates are masked best by zirconia (low light transmission) and worst by feldspathic ceramic, where the underlying stump colour shows through (Goldstein et al, 2018, via study-material file `SubModule_6.2`).

**Mechanical.** The central weakness is brittleness. Feldspathic porcelain has a flexural strength of 60-80 MPa and a fracture toughness of 0.78 MPa·m^0.5; zirconia reaches 900-1500 MPa and over 6 MPa·m^0.5 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest). Compressive strength is high but tensile strength is low, so failure starts from surface or processing flaws and is made worse by residual tensile stress, thermal-gradient stress and occlusal contact damage (Zhang and Kelly, 2017). Metal-ceramic crowns survive better than several early all-ceramic systems because the metal carries the load (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest).

**Wear.** Ceramics are harder than enamel and can abrade the antagonist if rough. Zirconia produced less antagonist enamel wear than glass-ceramics when highly polished, and surface roughness predicts antagonist wear (Bacchi and Cesar, 2022). The primary-textbook wear data are specific and quotable: Al-Hiyasat et al. (1998) showed a carbonated-beverage wear effect, and glassy/glass-dominated ceramic hardness (>460 kg/mm²) well exceeds natural enamel (343 kg/mm²), explaining why ceramic preferentially wears opposing enamel; crystalline ceramics are harder still (1200-1500 kg/mm²), so the wear risk to the antagonist rises further with crystalline content (Phillips) [SRC, PHIL]. This is also why porcelain denture teeth opposing natural dentition are avoided in favour of polymer teeth.

**Clinical evidence.** Empress inlays/onlays 90% at 8 years, veneers 94.4% at 12 years, crowns 95.2% at 11 years, Empress 2 crowns 95.5% at 10 years, but 3-unit Empress 2 FDPs only 70% at 5 years because connectors fracture (Guess et al, 2011). Veneered zirconia crowns show veneer chipping of 2-9% at 2-3 years, and FDPs 3-36% at 1-5 years (Guess et al, 2011). Monolithic zirconia crowns on natural teeth show 91-100% survival (Nejat, 2024). Feldspathic veneers 95.5% at 10 years (Nejat, 2024).

**Handling and economics.** Technique-sensitive bonding; laboratory cost; CAD/CAM has lowered cost and time (Nejat, 2024). Etchable ceramics repair intraorally; zirconia does not.

### Verdict
Ceramic is the best-matching, most biocompatible restorative material but the most failure-sensitive: success depends on indication (thickness, support, occlusion), correct adhesive protocol and polishing.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim — modern all-ceramic restorations match metal-ceramic for single units but not yet for all multi-unit spans.** Single-unit survival is 90-100% across most systems at 8-12 years (Guess et al, 2011; Nejat, 2024), while connector fracture limits glass-ceramic FDPs (Guess et al, 2011). INTERPRETATION: the failure mode is geometric (connector size) and veneer-related, not intrinsic to the ceramic. LIMITATION: the survival series use different definitions of failure and different follow-up. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 560; about 25 min)**

### Introduction
Ceramics are non-metallic inorganic materials fired at high temperature; they are the principal tooth-coloured indirect restorative materials. Evaluation is made against the ideal requirements of a restorative material.

### Advantages
1. **Esthetics:** translucency, fluorescence and colour stability match enamel and dentin.
2. **Biocompatibility:** inert, no corrosion, low plaque retention when polished (note: lithium disilicate shows measurable initial cytotoxicity that diminishes over weeks as surface corrosion subsides — PW, Giordano et al.).
3. **Low thermal conductivity:** pulpal protection.
4. **High compressive strength and hardness; wear-resistant surface.**
5. **Dimensional stability and low solubility.**
6. **Adhesive bonding** (etchable ceramics) allows conservative, bonded restorations.
7. **Conservative prep** with modern adhesive glass-ceramics.

### Disadvantages
1. **Brittle:** low tensile strength and fracture toughness; flaw-sensitive.
2. **Technique-sensitive:** bonding protocol, preparation design, minimum thickness.
3. **Abrasive to opposing enamel** if rough or glazed-worn (glassy ceramic hardness >460 kg/mm² vs enamel 343 kg/mm²; crystalline ceramics 1200-1500 kg/mm² wear the antagonist even more — PHIL).
4. **Chipping** of veneered zirconia (2-9% single crowns at 2-3 years) and fracture of long-span glass-ceramic FDPs.
5. **Cost and laboratory dependence;** low-temperature degradation of zirconia.
6. **Difficult repair** (zirconia) and limited masking in thin translucent forms.

### Comparative properties
| Ceramic | Strength (MPa) | Fracture toughness | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feldspathic | 60-80 | 0.78 | Veneers |
| Leucite | ~120 | 1.2 | Anterior |
| Lithium disilicate | 262-420 | 2.75 | All single units |
| Zirconia | 900-1500 | >6 | Posterior, FDP |

### Clinical performance
Empress inlays 90% at 8 years, veneers 94.4% at 12 years, crowns 95.2% at 11 years; 3-unit FDPs 70% at 5 years.

### Indications and contraindications
Indicated: esthetic zone, discoloured teeth, adequate enamel, patient demand. Contraindicated: bruxism with thin ceramic, insufficient clearance, poor isolation, deep subgingival margins without access.

### Conclusion
Ceramic is the best esthetic and biological restorative material, but its brittleness demands correct material selection, thickness, bonding and occlusion; with these, single-unit survival is above 90% at a decade.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Metal-ceramic survives well because metal carries load; veneered zirconia fails by veneer chipping.
- 3-unit glass-ceramic FDPs fail at connectors.
- Etchable ceramics: HF + silane; zirconia: air abrasion + MDP.
- Polish, do not leave rough: antagonist wear.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing properties without a verdict.
2. Omitting clinical survival data.
3. Claiming ceramics are "strong"; they are hard and compression-strong but brittle.
4. Forgetting wear of antagonist.
