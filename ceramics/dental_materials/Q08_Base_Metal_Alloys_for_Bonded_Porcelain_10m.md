## STUDY MODE

### Definition and classification
Base-metal alloys are predominantly non-noble: ADA class "predominantly base metal" has noble-metal content under 25 wt% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest). For metal-ceramic work the systems are nickel-chromium (Ni-Cr), cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr), and occasionally titanium. Cast Ni-Cr is used specifically for ceramic-metal restorations; cast Co-Cr for removable frameworks and ceramic-metal restorations (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest).

### Composition
Nickel and/or cobalt with about 15-20% chromium (Sikri, 2017, via digest); Sturdevant describes 18-28 wt% chromium forming a passivating Cr2O3 film that is brittle but re-forms (Roberson et al, 2006, via digest). Beryllium is added to some Ni-Cr alloys; it lowers melting temperature and surface tension, improves castability and strengthens the porcelain-metal bond, but is hazardous (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest).

### Properties relevant to bonded porcelain
High modulus (sag resistance at firing temperature; one of the listed requirements for metal-ceramic alloys), high hardness, high fusion temperature, low cost [Likely]; thin copings and long spans follow from the high stiffness. Oxide formation (Cr2O3 or NiO; Be) supports porcelain bonding. Disadvantages: hard to cast, polish and adjust; technique-sensitive oxide layer that can be too thick and cause bond failure [Likely]; higher potential for corrosion if passivation is damaged.

### Biological concerns
Nickel allergy occurs in 5-8 to 10-20% of females in different passages of Craig's; patients with nickel allergy should receive nickel-free alloys such as Co-Cr (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest). Palladium allergy is almost always accompanied by nickel allergy, but only about one third of nickel-allergic patients react to palladium. Beryllium exposure is a hazard to technicians (Sakaguchi et al, 2019, via digest). Galvanic series: Ni-Cr and Co-Cr sit between amalgam and gold; avoid occlusal contact of widely separated alloys.

### Bonding
Resin bonding to base metal is relatively straightforward (more than 20 MPa normal) via the natural oxide layer, with sandblasting and steam cleaning (McCabe and Walls, 2008, via digest).

## EXAM MODE
**(Word count about 340; about 15 min)**

### Introduction
Base-metal alloys (noble metal under 25 wt%) are used as frameworks for bonded porcelain restorations in place of costly gold alloys.

### Types
| Alloy | Composition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Ni-Cr | Nickel base, about 15-20% Cr (18-28 wt% quoted by Sturdevant), Be in some | Cast ceramic-metal crowns and bridges |
| Co-Cr | Cobalt base, about 15-20% Cr | Ceramic-metal restorations and RPD frameworks |
| Titanium | CP Ti / Ti-6Al-4V (modulus about 100 GPa for CP Ti) | Implant systems; passivates by TiO2 |

### Properties
High modulus and hardness; low density; high fusion temperature; thin coping possible; good sag resistance; stable oxide; economical.

### Advantages over noble alloys
Strength, rigidity, low cost, thin copings (conserve tooth), higher bond-compatible oxide.

### Disadvantages
Difficult casting and finishing, high hardness (wear and trimming), thick oxide may cause porcelain debonding, nickel allergy, beryllium toxicity, galvanic corrosion, grey colour.

### Precautions
Avoid in nickel-allergic patients (use Co-Cr), technician protection against beryllium, controlled oxidation.

### Conclusion
Base-metal alloys give strong, economical frameworks for bonded porcelain; the price is technique sensitivity and biological precautions.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Predominantly base metal = noble content under 25 wt%.
- Cr 18-28% gives Cr2O3 passivation.
- Beryllium improves bond and castability, but is toxic.
- Co-Cr for nickel-allergic patients.
