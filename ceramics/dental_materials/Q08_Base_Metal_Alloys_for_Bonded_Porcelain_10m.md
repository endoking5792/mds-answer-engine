## STUDY MODE

### S1. Definition and groups [definition]
Base metal alloys have a noble-metal content below 25 wt% (Anusavice et al, 2013). They rely on chromium for corrosion resistance and fall into Ni-Cr (with or without Be) and Co-Cr groups (Anusavice et al, 2013). Chromium forms a thin surface oxide layer that improves corrosion resistance (Anusavice et al, 2013); Bhat describes a Cr2O3 film that also helps metal-ceramic bonding (Bhat et al, 2019).

### S2. Composition [composition]
| Book | Ni-Cr statement |
|---|---|
| Craig and Powers, 2002 | most contain 60% to 80% nickel, 10% to 27% chromium; beryllium-containing alloys 1.6% to 2.0% Be (about 11 atomic percent); Co-Cr 53% to 67% Co, 25% to 32% Cr, 2% to 6% Mo |
| Sakaguchi et al, 2019 (Craig 14th, Table 10.14) | Ni-Cr Ni 69 to 77, Cr 13 to 16; Co-Cr Co 55 to 58, Cr 15 to 25; Ti 90 to 100 (wt%) |
| Anusavice et al, 2013 (Table 16-7) | listed Co-Cr alloys Co 55 to 61, Cr 25 to 27 (wt%); a listed Ni-Cr-Be alloy has 1.6% Be |
| Bhat et al, 2019 | Ni 70 to 80%, Cr 12 to 20%, Mo or W 1.5 to 5% |
| van Noort, 2013 | Ni-Cr-Mo typically 77% Ni, 12% Cr, 3.5% Mo; Be below 0.9% |
| McCabe and Walls, 2008 | Ni-Cr typically 70 to 80% Ni and 10 to 25% Cr; high casting shrinkage and voids in castings |

### S3. Properties [properties]
Higher hardness and elastic modulus allow a coping 0.1 to 0.2 mm thick (Anusavice et al, 2013); van Noort gives 0.3 mm for Ni-Cr-Mo against 0.5 mm for high-gold alloys (van Noort, 2013). Density is about half that of a gold alloy (Anusavice et al, 2013). Fusion range is 1150 C to 1500 C (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S4. Casting [handling]
Acetylene-oxygen flame or induction melting, phosphate or silicate investment, a vent and larger sprue (Anusavice et al, 2013). Solidification shrinkage is greater than for gold alloys; type V die stone and more investment expansion are used (Anusavice et al, 2013). Powers and Wataha call these alloys the most difficult to cast and polish (Powers and Wataha, 2013).

### S5. Bonding and hazards [biology]
Ni-Cr fractures occur through the oxide layer (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Bond strength to porcelain is stated differently: McCabe and Walls say the Ni-Cr bond does not compare with that of the other alloys (McCabe and Walls, 2008); van Noort says clinical experience indicates the ceramic-to-metal bond is not as reliable (van Noort, 2013); Anusavice et al say the bond strength has not generally been shown superior or inferior to noble alloys, with no demonstrated difference in failure incidence (Anusavice et al, 2013); Bhat lists metal-ceramic bond strength like noble metal alloys (Bhat et al, 2019). Beryllium (below 0.9%) improves castability and bond, and is released on grinding at 5 to 6 times the bulk composition (van Noort, 2013). Phillips cautions that generalized statements on the superiority of beryllium-containing or non-beryllium alloys should not be made without supporting data (Anusavice et al, 2013). Nickel allergy is quoted differently (below). Titanium: porcelain sintering below 800 C is desirable and its low expansion needs special porcelains (Anusavice et al, 2013); van Noort gives 9.6 ppm per C and a weak bond if taken above 800 C (van Noort, 2013). Craig and Powers advise a nickel-free Co-Cr alloy for patients with a nickel allergy history (Craig and Powers, 2002); van Noort contraindicates nickel-containing alloys in known nickel allergy (van Noort, 2013).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Definition
Alloys with noble metal below 25 wt%, used as frameworks (copings) for bonded porcelain (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 2. Types and composition
| Type | Composition as printed |
|---|---|
| Ni-Cr | 60 to 80% Ni (Craig and Powers, 2002); 70 to 80% Ni (Bhat et al, 2019); Ni-Cr-Mo 77% Ni (van Noort, 2013) |
| Ni-Cr-Be | 1.6 to 2.0% Be (Craig and Powers, 2002); below 0.9% Be (van Noort, 2013) |
| Co-Cr | 53 to 67% Co, 25 to 32% Cr (Craig and Powers, 2002); Co 55 to 58, Cr 15 to 25 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); Co 55 to 61, Cr 25 to 27 (Anusavice et al, 2013) |
| Titanium | Ti 90 to 100 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) |

### 3. Properties and advantages
- Coping 0.1 to 0.2 mm (Anusavice et al, 2013); 0.3 mm against 0.5 mm for gold (van Noort, 2013).
- Density about half that of gold alloy (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Low cost (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Ni-Cr-Be castability: best in mesh tests; no superiority claim without data (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 4. Disadvantages
- Greater solidification shrinkage (Anusavice et al, 2013; McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Hard to cast and polish (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- Poor castability with voids; fractures through the oxide layer (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Porcelain bond: "does not compare" (McCabe and Walls, 2008); "not as reliable" (van Noort, 2013); not shown superior or inferior (Anusavice et al, 2013); like noble alloys (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Nickel allergen; beryllium dust (van Noort, 2013).

### 5. Nickel allergy (figures differ)
| Book | Figure |
|---|---|
| Anusavice et al, 2013 | patch test: 9.7% F, 0.8% M aged 24 to 44 with nickel prostheses; all ages 4.5% and 1.5%; no correlation with intraoral nickel alloy |
| Craig and Powers, 2002 | 5% to 8% of females; no correlation with intraoral nickel restorations |
| Powers and Wataha, 2013 | 8% to 15% of the population |

### 6. Technique
Phosphate or silicate investment, type V die stone (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 7. Conclusion
Base metal alloys give rigid, cheap, thin frameworks; the cost is technique sensitivity and biological precautions (Anusavice et al, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Noble content below 25 wt% (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Coping 0.1 to 0.2 mm (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Be below 0.9%, released 5 to 6 times bulk (van Noort, 2013).
- Greater shrinkage: type V die stone (Anusavice et al, 2013).
