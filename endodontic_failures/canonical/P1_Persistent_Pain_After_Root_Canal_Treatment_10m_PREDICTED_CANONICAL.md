## Explanation

**Frequency.** Persistent pain was reported in 12% of cases even when treatment met acceptable standards, whereas a systematic review found 5% with pain more than 6 months after treatment (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]; Chugal and colleagues quote about 5% for persistent all-cause pain 6 months or longer after treatment, which may include pain from an adjacent tooth, referred nonodontogenic pain or deafferentation pain (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC]. The 12% and 5% figures come from different sources (a single study and a systematic review) and are shown side by side.

**Principle.** Decisions need an accurate diagnosis (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]. Causes can be endodontic (infection, technical problems, fracture) or non-endodontic.

**Endodontic causes and management** (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]
- *Persistent infection:* bacteria in the apical canal unaffected by treatment; managed by retreatment or periradicular surgery.
- *Secondary infection:* organisms carried in during or after treatment, including by saliva leakage from leaky margins or fracture or loss of the restoration; even teeth with vital pulps can develop chronic pain this way.
- *Radiographically undetectable apical periodontitis:* lesions limited to cancellous bone can pass unnoticed on films; CBCT can detect such lesions.
- *Overfilling:* irritation by compression of the periodontal ligament and by chemical irritants; in a well-treated canal analgesics may suffice.
- *Perforation:* ongoing pain when linked to persistent infection or overfilling.
- *Missed canals:* in one study, apical periodontitis was present in 98% of root-filled teeth with a missing canal; they must be treated.
- *Vertical or oblique root fractures and cracks:* can cause pain; for a split tooth or vertical/oblique root fracture the only predictable treatment is root resection, hemisection or extraction, whereas for a crack, if extraction is not indicated, a cusp-reinforced crown or onlay may be placed, with an unpredictable prognosis.
- *Wrong tooth:* the pain may come from an adjacent tooth; the tooth causing the pain is treated according to its own pathology.

**Non-endodontic causes.** In a systematic review (Nixdorf et al, quoted by Siqueira and Rôças), at least half of cases of persistent pain more than 6 months after treatment had a nonodontogenic cause; sinusitis, neuropathic disorders, myofascial pain, migraine, neoplasia and occlusal trauma can mimic endodontic pain; refer to the appropriate specialist (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC].

**Central sensitization.** Abnormal amplification of incoming sensory signals in the central nervous system, particularly the trigeminal nucleus; patients with preoperative symptoms are more prone to postoperative symptoms; preemptive analgesia, long-acting local anesthetics and postoperative analgesia reduce the risk (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC].

A checklist of possible causes helps the clinician (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition and frequency.** Pain persisting after root canal treatment; reported in 12% of cases in one study and in 5% beyond six months in a systematic review (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**A. Endodontic causes and management**

| Cause | Management |
|---|---|
| Persistent infection (apical bacteria untouched by treatment) | Retreatment or periradicular surgery |
| Secondary infection (leakage, breach of asepsis) | Retreatment or periradicular surgery |
| Undetected apical periodontitis (cancellous lesion) | CBCT can detect it; retreatment or surgery |
| Overfilling | Analgesics if well-treated; retreat poor fills; surgery to curette extruded material |
| Perforation | Intracanal repair or surgery, by site and bone disease; extraction sometimes unavoidable |
| Missed canal (98% with apical periodontitis in one study) | Locate and treat; surgery if not negotiable |
| Crack | Cusp-reinforcing crown or onlay if extraction not indicated; prognosis unpredictable |
| Split tooth or vertical/oblique root fracture | Root resection, hemisection or extraction |
| Wrong tooth treated | Treat the correct tooth according to its pathology |

**B. Non-endodontic causes:** in a systematic review (Nixdorf et al), at least half of persistent cases beyond six months; sinusitis, neuropathic, myofascial, migraine, neoplasia, occlusal trauma; refer (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**C. Central sensitization:** amplified incoming signals in the central nervous system, particularly the trigeminal nucleus; patients with preoperative symptoms are more prone; prevent with preemptive analgesia and long-acting local anesthetic.

**Details to quote**
- *Persistent infection:* bacteria remain on untouched canal wall, in recesses of oval canals, in lateral canals, ramifications and isthmuses, or beneath dentine and pulp debris packed into the apical canal; instrumentation short of the foramen or with small instruments raises the risk.
- *Secondary infection:* a breach in the aseptic chain or saliva leakage from leaky margins, fracture or loss of restoration; vital teeth can be affected.
- *Overfilling and perforation* can be missed on a single film because of beam angulation; change the angle or use CBCT.
- *Fractures and cracks* may not show on films; CBCT can help.

**D. Approach**
1. [Likely] History (onset, character, relation to treatment) and periodontal probing for fracture.
2. Pulp and periradicular tests of the suspect and adjacent teeth to exclude a wrong-tooth source.
3. Radiographs at more than one angle; CBCT when films are not conclusive.
4. Treat the endodontic cause or refer for the non-endodontic one.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Persistent pain: 12% (one study) or 5% beyond 6 months (systematic review).
- Systematic review (Nixdorf): at least half of persistent pain beyond 6 months was non-odontogenic.
- Missed canals: apical periodontitis in 98% in one study.
- CBCT can reveal undetected lesions, overfilling, perforations, fractures.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
- Flowchart: persistent pain → odontogenic? → infection/overfill/perforation/missed canal/fracture → management; non-odontogenic → refer (3 min).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Assuming all persistent pain is infection and retreating without a diagnosis.
2. Omitting non-odontogenic and neuropathic causes.
3. Forgetting CBCT for hidden lesions, perforations and fractures.
4. Not treating missed canals.
5. Failing to give pre-emptive analgesia in patients with preoperative pain.
