## 1. Why the restoration matters

### Explanation

Restoring a root-filled tooth is inseparable from the root canal treatment in planning, execution and prognosis, although high-quality clinical outcome data are scarce (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. The main reason for extracting root-filled teeth is not failure of the endodontic treatment but caries, periodontal disease, restorative factors and root fracture, and the quality of the restorative treatment is equally as important as the root canal treatment to prognosis (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. Likewise Malek and Lin note that studies show the main reason for extraction is usually restorative failure or periodontal disease, not endodontic failure (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** A root-filled tooth is usually extracted for restorative failure, caries, periodontal disease or fracture rather than failure of the root canal treatment itself (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017; Malek and Lin, 2017). The quality of the restoration is as important to prognosis as that of the root canal treatment (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017).

**Scope** [Likely]: failure of the restoration of a root-filled tooth is taken to include loss of the coronal seal (leakage, caries, lost or fractured restoration), failure of a post and core (loss of retention, post fracture) and fracture of the crown or root.

## 2. Causes of failure

### Explanation

**A. Loss of the coronal seal.** Coronal microleakage is regarded as the most common cause of endodontic failure, and leakage under failing restorations is a major cause of new and recurrent disease (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. Goldberg also lists coronal leakage over time as a reason for failure and, although he calls the role of coronal filtration debatable, says a satisfactory restoration is necessary for functional and esthetic success (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. Hülsmann and colleagues call coronal restoration quality one of the most reliable predictors of success or failure (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Sikri notes that both bacteria and endotoxins can penetrate an obturated canal (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Teeth with satisfactory coronal restorations healed significantly better (OR 3.3; 95% CI 1.1 to 10.3); in the London Eastman study, which counted exposed root filling, or marginal defects with a history of de-cementation, as unsatisfactory, the effect was much larger (OR 10.7; 95% CI 3.7 to 31.5), which the authors suggest may be due to that definition (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]; none of the restorative materials gives a permanent leakproof seal (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira note, however, that recent indirect evidence seems to point to persistent infection, rather than secondary infection through coronal leakage, as the most common cause of posttreatment disease (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]; Hülsmann and colleagues add that the two cannot be told apart clinically and may coexist (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. The sources are shown side by side.

**B. Provisional restorations and delay.** Provisional restorations generally give a poor seal, and none of the provisional materials tested prevented coronal leakage over 30 days. In one retrospective study teeth restored within two weeks of root canal therapy had higher survival, and teeth with no permanent restoration have dramatically lower survival; timely placement of a definitive restoration improves survival (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. Damp cotton left beneath a restoration is an excellent culture medium (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC].

**C. Poor restorative technique.** Errors during the restorative process can compromise success; no space should remain between root filling and build-up, and rubber dam isolation is required whenever the root filling is exposed (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Glass ionomers are markedly inferior to other options for coronal restoration of root-filled teeth and should be liners or bases only (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC].

**D. Inadequate cuspal coverage and tooth structure.** Cuspal coverage protects posterior teeth against extraction; one systematic review found 84% survival in the first 3 years for root-filled teeth restored with a core build-up alone, without cuspal coverage, with a significant fall afterwards (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. In a study of 116 failed and extracted root-filled teeth (Vire, quoted by Baba and Goodacre), teeth with crowns were lost after an average of 87 months against 50 months without; in a retrospective cohort of molars intact except for the access opening, 5-year survival without cuspal coverage was 36%, a result the chapter gives without further comment although it introduces the cohort as restorable with composite (Baba and Goodacre, 2019) [SRC]. In anterior teeth crowns are indicated only when the tooth is structurally weakened by large or multiple restorations, or needs a form or colour change that bleaching, resin bonding or veneers cannot achieve (Baba and Goodacre, 2019) [SRC]. Unnecessary removal of tooth structure predisposes to fracture (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. Retaining substantial tooth structure matters more than any restorative choice (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. In clinical studies of fixed partial dentures, many with long spans and cantilevers, endodontically treated abutments failed more often than vital abutments, due to tooth fracture (Baba and Goodacre, 2019) [SRC].

**E. Posts and cores.** Posts do not strengthen the tooth; they weaken it through dentine removal and stress concentration, and teeth with posts are lost more often than teeth without. Threaded posts gave the highest root fracture incidence (7%) against tapered (2%) and parallel (1%) posts; glass-fibre posts show more restoration failure but fewer tooth fractures than metal ones; short posts distribute stress poorly and a post that leaves less than adequate apical filling risks the seal; in an anterior tooth with a prefabricated post in a coronally wide post space, minimal or no ferrule means the cement seal will eventually break, leading to leakage, caries, loss of post retention and crown failure; perforations during post preparation are not uncommon (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. Post-and-core failure averaged 9% (range 6% to 14%, 11 studies), the commonest causes being loss of retention and tooth fracture (Baba and Goodacre, 2019) [SRC].

**F. Other causes of loss.** Caries, periodontal disease and root fracture are the main reasons for extraction of root-filled teeth (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. Goldberg advises that the prognosis must be considered doubtful in patients with uncontrolled diabetes or severe immunocompromising disease (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC].

**Two further points from Terauchi and colleagues** (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]: outcome may depend more on the marginal adaptation of the restoration than on the quality of the root filling, and a recent study found significantly less tooth loss in teeth restored with fibre posts than in teeth without a post, regardless of a full crown. This conflicts with Baba, White and Bogen, who report that teeth restored with posts are generally lost more often than those without; the two positions are shown side by side and not reconciled.

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Loss of coronal seal**
- Coronal microleakage, the most common cause of endodontic failure in this source (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017). Satisfactory restoration: OR 3.3 for healing; OR 10.7 in one study that counted exposed filling or marginal defects as unsatisfactory (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019). Indirect evidence suggests persistent infection is commoner than leakage (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022).
- Provisional restorations leak; none of the tested materials prevented leakage in 30 days.
- Delay: in one study teeth restored within two weeks survived better; no permanent restoration sharply lowers survival.
- Unsatisfactory-restoration criteria: marginal discrepancy or discoloration, recurrent caries, past decementation (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019). Damp cotton under the restoration. Marginal adaptation may matter more than root-filling quality (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021).

**B. Poor restorative technique:** space between root filling and build-up; no rubber dam; glass ionomer used as core; residual sealer, gutta-percha or provisional material left in the access (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Baba, White and Bogen, 2017).

**C. Lack of cuspal coverage or excess tooth loss:** in one study of extracted teeth, crowned teeth were lost at 87 months, uncrowned at 50; fracture risk after excess dentine removal; root-filled abutments fracture more than vital ones (Baba and Goodacre, 2019; Goldberg, 2019).

**D. Posts and cores:** loss of retention (about 5% of posts) and tooth fracture (about 2%) are the two commonest causes; post-and-core failure averages 9% (11 studies); threaded posts give most fractures; short posts, no ferrule, perforation (Baba and Goodacre, 2019; Baba, White and Bogen, 2017).

**E. Other:** caries, periodontal disease, root fracture (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017); uncontrolled diabetes or severe immunocompromise make the prognosis doubtful (Goldberg, 2019).

**Details to quote**
- *Interim phase:* durable thick provisional restoration between visits; a synthetic foam sponge is preferred to cotton because it does not support bacterial growth; zinc oxide eugenol only for periods up to 1 to 2 weeks (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017).
- *Post design:* short posts do not distribute spreading forces equally along the root axis and can often fracture the coronal and mid-root; in an anterior tooth with a wide post space and minimal ferrule the cement seal breaks, causing leakage, caries, loss of post retention and crown failure (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017).
- *Fixed prostheses:* endodontically treated abutments failed more often than vital abutments, due to fracture (Baba and Goodacre, 2019).
- *Periodontal status:* evaluate before endodontic treatment (Malek and Lin, 2017); assess need for crown lengthening before restoration (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017).

## 3. Management

### Explanation

**Diagnose.** A differential diagnosis is needed to clarify whether the disease is endodontic, non-endodontic or mixed (for example combined endodontic-periodontal lesions) (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. [Likely] In a root-filled tooth the candidate causes include leakage, caries, fracture and periodontal disease.

**Coronal leakage.** If coronal leakage is the cause, re-restoration and/or revision of the root canal treatment followed by a coronal restoration is the treatment of choice, though re-restoration alone should be avoided in chronic cases where leakage continued for a long time (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Before restorative treatment all existing restorations should generally be removed unless recently placed by the treating dentist (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]; nonsurgical retreatment is indicated when a root-filled tooth is suspected to have been contaminated by leakage or loss of its restoration, to decontaminate the canal system and place a new root filling and restoration (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC] (retreatment technique is in the retreatment answers).

**Post or core failure.** Remove the failed post and core (ultrasonics for metal posts; tapering posts are easier than parallel ones; fibre posts need new drills), assess the remaining dentine, and rebuild; an enclosed perforation is repaired with MTA, and one exposed to the oral cavity with glass ionomer cement (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Restorability.** The line between restorable and nonrestorable is a movable one; extraction is recommended for unrestorable teeth, vertical root fracture or insufficient periodontal support, among other situations (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

**Prevention.** Place the foundation restoration immediately after obturation with rubber dam still in place; seal the canal orifices with a barrier if the tooth cannot be restored at once; use cuspal coverage on posterior teeth; avoid posts when enough tooth remains; leave at least 4-6 mm of apical filling when a post is needed.

#### ✎ Exam core

1. **Establish the cause.** Differential diagnosis: endodontic, non-endodontic or mixed (Goldberg, 2019). [Likely] Consider leakage, caries, fracture, periodontal disease.
2. **Leaking restoration only:** replace it; if the leakage has been long-standing, revise the root canal treatment as well (Sikri, 2017).
3. **Contaminated or failing root filling:** remove existing restorations and retreat nonsurgically; rebuild and seal (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).
4. **Failed post:** remove with ultrasonics (tapered posts are easier than parallel; fibre posts need new drills), repair an enclosed perforation with MTA (glass ionomer if open to the mouth), re-restore with a conservative post only if required (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Sikri, 2017; Baba, White and Bogen, 2017).
5. **Fracture or non-restorable tooth:** extraction if vertical fracture, insufficient periodontal support or too little structure; replace with an implant or prosthesis (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
6. **Prevention**
   - Place the definitive foundation immediately, under rubber dam.
   - Orifice barrier if delayed.
   - Cuspal coverage on posterior teeth; direct restoration in intact anterior teeth.
   - Avoid posts unless little coronal structure remains; leave at least 4-6 mm of apical filling.

**Principles of re-restoration**
- Remove all existing restorations (unless recently placed by the treating dentist) and recurrent caries, check for cracks and periodontal need, and assess whether enough tooth remains for a lasting restoration (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).
- Extraction is the last resort, only when the tooth is shown to be nonrepairable (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). [Likely] Tell the patient the prognosis and fall-back options.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: coronal leakage is a major cause of failure.**
- *Baba, White and Bogen:* most common cause. *Gulabivala and Ng:* restoration quality OR 3.3, and 10.7 in one study with a leakage-based definition (wide confidence intervals). *Rôças and colleagues:* indirect evidence points to persistent infection as the commoner cause.
- *Strength:* observational data; leakage is hard to separate from persistent infection clinically (Hülsmann and colleagues: the two may coexist).
- *Conclusion:* both mechanisms operate; place a sealed definitive restoration promptly regardless. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Teeth are lost more from restorative failure, caries, periodontal disease and fracture than from endodontic failure.
- Coronal leakage; provisional restorations leak; restore immediately (one study: better survival within two weeks).
- One study of extracted teeth: crowned lost at 87 months vs 50 uncrowned.
- Post-and-core failure 9%; loss of retention and fracture commonest; threaded posts 7% root fractures.
- Posts weaken teeth; avoid when possible; at least 4-6 mm apical filling.
