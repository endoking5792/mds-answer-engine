## 1. What retreatodontics means and why research matters

### Explanation

The AAE glossary defines retreatment as removing root canal filling material followed by cleaning, shaping and obturating the canals; Ingle's calls this too narrow (it excludes cases with nothing to remove, such as a missed canal) and prefers Carr's definition: a procedure on a tooth that previously had attempted definitive treatment and now needs further treatment to ensure success (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Schilder coined "retreatodontics" in 1986 (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Its aims equal those of initial treatment, but original canal patency is often lost (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

Demand is high: apical periodontitis is found in 36% of 28,881 root-filled teeth in pooled cross-sectional data (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Retreatment (retreatodontics, Schilder 1986) is further treatment of a tooth that previously had attempted definitive treatment, to ensure a successful result (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Aims.** The same as initial treatment (eradicate microorganisms, three-dimensional cleaning, fill and seal), but patency is often lost and obstacles include fillings, posts and fragments (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Demand.** About 36% of root-filled teeth show apical periodontitis (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Indications** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014): confirmed failure with accessible intraradicular infection; a new complex restoration over a technically poor filling; suspected contamination from leakage or a lost restoration.

## 2. Research on decision making

### Explanation

Radiographic judgement is unreliable: a retreatment decision was made in only 39% of cases despite a lesion, and about 20% of dentists changed their opinion when they re-read the same films (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Kvist's *Praxis* model treats periapical status as a continuum (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Risk data: for a technically deficient filling with a coronal seal and no symptoms or lesion, a six-year wait-and-see study (Van Nieuwenhuisen and colleagues) found complications in as little as 2.8%. Information on flare-up risk when retreating quiescent lesions is scarce; by Eriksen's estimate, quiescent lesions flare in under 5% per year untreated, whereas asymptomatic patients with quiescent lesions show a flare-up incidence of 20% to 40% when retreatment is initiated (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Hülsmann and colleagues cite a retrospective analysis of 915 treatments with an overall flare-up prevalence of 2.3% (5.3% in non-vital teeth, 4.4% in the retreatment group) and conclude, from these very limited data, that retreatment does not seem in itself to raise the risk (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. The two sources differ in design, and both are reported.

#### ✎ Exam core

- Radiographic decisions vary: retreatment chosen in only 39% of cases with a lesion; about 20% of dentists changed opinion on re-reading the same films.
- The *Praxis* concept treats periapical health as a continuum, with an individual cut-off for treatment.
- Quiescent lesions flare (estimated) in under 5% a year untreated but 20-40% in asymptomatic patients when retreatment starts (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019). A series cited by Hülsmann and colleagues found 2.3% overall (4.4% in retreatment); on very limited data, retreatment did not seem to raise the risk (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**Principles from the decision research.** Ingle's frames retreatment as three decisions and quotes Reit's principle: retreat any persistent lesion unless the patient declines, risk outweighs benefit, or cost exceeds predictable alternatives (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019). Case selection underlies a good success rate; the AAE rates all retreatment at least moderately difficult, and most needs a microscope and fine ultrasonic tips (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

## 3. Research on imaging: CBCT

### Explanation

Cohen's calls CBCT's usefulness in retreatment "unquestioned" and says that in posttreatment disease almost every case will benefit from 3D imaging. Treatment plans changed in up to 62% of cases when a CBCT volume was added, and evaluators missed at least one canal system in about 4 of 10 teeth on digital radiographs. The 2016 AAE/AAOMR update supports 3D imaging for posttreatment disease, while most clinicians still do not think CBCT is needed for every patient (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Selective retreatment of diseased roots only has been proposed on CBCT evidence (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. CBCT can also estimate working length more accurately than periapical radiographs (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's indicates CBCT when a patient has signs or symptoms and the films are nondiagnostic (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. CBCT detects about 20% more apical lesions than periapical films (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC] and may lower measured healing rates (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Chugal and colleagues note that the AAE-AAOMR guidelines recommend against CBCT as a routine diagnostic and outcome assessment tool and that using it only to monitor outcome in asymptomatic teeth is unjustified (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC].

Terauchi and colleagues cite an investigation by Torabinejad and colleagues in which 20% of root-filled teeth with no visible periapical lesion on films showed periapical radiolucencies of more than 1 mm on CBCT; the investigators cautioned against calling all such lesions treatment failures, because they may be healing, persistent disease or scar, and recommended follow-up and a work-up (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- CBCT shows the cause of posttreatment disease in three dimensions; treatment plans changed in up to 62% of cases; about 4 in 10 teeth had a canal system missed on digital radiographs.
- The 2016 AAE/AAOMR update supports it for posttreatment disease, but not routine use in every case or follow-up of asymptomatic teeth.
- It also helps estimate working length and select roots to retreat (a concept needing research).
- It detects about 20% more lesions and so may lower measured healing rates.

(Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021; Chugal et al, 2017)

- In an investigation cited by Terauchi and colleagues, 20% of root-filled teeth with no visible lesion on films showed radiolucencies over 1 mm on CBCT; the investigators cautioned that not all are failures (healing, scar or persistent disease) and advised follow-up and work-up (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021).

## 4. Outcome research

### Explanation

The reported healing of retreatment varies. Ingle's pooled mean is 66%, about 6% below primary treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis, with similar tooth survival; Cohen's outcomes chapter gives the same figure (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]; Treatment of Endodontic Infections 2nd ed reports 78% (Gulabivala and Ng, 2022) [SRC]; Setzer and colleagues quote a systematic review of 17 studies (1961 to 2005): 77.2% overall, 65% with apical periodontitis and 41% for lesions over 5 mm, though follow-up may have been too short for the large lesions (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's retreatment chapter quotes Friedman and Mor: 92% to 98% healed without and 74% to 86% with prior apical periodontitis, whether initial treatment or orthograde retreatment, a similarity they say challenges the historic view that retreatment has the poorer prognosis (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. The most significant retreatment-specific factor is the ability to remove or bypass the old filling or separated instrument (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues note that no higher-level outcome study includes modern techniques (NiTi, microscope, CBCT) (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and colleagues state that the primary goal of retreatment is repeating the primary treatment with repeated and improved disinfection, not exchanging the obturation material (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Berutti and Castellucci quote Chevigny and co-workers: 88% success for retreatment where prior treatment was unsatisfactory but only 66% where it was adequate (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]; that 66% is a different basis from Ingle's pooled mean of 66% above, and both are reported.

Surgery versus retreatment. Older surgery healed 59%, 80% when preceded by retreatment, and microsurgery 89% to 94%; Cohen's says surgery is no longer the second choice (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues: nonsurgical retreatment is the first choice, and a systematic review by Torabinejad and colleagues, which they quote, found more favourable long-term outcomes than endodontic surgery (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Surgery heals faster at one year but results balance at four years (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Nonsurgical and surgical retreatment are more cost-effective than replacement with a prosthesis (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

Terauchi and colleagues add that retreatment can match primary treatment if the apical foramen can be negotiated and the cause of failure overcome (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. Pontius notes that 25% to 35% of teeth treated in general practice have persisting apical periodontitis and, quoting Ørstavik (1996), that apical periodontitis can take 4 years or more to heal completely but radiographic signs of healing are seen after 1 year in 89% of cases (Pontius, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Source | Retreatment healing |
|---|---|
| Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021 | 66%, about 6% below primary treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis |
| Gulabivala and Ng, 2022 | 78% |
| Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021 | 77.2%; 65% with apical periodontitis; 41% for lesions over 5 mm |
| Friedman and Mor, in Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021 | 92-98% without, 74-86% with prior apical periodontitis (initial and retreatment alike) |

- Tooth survival is similar to primary treatment; the most significant retreatment-specific factor is removing or bypassing the old filling or fragment.
- No high-level study yet covers retreatment with modern techniques.
- Surgery: 59% (older), 80% after retreatment, 89-94% (microsurgery); Cohen's: no longer the second choice (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). Nonsurgical retreatment is first line (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021); surgery heals faster at 1 year but equals retreatment by 4 years (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**What the research changes in practice.** Ingle's finds retreatment healing about 6% lower but tooth survival similar, whereas Friedman and Mor (in Cohen's) find healing similar to initial treatment; either way surgery is reserved for failures or when nonsurgical access is not feasible (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 5. Technology: magnification, ultrasonics, bioceramics, files

### Explanation

The operating microscope, dedicated ultrasonic tips, MTA and bioceramic cements push the limits and allow successful retreatment of cases once untreatable; the second mesiobuccal system, present in more than 90% of maxillary first molars, can be identified and treated in over 75% of cases without a microscope and in about 90% with one; Cohen's adds that ultrasonic instruments have allowed predictable retreatment where surgery would previously have been indicated (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]; separated instruments can usually be removed because of better vision and ultrasonics, although rotary NiTi files increased breakage (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Accessible perforations repaired with calcium-silicate cements reach success up to 90% (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. NiTi retreatment files must be used with caution because most have cutting tips, and most studies show that complete removal of the gutta-percha and sealer is not possible with the techniques tested (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC], whereas Ingle's says a combination of methods can give potentially complete elimination (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Activation of irrigants appears promising (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- *Microscope:* MB2 identified and treated in about 90% of maxillary first molars (over 75% without).
- *Ultrasonic tips ("microsonics"):* post and fragment removal, which makes retreatment possible where surgery was once indicated.
- *MTA and bioceramics:* perforation repair with up to 90% success for accessible defects.
- *NiTi retreatment files:* rapid gutta-percha removal, but cutting tips can ledge; most studies find complete removal not possible, though Ingle's calls combined methods potentially complete.
- *Irrigant activation (sonic, ultrasonic, adaptive files):* promising for cleanliness.

(Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022)

**Principle that has not changed.** The commonest error in non-surgical retreatment is removing excessive dentine; remove restorative material atraumatically, where possible sacrificing it rather than tooth structure (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014). Technology widens what can be reached, but clinicians must still weigh risk against benefit (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

## 6. Safety research

### Explanation

In one in vitro study, ultrasonic vibration for post removal without coolant could raise root surface temperature by nearly 10°C in as little as 15 seconds, and rises over 10°C at the periodontal ligament can damage the attachment apparatus; Cohen's recommends water-ported tips or coolant, pauses and low power, but notes that root surfaces can still heat rapidly with coolant (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Apex locators may misread length in retreatment (one study found readings too short, others too long); a radiograph is advised, and electronic measurement is about 10% less reliable in filled teeth (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. The evidence on cracks after retreatment is inconclusive (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

Terauchi and colleagues give a different heating time: a 10°C rise within 1 minute on the external root surface without coolant, with ultrasonic energy to be applied for no more than 15 seconds at a time (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. They also report that retreatment can induce cracks, more so in teeth first filled by warm vertical compaction, and that some studies show more flare-ups after retreatment (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]; Hülsmann and colleagues found no excess flare-up and no firm conclusion on cracks (above).

#### ✎ Exam core

- *Heat:* in one in vitro study, ultrasonic post removal without coolant raised root surface temperature by nearly 10°C in as little as 15 seconds, and heating can still be rapid with coolant; use water-ported tips or coolant, breaks and low power (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). Another source gives 10°C within 1 minute (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021).
- *Working length:* electronic measurement about 10% less reliable in filled teeth; confirm by radiograph.
- *Cracks and flare-ups:* retreatment may induce cracks, more after warm vertical compaction, and some studies report more flare-ups (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021); Hülsmann and colleagues find the crack evidence inconclusive and, on very limited data, no flare-up excess (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

## 7. Disinfection and what remains unresolved

### Explanation

No instrumentation regime predictably removes all previous filling, and whether one visit or calcium hydroxide is needed is debated (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. There is no consensus on an optimal disinfection protocol and the role of chlorhexidine in retreatment is unsettled (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Ingle's states that not all failures are amenable to nonsurgical retreatment (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. On solvents, Cunha and Bueno report no consensus on whether they help gutta-percha removal and advise cautious use when no progress toward patency is made (Cunha and Bueno, 2015) [SRC], a conditional wording beside Hülsmann and colleagues' 'only if absolutely necessary' (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

Terauchi and colleagues report that removing the smear layer with NaOCl and EDTA significantly increased the number of healed retreatment cases and that intracanal activation was superior to passive irrigation in laboratory reviews; NaOCl at 2.5% and 5.25% dissolved *E. faecalis* biofilms better than 1%, while 2% chlorhexidine did not (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. Cunha and Bueno likewise state that, although chlorhexidine is effective against biofilm bacteria, NaOCl is still the only solution able to disrupt biofilms (Cunha and Bueno, 2015) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- No regime predictably removes all filling; residue can shelter bacteria (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- No agreed disinfection protocol; chlorhexidine's role is uncertain; one visit versus calcium hydroxide is debated.
- Not all failures are suited to nonsurgical retreatment: refer, operate or extract (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**What to tell the patient.** Healing may not occur in potentially one-quarter of retreatments; Cohen's lists the next choices as observation, surgery, extraction-replantation or extraction (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). [Likely] Agree the fall-back before starting.

- Removing the smear layer with NaOCl and EDTA significantly increased healed retreatment cases; activated irrigation beats passive irrigation in laboratory reviews (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: CBCT should be used before every retreatment.**
- *Cohen's retreatment chapter:* usefulness "unquestioned", plan changes in up to 62%, 2016 AAE/AAOMR update supports 3D imaging in posttreatment disease, and almost every such case is said to benefit, though most clinicians do not think CBCT necessary for every patient.
- *Cohen's outcomes chapter, Chugal et al, Setzer:* routine CBCT is not recommended for outcome monitoring because of dose, and its sensitivity may lower measured healing rates.
- *Limit of the evidence:* the plan-change figures describe behaviour of clinicians, not patient outcomes; the passages read give no outcome data showing better healing.
- *Conclusion:* the positions are consistent once diagnostic use (symptomatic, unclear cause) is separated from routine follow-up; that CBCT improves outcome is an inference, not demonstrated. [Likely]
