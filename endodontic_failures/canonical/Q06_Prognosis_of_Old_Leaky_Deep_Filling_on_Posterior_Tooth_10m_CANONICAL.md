## Explanation

**Why a leaky deep filling threatens the pulp.** Dentin is permeable; bacterial products released under a leaking restoration diffuse through the tubules to the pulp along a concentration gradient, and restorations without an impervious seal allow bacteria to colonise the cavity (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Permeability increases exponentially with cavity depth; 2 mm of remaining dentin is usually enough to shield the pulp from most forms of irritation, but there is always the threat of microleakage, so vital dentin should be sealed whatever its thickness (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Among dental treatments, restorative procedures are the greatest cause of pulpal injury, while caries remains the commonest cause of pulp disease (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**What decides the prognosis.** The status of the pulp before treatment: Walton and Torabinejad say indirect pulp capping is considered only if there is no history of pulpalgia or signs of irreversible pulpitis, and that the success of pulp-preserving procedures depends primarily on the status of the pulp before treatment (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. If the pulp is healthy the aim is to maintain it, since a healthy pulp is preferable to root canal treatment (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Outcomes of conservative options (figures differ by source).** In Cohen's, the weighted pooled success rate of indirect pulp capping was 81.7% by the one-step approach and 81.9% by stepwise excavation, which the authors call similar; trials disagree on which is better; in one study age, preoperative pain and pulp exposure during excavation were negative prognostic factors. The pooled success of direct pulp capping was 70.1% (95% CI 59.9 to 80.2), with no significant influence of exposure type, size or location, or of restoration type, size or quality (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. These are capping studies in deep caries and exposures, not studies of old leaking restorations [Likely]. Walton and Torabinejad call indirect pulp capping a matter of considerable controversy but say it is likely to succeed if irreversible pulpitis has not already developed, and give an older direct-capping success of about 80%, lower for carious exposures (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. The two books are shown side by side and not reconciled.

**The tooth.** In root-filled teeth, leakage under failing restorations is a major cause of new and recurrent endodontic disease, cuspal coverage protects posterior teeth against extraction, and in one study of failed, extracted root-filled teeth those with crowns were lost after an average of 87 months against 50 without (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017; Baba and Goodacre, 2019) [SRC]. [Likely] The same reasoning is applied here to a tooth that may later need root canal treatment, so a leaking deep filling should be replaced and cuspal coverage considered.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** An old, deep restoration that leaks allows bacteria to colonise the cavity, and their products diffuse through the dentinal tubules to the pulp (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002). The prognosis is that of both the pulp and the tooth.

**Mechanism.** Dentin is permeable; bacterial products diffuse to the pulp; permeability rises exponentially with cavity depth; 2 mm of dentin usually protects the pulp but microleakage is always a threat, so seal vital dentin (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Prognosis depends on**
- *Pulp status:* indirect capping only if no history of pulpalgia or signs of irreversible pulpitis (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); [Likely] otherwise root canal treatment.
- *Remaining dentin and depth* (2 mm).
- *Extent of recurrent caries and restorability* [Likely].
- *Quality of the replacement seal* [Likely].
- *Cuspal coverage* (in root-filled teeth; Baba, White and Bogen, 2017).

**Management and outcome**
1. Remove the restoration and caries [Likely]; seal vital dentin (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
2. Deep caries without irreversible pulpitis: indirect pulp capping, one-step or stepwise, pooled success about 82% (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021); Walton: controversial, but likely to succeed if irreversible pulpitis is absent (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
3. Pulp exposure: direct pulp capping, pooled success 70.1% (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021); older figure in Walton about 80%, lower for carious exposures (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); not reconciled.
4. Irreversible pulpitis or necrosis: root canal treatment [Likely].
5. Restore with cuspal coverage on posterior teeth [Likely]; in root-filled teeth it protects against extraction (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017).

**If the pulp has to be treated.** Root canal treatment of a vital tooth has a pooled probability of periapical health of about 0.83, against 0.72 for a necrotic tooth with a periapical lesion (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019). Root-filled teeth are mainly extracted for caries, periodontal disease, restorative factors and root fracture, not endodontic failure (Baba, White and Bogen, 2017). In a root-filled tooth failing from coronal leakage, re-restoration and/or revision of the root canal treatment is the treatment of choice, and re-restoration alone should be avoided in chronic, long-standing leakage (Sikri, 2017).

**Inform the patient** [Likely] of the guarded outlook, the possibility of later root canal treatment, and the need for a crown or cuspal coverage.

**Prognosis summary** [Likely]. Good if the pulp is healthy, dentin remains and a sealed cuspal-coverage restoration is placed; guarded with symptoms, large caries or poor remaining structure.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Dentin permeability rises exponentially with cavity depth; 2 mm of dentin is usually protective.
- Prognosis depends on pulp status, depth and remaining dentin.
- Indirect pulp capping pooled success about 82%; direct capping about 70% (about 80% in older data).
- [Likely] Replace the leaking restoration; place cuspal coverage on posterior teeth.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Discussing the filling only and ignoring pulp status.
2. Offering one prognosis without stating the pulp and symptoms.
3. Omitting recurrent caries and cuspal coverage.
4. Quoting capping success without the source and its date.
5. Forgetting that leakage can lead to the need for root canal treatment.
