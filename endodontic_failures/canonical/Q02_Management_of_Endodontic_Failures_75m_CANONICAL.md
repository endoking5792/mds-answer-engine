## 1. What "failure" means, and how big the problem is

### Explanation

Cohen's argues that the words "success" and "failure" are a holdover from a time when clinicians felt they needed to congratulate themselves on successes and blame themselves for failures (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Friedman's suggested term, **posttreatment disease**, means persistent or reintroduced endodontic disease (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. The authors of Treatment of Endodontic Infections choose the same term because it covers both persisting and secondary infection, whereas "treatment failure" can be read as the dentist's failure (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

Scale. About 22.3 million root canal procedures were performed in 2006 (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Many epidemiologic studies found technical defects in a high percentage of root canal treatments, and in these studies 40% to 65% of root-filled teeth had radiographic signs of periradicular inflammation, whereas controlled studies showed success of up to 90%, at least for vital cases with noninfected pulps, a large discrepancy between potential and clinical reality (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. A systematic review of 33 cross-sectional studies found apical periodontitis in 36% of 28,881 root-filled teeth (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

Sikri's classification of failure is practical: **established failure** (patient and operator are both convinced by the clinical and radiographic picture) and **potential failure** (one of them is not) (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri also says root canal treatment usually fails when the "triad" (correct diagnosis, adequate cleaning and shaping, three-dimensional obturation) is not followed (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. The same page states that the "percentage of success ... ranges from 10 to 50% globally"; this contradicts every outcome source in this answer (80-95% success) and is most likely a misprint for failure, so it is not used (Sikri, 2017) [Likely].

Gulabivala and Ng diagnose nonsurgical treatment failure by clinical signs or symptoms, an enlarging or new radiolucency, or persistence of a radiolucency in a tooth treated at least four years earlier (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Failure is persistent or newly arisen apical disease in a root-filled tooth. Cohen's prefers *posttreatment disease* (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

**Magnitude.** Apical periodontitis is seen in 36% of root-filled teeth in pooled cross-sectional data (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019); in epidemiological studies reporting technical defects 40-65% of root-filled teeth show periradicular inflammation, against up to 90% success in controlled studies of vital, noninfected cases (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**Types** (Sikri, 2017): *established failure* (patient and operator agree) and *potential failure* (only one is convinced).

**Criteria** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014): signs or symptoms; enlarging or new radiolucency; a persisting radiolucency 4 or more years after treatment.

**Why terminology matters.** "Failure" can be read as the dentist's failure; posttreatment disease covers persisting and secondary infection (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**Why it fails.** Treatment fails when the triad of correct diagnosis, adequate cleaning and shaping, and three-dimensional obturation is not followed; the common denominator is leakage and bacterial contamination (Sikri, 2017).

## 2. Causes of failure

### Explanation

**Cohen's four biological groups.** The clinician can group the causes as (1) persistent or reintroduced intraradicular microorganisms, (2) extraradicular infection, (3) foreign body reaction and (4) true cysts (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Clinical presentations include poor access, untreated canals, canals poorly cleaned and obturated, ledges, perforations, separated instruments and overextension (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Intraradicular infection.** Cohen's says it has been asserted that persistent or reintroduced microorganisms are the major cause of posttreatment disease. It is not the complication itself (for example a ledge or separated file) that causes disease; it is the inability to remove or entomb the bacteria. Infected root-filled teeth carry few species, predominantly gram positive, and a very commonly isolated species is *Enterococcus faecalis* (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Extraradicular infection.** *Actinomyces israelii* and *Propionibacterium propionicum* can exist in the periapical tissues and may prevent healing (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. TEI adds that most extraradicular infections are sustained by a concomitant intraradicular infection (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC].

**The microbiologic view.** TEI states: "Endodontic treatment failure is primarily a microbiologic problem." The causes are the same in poorly and well-treated teeth. Recent indirect evidence seems to point to persistent infection, rather than secondary infection from coronal leakage, as the commonest cause, and TEI bases this on three facts: failed teeth usually show bacteria in the apical third of the canal; bacteria found at the time of filling predict a poor outcome and can survive a filling for years; and failure rates differ between vital teeth, necrotic teeth and retreatment cases, which leakage alone would not predict. A study in which well-prepared and sealed canals resisted coronal bacterial leakage adds further doubt about secondary infection (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. Hülsmann and colleagues, however, note that the quality of the coronal restoration is one of the most important and reliable predictors of success or failure and that it seems impossible to distinguish persistent from secondary infection exactly in the clinic, and both may be present together (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng say that residual infection in the apical anatomy is the norm after completion of root-canal treatment, so that its continuing interaction with the filling and the host defences decides the outcome (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Sikri puts the common denominator as leakage and bacterial contamination (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. These positions are shown side by side, not ranked.

**Why bacteria survive.** In small or round canals, studies report that instrumentation systems leave about 10% to 50% of the canal surface unprepared, more when only the apical surface is assessed, and 10% to 80% in oval canals; bacteria can lie in dentinal tubules, and *E. faecalis* and *Candida albicans* show some resistance to calcium hydroxide (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. Hülsmann and colleagues note that parts of the canal wall, in some studies estimated at 30% or more, are not touched by any instrument (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

**Procedural errors act through infection.** Ledges, perforations or instrument fractures do not by themselves cause inflammation; they are associated with remaining biofilm and faulty disinfection (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Whether a missed canal counts as a treatment failure is partly semantic, since the canal was never treated, but it is clearly a failure of the clinician to locate and treat all canals (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].

**Other biological and diagnostic causes.** Sikri's list adds persistent extraradicular infection, root fractures, incorrect diagnosis and neuropathic problems (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Vertical root fracture gives a narrow periodontal defect, the J-shaped lesion, and, other than exploratory surgery, periodontal probing is often the only way to separate it from a developing periapical lesion (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Goldberg lists cracks not visible on the radiograph, extensive resorption and severe endodontic-periodontic disease as further causes, and notes that microbial and non-microbial reasons both exist (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC].

**Non-microbial causes and the cyst dispute.** TEI states that most, if not all, failures are associated with intra- or extraradicular infection, calls cholesterol crystals "rather speculative", and says the belief that true cysts cannot heal nonsurgically was based on a prevalence study of untreated teeth and has never been supported by solid evidence. TEI cites one study by Ricucci and colleagues which, apart from the relation of the cyst cavity to the root canal, found no significant clinical, radiographic, histopathologic or histobacteriologic differences between true and bay cysts, and considers it reasonable to assume that cysts can heal provided the root canal infection is properly controlled (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. Cohen's, in contrast, says true cysts, "due to their self-sustaining nature", probably do not heal following nonsurgical therapy (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's also notes that gutta-percha and sealers are usually well tolerated and cysts form 15% to 42% of periapical lesions (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues side with Cohen's: in a lesion series they cite, about 60% of the cysts were true cysts, which are more likely to heal only after extraction or surgery, whereas pocket cysts are expected to heal nonsurgically (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Weine, writing earlier, says there is considerable evidence that the apical periodontal cyst (a long-standing granuloma that has converted) will heal after nonsurgical treatment, although surgery sometimes becomes necessary (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng sit between the two camps: successful treatment of a true cyst may require surgery if conventional root-canal treatment fails to resolve it, a supposition they say remains to be proven (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. The books disagree; all positions are reported.

**Failure of restorative origin.** Goldberg lists coronal leakage over time as a reason for failure, although he calls the role of coronal filtration debatable, adds that uncontrolled diabetes or severe immunocompromise makes the prognosis doubtful, and that tooth extraction after endodontic treatment is more likely for periodontal or prosthetic reasons (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. Pooled data from 11 studies give an average post-and-core clinical failure rate of 9% (range 6% to 14%) over an average study length of 8 years, with loss of retention and tooth fracture the two commonest causes (Baba and Goodacre, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri notes that both organisms and endotoxins can penetrate an obturated canal (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**After surgery.** Persistent intraradicular infection is also the main cause of postsurgical apical periodontitis, and a poorly sealing root-end filling can allow leakage (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC].

**Other textbooks on cause.** Grossman's lists inadequate cleaning of the root canal as the most common operator-related cause of failure (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Pontius, citing Ruddle (2002), state that, whatever the initial cause, leakage is the major etiologic factor and that 25% to 35% of teeth treated in general practice have persisting apical periodontitis (Pontius, 2010) [SRC]. Terauchi and colleagues note that outcome may depend more on the marginal adaptation of the restoration than on the quality of the root filling (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. These emphases differ (cleaning, leakage, marginal seal) and are shown side by side.

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Biological causes (Cohen's four groups; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021)**
1. Persistent or reintroduced intraradicular microorganisms: asserted to be the major cause. Flora is few-species, mainly Gram-positive; *E. faecalis* is common.
2. Extraradicular infection (*Actinomyces israelii*, *Propionibacterium propionicum*).
3. Foreign body reaction (paper-point cellulose, extruded filling).
4. True cysts.

**B. Clinical presentations** (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Sikri, 2017)
- Missed or untreated canals; poor access; poorly cleaned, shaped or obturated canals; blocks.
- Ledges, transportation, perforations, separated instruments; overextension.
- Coronal leakage; defective or lost restoration; root fracture, incorrect diagnosis, neuropathic pain.

**C. The microbiological position** (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022)
- Failure is primarily microbiologic; causes are the same in well- and poorly-treated teeth.
- Indirect evidence points to persistent infection as commoner: apical-third bacteria, bacteria at filling predict a poor outcome, differing failure rates in vital, necrotic and retreatment cases; sealed canals resisted leakage in one study.
- Procedural errors act by retaining biofilm (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**D. Why bacteria survive** (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022)
- In small or round canals 10-50% of the canal surface is unprepared (10-80% in oval canals).
- Biofilm; tubules, isthmuses, ramifications.
- *E. faecalis* and *Candida* resist calcium hydroxide.

**E. Restorative and systemic** (Goldberg, 2019; Baba and Goodacre, 2019)
- Coronal leakage; post-and-core failure 9% (loss of retention, tooth fracture).
- Uncontrolled diabetes or severe immunocompromise make the prognosis doubtful.

**F. Disputed**: cholesterol crystals and true cysts. Cohen's and Setzer and colleagues say true cysts probably need surgery; TEI considers it reasonable that cysts heal once the infection is controlled; Weine cites considerable evidence that apical cysts heal nonsurgically, though surgery is sometimes necessary (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022; Weine, 2004).

**G. After surgery:** residual bacteria, poor root-end seal, root crack.

## 3. Diagnosis of failure

### Explanation

Persistent pain of endodontic origin must be distinguished from chronic non-odontogenic, neuropathic or neuromuscular pain (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]; non-endodontic mimics include occlusal trauma, concurrent periodontal disease and nondental pain (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri recommends inspection under dry conditions with illumination and magnification, and tracing a sinus tract with a gutta-percha point (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

Radiographic reading is unreliable: a periapical lesion was not a sufficient reason to retreat, and a retreatment decision was made in only 39% of cases, even when painful symptoms were present, and about 20% of dentists disagreed with their own earlier opinion on re-reading films (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. CBCT detects about 20% more apical lesions than periapical radiographs (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Cohen's calls its usefulness in retreatment "unquestioned", reports plan changes in up to 62% of cases and that endodontist evaluators missed a canal system in about 4 of 10 teeth on digital radiographs, and notes the 2016 AAE/AAOMR update supports 3D imaging in posttreatment disease (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's: CBCT is indicated when symptoms persist and radiographs are nondiagnostic (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng use CBCT to confirm periapical status in a tooth with persistent pain and no obvious lesion (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **History and examination:** sinus tract (trace with a gutta-percha point), percussion, bite and palpation, probing for narrow defects (vertical fracture) and periodontal status, occlusion (Sikri, 2017; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- **Exclude mimics:** occlusal trauma, periodontal disease, vertical fracture, neuropathic and neuromuscular or nondental pain (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- **Radiographs:** several angulations (Sikri, 2017); subjective, with poor inter- and intra-observer agreement (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).
- **CBCT:** indicated when symptoms persist and films are nondiagnostic (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019); finds about 20% more apical lesions (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022); changes plans in up to 62%, small field of view most useful (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

**Limitations of films.** A retreatment decision was made in only 39% of cases, even with painful symptoms, and about 20% of dentists disagreed with their own earlier reading (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019). Most clinicians think CBCT is not necessary for every patient, but Cohen's holds that almost every posttreatment case benefits from 3D imaging (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

**Also:** examine treated and untreated teeth; thermal tests on root-filled multirooted teeth can reveal untreated pulp tissue (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).

## 4. Decision making: whether and how to treat

### Explanation

**Ingle's three Ds.** First decide whether the tooth is a failure. With technically deficient treatment and no lesion, a wait-and-see approach led to complications in only 2.8% of cases over six years, but if a new restoration is planned retreatment is prudent, and even then retreatment of such teeth failed in 6%. Where an asymptomatic lesion exists most periradicular lesions heal within four years, though some took ten years or more (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Second, decide to treat by weighing risk: Ingle's notes that information on flare-up risk when retreating quiescent lesions is scarce; according to Eriksen, the estimated incidence of spontaneous exacerbation of quiescent lesions appears to be below 5% per year, whereas asymptomatic patients with quiescent lesions have a flare-up incidence of 20% to 40% when retreatment is initiated (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Third, decide the treatment: extraction, orthograde retreatment or surgery (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Kvist's *Praxis* model treats periapical status as a continuum from health to disease; Hülsmann and colleagues likewise see health and disease as a continuum with individually defined cut-off points (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Reit's first principle is that any persistent lesion after treatment must be retreated (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

**Cohen's four options** are do nothing, extract, nonsurgical or surgical retreatment. Doing nothing is the patient's decision after full information, and if the cause stays unknown any decision is empirical "trial and error", so a second opinion is advised. Information on options and likely outcomes must be given to the patient (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng add that clinician factors (judgement of cause, ability to improve the treatment, prognosis in their own hands or a specialist's, cost) and patient factors (understanding the problem, chances and costs) shape the decision, ideally recorded in a mutually signed agreement (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

**Waiting.** In 51% of asymptomatic cases the lesion increased in size, in 28.6% it stayed unchanged and in some 20% it decreased; the book advises that wait-and-see be adopted with caution while a link between apical infection and systemic health cannot be definitely ruled out. An interval of up to 4 years has been recommended before a final decision on a primary treatment, but a shorter period has been suggested more recently: retreatment should be considered if the lesion has not healed or shrunk after 1 year, with one further year of observation if it has shrunk, limiting the timeframe to 2 years (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. A tooth with persistent apical periodontitis may remain in asymptomatic function ("functional retention") (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Which treatment?** Ingle's: orthograde retreatment is the choice for radiographically poorly sealed canals and needs sufficient cervical tooth structure for a new restoration (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Hülsmann and colleagues: surgery (apicoectomy) is recommended for severely altered anatomy, a restoration that will not be removed, or inaccessible contaminated spaces; extraction for vertical root fractures, insufficient periodontal support, unrestorable teeth; and case selection is the basis of retreatment success (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Only one root should not normally be retreated in a multirooted tooth (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. The AAE places every retreatment at least in the moderate difficulty group, and most cases need a microscope and fine ultrasonic tips (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Cohen's: the line between restorable and nonrestorable "is a movable one"; previous failed apical surgery should be retreated nonsurgically first, since many surgical failures are due to poorly cleaned and filled canals, unless root fracture is suspected (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Nonsurgical versus surgical, with numbers.** Cohen's: older literature reported nonsurgical retreatment healing of 74% to 98%, against only 59% complete healing with older methods of apical surgery alone, rising to 80% when surgery was preceded by orthograde retreatment; meta-analyses of microsurgery give 89% to 94%, so surgery is "no longer the second choice" (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's: healing is faster after surgery at one year but balanced at four years (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Prognosis chapter: surgery heals well initially but tends to fail over time and microsurgery outperforms traditional surgery; nonsurgical retreatment is the first-line treatment (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC].

**Other textbooks on options.** Grossman's gives four options: observation, nonsurgical retreatment, surgery and extraction; observation is usually chosen when the patient is not convinced of the need to treat, surgery is recommended where complex anatomy prevents reaching the apical third, and extraction should be the last option (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Pontius: nonsurgical retreatment remains the treatment of choice; extraradicular infection, foreign-body reactions, infected cysts and scarred healing of the periapical tissues (Nair, 2006) call for a microsurgical approach; success depends on the clinician's training; apical periodontitis may take four years or more to heal, but radiographic signs of healing are seen in 89% of cases at one year (Ørstavik, 1996, as quoted); deep probing depths, mobility and furcation involvement worsen the long-term prognosis (Pontius, 2010) [SRC]. Terauchi and colleagues illustrate the AAE difficulty form with one retreated premolar with an apical perforation, which they categorize as highly difficult; this fits Hülsmann and colleagues, who place every retreatment at least in the moderate group and most retreatments with broken instruments, perforations, undetected canals or ledges probably in the severe group (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Terauchi and colleagues add that obstructions are typically complex and that referral to an endodontist should be considered and offered. Terauchi and colleagues advocate a shared decision-making process (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Step 1: is the tooth a failure?** (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)
- No lesion, good seal: monitor (complications 2.8% over six years); retreat if a new restoration is planned.
- Asymptomatic lesion with adequate treatment: expect healing, most within four years; contamination during treatment: retreat.

**Step 2: should it be treated?** Ingle's: information is scarce, but quiescent lesions are estimated to exacerbate spontaneously in under 5% a year, whereas asymptomatic ones flare in 20-40% when retreatment starts; retreatment itself risks fracture, perforation and thinning. Reit: retreat a persistent lesion unless the patient declines or risk or cost exceeds the benefit.

**Step 3: options** (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021)
1. Do nothing (patient's choice, documented).
2. Nonsurgical retreatment.
3. Surgical retreatment (apicoectomy, root resection, hemisection, intentional replantation).
4. Extraction and replacement.

| Option | Choose when |
|---|---|
| Nonsurgical retreatment | Poorly sealed canals; accessible intraradicular infection; enough tooth structure |
| Surgery | Altered anatomy, restoration not to be removed, inaccessible contaminated space, apical fragment, extraradicular cause |
| Extraction | Vertical fracture, insufficient periodontal support, unrestorable |

(Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)

**Outcomes** (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021): older literature: nonsurgical retreatment 74-98%, older surgery alone 59% complete healing (80% after retreatment); microsurgery 89-94%, so surgery is no longer the second choice. retreatment stays first-line (White and Torabinejad, 2017).

**Evidence on waiting.** In asymptomatic teeth, 51% of lesions increased in size, 28.6% were unchanged and about 20% decreased; wait-and-see needs caution while a link with systemic health cannot be excluded. Up to 4 years was recommended before a final decision on a primary treatment, 1-2 years more recently (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**Case selection.** Severely altered anatomy, costly restorations and massive posts argue against orthograde retreatment. The AAE places every retreatment at least in the moderate-difficulty group, and cases with fragments, perforations, missed canals or ledges are probably severe: consider referral. Retreating one root of a multirooted tooth is not normally indicated (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**Consent and decision factors** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014): explain causes, options, costs and prognosis; weigh cause, ability to improve the treatment, prognosis in your hands or a specialist's, cost and the patient's understanding; record the plan.

## 5. Nonsurgical retreatment: indications, principles and preparation

### Explanation

Sikri lists indications (poorly sealed canals where instrumentation and obturation can be improved) and contraindications (thin canal walls prone to perforation, failure to remove a post or broken instrument, root fracture). The objectives are the same as for primary treatment (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng give three indications: confirmed failure with accessible intraradicular infection; a new complex restoration over a poor root filling; and suspected contamination from leakage or loss of restoration (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

The principles are identical to de novo treatment, except that the resident infection is likely to have been altered by selection to a less diverse, more resistant Gram-positive flora. The most common error is removing excessive dentine, so restorative material should be sacrificed rather than tooth structure (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Ingle's frames this as "analyze, plan, and execute" with local objectives of disassembly, complete access, excluding fractures, identifying all canals, removing filling and posts or fragments, and repairing perforations; the objectives equal initial treatment but patency is often lost (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. A temporary build-up (pretreatment) is mandatory (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

**Coronal access.** Whether to remove the restoration completely is debated (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng: a sound restoration is better left and the chamber accessed through it, while a cast restoration is safest cut off (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Sikri lists advantages of coronal disassembly (access to the true axis, detection of caries, cracks and canals) and of keeping the crown (easy isolation, retained esthetics), with poorer visibility as the price; resin and glass-ionomer cements make removal harder than zinc phosphate or zinc oxide eugenol (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Retain or remove the restoration: four positions.** Terauchi and colleagues: in most cases remove all existing restorations before starting, because of possible leakage and recurrent caries, although access through a recently placed suitable restoration may be recommended to avoid extra cost (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's: the best solution is to remove the crown, but if marginal integrity is satisfactory it is advisable to retain it and access through it (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Pontius: existing restorations often must be removed for access, though retreatment can often be carried out without removing an adequate one, at the cost of possible blockage of canals by metal chips (Pontius, 2010) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng: leave a sound restoration (above). Grossman's also cuts metal crowns with carbide burs (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. All the books remove leaking or carious restorations; they differ on how readily a sound or recently placed one is kept. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications** (Sikri, 2017; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014)
- Poorly sealed canals that can be improved.
- Accessible residual intraradicular infection.
- A new complex restoration planned over a technically poor filling in a tooth with no signs or symptoms.
- Suspected recontamination (leakage, lost restoration).

**Contraindications** (Sikri, 2017): thin walls prone to perforation; unremovable post or fragment; sclerosed canal that cannot be negotiated; root fracture, especially vertical; uncooperative patient. Extraction instead for an unrestorable tooth (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**Principles** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)
1. Analyse, plan, execute; find the cause of failure first.
2. Sacrifice restorative material rather than tooth structure.
3. Temporary build-up; rubber dam.
4. Remove the old filling without extra dentine loss; establish patency; find missed canals.
5. Disinfect, obturate and restore as for a new case.

**Access** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014): cut through a sound crown; remove leaking or carious restorations; leave a recently placed sound one that can be excluded as a cause; cut cast crowns off. Porcelain and resin or glass-ionomer cements make removal harder (Sikri, 2017).

**Retain or remove the restoration?** Keeping a sound crown eases isolation, preserves esthetics and is cost-effective but limits visibility; disassembly gives access to the true axis (Sikri, 2017).

## 6. Removal of posts and cores

### Explanation

Metal posts are first attacked with ultrasonic vibration (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Tapering posts are easier to remove than parallel ones, cast cores harder than composite or amalgam, and screw posts can be unscrewed with an ultrasonic tip moved anticlockwise; fibre posts wear out burs quickly (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Posts bonded with resin or glass ionomer are harder than those in zinc phosphate, and removal becomes harder from anterior to posterior teeth (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. There is a risk of deviation from the root axis, heat transfer and lateral perforation (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

Heat. In vitro, ultrasonic vibration for post removal without coolant raised root surface temperature by nearly 10°C within as little as 15 seconds, and rises over 10°C at the periodontal ligament can damage the attachment apparatus. Cohen's advises water-ported tips or a continuous coolant air/water spray, frequent breaks and avoiding the high-power setting, and warns that root surfaces can heat up rapidly even with water coolant (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Heat: the books quote different times.** Cohen's cites an in vitro study with a rise approaching 10°C in as little as 15 seconds (above). Terauchi and colleagues report a 10°C rise within 1 minute on the external root surface without coolant and advise applying ultrasonic energy for intervals no longer than 15 seconds (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. The figures are not reconciled; the practical advice (coolant, short intervals) is the same. Pontius: about 10 minutes of ultrasonic vibration, cooled periodically with water, removes metal posts, whereas zirconium posts cannot be removed by ultrasonics or the Gonon and Ruddle systems (Pontius, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Assess:** post type (parallel or tapered, active or passive, metallic or nonmetallic), cement, length, supra- or subcrestal position (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019); remaining dentine thickness (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- **Metal posts:** remove the core, expose the post, apply ultrasonics around it with coolant; use a post puller only if that fails (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).
- **Screw posts:** ultrasonic tip anticlockwise.
- **Fibre posts:** drill out with a new drill along the axis; cement softener; hollow and remove (Sikri, 2017).
- **Cast cores:** remove in one piece if possible, otherwise separate the core first.
- **Risks:** root fracture, perforation, heat damage (about 10°C in 15 s without coolant in Cohen's, within 1 minute in Terauchi and colleagues); use water-ported tips or spray, frequent breaks, avoid high power, though coolant does not remove the risk (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- **Alternative:** surgery may sometimes be wise, but not merely for lack of post-removal training (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Choice of technique** (Sikri, 2017; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022)
- Tapering and passive posts and zinc phosphate cement are easier than parallel, active and resin or glass-ionomer.
- Beneath a removed post a hard layer of cement or composite often blocks further penetration: use fine ultrasonic tips or long-shank burs.

## 7. Removing the old filling and regaining apical access

### Explanation

Bacteria can lie on the gutta-percha, between gutta-percha and sealer, in tubules and in uninstrumented areas, so removal of gutta-percha and sealer and exposure of the dentine is the aim (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Gutta-percha should be removed progressively with a combination of methods (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. A single cone can often be removed quickly with a Hedström file; NiTi retreatment files should be used with the utmost caution because most have a cutting tip that may easily create ledges and push softened gutta-percha apically (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Gutta-percha left in place for many years tends to vulcanise and can be brittle and hard (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Solvents are used only if necessary because dissolved gutta-percha smears and blocks tubules and chloroform is prohibited in some countries (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]; Sikri notes it is potentially carcinogenic and mutagenic, but the small quantity used in retreatment (normally under 1 ml) is unlikely to be hazardous (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Most studies show that complete removal is not possible with rotary or reciprocating NiTi instrumentation, and Cohen's adds that no single technique, instrument or solvent removes all the old filling; activating the irrigant with sonic or ultrasonic devices or adaptive files appears promising (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Carrier-based fillings such as Thermafil can be removed with hand, rotary or reciprocating NiTi instruments, wire-loop or tube techniques (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

Other materials. Silver points are relatively easy to remove; Endosolv E and R soften soluble cements but there is no solvent for N2 and Russian Red cements, and attempts to remove them with a small long-shank round bur or ultrasonic tip may create a false canal and perforation (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

Working length. Electronic measurement in filled teeth is about 10% less reliable; a radiograph is advised to confirm, or CBCT-derived length can be used (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

**Chloroform: the books disagree.** Cohen's says that, used carefully, chloroform is regarded as a safe and effective solvent, that the evidence for its carcinogenicity in humans is suspect, and that its continued use as a gutta-percha solvent is recommended, while also noting that all solvents have some toxicity and should be avoided if possible (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's calls chloroform still the reagent of choice (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng list chloroform among the agents useful for removing gutta-percha and flood the canal with it in the final wicking stage (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Pontius, citing McDonald and Vire (1992), state that in limited doses and with well-controlled application chloroform was not associated with any harmful effects (Pontius, 2010) [SRC]. Terauchi and colleagues note that chloroform has been reported to reduce intracanal *E. faecalis* levels, though used in the early stages of removal it leaves more filling in the canal and may promote extrusion, and that in one investigation the amounts of chloroform, halothane and xylene expressed were below levels of health concern (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. Cunha and Bueno take a conditional position: in one study chloroform used during gutta-percha removal gave negative *E. faecalis* cultures in 11 of 17 samples against 0 of 17 with saline, and they conclude that solvents should be avoided where the filling is easily removed but used cautiously when patency cannot otherwise be reached (Cunha and Bueno, 2015) [SRC]. Against this, Hülsmann and colleagues say its use is prohibited in some countries and no longer recommended (above), and Grossman's says it is no longer recommended because of reported toxicity and potential carcinogenicity; Grossman's also advises avoiding solvents in the middle and apical thirds (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's position is intermediate (above). The positions are shown side by side, not ranked.

**Other materials.** Hard pastes are removed most predictably by mechanical instrumentation (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]; there is no known effective solvent for Russian paste; any paste must be removed with great care to avoid apical extrusion; corrosion products of silver points can be highly cytotoxic and the key to removing them is breaking the seal around the point; gutta-percha is removed crown-down (Pontius, 2010; Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Compacted gutta-percha:** Gates-Glidden drills coronally, NiTi files apically, heat, hand files; combine methods; remove progressively (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- **Single cone and cautions:** a single cone can often be removed quickly with a Hedström file (corkscrew); retreatment files may ledge and push material apically; solvents only when needed (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- **Carrier-based, paste and silver points:** remove with hand or NiTi files, wire loop or tube techniques; solvents for soluble cements; no solvent for N2/Russian Red (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Sikri, 2017).
- **Residue:** most studies show complete removal is impossible with NiTi, and no single method removes all filling; activate irrigants (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- **Working length:** electronic measurement is about 10% less reliable; confirm with a radiograph.

**Stepwise approach** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014): ultrasonic scaler tip or NiTi rotary instrument for the coronal bulk; Hedström files, the largest that does not bind, for the rest; finally paper-point wicking after chloroform flooding, once the bulk has gone.

- **Chloroform (books differ):** Cohen's recommends continued careful use, Ingle's calls it still the reagent of choice, Gulabivala and Ng list it as useful; Pontius (citing McDonald and Vire) report no harm in limited controlled use, Terauchi and colleagues exposure below health-concern levels in one study; Sikri: potentially carcinogenic, small quantity unlikely hazardous; Hülsmann and colleagues and Grossman's: no longer recommended (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Pontius, 2010; Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021; Sikri, 2017; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 8. Missed and sclerosed canals

### Explanation

Missed canals hold tissue and, at times, microorganisms and related irritants, and surgery that "corks" the end of the canal is less predictable than nonsurgical retreatment; the second mesiobuccal system can be identified and treated in about 90% of cases with a microscope (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. An extra canal, mostly MB2, was present in 81% of root-filled maxillary molars, 39% to 80% of untreated canals have an apical lesion, and a search is mandatory in every retreatment (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. "One cannot find a canal unless one suspects that it is there" (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri adds that extra canals should be sought before retreating obturated canals, with dyes such as methylene blue and sodium hypochlorite bubbling (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. For sclerosed canals, locate the orifice first (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC].

Calcifications are managed with chelating agents, stiff hand files and, in the straight part, ultrasonic tips or Mueller burs, under the microscope (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Why important:** missed canals hold tissue and, at times, bacteria; MB2 in 81% of root-filled maxillary molars (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- **Clues:** lesion at one root tip only; eccentric filling (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022); persistent pain (Sikri, 2017).
- **Search:** radiographs at different angles, CBCT, magnification, widened access, explorer, dyes (methylene blue), sodium hypochlorite bubbling, ultrasonic tips (Sikri, 2017).
- **Sclerosed canals:** find the orifice first, then small files with EDTA lubricant (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

**Evidence.** 39-80% of untreated canals have a lesion; maxillary molar retreatment found 10% more MB2 canals than primary treatment (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022). The second mesiobuccal system can be identified and treated in over 75% of cases without and about 90% with a microscope (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

## 9. Separated instruments, blocks, ledges and transportation

### Explanation

A fractured instrument does not itself cause failure; it matters when it blocks access to apical infection (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Not all need removal; removal is easier in straight canals. Gulabivala and Ng classify: class 1 lies in the canal but protrudes into the chamber, class 2 in the straight part, class 3 beyond a curve; as canals are not round, a gap usually allows bypass (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Ultrasonic energy is applied to the side, not the coronal end, of a fragment, the Gary-Carr technique makes bypass space, and a drop of acid etch is left in the canal at the fragment for a couple of minutes before a small file is used to bypass it (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Today fragments can usually be removed because of improved vision, ultrasonics and microtubes (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Details are in the Procedural Errors answers.

Blocks and ledges. A ledge is usually preceded by blockage with dentine debris, which is picked out with a small file and dissolved with alternating EDTA and sodium hypochlorite, although tightly packed debris may lead to a false canal and perforation; the ledge itself is explored with a size 08 or 10 file with a sharp precurve at the tip (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Dentinal mud is frequently infected and a ledge makes the instrument invariably land at the ledge (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Noncutting tips are recommended to avoid creating new ledges (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Moderate transportation needs a barrier giving hemostasis and a platform for condensation; severe transportation needs surgery (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Terauchi and colleagues' framework.** There are three approaches to a fractured instrument: remove it (nonsurgically or surgically), bypass it, or prepare and obturate with it left in place. The sealing ability of the filling is not compromised by a fractured file or other metallic debris, and bypassing may be less successful than ultrasonic removal and risks ledges, perforations and transportation (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's names cyclic fatigue or repeated use as the primary cause of separation (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Separated instruments** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Sikri, 2017; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)
- Leave it if removal needs excessive dentine removal; a fragment alone does not cause failure.
- Class 1: forceps. Class 2: bypass or ultrasonic trough, then extractor/microtube. Class 3: bypass with a precurved file and obturate around it.
- Expose the fragment (staging platform, Machtou and Ruddle, 2019), ultrasonic energy to its side not the coronal end; microtube devices; Gary-Carr or acid-etch bypass.

**Blocks and ledges:** alternate EDTA and sodium hypochlorite (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014); precurved 08-10 file; noncutting tips (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022); blend the ledge by anticurvature filing (Sikri, 2017).

**Transportation** (Sikri, 2017): minimal → blend; moderate → barrier (MTA) platform; severe → surgery.

**Leaving a fragment.** One left after preparation is incorporated in the filling (Sikri, 2017); one beyond a curve usually cannot be removed without excessive dentine loss, so bypass is attempted (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014); for an apical fragment Sikri says surgery is the only treatment of choice, yet bypassing and obturating around it is preferred (Sikri, 2017).

**Take care:** do not push bypassed material into the periapex; with ultrasonics on a silver point (the tip cuts into soft silver) or NiTi file (it shatters small pieces) use excellent vision and magnification (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

## 10. Perforations

### Explanation

Perforations may be due to internal or external resorption or operator error. Success of repair depends on site, size and contamination; MTA is the contemporary material for enclosed defects, and surgical repair is indicated when access is impossible (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Sikri classifies by size (micro below 0.5 mm); seal early; avoid ferric sulfate; MTA is preferred (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Ingle's: the two challenges are hemostasis and controlled placement of the restorative material, and calcium hydroxide, a time-honoured material, can be passively syringed into the canal and left for several minutes, then flushed out with sodium hypochlorite, to control bleeding (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. An electronic apex locator signal at mid-root or coronal level can indicate a perforation or root fracture (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Perforation repair, preferably with hydraulic calcium-silicate cements when the defect is accessible, has been reported to succeed in up to 90%; perforations communicating with the mouth, old ones, or ones with a lesion do worse (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. A preexisting perforation can lower retreatment success by up to 32% (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Prognosis depends on** location, size and time elapsed (Sikri, 2017), and on the risk or degree of contamination (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).
- **Steps** (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Sikri, 2017): locate (apex locator, radiographs); control bleeding (calcium hydroxide, collagen, calcium sulfate; avoid ferric sulfate); internal matrix; repair with MTA or another hydraulic calcium-silicate cement; seal coronally [Likely].
- **Success** reported up to 90% for repair, preferably with hydraulic calcium-silicate cements; poorer when old, communicating with the mouth or with a lesion (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- **Surgical repair** if no canal access (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

**Early repair.** Repair as soon as possible to stop contamination; defects enclosed by bone may have a favourable outcome if infection is controlled (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014). MTA is biocompatible but its long setting time makes it unsuitable where saliva contact is likely (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).

## 11. Disinfection, re-preparation, obturation and the risks of retreatment

### Explanation

The primary goal is repetition of the primary treatment with correction of shortcomings and improved disinfection, not exchange of filling material. Re-preparation aims at uninstrumented, undisinfected spaces, while preparation continues as long as filling is visible in the flutes and overpreparation must be avoided; thorough disinfection cannot be overstressed (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Cohen's recommends a crown-down approach. No instrumentation regime can predictably remove all of the previous filling, and whether one visit or calcium hydroxide is needed is still debated (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. TEI: there is no consensus on an optimal evidence-based protocol; EDTA 17%, NaOCl 2.5-5% and CHX 2% are preferred; the role of CHX in retreatment cannot be finally decided, although 1% NaOCl and 2% CHX were equally efficient in bacterial reduction in one trial (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

Risks. In one retrospective analysis of 915 root canal treatments the overall flare-up prevalence was 2.3% (none after treatment of vital teeth, 5.3% in nonvital teeth and 4.4% in the retreatment group); on these very limited data, retreatment did not seem to be per se associated with an increased risk of flare-ups. The evidence on dentinal cracks is inconclusive (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Ingle's reports a 20-40% flare-up incidence in asymptomatic patients with quiescent lesions when retreatment is initiated (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]; the sources differ in design and population and are shown side by side.

**Irrigation and other findings from Terauchi and colleagues** (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]: NaOCl at 2.5% and 5.25% was significantly more effective on old and young *E. faecalis* biofilms than 1%, and 2% chlorhexidine could not effectively dissolve biofilms compared with 5.25% NaOCl (Cunha and Bueno agree that NaOCl is still the only irrigation solution able to disrupt biofilms (Cunha and Bueno, 2015); Hülsmann and colleagues report a different outcome, not a contradiction: 1% NaOCl and 2% chlorhexidine were equally efficient in bacterial reduction and gave a similar clinical and radiographic outcome in a trial, so the two statements measure different things: above); smear layer removal can significantly increase the number of healed retreatment cases and intracanal activation was superior to passive irrigation in laboratory reviews; crown-down is the instrumentation method of choice; contracted access cavities in oval canals leave more filling behind; apical patency raises the success of retreatment; electronic locators should be combined with radiographs. Retreatment may induce cracks during filling removal, with more cracks after warm vertical compaction than after cold lateral condensation, whereas Hülsmann and colleagues call the evidence inconclusive (above). Some studies report more flare-ups after retreatment, whereas Hülsmann and colleagues, from one small retrospective series, found no apparent excess (above). The first review is usually at 6 months, then yearly, and sooner if there are extenuating circumstances.

Grossman's recommends multiple-sitting therapy for infected retreatment cases because secondary infections are more resistant to therapy, and names calcium hydroxide as the most popular medicament (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's calls the choice between one visit and a medicament a matter of debate (above), and Cunha and Bueno say multiple visits and intracanal medicaments should be considered in difficult cases (Cunha and Bueno, 2015) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Aim:** repeat the primary treatment, correct earlier shortcomings, improve disinfection (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- **Preparation:** crown-down, enlarging the apical portion to remove apical debris (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021); stop when only dentine chips remain on the instrument (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- **Irrigation:** no consensus on an optimal protocol, but EDTA 17%, NaOCl 2.5-5% and CHX 2% are mostly preferred; Hülsmann and Schäfer end with activated NaOCl, ethanol, then CHX 2%, Zehnder and Paqué with activated NaOCl; role of CHX uncertain (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- **Medicament:** one visit or calcium hydroxide is still debated (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- **Obturation and restoration:** bacteria-tight re-obturation and restoration to prevent reinfection (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- **Risks:** flare-up (2.3% across all treatments, 4.4% in retreatment, in one small retrospective series, Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; 20-40% in asymptomatic quiescent lesions when retreatment starts, Machtou and Ruddle, 2019), perforation, ledging, root weakening, extrusion.

**Practical rules** (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022)
- In many canals, enlarging by two to four sizes still does not remove the filling completely, while overpreparation weakens the root.
- Mistakes of primary treatment (straightening, ledging, blockage) often cannot be corrected, so disinfection carries the treatment.

## 12. Surgical retreatment, replantation and extraction

### Explanation

Endodontic surgery gives direct access to the periradicular tissues through incision of the overlying mucogingival complex; its indications have been reduced substantially because most objectives can be achieved by nonsurgical root-canal treatment (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Its aim is to eliminate bacteria from the apical root and prevent access to the periradicular tissues (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Indications include calcified or non-negotiable canals and apical perforations that cannot be managed nonsurgically (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Technique points: curette first and resect later; resect perpendicular to the long axis; the apical 3 mm is often removed routinely though Gulabivala and Ng think this "may prove grossly excessive" for roots without complex apical anatomy; the root-end cavity runs along the root axis with ultrasonic tips; a biopsy is standard of care (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Intentional replantation is for a strategic tooth with no other option, with ankylosis and resorption the main failure modes (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC].

Extraction and replacement. Cohen's (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021): it should be the treatment of last resort, selected only when the tooth is shown to be nonrepairable, and postimplant disease does occur. White and Torabinejad: root canal treatment and implants are complementary; common reasons for extraction of root-treated teeth are decay, periodontal disease, nonrestorability, prosthetic failure and fracture; retaining tooth structure matters more than any restorative choice; the implant single crown is the replacement of choice; costs of treatment and restoration are lower than implants. The order is retreatment, microsurgery, then extraction (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's cost-effectiveness statement agrees that retreatment is more cost-effective than prosthetic replacement (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications for surgery** (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022): considered when symptoms or radiographic apical periodontitis are present and
- Calcified or non-negotiable canals; persistent disease not improvable nonsurgically, or only at disproportionate risk.
- An unremovable apical fragment with pain or a non-healing lesion; an apical perforation not manageable nonsurgically; extruded filling causing symptoms.
- Previous surgery, as an alternative or addition to orthograde retreatment (Cohen's, below, retreats nonsurgically first).

**Procedure** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014): flap; osteotomy; curettage and biopsy; root-end resection (perpendicular; the routine apical 3 mm may be grossly excessive, so pare gradually); ultrasonic root-end cavity along the axis; root-end filling; wound closure.

**Intentional replantation:** strategic tooth, no other option; ankylosis and resorption are the failure modes (White and Torabinejad, 2017).

**Extraction and replacement:** last resort, for a nonrepairable tooth (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021); White and Torabinejad also accept lack of tooth structure or high caries or periodontal risk, and name the implant single crown as the replacement of choice (White and Torabinejad, 2017); retreatment is more cost-effective than prosthetic replacement (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).

**Surgical principle.** The aim is to remove bacteria from the apical root and deny survivors access to the periradicular tissues, not to disinfect the whole canal (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**After failed surgery:** Cohen's retreats nonsurgically first, since many failures are due to poorly cleaned and filled canals, unless root fracture is suspected (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

## 13. Prognosis of retreatment

### Explanation

The sources give different pictures. Ingle's reports a mean weighted success (healed) rate of 66% and similar tooth survival to primary treatment; Cohen's outcomes chapter gives the same 66% (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's retreatment chapter cites Friedman and Mor's systematic review: healing in 92% to 98% without prior apical periodontitis and 74% to 86% with it, for initial treatment and orthograde retreatment alike, which "challenges the historic perception" of a poorer prognosis for retreatment; yet Cohen's adds that the desired outcome will not occur in potentially one-quarter of retreatment cases (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. TEI: retreatment is about 10% to 15% lower than primary treatment; 94% without lesion and with unaltered anatomy but 40% with a lesion and altered anatomy (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Malek and Lin: a preexisting lesion lowers the odds of retreatment success about 6.32 times; lesions of 5 mm or more reduce success; in Gorni and Gagliani's study, success was 48.7% with altered root canal morphology against 83.3% with morphology respected, a difference more significant with a periapical lesion and insignificant without one; it has been suggested that, if patency is achieved at the canal terminus, the type of foreign material, removed or bypassed, does not affect retreatment success; and separated-instrument studies disagree (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]. Of the factors unique to retreatment, the most significant is the ability to remove or bypass old filling material or separated instruments (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

Berutti and Castellucci add a different 66%: in the study of De Chevigny and colleagues that they cite, retreatment success was 88% where prior treatment was unsatisfactory but only 66% where it was adequate (Berutti and Castellucci, 2025) [SRC]; this is a different figure from the pooled 66% above, so the two should not be mixed [Likely]. Terauchi and colleagues say that nonsurgical retreatment generally has a lower success rate than primary root canal therapy, but can have a high outcome comparable with it if the apical foramen can be negotiated and the cause of failure overcome (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's opens the chapter with a statement about endodontic treatment in general, not retreatment: more than 90% of cases would heal if the therapy were done meticulously (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Source | Retreatment outcome |
|---|---|
| Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021 | 66% mean healed, about 6% below primary treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis; survival may be similar |
| Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021 (Friedman and Mor) | 92-98% without, 74-86% with prior apical periodontitis; similar to primary treatment |
| Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022 | 10-15% lower than primary; 94% (no lesion, unaltered anatomy) vs 40% (lesion, altered anatomy) |

- **Predictors:** preoperative lesion (odds of success 6.32 times lower), lesion of 5 mm or more, altered canal morphology (48.7% vs 83.3%, mainly with a lesion), preexisting perforation (up to 32% lower), coronal restoration quality, and, most significant of the retreatment-specific factors, the ability to remove or bypass old filling or fragment (Malek and Lin, 2017; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).
- **Practical point:** Cohen's: the desired outcome may fail in potentially a quarter of retreatments (a 66% pooled rate implies more); if healing fails: observation, surgery, extraction-replantation or extraction (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). [Likely] Discuss these fall-backs with the patient before starting.

Quote the source with the number: reported outcomes range from about 40% (lesion, altered anatomy) to over 90%.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: retreatment has a poorer prognosis than primary treatment.**
- *Textbooks:* Ingle's and Cohen's outcomes chapter 66%, about 6% lower than primary treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis; TEI 10-15% lower; Cohen's retreatment chapter (Friedman and Mor) "similar potential to heal".
- *What the evidence shows:* the books agree that preoperative apical periodontitis matters; TEI and Malek and Lin add altered canal anatomy, and Ingle's and Cohen's outcomes chapter add the ability to remove or bypass old filling or fragments as the main retreatment-specific factor; they differ on whether retreatment itself adds a penalty.
- *Strength:* [Likely] mainly pooled observational studies with variable criteria and follow-up.
- *Conclusion:* the data support a modest or no penalty after adjusting for lesion status, not a large one. What cannot be concluded: that retreatment is as good as primary treatment in teeth with altered anatomy (40%). [Likely]
- *Clinical implication:* give a case-specific prognosis, not a single figure.

**Claim 2: true cysts need surgery.**
- *Cohen's* and *Setzer and colleagues* say true cysts probably do not heal after nonsurgical therapy; *TEI* says this belief rests on a prevalence study of untreated teeth and has never been supported by solid evidence, and considers it reasonable to assume that cysts can heal if the infection is properly controlled; *Weine* says there is considerable evidence that the apical periodontal cyst heals after nonsurgical treatment, although surgery is sometimes necessary (he does not separate true from pocket cysts).
- *Strength:* TEI relies on serial-section histobacteriology and one study of true and bay cysts; Cohen's statement is brief. Each reports its view; they are not reconciled here. [Guessing that infection control matters most, but the books disagree]
- *Clinical implication:* attempt orthograde retreatment first; surgery for a persisting lesion.

**Claim 3: coronal leakage versus persistent infection as the main cause.**
- TEI says indirect evidence (apical-third bacteria, bacteria at filling, failure rates by tooth type, one study of sealed canals resisting leakage) favours persistent infection; Hülsmann and colleagues call coronal restoration quality one of the most important predictors, and Goldberg lists coronal leakage as one reason for failure; Sikri names leakage as the common denominator.
- *Conclusion:* both mechanisms may operate together and cannot readily be separated clinically; restoration quality may also mark overall treatment quality. [Likely]

**Claim 4: chloroform is acceptable for gutta-percha removal.**
- *Cohen's:* used carefully a safe and effective solvent, evidence of carcinogenicity in humans suspect, continued use recommended (though all solvents have some toxicity). *Ingle's:* still the reagent of choice. *Gulabivala and Ng:* among useful agents. *Hülsmann and colleagues; Grossman's:* no longer recommended (toxicity, carcinogenicity, prohibited in some countries). *Terauchi and colleagues:* in one investigation chloroform, halothane and xylene expressed apically were below health-concern levels. *Pontius,* citing McDonald and Vire (1992): no harmful effects from limited, well-controlled use. *Sikri:* potentially carcinogenic and mutagenic, but a small quantity (under 1 ml) is unlikely to be hazardous.
- *Strength:* the supportive statements rest on cited measurements of solvent expressed, a cited report of no harm, and clinical use; [Likely] none of the books reports long-term patient outcomes of solvent use.
- *Conclusion:* the books split, with Cohen's, Ingle's and Gulabivala and Ng accepting careful use and Hülsmann and Grossman's advising against it, so the safety position differs between books; where a solvent is not essential, mechanical removal and heat avoid the question, but Cohen's says xylene and eucalyptol dissolve gutta-percha slowly and TEI that eucalyptol and orange oil have negligible antibacterial effect. ## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Posttreatment disease (term suggested by Friedman, per Cohen's) covers persisting and secondary infection (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- Cohen's four causes: intraradicular microbes, extraradicular infection, foreign body, true cyst (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- Failure is primarily microbiologic; errors act by retaining biofilm (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- Established versus potential failure (Sikri, 2017).
- Ingle's 3 Ds: decide failure, decide to treat, decide the type (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).
- Retreatment: 66% (Ingle's, Cohen ch.17; about 6% below primary treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis), 92-98% / 74-86% (Friedman and Mor, cited in Cohen's), 10-15% lower (TEI).
- Microsurgery 89-94%; older surgery alone 59% complete healing (80% after retreatment) (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- Heat: in one in vitro study, nearly 10°C in as little as 15 s without coolant during ultrasonic post removal; rises above 10°C can damage the attachment apparatus (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
- Extraction is the treatment of last resort (Cohen's); White and Torabinejad name the implant single crown as the replacement of choice.
