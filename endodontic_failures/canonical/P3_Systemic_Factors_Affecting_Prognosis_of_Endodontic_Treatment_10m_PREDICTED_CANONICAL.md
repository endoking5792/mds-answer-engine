## Explanation

**Overall health.** A recent systematic review did not find general medical health to significantly affect the outcome of root canal treatment, yet there is evidence suggesting that a few systemic problems may affect it (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad: most patient factors do not influence prognosis, but diabetes and steroid therapy may decrease it; overall patient factors are few and their impact only moderate (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Goldberg states that the prognosis must be considered doubtful in uncontrolled diabetes or severe immunocompromising disease (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC].

**Demographic.** Age and gender have not been shown to significantly affect outcome, although studies of tooth survival vary significantly; age is not a determining factor in success, but this may be confounded by the smaller number of older patients studied, and wound healing may take longer in older patients (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].

**Genetic.** Few studies exist; individuals with the rare IL-1β allele were reported seven times more likely to have posttreatment apical periodontitis than those homozygous for the common allele (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].

**Diabetes.** Fouad has suggested that diabetic patients with preoperative periapical lesions are less likely to be judged successful two years or longer after treatment, and another outcome study associated a history of diabetes with reduced success. One study found diabetes and hypertension to be independent risk factors for extraction two years after treatment, although in that study most teeth were extracted for restorative reasons and fractures (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues note that the association with extraction does not indicate causality (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Steroids and smoking.** The negative effect of steroid therapy has been attributed to delayed healing and a compromised immune system; one prospective study found that patients on systemic steroids might have a higher chance of tooth extraction. Another study reported that smoking is significantly associated with endodontic disease and prognosis, but more evidence is needed (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].

**HIV.** Most available evidence does not show lower success in HIV-positive patients; one outcome study found no difference between patients with and without HIV/AIDS (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].

**Bisphosphonates and medical compromise.** Most knowledge of the effect of bisphosphonates on endodontic outcome comes from case reports. According to the American Board of Endodontics position statement, quoted by Malek and Lin, patients taking IV bisphosphonates are at higher risk than those taking oral ones; the chapter mentions bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaws, few cases of which result from endodontic treatment. For IV bisphosphonates, conservative nonsurgical root canal therapy is described as an appropriate treatment where the tooth may otherwise be extracted (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad: root canal treatment may greatly benefit patients with bleeding disorders, head and neck radiation or high-dose bisphosphonates by avoiding high-risk extractions (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues agree (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Most systemic conditions have not been shown to affect outcome; a few (diabetes, steroid therapy) may lower it, and the overall impact is only moderate. A systematic review did not find general medical health to significantly affect outcome (Malek and Lin, 2017; White and Torabinejad, 2017).

**Notes to write beside the table** (Malek and Lin, 2017; White and Torabinejad, 2017)
- *Diabetes:* increased susceptibility to infection, neuropathy and delayed healing may affect outcome; in Ng's study tooth loss was mostly due to persistent postoperative pain, possibly neuralgia, not delayed healing.
- *Steroids:* the negative effect has been attributed to delayed healing and compromised immunity; one prospective study found patients on systemic steroids might have a higher chance of extraction.
- *Bisphosphonates:* IV drugs carry greater risk than oral (American Board of Endodontics statement); evidence is mainly case reports; conservative root canal treatment is appropriate where the tooth would otherwise be extracted.
- *Genetics:* few studies; IL-1β, MMP-2 and MMP-3 polymorphisms are linked to periapical susceptibility.
- *Overall:* patient factors are few and moderate; infection control and prevention of secondary infection matter most.

**Management implications**
1. Review the medical history for conditions that may affect outcome, and inform the patient (Malek and Lin, 2017). [Likely] Consult the physician if disease is uncontrolled.
2. Prognosis is doubtful in uncontrolled diabetes (Goldberg, 2019). [Likely] Seek glycaemic control first.
3. Root canal treatment may greatly benefit patients with bleeding disorders, head and neck radiation or high-dose bisphosphonates by avoiding high-risk extractions (White and Torabinejad, 2017; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Age and gender have not been shown to affect outcome.
- Diabetes and steroid therapy may decrease prognosis (White and Torabinejad); HIV: no difference found in outcome studies.
- American Board of Endodontics statement: IV bisphosphonates carry higher risk than oral.
- Root canal treatment may avoid high-risk extractions in medically compromised patients.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Stating that older age lowers success.
2. Treating all systemic disease as equal risk.
3. Extracting without considering root canal treatment in patients with bleeding disorders, radiation or bisphosphonate exposure.
4. Ignoring the limited evidence for smoking and genetic factors.
5. [Likely] Omitting the medical history review and physician consultation in uncontrolled disease.
