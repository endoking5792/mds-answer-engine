## 1. Definition, objectives and indications

### Explanation

Retreatment is a procedure on a tooth that previously had attempted definitive treatment and now needs further treatment to ensure success; the AAE glossary version (removal of filling followed by cleaning, shaping and obturation) is narrower (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. The objectives are the same as for primary treatment: eliminate the substrate harbouring microorganisms and seal the space (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

Indications (Sikri, 2017): poorly sealed canals where the previous instrumentation and obturation can be improved. Gulabivala and Ng: confirmed failure with accessible intraradicular infection; a new complex restoration over a technically poor filling; suspected contamination from leakage or a lost restoration (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Contraindications (Sikri, 2017): canal walls prone to perforation, failure to remove a post or fragment, a sclerotic canal that cannot be negotiated, root fracture, especially vertical, and a non-cooperative patient.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Further treatment of a root-filled or previously attempted tooth to ensure a successful result (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

**Objective.** Eliminate remaining microorganisms and seal the canal system, as in primary treatment (Sikri, 2017).

**Indications** (Sikri, 2017; Ng and Gulabivala, 2014): poorly sealed canals that can be improved; confirmed failure with accessible intraradicular infection; a new complex restoration over a technically poor filling; suspected recontamination from leakage or a lost restoration.

**Contraindications** (Sikri, 2017): thin walls prone to perforation; post or fragment that cannot be removed; sclerotic canal that cannot be negotiated; root fracture, especially vertical; non-cooperative patient.

## 2. Treatment planning

### Explanation

Ingle's treatment planning is a "3D" process: decide whether the tooth is a failure, decide whether to treat, decide which treatment; failure is usually judged from clinical symptoms and/or radiographic findings; the options are extraction, orthograde retreatment or surgery. Orthograde retreatment is preferred for radiographically poorly sealed canals and needs enough cervical tooth structure for a new restoration (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Information on flare-ups when retreating quiescent lesions is scarce; by Eriksen's estimate a quiescent lesion flares in under 5% per year untreated, whereas asymptomatic patients with quiescent lesions have a 20% to 40% flare-up incidence when retreatment is initiated. CBCT is indicated when a patient has signs or symptoms and the films are nondiagnostic (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

Case selection. A thorough, strict case selection is the basis of a good retreatment success rate. Severe alteration of anatomy argues against nonsurgical retreatment, while the AAE places all retreatments in at least the moderate difficulty group and a microscope and fine ultrasonic tips are needed for most. A lesion that has not healed or reduced in size after 1 year should prompt consideration of retreatment. Retreating a single root of a multirooted tooth is not normally indicated (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Clinician factors include whether you can improve the treatment and your prognosis against a specialist's; the patient needs to understand the options, and the decision preferably recorded in a mutually signed agreement (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Information on options and outcomes must be given, and if the cause remains unknown the decision is "trial and error" (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri: inspect under dry conditions with illumination and magnification, trace a sinus tract with gutta-percha, and evaluate the restoration and filling of a potential failure before waiting (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

Other books. Grossman's lists four options: observation (usually when the patient is not convinced), nonsurgical retreatment, surgery (complex anatomy preventing access to the apical third) and extraction as the last option (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Terauchi and colleagues stress shared decision making and rate retreatment as highly difficult on the AAE form (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]; Hülsmann and colleagues grade all retreatment at least moderate (above); Gulabivala and Ng instead grade difficulty on a scale of 10 by the cause of canal obstruction (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Pontius adds that deep probing, mobility and furcation involvement worsen the long-term prognosis and that success depends on training (Pontius, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Assess** (Sikri, 2017; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021; Machtou and Ruddle, 2019): history; examination under magnification; percussion, palpation, probing (vertical fracture); trace a sinus tract with a gutta-percha point; CBCT if symptomatic and films are nondiagnostic; rule out non-endodontic pain.

**2. Decide failure, then treat, then choose** (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019)
- Failure is judged from symptoms and/or radiographic findings; retreat a lesion neither healed nor smaller at 1 year (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
- Treat if the benefit exceeds the risk; quiescent lesions flare in 20-40% of asymptomatic patients when retreatment starts.
- Choose: orthograde retreatment (poorly sealed canals), surgery or extraction.

**3. Case selection** (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022)
- Restorability: severe anatomic alteration, valuable restorations and massive posts argue against retreatment.
- Skill and equipment: all retreatments are at least moderately difficult; refer severe cases.
- Do not retreat one root only of a multirooted tooth unless risk dictates.

**4. Consent.** Explain causes, options, prognosis, cost and the fall-back; record the decision (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).


## 3. Procedure of nonsurgical retreatment

### Explanation

The principles are identical to primary treatment; the most common error is removing excessive dentine. A temporary build-up (pretreatment) is mandatory. Access through a sound crown is better than removing it, as rubber dam is easier, whereas a cast crown is safest cut off; resin and glass-ionomer cements make crowns harder to remove. Posts: ultrasonic vibration first for metal posts; tapered posts are easier than parallel; fibre posts wear out drills quickly. Heat from ultrasonics: in one in vitro study, nearly 10°C within 15 seconds without coolant; use coolant, although root surfaces can still heat rapidly with it. Gutta-percha is removed progressively by a combination of methods, rotary instrumentation being the most efficient (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]; Hedström files remove single cones, solvents are used only if absolutely necessary, and most studies show that complete removal of gutta-percha and sealer is not possible. Ingle's, by contrast, regards the combined methods as potentially complete elimination of gutta-percha and sealer (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Missed canals must be sought in every retreatment (one study found an additional canal, mostly MB2, in 81% of root-filled maxillary molars) (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Separated instruments: a fragment in itself does not cause failure but compromises the outcome if it prevents access to the apical infection; class 1 is removed with forceps, class 2 by bypass first and otherwise a trough and extractor, and class 3 by bypass or by leaving the fragment. Packed dentine debris is picked out with a small file, alternating EDTA and NaOCl. Ledges: precurved 08-10 file. Hard cements: Endosolv E and R soften soluble cements, but there is no solvent for N2 and Russian Red, and drilling risks a false canal. Perforations: MTA is the contemporary material for enclosed defects, glass ionomer where the defect is exposed to the mouth (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014) [SRC]. Working length: electronic measurement is about 10% less reliable in filled teeth. Preparation: crown-down, disinfect, obturate and restore.

Further technique points. Terauchi and colleagues: remove all existing restorations in most cases, though access through a recently placed suitable one may be accepted to save cost; crown-down preparation is the method of choice; electronic locators must be checked with radiographs; patency raises success; hard pastes are best removed mechanically; silver points need the cement seal broken; follow-up at 6 months and then yearly (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's: cut through metal crowns with carbide burs; avoid solvents in the middle and apical thirds; use multiple-sitting therapy with calcium hydroxide in infected retreatment cases (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Terauchi and colleagues, however, report no general agreement on one versus several visits and insufficient evidence that more visits raise success (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC], and Cohen's calls one visit versus an interappointment medicament a matter of debate (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]; Cunha and Bueno say multiple visits and intracanal medicaments should be considered in difficult cases (Cunha and Bueno, 2015) [SRC]. Pontius: remove gutta-percha crown-down. Ingle's finds it prudent to disassemble all restoratives from the pulp chamber, to find hidden orifices, though access through a well-fitting existing crown is occasionally possible (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

1. **Isolation and temporary build-up.** Rebuild so rubber dam can be placed (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).
2. **Access and restorations.** Terauchi and colleagues remove all in most cases, though a recently placed suitable one may be accessed to save cost (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021); Ng and Gulabivala prefer cutting through a sound one (easier rubber dam) and cut cast crowns off. Locate all canals; rule out fractures (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014).
3. **Post removal.** Ultrasonics with coolant (one in vitro study: nearly 10°C rise in 15 seconds without coolant, still rapid with it); post pullers if needed; risk of fracture and perforation (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).
4. **Missed canals.** Search under magnification in every retreatment (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
5. **Filling removal.** Progressively in thirds (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019): rotary NiTi for the bulk, Hedström files for single cones, solvent only if necessary (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022); most studies find complete removal impossible, though Ingle's calls combined methods potentially complete.
6. **Fragments, blocks, ledges, perforations.** Class 1 forceps; class 2 bypass first, else ultrasonic trough and extractor; class 3 bypass; blocks: small-file picking, alternating EDTA and NaOCl; ledges: precurved file; perforations: MTA if fresh and enclosed by bone (old or oral-communicating ones do worse), glass ionomer if exposed to the mouth (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).
7. **Working length** by electronic locator confirmed by radiograph (about 10% less accurate in filled teeth).
8. **Shaping and disinfection.** Crown-down with patency, enlarge enough to reach the apex, irrigate (NaOCl 2.5-5%, EDTA 17%, chlorhexidine 2% commonly used), activate the irrigant; no agreed protocol, and number of visits debated.
9. **Obturation and coronal restoration** without delay.

**Special situations** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2014; Sikri, 2017; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022)
- *Silver points:* relatively easy to remove if they project into the chamber (forceps); otherwise bypass; ultrasonic tips cut into soft silver, so take care.
- *Sclerosed canals:* locate the orifice first, then negotiate with small files and a chelating lubricant.
- *Overextended filling:* corkscrew removal of overextended gutta-percha; overextended sealer usually needs surgery.

10. **Review** at 6 months, then yearly; hard pastes: mechanical instrumentation is most predictable (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021).

## 4. Surgical retreatment and prognosis

### Explanation

Surgical alternatives. Surgery (mainly apicoectomy) is recommended for a severely altered anatomy, a restoration that cannot or will not be removed, inaccessible contaminated spaces or a cause outside the root; extraction for vertical fracture, insufficient periodontal support or unrestorable teeth (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Ingle's adds that surgical correction may be considered for iatrogenic errors noted radiographically in the apical region (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's: older surgery healed 59%, 80% after retreatment, microsurgery 89% to 94%; failed apical surgery should usually be retreated nonsurgically first, unless there is root fracture or a J-shaped radiolucency (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

Prognosis. Ingle's: mean weighted healing 66%, similar survival (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's quotes Friedman and Mor: 92-98% healed without prior apical periodontitis and 74-86% with it, whether initial treatment or orthograde retreatment, which they say challenges the belief that retreatment has the poorer prognosis; in potentially one-quarter of retreatment cases the desired outcome will not occur (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]; TEI: retreatment 10-15% below primary, 94% without lesion and unaltered anatomy and 40% with a lesion and altered anatomy (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. The sources disagree; each is reported. Risks include flare-up: a series of 915 treatments cited by Hülsmann and colleagues found 2.3% overall and 4.4% in the retreatment group (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC], while some studies show more flare-ups after retreatment (Terauchi, Parirokh and Handysides, 2021) [SRC]. Perforation and dentine loss are further risks.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Alternatives if retreatment is not feasible** (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022)
- Surgery: severely altered anatomy, restoration that cannot or will not be removed, inaccessible contaminated space, extraradicular cause; Ingle's adds iatrogenic apical errors (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).
- Extraction: vertical fracture, insufficient support, unrestorable tooth.
- Failed surgery: retreat nonsurgically first, unless root fracture or J-shaped lesion (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

**Prognosis** (state the source)

| Source | Retreatment healing |
|---|---|
| Gulabivala and Ng, 2019 | 66% mean weighted; survival similar to primary |
| Friedman and Mor, in Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021 | 92-98% without, 74-86% with prior apical periodontitis (initial and retreatment alike) |
| Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022 | 94% (no lesion, unaltered anatomy) vs 40% (lesion, altered anatomy) |

Microsurgery gives 89-94%. Potentially a quarter of retreatments do not heal (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). [Likely] Plan the fall-back with the patient.

**Risks:** flare-up, perforation, dentine loss, fracture, heat damage with ultrasonics.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: retreatment is less successful than primary treatment.**
- Ingle's (66%, about 6% below) and TEI (10-15% below) say yes; Cohen's retreatment chapter (Friedman and Mor) says healing is similar once lesion status is matched.
- *Limit of the evidence:* pooled observational studies with differing criteria. Hülsmann names apical lesion, filling extrusion, coronal restoration and altered anatomy as predictors; Gulabivala and Ng name removing or bypassing the old filling or fragment; Cohen's (Friedman and Mor) stresses prior apical periodontitis.
- *Conclusion:* a modest or no penalty after adjusting for lesion status; do not generalise to teeth with altered anatomy (40%). [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Objectives of retreatment equal primary treatment; patency is the problem.
- Indications: poor seal, confirmed failure with accessible infection, new restoration, recontamination.
- Ingle's 3 Ds; treat if benefit exceeds risk; flare-ups 20-40% when starting.
- Remove restoration rather than dentine; ultrasonics need coolant (nearly 10°C in 15 s in one in vitro study).
- Extra canal found in 81% of root-filled maxillary molars (one study).
- Healing: 66% / 92-98% & 74-86% / 94% & 40%, by source.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Starting with technique and omitting indications, planning and consent.
2. Giving one success figure for retreatment.
3. Omitting the search for missed canals and the heat risk of ultrasonics.
4. Forgetting to restore the tooth, or to mention alternatives (surgery, extraction).
5. Treating all retreatment as one routine procedure without case selection.
