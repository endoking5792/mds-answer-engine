## 1. The framework

### Explanation

White and Torabinejad regard root canal treatment and implants as complementary therapies, not competing treatments; the vast majority of root-filled teeth heal without further intervention, and the commonest reasons for extracting root-filled teeth are decay, periodontal disease, non-restorability, prosthetic failure and fracture, not failed endodontics (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Machtou and Ruddle describe a 3 "D" process: decide whether the tooth is a failure, decide to treat and decide what type of treatment to undertake; the options are extraction, orthograde retreatment or surgical retreatment (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's lists four options: do nothing, extract, retreat nonsurgically or surgically (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** Endodontic treatment and implants are complementary; most extractions of root-filled teeth are for decay, periodontal disease, non-restorability, prosthetic failure or fracture (White and Torabinejad, 2017). Decide (1) is it a failure, (2) should it be treated, (3) which treatment (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019).

## 2. Step 1: is the tooth worth saving?

### Explanation

Lack of remaining tooth structure, high caries risk and high periodontal risk are valid reasons to extract and replace, and retaining substantial tooth structure matters more than any restorative choice (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's: the line between restorable and nonrestorable "is a movable one" (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Hülsmann and colleagues recommend extraction for vertical root fractures, insufficient periodontal support, unrestorable teeth, teeth not suited to retreatment, or where an implant or prosthesis is clearly superior (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Sufficient cervical tooth structure is needed for retreatment (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Extract and replace when there is insufficient tooth structure for a new restoration, vertical root fracture, insufficient periodontal support, high caries or periodontal risk, or a tooth unsuitable for surgical or nonsurgical retreatment (White and Torabinejad, 2017; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022). The boundary between restorable and non-restorable is a matter of judgement (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

## 3. Step 2: the endodontic options

### Explanation

Nonsurgical retreatment is generally the first line and the first treatment choice for most failed cases; outcomes vary widely, from 40% to 100% in one systematic review (White and Torabinejad, 2017; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Surgery is reserved for failure of retreatment or where it is not feasible; hence Hülsmann and colleagues recommend surgery for severely altered anatomy, a restoration that will not be removed, or inaccessible contaminated spaces (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Microsurgery has outperformed traditional surgery; teeth heal well initially after surgery but tend to fail over time; success is 91.4-94.4% for true endodontic lesions with microsurgery (White and Torabinejad, 2017; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Intentional replantation is for a strategic tooth with no other option, with ankylosis and resorption the main failure modes; root amputation is effective when disease is limited to a single root (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Wait-and-see is frequently preferred for asymptomatic teeth, but in the study cited 51% of lesions increased in size, 28.6% were unchanged and about 20% decreased; it should be used with caution and is not indicated in severe systemic disease (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. The usual order is nonsurgical retreatment, then microsurgery; case-specific options such as replantation or root amputation should be considered before extraction, and the tooth is extracted if surgery fails to produce healing (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Option | Indication | Outcome (source) |
|---|---|---|
| Observation | Asymptomatic, patient accepts; with caution | 51% of lesions enlarge, 28.6% unchanged, about 20% shrink (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) |
| Nonsurgical retreatment | First line; poor seal; accessible infection | Pooled 77.2% (40-100% across studies); 65% with apical periodontitis; 41% for lesions over 5 mm (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) |
| Microsurgery | Retreatment fails or is not feasible; altered anatomy; restoration not to be removed | 91-94% (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021); heals well initially but may fail over time (White and Torabinejad, 2017) |
| Intentional replantation | Strategic tooth with no other option | Ankylosis, resorption main failures |
| Root amputation | Disease limited to one root | Effective with adequate support |
| Extraction | Non-restorable, fracture, high risk | Last resort |

Usual order: nonsurgical retreatment, then microsurgery; consider replantation or root amputation before extraction; surgery may come first if the restoration will not be removed or anatomy is severely altered (White and Torabinejad, 2017; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**Points to add for marks**
- Retreatment addresses the usual cause, residual or reentering bacteria; healing rates tend to rise over time, a more favourable long-term outcome than surgery (White and Torabinejad, 2017).
- A systematic review of 31 studies found 40-100% retreatment success; a meta-analysis gave a pooled 77.2%, 65% with apical periodontitis and 41% for lesions over 5 mm (follow-up for large lesions may have been too short); no high-level study covers modern techniques (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Older surgery healed 59%, loupes with ultrasonic preparation and biocompatible fillings 86%, microsurgery 91-94% (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Previous failed surgery should be retreated nonsurgically first, because many surgical failures come from poorly cleaned and filled canals (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

## 4. Step 3: replacement

### Explanation

The first question after extraction is whether to replace; losing a single tooth has little effect on function, but loss of several teeth may be more problematic, and some teeth are strategic (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Removable partial dentures offer excellent cost-benefit but are least preferred by patients; fixed bridges have much lower survival and success than an implant single crown; the implant single crown is the replacement of choice (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues: the 10-year complication rate is about 4% for root-treated teeth and 18% for single implants; a matched study showed identical failure rates (6.1%) and restored natural teeth outlast implant restorations in comparable situations; success data rank implants above endodontics but the criteria differ and comparisons lack validity (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's: extraction and replacement is the last resort and postimplant disease does occur (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Costs for retention and restoration are lower than implants and retreatment is more cost-effective than replacement with a prosthesis (White and Torabinejad, 2017; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad add that the implant single crown has better survival than microsurgery on teeth that had no prior nonsurgical retreatment and that bridges require removal of healthy abutment structure unless already prepared (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Weine (2004) stated that 95% success is obtainable in endodontics and that implants do not approach this percentage (Weine, 2004) [SRC]; [Likely] the later sources are more qualified.

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Replace or not?** One missing tooth has little functional effect; several missing teeth or a strategic tooth may demand replacement (White and Torabinejad, 2017).
- **Mode of replacement:** implant single crown (choice), fixed bridge (lower survival and success than an implant crown; removes healthy abutment structure unless already prepared), removable partial denture (good cost-benefit, least preferred) (White and Torabinejad, 2017).
- **Comparison:** survival of root-treated teeth and single implants is similar; 10-year complications 4% for teeth vs 18% for implants; matched study: identical failure rates (6.1%); restored natural teeth outlast implants in comparable situations (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021). Success criteria differ, so success comparisons lack validity.
- **Cost:** retention and restoration cost less than implants; retreatment is more cost-effective than replacement with a prosthesis (White and Torabinejad, 2017; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).
- **Caution:** postimplant disease occurs (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021); an implant crown survives longer than microsurgery on a tooth that had no prior nonsurgical retreatment (White and Torabinejad, 2017).

**Survival data to quote.** Systematic reviews quoted by White and Torabinejad gave 6-year survival of 97% (Iqbal and Kim; Torabinejad et al); a smaller review (Ng et al) estimated 87% at 8-10 years (White and Torabinejad, 2017); large cohorts gave 94% at 3.5 years, 97% at 8 years and 93% at 5 years (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021). Pain falls to minimal levels within a week and satisfaction is extremely high (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 5. Putting it together: a decision pathway

### Explanation

Reit's principles, quoted by Machtou and Ruddle: a persistent lesion after treatment must be retreated unless retreatment is not the patient's choice, the risks outweigh the benefits or the global cost exceeds predictable alternatives (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]. If the cause is unknown, any decision is empirical and a second opinion is extremely useful (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]. Comprehensive case assessment and risk assessment for caries and periodontal disease are always required (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

1. Confirm failure and exclude non-endodontic pain (CBCT if radiographs are nondiagnostic).
2. Assess restorability, periodontal and caries risk.
3. If saveable: nonsurgical retreatment first; microsurgery if retreatment fails or is not feasible; replantation or root amputation selectively.
4. If not: extract and replace, preferring an implant single crown.
5. Throughout: explain options, prognosis and cost; record the patient's choice (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

**Patient role.** Cohen's states that the decision belongs to the patient once fully informed, and that doing nothing must be documented (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021). Referral is appropriate when the case is beyond the clinician's skill or equipment (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim: an implant is better than retreating a failed root-filled tooth.**
- *Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad:* success data rank implants above endodontics but with different criteria; complications fewer in teeth; restored natural teeth outlast implants. *White and Torabinejad:* the body of the chapter says an implant single crown has survival equal to a root-treated tooth and better than microsurgery without prior retreatment, whereas its summary says single-tooth implants have higher survival rates but the natural state has intrinsic value. *Weine:* 95% success is obtainable in endodontics and implants do not approach it (an earlier source).
- *Strength:* [Likely] based on the chapters' summaries of systematic reviews and one matched cohort, which were not opened; criteria for success differ, so the strongest comparison is survival.
- *Conclusion:* retention is not inferior when the tooth is restorable and retreatment is possible; implants are the preferred replacement when it is not. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Root canal treatment and implants are complementary.
- Usual order: retreatment, microsurgery, extraction and implant; case-specific options (replantation, root amputation) before extraction.
- Microsurgery 91-94%; older surgery worse; surgical teeth heal well initially but may fail later.
- Survival of root-treated teeth and single implants is similar; complications 4% vs 18%.
- Extract for fracture, insufficient support, non-restorable teeth.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Offering extraction and implant first without considering retreatment.
2. Comparing implant "success" with endodontic "success" directly.
3. Ignoring restorability and periodontal risk in the decision.
4. Leaving out the patient's choice and cost.
5. Forgetting that surgery heals well early but tends to fail later.
