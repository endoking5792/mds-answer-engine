## 1. What "success" means and why it is hard to define

### Explanation

A successful outcome can mean different things to patients, dentists and third-party payers: patients expect relief of symptoms, comfortable chewing and satisfying esthetics, and dentists are concerned with preventing and eliminating apical periodontitis (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. For a tooth with a vital pulp and no lesion, success means it remains asymptomatic and no periapical disease develops; for a necrotic tooth, a preexisting lesion must heal and none may develop, and new symptoms or a new or enlarging lesion count as failure (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Problem Solving states the simple version: no radiographic pathosis before treatment means none should appear afterwards, and any preoperative symptoms should resolve (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].

The tissues are hidden, so clinicians rely on indirect "proxy" changes to judge disease (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Outcome can be judged in four dimensions: physical (health, pain, function), longevity (tooth survival), economic cost, and psychological aspects and esthetics (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Histology is impossible for routine nonsurgical cases (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC].

Why this matters historically. The focal infection theory led to routine extraction of pulpless teeth for about 50 years (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC], or about 40 years in Treatment of Endodontic Infections (Gulabivala and Ng, 2022) [SRC]; the two books differ on the duration. Weine describes the period after Hunter's address of 1910 as the lowest point in the history of endodontics, and notes that despite the high degree of success some failures will occur (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

Ideal healing is regeneration with cementum over the apical termini, but incomplete removal of infection reduces without eliminating periradicular inflammation, so residual infection typically remains (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Hence a persistent radiolucency may be fibrous repair or persistent inflammation (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Success means different things to different stakeholders: patients expect relief of symptoms, comfortable chewing and satisfying esthetics; dentists are concerned with preventing and eliminating apical periodontitis; insurers judge by access to care, quality, cost efficiency and longevity (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Definitions** (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021)
- *Vital pulp, no lesion:* success = tooth stays asymptomatic and no periapical disease develops.
- *Necrotic pulp / apical periodontitis:* success = asymptomatic tooth and healing of the existing lesion, with no new lesion.
- *Failure:* new or persistent symptoms, or a new, persistent or enlarging lesion.

**Dimensions of outcome** (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021): physical health and function; longevity (survival); cost; psychological well-being and esthetics.

**Historical note.** The focal infection theory led to extraction of pulpless teeth for 40-50 years (the books differ) until about 1940; recorded outcomes restored the discipline, which became a specialty in the United States in 1952 (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021; Gulabivala and Ng, 2022).

**Why a clinician evaluates outcome.** Evaluation shows whether a procedure is effective, which factors alter it and what prognosis to quote; personal outcome data are more precise for an individual operator than pooled data (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).

**Surrogate (proxy) measures.** Because chronic inflammation is silent and the tissues are hidden, the clinician combines history, clinical signs and radiographic changes to judge whether disease is present (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021). Histology is impossible for routine nonsurgical treatment, so periapical radiography remains the main outcome tool (Chugal et al, 2017).

**What healing looks like.** The ideal is regeneration with cementum closing the apical termini, but residual infection in the apical anatomy usually persists after treatment and keeps interacting with the root filling and host (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021). A persistent radiolucency without symptoms may be fibrous repair or chronic infection; time and an acute exacerbation distinguish them.

**Simple clinical statement of success** (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011): no pathosis before treatment should mean none afterwards; any lesion present should be healed or healing on recall; preoperative symptoms should resolve and no new symptoms should appear.

## 2. Criteria and classification systems

### Explanation

**Strindberg (1956).** Strindberg's study set the criteria that are still commonly used and the follow-up (every 6 months for 2 years, then yearly, minimum 4 years) (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC]. Success needs no symptoms and normal periodontal ligament contour and width (or widening only around excess filling) with an intact lamina dura; failure is symptoms or unchanged, reduced-but-unresolved, new or enlarging rarefaction, or a poorly defined lamina dura; uncertain is an uninterpretable film or a tooth lost for unrelated reasons (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC]. The significant difference between teeth with and without preoperative radiolucency was already shown, and the criteria were criticised as very rigid (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC].

**Proliferation of terms.** "Stringent" criteria require clinical and radiographic normalcy; Chugal and colleagues note that comparing endodontic and single-implant "success" was misleading because the criteria differ (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues agree: very different criteria are used in implant dentistry, endodontics and prosthodontics, so such comparisons lack validity (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**Periapical index (PAI).** A five-point ordinal scale; scores 1 and 2 count as sound and 3 to 5 as changed (Chugal et al, 2017; Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. Hülsmann and colleagues add that it is mostly dichotomised as success and failure, needs proper calibration, and, because it was based on maxillary incisors, its suitability for molars has been questioned (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues note that complete healing by PAI may take up to 4 or 5 years, especially after endodontic surgery (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

**ESE criteria.** Favorable (complete healing), uncertain (incomplete healing) and unfavorable (no healing) (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

**Healed, healing, disease.** Friedman and Mor consider "success" and "failure" ambiguous and propose healed, healing and disease; they define disease as emergence or persistence of radiolucency with clinical normalcy, or signs and symptoms despite a normal radiograph (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. The Toronto study used PAI below 3 as healed (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC]. Functional retention (Friedman, 2004) is retention of the tooth without signs or symptoms regardless of any lesion; it should also cover whether the patient can actually use the tooth (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

**AAE categories.** Healed, nonhealed, healing and functional (Chugal et al, 2017; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Gutmann uses acceptable, uncertain and unacceptable, with an observation period of four years (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC].

**CBCT-based systems.** The CBCT-PAI scale grades lesion diameter (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC]. Wu and colleagues classify treatment as effective or ineffective on CBCT; Hülsmann and colleagues note that Estrela's CBCT index has no clearly defined histologic background (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Reit and Kvist, cited by Hülsmann and colleagues, suggest viewing periapical health as a continuum from perfect health to a high degree of poor health, with an individually defined cut-off between intervention and non-intervention (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Clinical criteria:** no pain, swelling, mobility or sinus tract (Goldberg, 2019); no soft-tissue defects or pathological probing depths (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

**B. Radiographic criteria:** normal ligament space and intact lamina dura, with no unresolved or new rarefaction (Chugal et al, 2017); no root resorption (Goldberg, 2019).

**C. Classification systems** (Chugal et al, 2017; Goldberg, 2019; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022)

**D. Problems with terms.** Many modifiers confuse: "stringent" and "strict" criteria demand clinical and radiographic normalcy, "lenient" or "loose" ones accept incomplete healing; endodontic and implant "success" use different criteria and cannot be compared directly, so survival is the fairer comparison (Chugal et al, 2017; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Strindberg's criteria in detail** (Chugal et al, 2017; recall every 6 months for 2 years, then yearly, at least 4 years)
- *Success:* no symptoms; periodontal ligament contour and width normal, or widened only around excess filling; lamina dura intact.
- *Failure:* symptoms; or unchanged, reduced-but-unresolved, new or enlarged rarefaction; poorly defined lamina dura.
- *Uncertain:* uninterpretable film, rarefaction under 1 mm with disrupted lamina dura, or tooth extracted for unrelated reasons.

**Periapical index.** Five points: normal; small changes without demineralisation; changes with some demineralisation; well-defined radiolucency; severe periodontitis with exacerbating features. It is usually dichotomised at 3 and needs observer calibration (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**Healing and disease terms** (Goldberg, 2019; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021)
- *Healed:* clinical and radiographic normalcy.
- *Healing:* normal clinical findings with a reduced radiolucency.
- *Disease:* emergence or persistence of a radiolucency with clinical normalcy, or signs and symptoms with a normal film.
- *Functional retention:* retained without signs or symptoms regardless of any lesion (Friedman, 2004).

**AAE definitions** (Chugal et al, 2017; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021)
- *Healed:* functional, asymptomatic tooth with no or minimal radiographic pathosis.
- *Nonhealed:* nonfunctional, symptomatic tooth, with or without radiographic pathosis.
- *Healing:* asymptomatic functional tooth with radiographic pathosis, or a symptomatic tooth whose function is unaltered.
- *Functional:* serving its intended purpose in the dentition.

**CBCT scales.** The CBCT-PAI grades lesion diameter from 0 to 5 (over 8 mm), E and D marking cortical expansion and destruction; Wu and colleagues classify treatment as effective or ineffective on CBCT (Chugal et al, 2017; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**Continuum concept.** Reit and Kvist, cited by Hülsmann and colleagues, view periapical status as running from perfect health to a high degree of poor health, with an individual cut-off at which intervention begins (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022).

**ESE outcome definitions** (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022)
- *Favourable:* no pain, swelling or sinus tract, no loss of function, and radiographic bone regeneration with a normal ligament space.
- *Uncertain:* no clinical signs; lesion unchanged or only reduced in size.
- *Unfavourable:* signs and symptoms of infection; a new or enlarged lesion; a lesion unchanged or smaller after 4 years; continuing resorption.

## 3. When and how to evaluate the outcome

### Explanation

Recommended follow-up periods have ranged from 6 months to 5 years; six months is a widely accepted early interval. A lesion unchanged or larger after 1 year is unlikely to resolve, while a smaller one at 6 months is probably healing; larger lesions take longer to heal, and apparent success may revert to failure later (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Most lesions heal within a year, but healing can continue for four years or longer (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Absence of symptoms does not signify success (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Chugal and colleagues report that about 5% of patients have persistent all-cause pain 6 months or longer after treatment, which may come from an adjacent tooth, a nonodontogenic source or deafferentation (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC]. Comparing success rates with and without a clinical examination found no, or a very small, difference (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

Radiographic success is absence of an apical radiolucency. Radiographs are subjective: different observers may not agree, and the same observer may disagree with themselves; six evaluators agreed only 47% of the time; lesions confined to cancellous bone are not predictably detected, in an experimental-lesion study cited by Chugal and colleagues; the film is a two-dimensional image of a three-dimensional reality; and a fibrous scar can mimic a lesion (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Chugal et al, 2017; Goldberg, 2019; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

CBCT. Hülsmann and colleagues report that about 20% more apical lesions are detected than with periapical films and that CBCT has been suggested as the best way to assess treatment outcome (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. In contrast, the AAE/AAOMR guidelines recommend against routine CBCT for outcome assessment, and Chugal and colleagues add that using CBCT only to monitor treatment outcome for asymptomatic teeth is unjustified (Chugal et al, 2017) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues also do not recommend routine use and report more uncertain and unsatisfactory healing at 1 year with CBCT (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Ng and Gulabivala say it may give lower healing rates and longer durations to complete healing (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. These positions are placed side by side, not ranked. The reliance on radiographs is itself a shortcoming of all current classifications (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC].

Histology. Two cadaver studies disagreed: one found nearly all treated teeth had some inflammation despite apparent success, the other that most teeth with a normal radiographic periapex were free of inflammation (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

Patient-based outcomes. Pain falls substantially within a day of treatment and to minimal levels in a week; overall satisfaction is extremely high (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. There is no definitive endodontic quality-of-life instrument yet (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. When:** first review at 6 months; most lesions heal within a year but healing can take 4 years or longer; a lesion unchanged or larger after a year is unlikely to resolve; large lesions heal more slowly (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).

**2. Clinical:** symptoms, swelling, sinus, probing, percussion; absence of symptoms alone is not success (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021). About 5% of patients have persistent all-cause pain 6 months or longer after treatment, which may come from an adjacent tooth or a nonodontogenic source (Chugal et al, 2017).

**3. Radiographic:** standardised films with a paralleling device, compared with preoperative films; straight-on and off-angle views for multirooted teeth or canals (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021); the PAI is used for calibration (Goldberg, 2019).

**4. Limitations of radiographs**
- Two-dimensional view of a three-dimensional structure; cancellous-bone lesions not predictably detected.
- Observer disagreement: six evaluators agreed only 47% of the time.
- Fibrous scars mimic lesions.

(Chugal et al, 2017; Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022)

**5. CBCT** (positions side by side): Hülsmann and colleagues: about 20% more lesions found, suggested as the best way to assess outcome; Chugal and colleagues: AAE/AAOMR guidelines advise against routine use, and CBCT-only monitoring of asymptomatic teeth is unjustified; Ng and Gulabivala: may give lower healing rates (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Chugal et al, 2017; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).

**6. Histology:** the ideal assessment but impractical and impossible without surgery; Brynolf found some inflammation in almost all treated teeth despite apparent success, another study found most teeth with a normal periapex free of inflammation (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

**7. Patient-based outcomes:** pain falls substantially in a day and to minimal levels in a week; satisfaction is extremely high (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021); no definitive endodontic quality-of-life instrument yet (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).

**Interpreting recall findings.** A lesion smaller at 6 months is probably healing; apparent success may later revert to failure, often through coronal leakage; late healing also occurs (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Practical recall protocol** [Likely] (the answer's synthesis of Goldberg, 2019 and Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021)
1. Review at 6 months.
2. If healed, continue routine recall and monitor the coronal restoration.
3. If healing (lesion smaller), review every 6 months to 1 year.
4. If the lesion is unchanged or larger at 1 year (Setzer: unlikely to resolve), or symptoms appear, investigate and plan retreatment.
5. Goldberg applies a prudent four-year observation period, longer than Setzer's 1-year judgement.

## 4. How often does root canal treatment succeed?

### Explanation

The traditional belief is 80% to 95% success and survival (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]; Goldberg gives 80% to 95% for all forms of endodontic treatment (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. A systematic review of 51 studies of at least 100 teeth gave an overall radiographic success of 81.5% at 5 years (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng pool the probability of periapical health at 0.83 for vital teeth and 0.72 for necrotic teeth with a periapical radiolucency (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]. Weine reports 95% success obtainable in studies of patients treated in dental schools and by general practitioners and says implants do not approach it, citing Ingle and Beveridge's finding that undergraduate students at the University of Washington obtained 95% success (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. [Likely] Weine's older, higher figure may reflect different criteria and populations; it is placed beside the pooled values, not reconciled.

Survival is much higher than healing. Very large studies give 94% functional survival for 44,613 cases at 3.5 years, 97% for 1.1 million patients at 8 years and 93% for 1.5 million teeth at 5 years in Taiwan (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad quote a 6-year survival of 97% (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC].

Complications. The 10-year complication rate is about 4% for retained root-treated teeth against about 18% for retained single implants (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].

Studies of prognostic indicators with strict radiographic criteria are studies of indicators, not of patient outcomes (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC].

Other figures. Pontius states that 25% to 35% of teeth treated in general practice have persisting apical periodontitis and that about 89% show radiographic signs of healing after one year (Pontius, 2010) [SRC]. Grossman's says more than 90% of cases heal if therapy is done meticulously (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Machtou and Ruddle quote a pooled prevalence of apical periodontitis of 36% in 28,881 root-filled teeth, from 33 cross-sectional studies in several countries (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019) [SRC]; it is placed beside Pontius's general-practice figure above, not reconciled with it. Gulabivala and Ng (2014) add that a high proportion of root canal treatment in general practice across the world is technically substandard, and that failure is 10-20% when guideline-standard treatment is performed (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

**Comment.** Weine's 95% comes from studies of patients treated in dental schools and by general practitioners (Weine, 2004). Cross-sectional studies pooled by Machtou and Ruddle found apical periodontitis in 36% of root-filled teeth (Machtou and Ruddle, 2019). Pontius put persisting apical periodontitis at 25-35% in general dental practice, with healing signs in 89% of lesions at 1 year (Pontius, 2010). Quote the criteria with the figure.

**How to quote prognosis to a patient.** Quote the figure with its criteria and follow-up period; give separate figures for vital teeth and for necrotic teeth with lesions; mention that survival is higher than complete healing (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021). [Likely] Weine's older claim of 95% success and of implants not approaching it (Weine, 2004) may reflect different criteria and populations and is not reconciled with later figures: 80-95% for success and survival together (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) and a 6-year survival of 97% (White and Torabinejad, 2017).

**Prognostic-indicator studies** use strict radiographic criteria, so they are not studies of patient outcomes (White and Torabinejad, 2017).

## 5. Factors that determine success and failure

### Explanation

Goldberg groups the factors as those from the endodontic process, general patient factors and the practitioner's own (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues list four predictors: apical pathosis, bacterial status of the canal, extent and quality of the obturation, and quality of the coronal restoration (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Malek and Lin remind that studies disagree because of different definitions of success, follow-up, sample sizes and designs (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].

**A. Patient and disease factors**
- *Periapical status:* non-vital teeth without a lesion have about 1.95 times higher odds of success than those with a lesion (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng call the presence and size of a lesion the dominant negative factor (pooled OR 2.2 for large over 5 mm versus small), explained by greater microbial diversity in larger lesions (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]. Malek and Lin report that for primary treatment lesion size had no significant effect, but for retreatment lesions of 5 mm or more had significantly lower success (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng (2014) list presence and size of the periapical lesion among the factors with a major impact on outcome and say that the factors affecting retreatment outcome are otherwise identical to those for primary treatment (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Weine takes a different position: large radiolucencies "will usually heal extremely well", contrary to published studies, according to his own observation (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. The books disagree; each is reported.
- *Pulp status:* Malek and Lin cite a systematic review that found no significant difference between vital and non-vital teeth after allowing for the periapical lesion (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]; White and Torabinejad report a slightly better prognosis for vital pulps (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC].
- *Symptoms:* clinical symptoms correlate poorly with histology, and most outcome studies reviewed by Malek and Lin found that preoperative symptoms had no significant effect on initial treatment (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]; Gulabivala and Ng report that only preoperative pain, sinus tract, swelling and apical resorption were found to be prognostic factors significantly reducing success (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]. The books differ; each is reported.
- *Age and sex:* no significant effect; Weine says prognosis in older patients is better (Malek and Lin, 2017; Weine, 2004) [SRC].
- *Tooth type and number of roots:* findings differ between studies; survival is better in non-molar teeth, with two proximal contacts and with cast restorations on molars (Malek and Lin, 2017; Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC].
- *Systemic factors:* Fouad has suggested that diabetic patients with a periapical lesion are less likely to be successful, and diabetic patients had a higher chance of tooth extraction; patients on systemic steroids might have a higher chance of extraction; yet a systematic review did not find general medical health to affect outcome significantly; HIV status showed no difference; IV bisphosphonates carry a higher risk than oral ones (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad say diabetes and steroid therapy may decrease prognosis (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng call the evidence for the effect of host immune response weak (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]. Goldberg: prognosis is doubtful in uncontrolled diabetes or severe immunocompromise (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC].
- *Host response and genes:* Malek and Lin note a paucity of studies on genetic polymorphism and posttreatment apical periodontitis, though IL-1β allele carriers were reported seven times more likely to have it; Gulabivala and Ng note emerging evidence that polymorphisms of genes involved in periapical healing may have an effect on outcomes (Malek and Lin, 2017; Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC].
- *Periodontal status:* the main reason for extracting root-filled teeth is usually restorative failure or periodontal disease rather than endodontic failure (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]; Weine says an untreated periodontal condition makes the prognosis poor (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

**B. Treatment (operative) factors**
- *Rubber dam* is mandatory (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].
- *Operator:* greater experience and training give better outcomes; magnification lacks objective evidence (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC].
- *Culture before obturation:* negative culture doubled the odds in teeth with a lesion; Gulabivala and Ng list it among the factors with major impact (Malek and Lin, 2017; Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng (2014) say a negative pre-obturation culture can raise success by an average of 12% (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].
- *Patency and length:* patency doubled the odds of success; in teeth with apical periodontitis, each millimetre of lost working length raised failure risk by 14%; Sjögren found that in teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis 90% healed when instrumented to the constriction against 69% if not, a difference not found in retreatment cases (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].
- *Filling length:* flush 0.81 and long 0.66 pooled success; flush fillings always did better than long fillings; Weine says a few millimetres short is acceptable in a vital tooth but not in one with a lesion, where the apex must be reached (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Malek and Lin, 2017; Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues cite Sjögren: in teeth with apical periodontitis 94% healed when the root filling was within 0-2 mm of the apex, and the rate fell if the canal was underinstrumented by more than 2 mm (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].
- *Filling quality:* satisfactory root fillings, judged radiographically, had significantly higher success (pooled OR 3.9, 95% CI 2.5-6.2); satisfactory meant adequate seal and absence of voids. One study found that when no bacteria were present healing occurred regardless of obturation quality (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng (2014) likewise report that when no bacteria remained after debridement in an experimental infection study healing was independent of root-filling quality, and they see the role of the root filling as subsidiary to infection control (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].
- *Perforation:* lowers odds of success by 30% (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]. *Separated instruments:* studies disagree; procedural errors may not be the direct cause of disease (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].
- *Irrigants and medicaments:* systematic reviews have failed to prove any superior (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]; Setzer and colleagues say some evidence suggests a calcium hydroxide intracanal medicament may improve prognosis (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].
- *Visits:* randomised trials found no significant difference but lacked power; systematic reviews found similar healing (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].
- *Flare-up:* prevalence about 8.4%; most studies found no effect on outcome, a more recent study found a significant reduction (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].

**C. Restorative (postoperative) factors**
- Restored teeth do significantly better than unrestored teeth (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]. A satisfactory coronal restoration improved healing (pooled OR 3.3, 95% CI 1.1-10.3), while the single London Eastman study found a much larger effect (OR 10.7, 95% CI 3.7-31.5), perhaps because it defined unsatisfactory restorations by exposed root filling or by marginal defects and history of de-cementation (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC].
- Crowned teeth survived 81% at 10 years against 63% for direct restorations (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC]; Setzer and colleagues add that full coverage improves posterior prognosis but does not lengthen the life of anterior teeth with enough structure (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC].
- Quality of filling versus restoration: one study found high-quality restorations did better than good endodontics (80% vs 75.7%); other studies disagree (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC].

**D. What does not matter much.** Technique and material have minimal effect, and improvements in canal preparation have not raised success rates; TEI says there has been no observable change in healing rates over the last century; every strong factor is linked to canal infection (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Gulabivala and Ng, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Patient and disease factors** (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Malek and Lin, 2017)
1. *Preoperative apical periodontitis:* the most important; vital teeth 0.83, necrotic with lesion 0.72; odds 1.95 times lower; lesion over 5 mm lowers success (OR 2.2; disputed for primary treatment, see below).
2. *Pain, sinus tract, swelling, apical resorption:* reduce success in Gulabivala and Ng's analysis, but most studies reviewed by Malek and Lin found preoperative symptoms had no significant effect.
3. *Age, sex:* no consistent effect.
4. *Tooth type:* inconsistent; non-molars survive better.
5. *Systemic:* diabetes (especially with a lesion) and steroids may lower success or raise extraction risk, but the evidence is weak (Gulabivala and Ng) and one systematic review found no effect of general health (Malek and Lin); HIV no difference; IV bisphosphonates riskier than oral.
6. *Host:* genetic polymorphisms (for example IL-1β) may influence outcome; evidence is limited.

**B. Treatment factors**
1. Aseptic technique and rubber dam.
2. Negative culture before obturation (odds doubled in teeth with a lesion, not in teeth without).
3. Patency at the terminus (about twofold odds); working length (in teeth with apical periodontitis, each millimetre lost raised failure risk by 14%).
4. Apical extent of filling: flush 0.81, short 0.76, long 0.66 pooled success.
5. Quality of the filling (OR 3.9), no voids.
6. Perforation (30% lower odds); separated instruments (mixed evidence).
7. Operator training and experience.
8. No irrigant or medicament proven superior (Malek and Lin), though Setzer and colleagues cite some evidence for calcium hydroxide; technique has minimal effect and visit trials lacked power (Gulabivala and Ng).

**C. Restorative factors**
1. Satisfactory coronal restoration (pooled OR 3.3, 95% CI 1.1-10.3; OR 10.7, 95% CI 3.7-31.5, in one study that defined leakage by exposed filling or margin defects) (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019).
2. Full coverage improves prognosis for posterior teeth but adds nothing for anterior teeth with enough structure (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).
3. Abutment function and few proximal contacts reduce survival.

**D. Essential message.** Every strong factor is linked to canal infection; technique has not changed pooled success over the last century (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Gulabivala and Ng, 2022).

**Evidence notes for exam answers**
- Odds of success are about 1.95 times higher without a lesion in primary treatment and about 6.32 times higher in retreatment; in retreatment cases a preexisting perforation might lower the success rate by up to 32% (Malek and Lin, 2017).
- Larger lesions have more diverse microbiota, may represent cystic transformation, and infected apical anatomy is harder to disinfect (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019).
- One study found healing was the same whether or not obturation was perfect if no bacteria were present, but when bacteria were present at filling, quality of obturation correlated with nonhealing (Malek and Lin, 2017).
- A satisfactory root filling is associated with more success, and the effect is larger in retreatment than in primary treatment (Malek and Lin, 2017).
- Crowned teeth had 81% 10-year survival against 63% for direct restorations; study results on posts vary, but post-retained restorations may have slightly lower survival (Malek and Lin, 2017).

**Where the textbooks disagree** (state the author with each position)
- *Large periapical lesions:* Gulabivala and Ng (2019) report a strong negative effect (pooled OR 2.2); Malek and Lin (2017) report no significant effect in primary treatment but a significant one in retreatment; Weine (2004) says large radiolucencies usually heal extremely well, contrary to published studies.
- *Age:* Malek and Lin (2017) find no significant effect; Weine (2004) says older patients have a statistically better prognosis, probably because of tighter apical foramina, denser bone and healthier teeth.
- *Pulp status:* Malek and Lin (2017) cite a systematic review finding no difference between vital and non-vital teeth once periapical status was controlled; White and Torabinejad (2017) report a slightly better prognosis for vital pulps.
- *Tooth type and root number:* some studies found single-rooted teeth failed more often, others found single canals did better (Malek and Lin, 2017).
- *Flare-up:* most studies found no effect on outcome, a recent one found a significant reduction (Malek and Lin, 2017); Gulabivala and Ng (2019) report one study in which pain or swelling occurred in 15% of cases and reduced success, and suggest either extrusion of contaminated material or incomplete first-visit debridement as possible explanations.
- *Separated instruments and perforations:* effect varies with lesion status and with the study (Malek and Lin, 2017); Gulabivala and Ng (2019) give a 30% reduction in odds for perforation.
- *Filling quality versus restoration quality:* one study favoured restoration, another found no difference, and a third found the radiographic quality of endodontics more important than coronal restoration (Malek and Lin, 2017).

**Weine's classical points on prognosis** (Weine, 2004)
- Reaching the apical foramen matters: with a lesion present, inability to debride the apical canal gives a poor prognosis, so apical surgery or extraction is considered.
- If the pulp was vital and the periapex normal, a filling a few millimetres short still has a good prognosis.
- An untreated periodontal condition retards healing of the periapical area.

**Predictor summary.** Setzer and colleagues list four predictors: apical pathosis, bacterial status of the canal, extent and quality of the obturation, and quality of the coronal restoration (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021). Malek and Lin name periapical status, apical extent, filling quality and restoration quality as strongly associated with outcome (Malek and Lin, 2017).

## 6. Why treatment fails (summary)

### Explanation

Failure is primarily a microbiologic problem (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues list common errors: diagnosis and planning errors, missed canals, inadequate debridement, operative errors, deficient root filling, no cuspal coverage in posterior teeth, excessive tooth removal and coronal leakage (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Organisms such as *E. faecalis* can form biofilms, and bacteria in biofilms are estimated to resist antimicrobials about 1000-fold more than planktonic forms (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cysts: about 60% of cysts are true cysts, which Setzer and colleagues say are more likely to heal only after extraction or surgery whereas pocket cysts are expected to heal nonsurgically; Weine says considerable evidence suggests cysts heal after nonsurgical treatment, and Rôças and colleagues say true cysts can heal when infection is controlled (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Weine, 2004; Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. The three books are placed side by side.

The full classification (Cohen's four groups, TEI's persistent, secondary and extraradicular infection, non-microbial causes, restorative failures) is in the 75-mark answer.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Failure arises from** (Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021)
1. Persistent intraradicular infection (probably the commonest cause on indirect evidence; persistent and secondary infection cannot be separated clinically).
2. Secondary infection (coronal leakage).
3. Extraradicular infection.
4. Foreign body reaction.
5. True cysts (opinions differ: surgery needed versus heal when infection is controlled).
6. Conditions that mimic or accompany failure: periodontal disease, occlusal trauma, nondental pain (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021); root fracture is covered in the 75-mark answer.

**Persistence.** *E. faecalis* can form biofilms, and biofilm bacteria are estimated to be about 1000 times more resistant than planktonic forms (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

**Summary of reasons** (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Rôças, Ricucci and Siqueira, 2022)
- *Preoperative:* misdiagnosis, poor case selection, treatment beyond skill.
- *Operative:* missed canals, inadequate debridement, procedural errors, deficient filling, excess dentine removal.
- *Postoperative:* leakage from an inadequate temporary or permanent restoration, no cuspal coverage, fracture.
- *Biological:* persistent or secondary intraradicular infection; extraradicular infection; cysts and foreign body reaction (disputed).

## 7. Healing patterns and decisions after treatment

### Explanation

After treatment the outcome falls into healed, healing or non-healed. Healed teeth need no further treatment and the coronal restoration should be monitored; healing teeth need clinical and radiographic checks every 6 months to a year; non-healed teeth (persistent or progressive lesion with symptoms) need retreatment or extraction (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. Functional-retention cases need advice and regular checks (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. A differential diagnosis (endodontic, non-endodontic, mixed) must be made (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC].

Retreatment is the first choice for most failures (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Outcomes after failure are compared below and managed in detail in the 75-mark answer.

#### ✎ Exam core

| Outcome | Action |
|---|---|
| Healed | Routine review; monitor the coronal restoration |
| Healing | Review every 6 months to 1 year; check the seal |
| Non-healed | Differential diagnosis; retreat (nonsurgical or surgical) or extract |
| Functional retention | Inform the patient; regular review |

(Goldberg, 2019; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021)

**Differential diagnosis at recall.** A persisting lesion or symptom may be endodontic, non-endodontic (for example a nearby lesion or periodontal disease) or mixed (combined endodontic-periodontal lesion); this must be settled before retreatment (Goldberg, 2019).

**Time to decide.** An interval of up to 4 years has been suggested before declaring a primary treatment a failure, though a lesion unchanged at a year is unlikely to resolve (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022; Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 8. Outcomes of retreatment and surgery

### Explanation

Reports of retreatment success vary. Gulabivala and Ng give a mean weighted healed rate of 66%, about 6% below primary treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]; the second edition of Treatment of Endodontic Infections by the same authors gives 78%, about 6% lower than that primary treatment (Gulabivala and Ng, 2022) [SRC]. Setzer and colleagues report a range of 40% to 100% across studies, a pooled success of 77.2% falling to 65% with apical periodontitis and 41% for lesions larger than 5 mm, though follow-up for larger lesions might not have been long enough to show complete healing (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's cites Friedman and Mor with healing of 92-98% without and 74-86% with prior apical periodontitis, for initial treatment and orthograde retreatment alike (up to 10 years) (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC]; Hülsmann and colleagues report retreatment 10% to 15% lower than primary treatment and 94% versus 40% depending on lesion and anatomy (Hülsmann, Schäfer and Bürklein, 2022) [SRC]. Weine says the prognosis for retreatment is good when the cause is an obvious lack of apical seal (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng (2014) likewise give a mean weighted success of 66%, about 6% lower than primary treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis, and attribute the lower retreatment success to obstructed access to the apical infection and a potentially more resistant flora (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

Surgery. A systematic review found success rates of 37% to 91%, depending on the operator and the specific techniques used; traditional surgery gave 59.0%, loupes with ultrasonic preparation and biocompatible fillings 86%, and microsurgery 91.4% to 94.4% for true endodontic lesions (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. Success by complete healing plateaued after two years (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's: older surgery 59%, 80% when preceded by orthograde retreatment, microsurgery 89% to 94% (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021) [SRC].

(Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021)

Setzer and colleagues: retreatment is first line and surgery is reserved for failed retreatment or where retreatment is not feasible (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021); Cohen's notes that apical surgery is no longer the second choice (Roda, Gettleman and Johnson, 2021).

**Comment.** The retreatment figure depends on the source, the criteria and lesion status: Cohen's (Friedman and Mor) reports similar healing for initial treatment and retreatment, whereas Gulabivala and Ng (about 6% lower) and Hülsmann and colleagues (10-15% lower) report lower healing. They add that tooth survival after retreatment is similar and that the retreatment-specific factor is ability to remove or bypass the old filling or fragment (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019; Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).

**Time-course of surgery.** Healing by complete healing plateaued after two years, so follow-up should be at least two years, preferably four (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019).

**Reading the numbers.** Ranges differ with criteria, lesion status, follow-up and surgical technique; quote a figure with its source and conditions (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Gulabivala and Ng, 2019).

## 9. Success compared with the alternatives

### Explanation

Setzer and colleagues report a systematic review in which success data ranked implants above endodontic treatment, which ranked above fixed prostheses, but note the criteria differ; survival is the fairer comparison: Torabinejad and colleagues reported 97% for both procedures, while Iqbal and Kim found that over all periods 95.0% of implants and 94.0% of root-treated teeth survived (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. A matched study of 196 implants and 196 root-treated teeth found identical failure rates (6.1%), with success of 82.1% for teeth against 73.5% for implants and more survival with intervention for implants (17.9% against 2.6%); Setzer and colleagues add that it is now agreed that restored natural teeth outlast implant restorations in comparable situations, and that both options are complementary (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad: implant single crowns have survival equal to a root-treated tooth (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC]. Weine, writing earlier, states that implants do not approach endodontic success (Weine, 2004) [SRC]; the newer books give a more qualified comparison. Treatment pendulum: evidence-based practice has swung back toward keeping restorable teeth (Gulabivala and Ng, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- Success rates differ because criteria differ; compare survival (about 97% for both) (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).
- Complications over 10 years: 4% for root-treated teeth, 18% for implants.
- Matched study (196 implants, 196 root-treated teeth): failure 6.1% in both; success 82.1% (teeth) against 73.5% (implants); more interventions for implants (17.9% against 2.6%). Setzer and colleagues add that restored natural teeth are now agreed to outlast implants in comparable situations.
- Endodontic treatment and implants are complementary (White and Torabinejad, 2017); evidence has swung back to saving restorable teeth (Gulabivala and Ng, 2022).

**Why a direct comparison is difficult.** Endodontic, implant and prosthodontic success use different definitions; success data have ranked implants above endodontic treatment, above fixed prostheses, but survival is similar at about 97% (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021).

## 10. Improving success: practical implications

### Explanation

Because all strong factors relate to canal infection, further improvement depends on understanding the infection, especially apically, how treatment alters it, and its interaction with the host (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC]. [Likely] Measures linked to better outcomes in the books include rubber dam (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC], patency at the canal terminus (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019) [SRC], a good-quality root filling and a good coronal restoration (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021) [SRC], and placing the permanent restoration as soon as possible (Goldberg, 2019) [SRC]. Malek and Lin found that a negative culture helped only in teeth with a periapical lesion (Malek and Lin, 2017) [SRC], and Ng and Gulabivala note that culture testing has fallen out of favor in contemporary practice (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. White and Torabinejad say root canal treatment may greatly benefit patients with bleeding disorders, previous head and neck radiation or high-dose bisphosphonates, by avoiding high-risk extraction (White and Torabinejad, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

1. Correct diagnosis and case selection; refer when beyond your skill.
2. Aseptic technique: rubber dam, disinfection of the field.
3. Complete cleaning, shaping and disinfection; patency; correct working length.
4. Three-dimensional obturation within the canal.
5. Prompt, sealed coronal restoration with cuspal coverage on posterior teeth.
6. Plan recall and explain outcome categories to the patient (Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad, 2021; Gulabivala and Ng, 2019).

**Why improvement is difficult.** Mechanical and chemical improvements have not raised pooled healing; research should target the apical infection, how treatment alters the flora, and the host response (Gulabivala and Ng, 2019).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: success rates mean the same thing across studies.**
- *Textbooks:* 80-95% (Setzer; Goldberg), 95% (Weine), 81.5% at 5 years, 0.83 and 0.72 (Gulabivala and Ng), 94-97% survival.
- *Evidence:* the numbers rest on different criteria (Strindberg, PAI, AAE), recommended follow-up (6 months to 5 years) and settings (dental schools, general practice, pooled cross-sectional surveys); observer agreement on films is poor (47%).
- *Conclusion:* figures are comparable only with matched criteria and follow-up, and survival is the fairer measure than healing. What cannot be concluded: that Weine's 95% applies to current routine practice. [Likely]
- *Clinical implication:* state criteria and recall interval when giving a prognosis.

**Claim 2: improved techniques have raised success rates.**
- *Textbooks:* Gulabivala and Ng (Ingle's, 2019) and TEI (2022) say pooled healing has not changed in a century and technique has minimal effect; Setzer and colleagues note no high-level study of retreatment with NiTi, microscope and CBCT.
- *Strength:* pooled observational plots by decade; vulnerable to changing criteria; CBCT may lower measured healing.
- *Conclusion:* "no demonstrated gain", not "no possible gain". [Likely]

**Claim 3: large lesions lower success.**
- *Ingle's:* yes (OR 2.2). *Malek and Lin:* not for primary treatment, yes for retreatment. *Weine:* large lesions heal extremely well, contrary to published studies.
- *Strength:* Ingle and Malek rest on pooled observational data; Weine states clinical observation and unpublished findings.
- *Conclusion:* the effect seems real in the pooled data, especially for retreatment, but sources disagree and are not ranked. [Guessing]

**Claim 4: coronal restoration is as important as the root filling.**
- *Textbooks:* Ingle's (pooled OR 3.3; OR 10.7 in one study), Malek and Lin (high-quality restoration outperformed good endodontics in one study, other studies differ), TEI (indirect evidence points to persistent infection rather than coronal leakage as the commoner cause).
- *Conclusion:* both matter; how leakage is defined may explain the very large effect in one study. [Likely]

- Success needs clinical and radiographic criteria; Strindberg (1956); PAI 1-5; AAE: healed, nonhealed, healing, functional.

- Follow-up: 6 months first review; at least 4 years; healing may take 4+ years.
