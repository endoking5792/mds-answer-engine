## STUDY MODE

### Ideal requirements of a root-end/repair filling material
A root-end filling material's sole functional purpose is to seal a resected/perforated/prepared cavity so microorganisms and byproducts cannot pass between the canal space and periradicular tissues. An ideal material should be biocompatible, dimensionally stable, nonresorbable, nonmutagenic, insoluble in periradicular tissues, radiopaque, easy to handle with reasonable working/setting time, should not discolour the tooth, and should actively promote **cementogenesis** — new cementum deposition over the material's surface, representing true biologic (not merely mechanical) closure (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, 2019). Ingle's Ch.24 gives an independently-numbered 11-item version of this same list, adding **"nonstaining"** and an explicitly separate **"bioinductive (promotes cementogenesis)"** criterion. No material in current or historical use satisfies every criterion simultaneously — material selection is a genuine clinical trade-off.

### A classification framework for perforations themselves (not just the material used to repair them)
Ingle's Ch.23 gives a **"four dimensions of a perforation"** classification, absent from this file before the audit: **Level** (coronal/middle/apical — the more apical, the more favourable the prognosis), **Location** (circumferential position — matters for surgical access, not for a nonsurgical approach), **Size** (area = πr²; quantified: "doubling the perforation size with any bur or instrument increases the surface area to seal by **four-fold**"), and **Time** (sooner repair is better; chronic perforations risk sulcular breakdown). Strip perforations — the thinnest, most common perforation site — occur most often in "the thin distal aspects of the mesial roots of mandibular molars and the mesiobuccal roots of maxillary molars" (Ingle's Ch.24).

### Historical and ZOE-based materials (brief, for context)
Amalgam — the traditional choice for most of the 20th century — has been abandoned (leakage, corrosion/migration producing "amalgam tattoo," unsatisfactory long-term outcomes, systemic-safety concerns); a 2018-era ABE diplomate survey found only 3.4% still using it. IRM (reinforced ZOE) and SuperEBA (ZOE + EBA + alumina/resins) remain in genuine current use (SuperEBA: 34.3% of diplomates) with genuinely mixed comparative evidence against MTA — some sealing/biocompatibility studies show no significant difference, others favour MTA; a 5-year follow-up found MTA significantly better than SuperEBA, while a separate 4-year follow-up found no significant difference between them (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, 2019).

### Mineral Trioxide Aggregate — the calcium-silicate reference standard
MTA (full chemistry/mechanism covered in Q1) is the material most frequently used as the reference standard against which every newer repair/retrograde material is compared. Its performance advantage is **not uniform** across every property tested — the specific testing method used can change which material appears superior (one study found Fuji IX GIC leaked less than MTA by fluid filtration, but more than MTA by flow porometry, using the same specimens — a genuine methodological limitation). MTA uniquely promotes cementum deposition directly over its own surface, the biologic basis for its "bio-inductive" capability (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, 2019).

### Historical origins, not previously captured in this file
Amalgam as a root-end filling material was first proposed by Farrar in *Dental Cosmos*, 1884; Omnell (1959) found a cytotoxic zinc-carbonate precipitate from amalgam retrofills, prompting a shift to zinc-free amalgam before the material was abandoned altogether (see below). Oynick & Oynick (1978) observed PDL collagen fibres abutting and extending into SuperEBA matrix — the original observation that made ZOE-based cements (SuperEBA, desiccated IRM) the first "modern" retrofilling materials of choice, predating MTA by over a decade (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, Ch.10).

### Perforation repair — hemostatic agents, resorbable barriers, and location-specific material choice
Hemostatic agents specific to controlling perforation bleeding (a distinct list from vital-pulp-therapy hemostasis): calcium hydroxide (passively syringed, left 4-5 minutes, flushed with NaOCl, two or three applications usually controls bleeding), collagen, calcium sulfate, freeze-dried bone, and MTA itself. A real caution: "some of the best hemostatics, like ferric sulfate, leave a coagulum behind which may promote bacterial growth, compromise the seal... and jeopardize the prognosis" (Ingle's, Ch.23).

Two named **resorbable barrier materials**, not previously in this file: **CollaCote** (collagen) — resorbable in 10-14 days, haemostasis typically achieved in 2-5 minutes, but contraindicated if adhesive/bonding restoratives are planned (it absorbs moisture and contaminates bonding). **Capset** (calcium sulfate) — creates a tamponade effect, mechanically plugging vascular channels; bioresorbable in 2-4 weeks; sets "brick-hard" and can be sanded flush with ultrasonics; explicitly "the barrier of choice when utilising the principles of wet bonding" — i.e. compatible with adhesive dentistry, unlike CollaCote (Ingle's, Ch.23).

**Location-specific material selection, a real clinical decision rule**: calcium sulfate + adhesive dentistry for coronal-third perforations needing esthetics; MTA/Biodentine for non-esthetic coronal defects; MTA specifically as the material of choice for middle- and apical-third perforations when the field cannot be kept dry (Ingle's, Ch.23). Cervical external-resorption/caries defects follow the same logic: composite resin or glass ionomer where the defect is in a cosmetic area; MTA where cosmetics are not a concern (Ingle's, Ch.24).

**Biodentine specifically as a perforation-repair/root-end material**, directly compared with MTA — not previously distinguished from its pulp-capping role elsewhere in this project: "Its chemical composition is different from that of MTA, as it has no gypsum and less water... combines the biocompatibility of MTA with... bioactivity-inducing hard tissue formation, good handling characteristics, self-adhesion to dentine, no shrinkage, and a better antibacterial action... setting time significantly shorter than MTA (12-15 minutes)" (Ingle's, Ch.23). As a root-end filling material specifically (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, Ch.10): powder = tricalcium silicate, mixed in an amalgamator for 30 seconds with aqueous calcium-chloride liquid, giving ~12 minutes working time before set.

### New root-end filling materials beyond the already-covered bioactive-endodontic-cement family
Five further named materials, each with real chemistry, genuinely absent from this file before the audit (Chopra's, Ch.4):
- **Capasio** (Primus Consulting) — calcium aluminosilicate-based (dental glass + bismuth oxide + calcium aluminosilicate gel); pH 10.9 (vs white MTA 11.6); sets at 9 minutes; mean particle size 5.3μm (enters dentinal tubules more readily than MTA); unlike MTA/Generex A, does **not** boost primary osteoblast growth or nodule formation.
- **EndoBinder** (Federal University of São Carlos, Brazil, 2007 patent) — a calcium **aluminate**-based cement (not silicate): Al2O3 68.5%, CaO ~31%, SiO2 0.3-0.8%, calcined at 1315-1425°C; **less** tissue-reactive than MTA in rat subcutaneous testing; unlike MTA's Fe2O3 (linked to staining) and free MgO/CaO (cause unwanted expansion on moisture contact), EndoBinder's calcium-aluminate phase balance avoids both problems, and shows better cell responsiveness at an advanced stage of osteoblastic differentiation than MTA.
- **Generex A** — bismuth oxide + tricalcium aluminate + dicalcium/tricalcium silicate + hydroxyapatite powder, mixed with a sodium-lauryl-sulfate-containing gel into a dough/rope consistency like IRM; the most biocompatible of this "newer" group tested (osteoblast attachment comparable to MTA) but, unlike MTA, did **not** show nodule formation.
- **Quick-Set/Quick-Set2** — Capasio-derived but with the cationic surfactant removed; Quick-Set2 adds a silicate phase and replaces bismuth oxide with **tantalum pentoxide** (discoloration-free, like NeoMTA Plus); comparable in-vitro osteogenic/dentinogenic behaviour to ProRoot MTA.
- **Ceramicrete-D** — hydroxyapatite powder + phosphosilicate ceramic + cerium oxide (±bismuth oxide) radiopacifier; initial set 6 minutes, final set 12 minutes; better handling/washout resistance than white MTA but less radiopaque, less strong, and more acidic initially than Capasio/Generex A.

**CEM cement (Calcium Enriched Mixture)**, already named elsewhere in this project but with genuinely new composition/performance data here: composition by weight ~51.75% CaO, 9.53% SO3, 8.49% P2O5, 6.32% SiO2, plus Al2O3/Na2O/MgO/Cl totalling 23.91%; forms hydroxyapatite even in **plain saline** (unlike MTA, which needs a phosphate-containing fluid to do so). A genuine quality-standard failure worth naming for a "critically discuss" answer: CEM cement's radiopacity measured at **2.227 mm Al** — below ProRoot MTA's 5.009mm Al — "did not fulfill the requirement of ANSI/ADA specification 57/2000 and ISO 6876/2001" (3mm Al minimum) (Chopra's, Ch.1/Ch.4).

A named-study success-rate table for root-end filling materials (Chopra's, Ch.4, Table 4.2), more granular than the single "938-tooth study" already cited: Asgary & Ehsani 2013 (CEM, 13 teeth, 1.5yr, 93%); Çalışkan et al. 2016 (MTA, 90 teeth, 2-6yr, 80%); Kim et al. 2016 RCT (MTA 91.6% vs SuperEBA 89.9%, 182 teeth, 4yr); Shinbori et al. 2015 (ERRM, 113 teeth, 1yr, 92%); von Arx et al. (MTA vs Retroplast: 91.3%/79.5% at 1yr, 92.5%/76.6% at 5yr, 353→271 teeth); Zhou et al. 2017 RCT (MTA 93.1% vs iRoot BP Plus 94.4%, 158 teeth, 1yr).

### Surgical preparation technique and a new named technology: piezosurgery
**Piezosurgery** — a bone/root-cutting technology, entirely absent from this file before the audit: piezoelectric vibrations produce cavitation (vaporisation/bubble implosion) with reported antibacterial action; advantages include soft-tissue protection, better visualisation, less blood loss/vibration/noise, and root-structure protection; disadvantages are equipment cost, longer procedure time, and contraindication with cardiac pacemakers per many manufacturers. Developed in 1998 by the Italian oral surgeon Tomaso Vercellotti (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, Ch.10) — the same piezoelectric principle already credited in this project's "microscope + MTA + piezoelectric instrumentation" fivefold-success finding, now given its own named origin and mechanism.

Quantified bevel-angle/preparation-depth data (Gilheany et al. 1994, cited Torabinejad & Rubinstein Ch.10): 0° bevel needs 1mm preparation depth; 30° bevel needs 2.1mm; 45° bevel needs 2.5mm; a 3.5mm depth is recommended radiographically to buffer angulation error. Gutmann & Lovdahl's Ch.16 independently corroborates the 0°→1mm and 45°→2.5mm figures from a separate source — cross-confirmed, not merely repeated. Isthmus data: most isthmuses occur 3-5mm from the apex (Teixeira et al.); isthmus biofilm cannot be eradicated nonsurgically, and Siqueira (2005) proposed regarding **all** retreatment cases as infected, since organisms in isthmuses/fins/debris/deltas/voids are removable only by surgical excision plus ultrasonic cleaning, not by host defences or chemotherapy. Diamond-coated ultrasonic retrotips remove gutta-percha more efficiently than stainless-steel tips (grab vs. spin) and do **not** cause statistically significant additional root-end cracking versus stainless tips (Brent et al. 1999) (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, Ch.10).

Root-end material handling specifics, genuinely new procedural detail: SuperEBA/desiccated IRM carried as 1-2mm truncated cones on a #12 spoon excavator, condensed with small pluggers, finished with a ball burnisher; EndoSequence RRM putty finished by wiping with a moist cotton pellet (also available syringeable, same material, different viscosity); ProRoot MTA delivered via the MAP system (PEEK plungers, 0.9-1.5mm) — MTA "is cohesive to itself but only slightly adhesive to the walls of the preparation," risking pull-out on plugger withdrawal (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, Ch.10).

A broader bioceramics taxonomy, context for why this entire material family is called "bioceramic": beyond calcium silicates, the general class also covers alumina/zirconia, bioactive glass, glass-ceramics, coatings/composites, hydroxyapatite, resorbable calcium phosphates, and radiotherapy glasses — used generally for joint/tissue replacement and implant-coating biocompatibility (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, Ch.10).

### The bone-graft/membrane debate — a genuinely counterintuitive evidence base
A 2011 web survey (Naylor et al.) found 40% of endodontists routinely use guided tissue regeneration (GTR) with root-end surgery. A review (Lin et al. 2010) concluded a **blood clot is biologically a better space filler than any bone-grafting material** — the host's own product, an excellent healing scaffold — and that membrane barriers have "not been shown to have a clear benefit" generally; the best-evidenced use of a membrane is specifically in combined endo-perio lesions or large periapical lesions communicating with the alveolar crest, with no conclusive benefit shown for large/through-and-through defects versus controls (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.16).

**"Healing by scar" deserves explicit recognition as a legitimate, stable long-term outcome, not an incomplete-healing failure**: Molven et al. followed 34 scar-healed-at-1-year cases for a further 8-12 years — 22 remained unchanged/stable, 1 progressed to full healing, and only 1 failed; such cases "could be considered successful in the absence of signs and symptoms" (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.16).

Named graft/membrane products with their mechanisms, for completeness: **DynaBlast** paste/putty (Keystone Dental) = demineralised bone matrix (osteoinductive, via BMPs) + mineralised cancellous bone (osteoconductive scaffold), from the same donor; **DynaMatrix** membrane = porcine small-intestine submucosa, retains native collagen and signalling proteins for tissue ingrowth/angiogenesis, remodels into native tissue in 2-4 months, and can be left exposed without clinical consequence (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.16).

Quantified animal-study osseous-healing data by treatment combination (Dean et al., cited Ingle's Ch.24) — a genuinely striking, directly quotable finding: perforation repaired + bone defect filled with decalcified freeze-dried bone + periodontal membrane cover → 75-100% osseous healing; perforation repaired + blood-clot fill + membrane → 92-100%; perforation repaired **alone** (no graft/membrane) → only 52-70%; perforation **not** repaired, even with graft/membrane/clot in the bone defect → 0-15%. The study's own conclusion: **"repair and sealing of the perforation site were necessary to achieve significant osseous healing"** — sealing the perforation itself matters more than what is done to the surrounding bone defect, directly quantifying a point this file previously only made qualitatively.

### Furcation and strip perforations — prognosis depends on the attachment, not just the material
Gutmann & Lovdahl's Ch.18 gives specific furcation/strip-perforation repair rules, absent from this file before the audit: small furcal perforations with an **undisturbed attachment** have good long-term stability with **internal MTA repair**; larger perforations (especially with major pulp-chamber-floor loss) carry a poor prognosis; perforations repaired with materials **other than MTA** show documented poor long-term outcomes, particularly when extruded into periodontal tissue (near-impossible to remove nonsurgically without enlarging the defect) (Tsesis & Fuss 2006). An illustrated case: an MTA-repaired furcation perforation was stable for 6.5 years before developing a furcation radiolucency; surgical re-repair left bone "not healed completely" at 1-year follow-up despite the tooth remaining symptom-free/functional — "the prognosis was guarded."

A related finding: a vertical root fracture extending coronally from the apex (not through the attachment) can be incorporated into, and sealed as part of, the root-end MTA filling itself — multiple illustrated cases healing at up to 4 years (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.16/18).

### Root/tooth resection — a strategic fallback distinct from any repair material
Root/tooth resection (amputation, hemisection, bicuspidization, trisection) is the strategic fallback when a perforation or fracture repair is surgically inaccessible, or a canal cannot be instrumented/filled at all (irreparable strip perforations, non-negotiable canals, irretrievable instruments/filling materials). Case-selection needs ≥50% remaining bone support and a favourable furcation-to-crestal-bone relationship; the orifice of the resected root must still be permanently sealed to prevent coronal leakage — the same sealing logic as this question's own "ideal material" criteria, just applied to a whole root rather than a point defect (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.18).

### Further historical/niche repair materials — each a distinct trade-off
- **Glass-ionomer cement (GIC)**: seals better than amalgam, comparably to IRM; a direct 1- and 5-year clinical comparison against amalgam found no difference in healing capacity (90% success at 1yr, 85% at 5yr for both) — the 1-year result predicted eventual 5-year outcome in >95% of cases. Real limitation: technique-sensitive, susceptible to moisture/blood contamination during initial set (significantly more common in unsuccessful cases of the same study) (Cohen et al., 2021).
- **Diaket**: a polyvinyl-resin material, originally a root canal sealer, with superior leakage performance in studies (though never directly compared with MTA on this measure) — a genuine biocompatibility paradox: cytotoxic/chronically inflammatory when thinly mixed as a sealer, but well tolerated by osseous tissue at the thicker root-end-filling consistency. Forms a unique osteoid/cementoid-like barrier of undefined biologic nature; no longer available in the US (Cohen et al., 2021).
- **Geristore and Dyract** (resin-ionomer/compomer): both leak less in vitro than IRM/amalgam/SuperEBA, Geristore comparable to MTA — but Geristore's actual periradicular healing has been genuinely unpredictable (10 of 18 root-end-filled teeth developed abscesses in one animal study, attributed to placement difficulty), and where cementum DID form, coverage never exceeded 25% — considerably less than MTA's. A useful corrective against assuming every resin-hybrid material performs similarly (Cohen et al., 2021).
- **Retroplast** (composite-based): a 388-case comparison against amalgam at 1 year found 74% complete healing vs. 59% for amalgam, significantly better, with no significant complication difference; a separate 351-case study reported 80-89% complete healing, and a 10-year follow-up of 34 of those cases found 33 still completely healed — genuinely strong longevity data. Real location-specific weakness: significantly higher failure rate specifically in mandibular premolars/molars versus MTA (Cohen et al., 2021).

### The newer bioactive endodontic (BAE) cement family
A large, still-expanding family beyond MTA — Biodentine, Calcium Enriched Mixture (CEM), BioAggregate, EndoSequence Root Repair Material (RRM, putty/paste), Endocem MTA/Zr, and more than a dozen further proprietary formulations — all aimed at addressing MTA's handling drawbacks while preserving/improving biologic performance. The comparative evidence is **genuinely inconsistent, with real study-specific reversals** rather than a clear hierarchy (detailed in Q1/Q2) — no material in this newer generation has yet displaced MTA as the evidence-supported default (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, 2019).

### Osseoinductive/osteoconductive materials in periapical surgery — folded in per user instruction
**DEFINITION — Osteoinductive vs. osteoconductive**: an osteoinductive material actively stimulates host cells to differentiate into bone-forming cells (true biological induction); an osteoconductive material instead provides a passive scaffold permitting bone growth from adjacent healthy bone, without itself inducing new bone formation. MTA's cementogenesis-promoting property (above) places it functionally closer to the osteoinductive category — real evidence of new cementum/bone formation directly over the material surface in animal studies, not merely a passive plug (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, 2019, cross-referencing the same biocompatibility data established in Q1).

**Adjunctive measures in periapical surgery**: a controlled study found placing **platelet-rich growth factor** over the MTA-filled root end, within the bony defect, and over the sutures reduced postoperative pain, swelling, and analgesic consumption, and improved patient-reported daily function (mouth opening, chewing, speaking, sleeping) compared with surgery without this adjunct — a real, quantified symptomatic benefit from combining a bio-inductive repair material with a biologically active adjunct (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, 2019). Guided tissue regeneration (GTR) membranes and bone grafts (hydroxyapatite, allografts) are the broader osteoconductive/osteoinductive materials used specifically for larger periapical bony defects — this is a genuinely distinct materials category from calcium-silicate cements themselves, cross-referenced to Surgical Endodontics rather than re-derived here, since it concerns the bony defect itself rather than the root-end seal.

### Clinical outcome evidence and current practice
Systematic reviews of root-end filling material choice on surgical outcome are themselves inconsistent — one found no significant material-type influence at all; two found IRM/MTA significantly better than amalgam/GIC/gutta-percha; a further review found only MTA had a significant positive influence. More recent, larger systematic reviews have tended to favour MTA more consistently — attributed to improved study quality over time, not a change in the material. The single strongest outcome evidence: a 938-tooth retrospective study found the **combined** use of operating microscope + gray MTA + piezoelectric instrumentation produced a **fivefold** increase in surgical success over traditional technique — material selection cannot be evaluated independent of magnification/preparation technique. ABE diplomate survey: MTA most popular (61.4%), SuperEBA second (34.3%), amalgam distant third (3.4%), GIC last (0.8%) — practice mirrors the outcome evidence (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, 2019).

**A wider set of named outcome studies, each measuring the same general "technique/magnification improves outcome" effect independently** (worth distinguishing from each other, not merging into one figure, since they differ in method and magnitude): Setzer et al.'s 2010 meta-analysis found pooled success of traditional (non-microscope) surgery 59% vs. apical microsurgery (SOM) 94% (relative risk 1.58×, significant); a second Setzer et al. meta-analysis (2012, magnification-specific) found weighted pooled success 88% (traditional) vs 94% (microsurgery/higher magnification), significant for molars. Rubinstein & Kim's prospective study found SOM+SuperEBA short-term success 96.8%, long-term 91.5% (5-7yr), with healing time averaging 7.2 months independent of lesion size, and 60% of the cohort premolar/molar (unlike earlier anterior-heavy cohorts). Song et al. (2012) followed cases successful at 5-year short-term follow-up (91.5%) for a further 6-10 years, finding long-term success in that healed population of 93.3%; von Arx et al. (2012) found the 5-year prognosis of microsurgery was "8% poorer than assessed at 1 year." Pre-microsurgery historical baselines, for contrast: Frank et al. 1992 — amalgam retrofill success dropped to 57.7% at 10 years; Friedman et al. 1991 — 44.1% success, 136 premolar/molar roots; Kvist & Reit 1999 RCT — no systematic surgical-vs-nonsurgical difference, 56-60% success either way. A direct RCT (Song et al.) comparing SuperEBA vs ProRoot MTA as root-end filling found "no significant difference in the clinical outcomes" — "the surgical technique may then have a greater bearing on the clinical result than the specific root-end filling material" (Torabinejad & Rubinstein, Ch.10).

**A genuine, well-quantified time-dependent reversal, not previously in this file**: Torabinejad et al.'s 2009 systematic review found that at 2-4 years, surgery outperformed nonsurgical retreatment (77.8% vs 70.9%, p<0.05) — but at 4-6 years this **reverses**, nonsurgical retreatment outperforming surgery (83.0% vs 71.8%, p<0.05). The same review's microsurgery-vs-single-implant survival comparison gave 94% (2-4yr) and 88% (4-6yr) for microsurgically-treated teeth, with by-study figures ranging 87-95% across four named cohorts (Chong 2003, Taschieri 2008/2011, von Arx 2012). Seltzer et al.'s meta-analysis gave a simpler top-line figure: endodontic microsurgery 94% vs 59% with older techniques/materials (Ingle's, Ch.24) — broadly consistent with, but not identical to, the Setzer et al. figures above; state each with its own source rather than averaging them.

A real, location-specific clinical finding: MTA's outcome does not vary meaningfully by anatomical location, but Retroplast showed significantly higher failure specifically in mandibular premolars/molars — a real caveat for a clinician considering a resin-based alternative to MTA in posterior mandibular teeth.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim — 10m)

**Claim — MTA's status as the evidence-supported default repair/retrograde material is driven as much by its bio-inductive (cementogenesis-promoting) biological behaviour as by its sealing performance alone.** EVIDENCE: MTA uniquely promotes cementum deposition directly over its surface in animal studies (true biologic closure), and the largest outcome study credits combined technique (including MTA) with a fivefold success increase. INTERPRETATION: when comparing repair materials, biological induction (not just physical sealing ability) is a genuine differentiator — materials like Geristore that seal well in vitro but show unpredictable in-vivo healing (only 25% cementum coverage, 10/18 abscess rate in one study) illustrate that sealing data alone does not predict biological outcome. LIMITATION: the specific causal contribution of MTA's bio-inductive property (versus simply its superior physical seal) to the fivefold outcome improvement is not isolated in the 938-tooth study, which credits the COMBINATION of material + microscope + piezoelectric technique — [Likely], not [Certain], on how much of the outcome benefit is attributable to biological induction specifically versus sealing quality.

### Osseoinductive/osteoconductive materials
Osteoinductive = actively stimulates bone-forming differentiation (MTA's cementogenesis). Osteoconductive = passive scaffold (GTR membranes, bone grafts — distinct category, cross-ref Surgical Endodontics). Adjunct: platelet-rich growth factor over MTA-filled root end + bony defect + sutures → reduced pain/swelling/analgesic use, improved daily function.

### Outcome evidence
938-tooth study: microscope + MTA + piezoelectric → 5-fold success increase. ABE survey: MTA 61.4% > SuperEBA 34.3% > amalgam 3.4% > GIC 0.8%. Retroplast: location-specific failure (mandibular premolars/molars). Setzer et al. meta-analyses: 59%→94% (traditional vs microsurgery) and 88%→94% (by magnification level). **Time-dependent reversal** (Torabinejad et al. 2009): surgery > nonsurgical retreatment at 2-4yr (77.8% vs 70.9%) but REVERSES at 4-6yr (71.8% vs 83.0%).

### Perforation classification and new materials
Perforation's "4 dimensions": Level/Location/Size/Time — doubling size = 4x surface area to seal. New root-end materials beyond MTA/Biodentine: Capasio, EndoBinder (calcium ALUMINATE, not silicate), Generex A, Quick-Set2, Ceramicrete-D, CEM cement (forms HA in plain saline; radiopacity FAILS ANSI/ADA 57). Dean et al.: perforation repair alone = 52-70% osseous healing; repair+graft/clot+membrane = 75-100%; NOT repaired = 0-15% regardless of graft. Piezosurgery (Vercellotti, 1998): cavitation-based cutting, soft-tissue-protective.

### Conclusion
No single repair/retrograde material satisfies every ideal-property criterion; MTA's combination of adequate sealing AND genuine bio-inductive (cementogenic) behaviour — reinforced by modern magnification/instrumentation technique — makes it the current evidence-supported default, with GIC, Retroplast, and the newer bioactive-cement family offering real but materially different trade-offs.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Ideal requirement unique to repair materials: promotes CEMENTOGENESIS (true biologic closure, not just mechanical seal).
- GIC vs amalgam: identical healing at 1yr (90%) and 5yr (85%) — 1yr result predicts 5yr outcome in >95% of cases.
- Geristore: low in-vitro leakage BUT unpredictable in-vivo healing (≤25% cementum coverage, 10/18 abscess rate in one study) — sealing data ≠ biological outcome.
- Retroplast: strong long-term data (33/34 still healed at 10yr) BUT significantly higher failure in mandibular premolars/molars.
- Osteoinductive (active differentiation stimulus) vs osteoconductive (passive scaffold) — MTA functions as osteoinductive via cementogenesis.
- 938-tooth study: microscope + MTA + piezoelectric = 5-fold success increase (technique + material combined).
- Platelet-rich growth factor adjunct over MTA: reduces postoperative pain/swelling, improves function.
- Perforation's "4 dimensions" (Ingle's): Level, Location, Size, Time — doubling size quadruples the surface area needing seal.
- Dean et al.: sealing the perforation itself matters MORE than the bone-defect graft/membrane (52-70% healing with repair alone vs 0-15% if NOT repaired, even with a graft present).
- Furcation perforation prognosis depends on the ATTACHMENT, not just the material: undisturbed attachment + internal MTA = good long-term stability; extrusion into periodontal tissue = poor prognosis regardless of material.
- Time-dependent reversal: surgery beats nonsurgical retreatment at 2-4yr, but nonsurgical retreatment beats surgery at 4-6yr (Torabinejad et al. 2009) — state both windows, don't average.
- EndoBinder is calcium ALUMINATE-based (not silicate) — a genuine chemical outlier among the "newer MTA-like" materials.
- Blood clot alone is a biologically BETTER space filler than bone graft material (Lin et al. 2010) — a genuinely counterintuitive finding for "adjunctive measures."

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing materials without noting the genuine trade-offs (in-vitro sealing data vs in-vivo biological outcome can diverge, as with Geristore).
2. Confusing osteoinductive and osteoconductive definitions.
3. Omitting the location-specific outcome caveat (Retroplast in mandibular premolars/molars) when discussing material selection.
4. Presenting MTA as uniformly superior on every single measure rather than acknowledging the genuine methodological inconsistency (e.g., the Fuji IX fluid-filtration-vs-porometry reversal).
5. Treating GTR membranes/bone grafts as part of the calcium-silicate-cement family rather than correctly identifying them as a distinct osteoconductive materials category.

## MNEMONIC
"**C**ements **C**lose, **C**ells **C**ementogenesis" — repair cements seal mechanically AND, for MTA specifically, biologically induce cementum (cells).
