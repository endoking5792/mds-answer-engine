## STUDY MODE

### 1. The paradigm this question tests
Hans-Hermann Rebel's 1922 doctrine held that "an exposed pulp is a lost organ" (Grossman's, Ch.11; *Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.7) — the dominant view for most of the 20th century, driving routine pulpectomy for any exposure. The modern VPT paradigm, decisively supported by the Kakehashi, Stanley & Fitzgerald (1965) germ-free rat study, overturns this: in the **absence of microorganisms**, an exposed pulp has genuine regenerative capacity to close the exposure site by hard-tissue formation **even without any capping material or final restoration** — proving bacteria, not the exposure itself, are the necrotizing agent (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.108; Seltzer & Bender's, Ch.13, p.544-545, independently citing the same 1965 study with added detail: conventional rats showed complete pulpal necrosis with periapical abscess by **day 14**, while germ-free rats formed an intact dentin bridge by **day 32** with normal underlying pulp despite food-debris impaction). This single experiment is the biological proof-of-concept for every strategy below, and should open an answer to this question.

Public-health framing worth stating: caries affects almost half the world's population (direct+indirect costs reaching US$442 billion in 2010, WHO data); traumatic dental injuries have the 5th-highest incidence among all diseases/conditions globally, and a US national survey found 1 in 4 individuals aged 6-50 show evidence of one (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.7-8). Reducing the number of teeth requiring root canal treatment via successful VPT "would lessen the economic burden of dental care and increase tooth retention" — in lower-income settings, root canal treatment is "currently not a viable option" for many patients, making VPT not just a biologic preference but an access-to-care argument (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.7, p.144).

### 2. Biological basis — dentin classification and the two repair pathways
Dentin is classified (Kuttler, 1959) as **primary** (formed during tooth development), **secondary** (formed after root completion, continuing throughout life), and **tertiary** (formed in direct response to a stimulus — the category VPT actually manipulates) (Seltzer & Bender's, Ch.2, p.61). Deposition rates differ by an order of magnitude: primary dentinogenesis proceeds at **~4 μm/day**, secondary at **~0.4 μm/day** (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.2, p.37).

Tertiary dentin itself splits into two mechanistically distinct pathways, the central organizing framework for this entire question (Seltzer & Bender's, Ch.2, p.73-74; *Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.2, p.41-42; cross-ref Q4):
- **Reactionary dentin** — secreted by **surviving** postmitotic primary odontoblasts that simply upregulate their own secretory activity in response to a **mild** stimulus (e.g. slow caries).
- **Reparative dentin** — secreted by a **new generation** of odontoblast-like cells, differentiated from progenitor/stem cells, after the original odontoblasts die in response to a **severe** stimulus — a far more complex sequence requiring progenitor recruitment, differentiation, then secretory upregulation.
Both can occur **simultaneously** in different zones of the same deep, actively progressing carious lesion.

Quantified reactionary-dentin data (Seltzer & Bender's, Ch.2, p.79): reactionary dentin area increases by **1.187 mm² for every 1-mm decrease in remaining dentin thickness (RDT)**; maximum reactionary dentinogenesis (~4-fold greater than baseline) occurs at RDT **0.25-0.50 mm**; below RDT 0.25 mm, secretion falls again because odontoblast survival drops to **~50%** (vs ≥85% survival in shallower cavities). Material ranking for stimulating reactionary dentinogenesis: **calcium hydroxide > composite resin > resin-modified glass ionomer > zinc oxide-eugenol (no effect)**. EDTA etching time shows a non-monotonic optimum: **60 seconds > 120 seconds > 0 seconds** for reactionary-dentin secretion (Seltzer & Bender's, Ch.2, p.79-80).

**A genuine, honestly-stated caution on what "dentin bridges" actually represent biologically**: several histological studies on direct pulp capping (with hydraulic calcium silicate cements AND with Ca(OH)2 alike) found **no evidence of true odontoblast or odontoblast-like-cell differentiation** — the newly formed hard tissue may instead be "dystrophic intrapulpal mineralization," not genuine dentinogenesis (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.123-124, citing Dammaschke et al. 2019 and Ricucci et al. 2014). Dentin bridges are also commonly **imperfect**: "tunnel defects" (multiple perforations through the bridge) were found in **89% of 192** Ca(OH)2-induced dentin bridges in one study, with **41%** of those bridges associated with recurring pulpal inflammation/necrosis, and an independent primate study found tunnel defects in **90% (172/192)** of bridges (Cox et al. 1996) — strong cross-corroboration. Mjör's caution, worth quoting directly: **dentin bridge presence is not a reliable criterion for successful pulpal healing** (Seltzer & Bender's, Ch.2 p.90, Ch.13 p.557).

### 3. The molecular signalling basis for recruiting a repair response
Dentin matrix itself is a sequestered, "fossilized" reservoir of growth factors (TGF-β1/2/3, BMP-2/4/6, IGF, FGF) that become bioavailable only when released by a stimulus — EDTA, acid etching, Ca(OH)2, or hydraulic calcium silicate cement (HCSC) contact (Cohen's, Ch.12, Table 12.1; cross-ref Q4). Ca(OH)2 and MTA both solubilize TGF-β1 and noncollagenous dentin matrix components, which diffuse to and signal odontoblasts/progenitor cells (Graham et al. 2006 for Ca(OH)2; Tomson et al. 2007 for MTA, both cited in Seltzer & Bender's Ch.2, p.77/87). EDTA is now considered the "gold standard" for releasing these dentin matrix components (DMCs), using TGF-β as the biomarker; 6% NaOCl has a **deleterious** effect on dental pulp stem cell (DPSC) survival/viability — the resulting clinical guideline is **irrigate with 1.5-3% NaOCl, then a final rinse of 17% EDTA** (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.1, p.42-43). Ultrasonic agitation of irrigants independently increases DMC release (Widbiller et al. 2017). p38-MAPK signalling and the "hormesis" concept (low-level inflammation/cytokines promote stem-cell differentiation and mineralization; high-level inflammation impedes it — an explicit biphasic dose-response) are the modern mechanistic frame for why controlling, not merely eliminating, inflammation matters (Ingle's, Ch.29).

Molecular detail for a "critically discuss"-level answer: DSPP (the predominant non-collagenous dentin matrix protein) binds collagen fibrils and spatially organizes Ca²⁺ aggregation during hydroxyapatite nucleation; DMP1's C-terminal organizes crystallographic planes while its N-terminal stabilizes the amorphous mineral phase, and DMP1 is downregulated once mineralization completes (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.7, p.163-164). MTA's own repair mechanism has a specific immune basis: a "temporary localized tissue cytokine burst" (IL-1α/β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8) from hard-tissue-forming cells (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.2, p.47). Biodentine's mechanism is distinct and still not fully characterised: it reduces TNF-α-induced TRPA1 expression/function in odontoblast-like cells (El Karim et al. 2016).

### 4. Caries-depth staging and case selection — the decision gateway for every strategy below
A named, exam-ready caries-depth classification (Bjørndal & Ramezanzade's "Caries Profunda" system), with matched treatment recommendations not present anywhere else in this project (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.4, p.79-80):
- **CP1**: lesion 2/3-3/4 dentin depth; radiopaque zone present; microorganisms confined to carious dentin; chronic inflammation without invasion → **selective caries removal, one step**.
- **CP2 ("deep caries")**: lesion ≥3/4 dentin depth; radiopaque zone still present; low probability of pulp-cavity invasion → **stepwise excavation**.
- **CP3 ("extremely deep caries")**: lesion full dentin thickness, no radiopaque zone, microorganisms present IN the pulp cavity, severe inflammation ± partial/full necrosis → **pulpectomy/RCT** (partial pulpotomy possible, but comparative long-term data are lacking).

Within CP3, exposure is treated as **inevitable** — "extremely deep caries" (Mejare et al.'s term) means selective removal is not appropriate "even if the tooth presents with clinical symptoms suggestive of reversible pulpitis"; the AAE classifies this scenario as chronic irreversible pulpitis despite a possibly asymptomatic presentation (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.6, p.128).

A second, independent contamination-based classification (Bjørndal's Class I/II, distinct from the depth staging above): **Class I** = no bacterial contamination (traumatic/"sterile" exposure) → standard protocol suffices. **Class II** = bacterial contamination present (carious exposure, especially deep/extremely-deep) → requires an "enhanced protocol" (antiseptic technique, antibacterial lavage, magnification, HCSC) (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.6, p.130-131).

A third, pulpitis-severity classification mapped directly to treatment choice (Wolters et al. 2017), proposed as superior to the plain reversible/irreversible dichotomy for VPT case selection: **initial/mild pulpitis** → indirect or direct pulp capping; **moderate pulpitis** → partial or complete pulpotomy; **severe pulpitis** → complete pulpotomy, or vital extirpation if haemorrhage is persistent/uncontrollable (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.3, Table 3.1, p.69-70). This is a genuine refinement because irreversible pulpitis can be clinically **asymptomatic in 14-60%** of cases, and clinical-vs-histological diagnostic discordance has been reported at **15.6%** in one study and **60-80% ("hypodiagnosis")** in others (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.106-107) — the symptom-based dichotomy alone is not a reliable gate.

Quantified RCT evidence comparing complete vs selective/stepwise caries removal on pulp-exposure rate (several independent trials, all favouring the more conservative approach on exposure risk): Leksell et al. (Sweden) 40% (complete) vs 18% (stepwise); Bjørndal et al. 2017, 5-yr RCT, 36% (complete) vs 21% (stepwise), with 46% vs 60% of non-exposed teeth showing normal conditions at 5 years (significant, favouring stepwise); Maltz et al., 5-yr RCT (Brazil), 80% cumulative success (one-step selective) vs 56% (stepwise) — significant the other way. A Cochrane review (Schwendicke et al. 2021) concluded complete caries removal should **not** be chosen over selective removal for deep lesions (moderate-certainty evidence vs stepwise, very-low-certainty vs one-step selective) (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.4, Tables 4.3-4.4, p.90-93). The International Caries Consensus Collaboration's exact position: "In deeper lesions in teeth with still sensible (vital) pulps, preserving pulpal health should be prioritized over restoration success" (strong recommendation) (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.4, p.93).

**★ A genuine, named cross-guideline contradiction, to be stated explicitly rather than reconciled**: the AAE's 2021 VPT position statement recommends **complete** removal of infected and demineralized tissue in deep caries — diametrically opposed to the ESE/Cochrane/ICCC consensus above for **selective** removal. The AAE position is based on a histological study of only 12 teeth (8 evaluated at just 3 months); the opposing evidence base is several years of larger comparative clinical trials (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.4, p.93, citing AAE 2021 vs. Ricucci, Siqueira & Rôças 2020). A second, independent tension exists **within the same monograph**: a separate chapter reports a direct histological comparison in which selective excavation showed chronic pulp inflammation in **68-100%** of cases (by histological criteria) versus only **7-33%** for complete excavation + Ca(OH)2 capping — yet both patient groups were clinically symptom-free for 3 months to 5 years, and the chapter uses this to argue the AAE's complete-removal position may be correct, concluding "a higher probability of success of 'selective caries excavation' compared with VPT after pulp exposure cannot be established" (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.108-109, citing Wang 1953 and Ricucci et al. 2019/2020). Both figures, both positions, and both named sources should be stated together in an exam answer, not smoothed into one verdict.

A mechanistic nuance undercutting the "bacteria starve under a good seal" rationale for selective removal: anaerobic **asaccharolytic** bacteria in infected dentin metabolise nitrogenous substrates (proteins/glycoproteins from demineralised collagen and tubular fluid) rather than fermentable carbohydrates, so they do **not** require an external substrate supply and can remain active under a dense, well-sealed restoration — their degradation products can sustain chronic pulp inflammation even when a lesion looks clinically "arrested" (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.108). Bacterial endotoxin (LPS) readily diffuses through dentin as thin as 0.5 mm (Nissan et al. 1995, cited same page). Pulpal response becomes severe/irreversible specifically once bacterial penetration comes within **~0.75 mm** of the pulp — not a linear/gradual relationship (Seltzer & Bender's, Ch.13, p.571-574, Reeves & Stanley 1966).

### 5. Strategy 1 — Indirect pulp capping / selective caries removal (one-step or stepwise)
**Stepwise excavation** (two-stage): stage 1 = selective removal to soft dentin + temporary restoration, re-entry after 6-12 months; stage 2 = removal to firm dentin + permanent restoration. **One-step selective removal**: biomaterial placed over a dentin barrier, soft/firm dentin removed, immediate permanent restoration. **Indirect pulp capping** (ESE terminology, one-stage removal to hard dentin) is paradoxically **more aggressive** than either of the above two, though it minimises unintended-exposure risk less effectively (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.4, Table 4.1, p.77).

Historically, indirect pulp capping relied on hard-setting Ca(OH)2 cements, which do **not** provide reliable long- or short-term pulpal protection; HCSCs are now preferred (Cohen's, Ch.24). A classic head-to-head finding worth quoting: one study found two-step (stepwise) excavation gave **60% vitality at 5 years** versus only **6%** for direct pulp capping and **11%** for partial pulpotomy in the same comparison — though this gap is partly attributed to a poor hemostasis protocol and the use of Dycal in that trial, not necessarily the modality itself (Cohen's Ch.24). Indirect pulp capping (two-step) carries two practical disadvantages versus one-step MTA direct capping: patient-compliance risk for the mandatory return visit, and doubled operator/office time and cost — current MTA evidence "raises the question of whether... there is any advantage to a two-step treatment regimen" (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.7, p.149). Swedish longitudinal registry data: survival of teeth after stepwise excavation (not needing RCT/extraction) = **72% at 4 years**, with patient age the only significant modifying factor, and failure incidence highest in the first 30 months before the survival curve flattens (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.4, p.91).

RDT-based prognosis bands for indirect capping (Grossman's, Ch.11, p.219-220): RDT 2.0-0.5 mm = good prognosis; 0.5-0.25 mm = reduced prognosis (less odontoblastic activity). Reparative dentin deposition averages **1.4 μm/day**, decreasing markedly after 48 days, and proceeding faster in primary than permanent teeth. "Soft" vs "firm" dentin is the modern replacement terminology for "infected"/"affected" dentin: soft dentin is bacteria-laden with irreversibly denatured collagen (cannot remineralise); firm dentin is demineralised but not bacterially invaded, with intact collagen cross-linking that **can** act as a remineralisation template (Grossman's, Ch.11, Table 11.2, p.218).

### 6. Strategy 2 — Direct pulp capping
Indications: mechanical/traumatic exposure (**not** recommended for carious exposures), haemorrhage controlled within 10 minutes, asymptomatic tooth, clean field, minimum 1.5 mm HCSC thickness over the exposure (Grossman's, Ch.11, p.221-222). Four quantified variables for a favourable outcome: mechanical > carious exposure; haemorrhage controlled **within 10 minutes**; exposure size **<1 mm** (though see below); treatment **within 48 hours** of exposure (Grossman's, p.221).

**Two commonly-taught "rules" are explicitly debunked by current evidence** (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.104-105): (a) the traditional "maximum ~1 mm² exposure size" criterion "cannot be sustained today" — exposure size has no demonstrated influence on success as long as infection is avoided, even for large trauma/pulpotomy-site exposures; (b) exposure location (occlusal/incisal vs cervical/lateral) and tooth group/jaw have no significant influence on prognosis. Exposure-duration thresholds are more decisive: microbial contamination after 2-24 hours of exposure is low (direct capping still appropriate); by 7 days, infection/inflammation reaches ~2 mm depth and direct capping is **no longer** indicated — partial/complete pulpotomy is required instead (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.103).

Hemostatic-agent specifics with real thresholds: H2O2 causes histological emphysema in the pulp and is now discouraged; NaOCl ~3% is recommended (<2.5% is insufficiently disinfectant/haemostatic, >5% too tissue-aggressive); 2% chlorhexidine is an acceptable alternative (no significant histological difference from NaOCl) but has a lower overall disinfecting effect on carious dentin, and CHX mouthrinse concentrations (0.1-0.2%) are inadequate for this use. Ferric sulfate is used for primary-tooth haemostasis but is explicitly **not** recommended in permanent teeth — it masks the pulp's true inflammatory state (risking capping an irreversibly inflamed pulp) and is associated with significantly more postoperative discomfort (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.109-111). An RCT found NaOCl 2.5% irrigation of the pulp wound before MTA direct capping gave **89%** 1-year survival versus **55%** for saline irrigation (Ballal et al. 2022, cited same chapter, p.110) — a striking, directly citable technique-level finding. HCSC's own mechanism for reduced postoperative pain: it decreases vascularisation/vasodilation and inflammatory-cell recruitment at the capped site, decreasing intrapulpal pressure (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.111).

Quantified outcome data by material and timepoint (Cushley et al. 2021 meta-analysis, cited *Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.117): Ca(OH)2 74% (6mo) / 65% (1yr) / 59% (2-3yr) / 56% (4-5yr); MTA 91%/86%/84%/81%; Biodentine 96%/86%/86% (no 4-5yr data). A GRADE analysis found the risk of failure **significantly lower** with HCSC than Ca(OH)2 in permanent teeth, with no significant difference between individual HCSCs. An exceptional long-follow-up dataset (Ricucci et al., 225 teeth/148 patients, Ca(OH)2 capping): clinical success 100%/95%/95%/86%/89% at 1/5/10/20/35 years — but **histological** success overall was only ~66%, substantially lower than the clinical figures (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.116-117) — an important honesty-of-evidence point for critically discussing "success."

**★ A genuine, quantified long-term-vs-short-term tension for Ca(OH)2 specifically**: a retrospective study of 123 teeth found Ca(OH)2 direct capping of carious exposures **failed in 45% at 5 years and 80% at 10 years** (Barthel et al. 2000); a separate 248-tooth study found overall survival of only **76.5% at 1.3 years**, worse in patients >60. This sits in real tension with the 50-96%+ short/medium-term success and dentin-bridge-formation figures quoted elsewhere in the same literature; the source material does not reconcile early bridge-formation/short-term success against these much worse long-term failure rates — state both, do not average (Seltzer & Bender's, Ch.13, p.558).

**★ A genuine species-dependent reversal, named explicitly in the source**: direct dentin-adhesive pulp capping performed **as well as or better than** Ca(OH)2 in long-term primate studies (Murray & García-Godoy 2006) but the **opposite** in human-tooth studies — adhesive-capped human pulps showed persistent inflammation, microabscesses, and **no** dentin bridge formation, while Ca(OH)2 controls in the same study healed and bridged normally (Pereira et al. 2000). The chapter concludes explicitly: "dentin adhesives are unacceptable and contraindicated as direct pulp capping agents" in humans — resin components are vasorelaxant, promoting bleeding and impairing healing once hemostasis has been achieved (Seltzer & Bender's, Ch.13, p.561-563).

A full numbered clinical protocol (Box 5.3, *Vital Pulp Treatment* Ch.5, p.111-113): LA → dental dam → disinfect crown (NaOCl 2.5-5%/CHX 2%/alcohol 70%) → complete caries excavation with a fresh sterile bur just before reaching pulp → clean exposure with NaOCl → haemostasis target ≤5 min, escalating to pulpotomy/pulpectomy if unresolved → HCSC ~1.5 mm thick over the exposure and adjacent dentin, leaving a clean dentin margin for bonding → wait full HCSC set time (≥15 min) → flowable liner if needed → definitive adhesive composite **same appointment** → cold-test at next visit.

### 7. Strategy 3 — Pulpotomy (partial/Cvek and full/complete)
ESE formal definitions: **partial pulpotomy** = removal of a small portion of coronal pulp directly beneath the exposure, biomaterial applied to the remaining pulp tissue; **full/complete pulpotomy** = removal of the entire coronal pulp, biomaterial applied at the canal orifice(s) (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.6, p.127). The **Cvek partial-pulpotomy** technique (traumatic exposures): ~2 mm cavity around the exposure, ~1 mm capping material (historically Ca(OH)2; "MTA would be the current material of choice") plus ~1 mm remaining space for a bonded restorative seal; the **1.5 mm exposure-diameter threshold** is an empirical, not absolute, guideline, alongside age, absence of a concomitant luxation injury, and root-development stage (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.7, p.155, citing Cvek 1978).

Quantified comparative success (Grossman's, Ch.11, p.225): partial pulpotomy **95%** vs direct pulp capping **80%** in young permanent teeth. Full pulpotomy meta-analysis data (Cushley et al.): 95% at 12 months falling to 88% at 3 years for symptomatic irreversible pulpitis; Ather et al. 2022: overall 86% [CI 0.76-0.92], higher in asymptomatic (91%) vs symptomatic (84%) irreversible pulpitis, and higher with open apex (96%) vs closed apex (83%) (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.6, p.128-129). Partial pulpotomy meta-analysis (Elmsmari et al.): 92% [CI 0.83-0.97] 2-year success for cariously exposed pulps, with preoperative irreversible-pulpitis status a significant negative predictor; partial vs full pulpotomy show no statistically significant success difference, but partial is more technique-sensitive — clinicians without magnification may default to full pulpotomy for this reason (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.6, p.129-132).

**A genuine paradigm shift worth naming explicitly**: full pulpotomy is now being extended to **mature** teeth with **symptomatic irreversible pulpitis and apical radiolucency** — cases traditionally mandating pulpectomy — with current clinical investigations reporting successful treatment without pulpectomy using HCSCs (Cohen's, Ch.24). A 413-molar RCT found MTA vs CEM direct pulp capping for symptomatic irreversible pulpitis achieved 95% vs 92% success at 1 year; a separate 407-patient multicentre RCT found vital pulp therapy (pulpotomy + CEM capping) achieved similar 6-month success to conventional root canal treatment but with **significantly less postoperative discomfort** (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.6, p.128-129, citing Ingle's Ch.29 data).

The "enhanced protocol" for carious (Class II/contaminated) exposures: aseptic multi-tooth isolation, field disinfection, mandatory **re-disinfection after old-restoration/caries removal** (since this step re-contaminates a previously cleaned field), magnification strongly recommended (microscope > loupes — partial pulpotomy's technical demands often push clinicians without magnification toward full pulpotomy instead), local anaesthesia via 2% lidocaine+1:80,000 epinephrine or 4% articaine+1:100,000 epinephrine (never intrapulpal, to avoid damaging the pulp), partial pulpotomy removes 2-3 mm of tissue vs full pulpotomy to the orifices, HCSC layer ≥2-3 mm, and a temporary restoration left beyond 2 days significantly raises failure risk (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.6, p.131-140). ESE 2019 follow-up protocol: review at 6 and 12 months, then yearly to 4 years; success = maintained vitality with no pulpal/periapical disease signs/symptoms for ≥1 year (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.6, p.144; Ch.10, p.225-226). Factors shown **not** to influence pulpotomy outcome: patient age, gender, tooth type, stage of root development. Factors that **do**: caries depth/preoperative inflammation severity and the enhanced-protocol operative factors themselves (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.6, p.145).

Historical materials context: formocresol (formaldehyde + cresol + glycerin, introduced 1930s by Sweet) has strong antimicrobial/tissue-fixing action but is now largely abandoned over toxicity/carcinogenicity concerns and restricted mostly to primary-tooth pulpotomy, where MTA has shown "much better success... than traditional formocresol" and is now recommended as the primary-tooth material of choice (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.7, p.147-148; *Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.7, p.157-158). CEM cement (Calcium Enriched Mixture) is a distinct named pulpotomy agent in the Asgary et al. literature, with pulp-survival data of 97.6% at 1 year (n=205) and 78.1% at 5 years in the same cohort (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.8, Table 8.2).

### 8. Strategy 4 — Apexification vs. apexogenesis (for the necrotic or inflamed immature tooth)
A foundational definitional pair, needed to frame the next section correctly: **apexification** = inducing an apical hard-tissue barrier in a **non-vital** immature tooth; **apexogenesis** = physiologic continued root development of a **vital**, inflamed/infected immature tooth after pulpotomy (Grossman's, Ch.11, Table 11.3). Open-apex (immature) pulps have a more generous blood supply, making vital treatment far more tolerant of injury/bacterial exposure and more likely to succeed than in mature roots — justification for attempting VPT even with pulpitis symptoms present (Gutmann & Lovdahl, Ch.7, p.144-145).

Traditional **multi-visit Ca(OH)2 apexification** takes 6 months to 4 years and risks weakening root dentin, predisposing to late root fracture; single-visit **MTA/HCSC apical-plug apexification** is the modern alternative (Grossman's, Ch.11, p.230-234). Quantified outcome comparisons (Chopra's, Ch.5): CH apexification success 79-96% vs MTA apical plug 81-100%; a systematic review of 4 studies found no significant clinical/radiographic success difference between CH and MTA, but the MTA barrier forms significantly faster (pooled mean difference −3.58 months, 95% CI −4.91 to −2.25, p<0.001, Lin et al. 2016); Chala et al.'s meta-analysis found MTA apical plug final success 100% vs CH 92%; treatment-time data show MTA total time 0.75±0.5 months vs CH 7±2.5 months (Pradhan et al.), and apical barrier formation 4.5±1.6 months (MTA) vs 7.9±2.5 months (CH), p<0.01 (Damle et al.).

### 9. Strategy 5 — Regenerative endodontic procedures (REP / revitalization)
**Historical origin predates the commonly-cited narrative by ~40 years**: Nygaard-Østby's 1961 paper "The role of the blood clot in endodontic therapy" first demonstrated experimentally that an induced apical blood clot could be partially/completely replaced by fibrous connective tissue inside the canal — forgotten for decades before revival (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9, p.207-208). Modern landmark cases: Rule & Winter (1966, double antibiotic paste); Hoshino (1993, triple antibiotic paste); Iwaya et al. (2001) — first modern case report, incomplete root formation + chronic apical periodontitis + sinus tract, 30-month follow-up showing radiographic root completion AND restored electric-pulp-test response; Banchs & Trope (2004) — coined the modern "revitalization" protocol (induced bleeding + MTA cap at the CEJ level), 24-month follow-up confirming osseous healing, root lengthening/thickening, and apical closure (Grossman's, Ch.12; *Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9, p.208).

**Four REP prerequisites** (Murray et al. 2007): presence of stem cells; complete disinfection; provision of a scaffold; provision of a signal for stem-cell differentiation (Chopra's, Ch.6, p.186). Stem cells of the apical papilla (SCAP) are the primary recruited population: evoked bleeding produces a **400- to 600-fold** increase in mesenchymal stem-cell markers in intracanal vs systemic blood (Lovelace et al. 2011, cited Chopra's Ch.6, p.187) — an independent chapter of the dedicated VPT monograph gives a **>700-fold** figure for the same CD73/CD105 marker increase (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.5, p.191) — both figures real, from the same underlying study cited differently by different chapter authors; cite whichever source is used, don't average them. The full named stem-cell-niche roster: SCAP, DPSC, SHED, PDLSC, BMSC, iPAPC, DFSC, TGPC, SGSC, OESC, GMSC, PSC (Cohen's Ch.12; Ingle's Ch.29).

Full two-visit clinical protocol (Grossman's Ch.12, p.240-241; *Vital Pulp Treatment* Ch.9, p.210-213 — both independently detailed, consistent): **Visit 1**: remove necrotic tissue without mechanical wall debridement; irrigate 1.5-3% NaOCl (stopping short of the apical foramen) via a 30-gauge side-vent needle 2 mm short of working length; saline rinse, then 17% EDTA (neutralises residual NaOCl cytotoxicity AND exposes collagen/bioactive dentin proteins on the canal wall); dry; fill with Ca(OH)2 **or** low-concentration (0.1-1.0 mg/mL) triple antibiotic paste (TAP = metronidazole + ciprofloxacin + minocycline, though minocycline is now often omitted to avoid discoloration); adhesive temporary seal; recall 2-4 weeks. **Visit 2** (if asymptomatic): remove medicament via 17% EDTA rinse; local anaesthetic **without** vasoconstrictor (a vasoconstrictor compromises bleeding induction); induce bleeding via periapical irritation with a pre-bent ISO #30 Hedström file (open apex to ≥1.1 mm, which independently predicts greater revascularization likelihood on reimplantation data — Huang 2008, Seltzer & Bender's Ch.5 p.191); fill to ~2 mm below the CEJ, wait 15 minutes for clot formation, cover with a resorbable collagen matrix (named products: PARASORB HD Cone, Hémocollagène, RCP), then HCSC directly over the collagen (avoiding barium-sulfate-containing HCSC, which risks crown discoloration — Biodentine is preferred); GIC/RMGIC coronal seal. Follow-up at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24 months, then annually for 5 years, with an extra 3-month visit if the etiology was persistent infection, resorption, or reimplantation trauma.

Microbiology/disinfection data: NaOCl irrigation alone gives ~10,000-fold bacterial reduction; a further 2 weeks of TAP gives an additional ~1000-fold drop (mean CFU 1.7×10⁸ → 1.4×10⁴ → 26, dog-model data); TAP at full clinical paste concentration (1000 mg/mL) kills 100% of SCAPs in vitro, while the recommended dilute concentration (1 mg/mL) leaves SCAP survival unaffected while remaining antibacterial (Cohen's Ch.12). TAP is poorly removable from dentin by irrigation (>80% remains, penetrating >350 μm into tubules) versus Ca(OH)2 (>80% removed) — relevant to the discoloration risk below. NaOCl+CHX combinations produce a cytotoxic "para-chloroaniline" precipitate and should be avoided; 2% CHX is cytotoxic to SCAPs and should not be the final rinse (Chopra's Ch.6, p.187-190).

**Root-development-stage decision rule** (Cvek staging 1-5): stages 1-3 (thin, fragile walls) → revitalization offers the greatest added benefit, since continued wall growth genuinely matters for fracture resistance; from stage 4 onward, either an apical plug or revitalization is mechanically adequate (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9, p.209-210). Indications/contraindications: best candidates = stages 1-3 (±apical periodontitis); not advisable if a dental dam cannot be placed or if an intracanal post is needed; avulsed immature teeth need caution (~1/3 spontaneously revascularize without any intervention); no recommendation possible for luxated teeth (insufficient evidence); ASA 3+ patients → prefer the conventional approach (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9, p.209, 213).

Outcome data: no significant difference between apexification, apical plug, and revitalization in periapical healing/survival in most comparative reviews; Panda et al. 2022's 7-trial meta-analysis found revitalization 83% vs apexification 86% success, not significant (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9-10, p.212, 229-230); a pooled systematic review (Torabinejad et al. 2017) gave MTA apical plug survival 97.1%/success 94.6% vs regenerative treatment survival 97.8%/success 91.3% (Chopra's Ch.5, p.6761) — a directly citable head-to-head for a "compare" command-word answer. Platelet concentrates (PRP/PRF) as scaffold adjuncts significantly improved sensibility-test-positive outcomes versus plain blood clot in meta-analysis, and reduced postoperative pain in one trial — but with "lack of good-quality trials," and venipuncture is "particularly unsettling for young patients" (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9-10, p.216, 231).

Tooth discoloration in REP has three distinct, separately manageable sources: minocycline in TAP, intracanal blood itself, and the MTA coronal plug (dentin-bonding-agent tubule sealing, Ca(OH)2 substitution for TAP, or switching to Biodentine, respectively) (Ingle's Ch.29).

**★ A major conceptual nuance that should anchor the critical-discussion portion of any answer to this question**: the histological outcome of revitalization is **not** true pulp regeneration. Tissue formed inside the canal typically lacks a structured odontoblast layer, with ectopic cementum or bone found instead of dentin-pulp tissue; the book states explicitly, "the biological outcome of revitalization does not represent pulp regeneration but must be considered a classic tissue repair in the biological sense" (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9, p.213-214). Diogenes et al. coined "guided endodontic repair" as the more accurate alternative term, since "regeneration" is a misnomer for what actually happens clinically — independently corroborated by Chopra's Ch.1/6 (histology of extracted REP-treated teeth shows ectopic bone/cementum/PDL-like tissue, not true tubular dentin, in most cases; one well-documented exception, a 6-year clinical success later extracted for orthodontic reasons, showed genuine odontoblast-like cells and newly formed tubular dentin — i.e. true regeneration is occasionally, not reliably, achieved). A tiered outcome framework follows from this honestly: **primary** goal = absence of pain/swelling/sinus tract + radiographic lesion reduction; **secondary** = root length/wall-thickness increase; **tertiary** = restored sensibility response — a case can be a clinical "success" with **zero** new mineralised tissue or root growth (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9, p.212). Persistent intracanal bacterial infection, even when **not** radiographically visible as a periapical lesion, is the single most important prognostic factor for revitalization failure (radiographic "success" can mask biological failure) (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9, p.212-213).

### 10. Strategy 6 — Emerging cell-based and cell-free tissue engineering (future direction, not yet routine)
A formal Endodontic Tissue Engineering (ETE) classification (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.9, p.215): **cell-based ETE (CB-ETE)** — cells harvested ex vivo, expanded in lab, transplanted in a customised scaffold with added growth factors; promising early data, but "considerable cost/regulatory/logistical challenges" mean routine clinical use "does not currently seem realistic." **Cell-free ETE (CF-ETE)**, based on "cell homing": scaffold + signalling molecules alone are delivered, and endogenous stem/progenitor cells already present near the root apex are chemotactically recruited — no ex vivo cell manipulation needed; painless (unless blood products used) and easy to administer by simple injection.

Named experimental scaffold/biomolecule strategies, useful for a "recent advances"-flavoured answer: nanofibrous scaffolds loaded with simvastatin (anti-inflammatory, favours regeneration in an LPS-rich environment) or dexamethasone (osteo-/dental-lineage differentiation); self-assembling-peptide electrospun scaffolds; chitosan scaffolds (±collagen/calcium materials, upregulating DMP1/DSPP/Collagen I); GSK3-inhibitor-loaded hydrogels (activate Wnt signalling, producing tubular dentin-like structures from as early as 4 weeks, outperforming plain MTA in one study); DMP1-derived oligopeptides; encapsulated TGF-β+FGF-2 controlled-release systems; alginate + dentin-matrix-derived "bioink" for 3D-bioprinted constructs; laser-ablated micropatterned gelatin scaffolds that guide single-DPSC polarisation to better mimic true odontoblast morphology — addressing the specific problem that accelerated repair usually yields only matrix-secreting "odontoblast-like" cells, not fully polarised true odontoblasts (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.7, p.163-167). Bioceramics' own mechanistic contribution to REP specifically: they enhance blood-clot stabilisation, provide antibacterial/anti-inflammatory action, and stimulate BMP secretion/action (Chopra's, Ch.1, §1.9.1).

### 11. Trauma-specific VPT and other special-situation strategies
Complicated crown fracture (CCF) outcome data is strongly protocol- and operator-dependent. IADT (2020) and AAE (2013) guidelines list pulp capping vs partial pulpotomy in **opposite** preferred orders for CCF without specifying when each should take priority; ESE (2021) is more specific — partial pulpotomy for large exposures or treatment delay, pulp capping only for minor exposures treated within a few hours (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.8, p.179-180). A large retrospective (375 teeth, Wang et al.) found pulp necrosis incidence 57.1% (direct pulp capping) vs 10.1% (partial pulpotomy) vs 9.8% (full coronal pulpotomy) — direct capping was "five times more likely" to end in necrosis than pulpotomy in this dataset; retreatment after a failed direct cap, by pulpotomy, achieved comparable success to primary pulpotomy (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.8, p.176-186). Operator experience mattered sharply in one hospital-setting study of 56 CCF/CCRF teeth (Cvek protocol): overall success only 61%, but 70.4% for postgraduate registrars, 71.4% for paediatric specialists, versus just 33% for general dental practitioners (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.8, p.177). Practical triage: a glass-ionomer "pulp bandage" over an exposed traumatised pulp pending referral outperforms primary-care pulp extirpation (same page). Delay up to 7 days does not meaningfully deepen the histological inflammatory zone in primate studies (1.8 mm mean at 48h vs 1.6 mm mean at 7 days, Cvek et al. 1982) — supporting pulpotomy even after delayed presentation (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.8, p.178, 186).

**Luxation injury is the dominant negative prognostic factor** for CCF-VPT outcome across multiple systematic reviews: necrosis risk rose from 0% (no luxation) to 14% (with periodontal/luxation damage) in one 103-tooth study, and up to 28% even for uncomplicated crown fractures with luxation; the highest reported VPT success rates (95-96%) come specifically from studies that **excluded** luxation injuries (*Vital Pulp Treatment*, Ch.8, p.191-192).

A genuinely novel strategy for severely periodontally compromised multi-rooted teeth, absent from Q3's current repair-material framing and worth citing here: pulpotomy (HCSC) followed 4 weeks later by vital **root resection** via periodontal microsurgery for furcation-involved maxillary molars — vitality/function maintained in **100%** of 15 molars at mean 4.9-year follow-up (Jepsen et al. 2020, cited Chopra's Ch.6, p.129-130), framed as advantageous over conventional RCT, which carries higher failure (root fracture) risk in periodontally compromised molars. A related combination strategy: conventional RCT in a necrotic root **while** pulpotomy is performed in a vital root **of the same multi-rooted tooth** (Koli et al. 2021, same chapter).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1 — the "exposed pulp is a lost organ" paradigm has been decisively overturned, but current evidence shows the newer paradigm needs its own honest caveats, not uncritical optimism.** EVIDENCE: Kakehashi et al. (1965) proved bacteria-free exposures heal without any capping material; modern HCSC-based direct capping/pulpotomy now achieve 80-96% success at short-to-medium follow-up, extending even to mature teeth with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis. INTERPRETATION: VPT is now a legitimate first-line strategy across a far wider range of presentations than the traditional reversible/irreversible dichotomy implied. LIMITATION: histological success is consistently lower than clinical success (66% vs up to 95%+ in the same literature), dentin bridges are frequently imperfect (89-90% show tunnel defects), and genuine long-term Ca(OH)2 data show failure rates (45% at 5yr, 80% at 10yr) in real tension with short-term success figures from the same evidence base. [Certain] on the overturned paradigm; [Likely], not [Certain], that short/medium-term success figures reliably predict true long-term, histologically-confirmed healing.

**Claim 2 — case selection (caries depth, pulpitis severity, contamination status) now drives strategy choice more than a single reversible/irreversible label.** EVIDENCE: the Caries Profunda (CP1-3), Bjørndal Class I/II, and Wolters severity-grade systems each map specific objective findings to a specific strategy, and irreversible pulpitis is demonstrably asymptomatic in 14-60% of cases — meaning symptom-based diagnosis alone is unreliable. INTERPRETATION: a modern VPT answer should structure case selection around these staged criteria, not a binary symptom check. LIMITATION: the AAE's complete-caries-removal position directly contradicts the ESE/Cochrane/ICCC selective-removal consensus, and the same monograph used for this answer reports an internal tension on this exact point — genuine, unresolved disagreement exists at the guideline level, not just between older and newer sources. [Likely], not [Certain], on which caries-removal strategy will ultimately prevail in guideline consensus.

**Claim 3 — regenerative endodontic procedures achieve clinically meaningful outcomes, but the tissue formed is repair, not true pulp-dentin regeneration, and this distinction matters for how "success" should be interpreted.** EVIDENCE: REP produces measurable root-length/wall-thickness gains and periapical healing comparable to apexification (83% vs 86%, not significant), but histology of extracted REP-treated teeth shows ectopic bone/cementum/PDL-like tissue rather than a true odontoblast layer in most cases, prompting the alternative term "guided endodontic repair." INTERPRETATION: REP's goals are better framed as a tiered hierarchy (symptom resolution → structural gain → sensibility restoration) than as a single pass/fail "regeneration" outcome. LIMITATION: persistent intracanal infection can cause biological failure even when the case looks radiographically successful, meaning reported success rates may overstate genuine long-term biological outcome in a way current follow-up protocols cannot fully detect. [Certain] on the repair-vs-regeneration histological finding; [Likely] on how much reported success rates should be discounted for this reason.

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes)**

### Introduction
Rebel's 1922 doctrine ("an exposed pulp is a lost organ") has been overturned by Kakehashi et al. (1965): bacteria, not exposure itself, necrotize the pulp. Modern vital pulp therapy (VPT) offers a graded set of strategies matched to caries depth/pulpitis severity, aiming for dentine repair/regeneration while preserving vitality.

### 1. Case-selection frameworks
Caries Profunda staging: CP1 (one-step selective removal) → CP2/deep caries (stepwise excavation) → CP3/extremely deep (pulpectomy; exposure inevitable). Bjørndal Class I (no contamination, standard protocol) vs Class II (contaminated, "enhanced protocol" — isolation, antibacterial lavage, magnification, HCSC). Wolters pulpitis grading: mild→capping; moderate→pulpotomy; severe→complete pulpotomy/extirpation. **Note**: AAE recommends complete caries removal; ESE/Cochrane/ICCC recommend selective/stepwise removal — a genuine, unresolved guideline-level contradiction.

### 2. Biological basis
Reactionary dentin (surviving odontoblasts upregulate, mild stimulus) vs reparative dentin (new odontoblast-like cells after death of originals, severe stimulus) — the central tertiary-dentin mechanism. Dentin matrix is a growth-factor reservoir (TGF-β, BMP) released by EDTA/Ca(OH)2/HCSC. Caution: dentin bridges show tunnel defects in ~90% of cases (two independent studies) — bridge presence ≠ reliable healing proof.

### 3. Indirect pulp capping / selective removal
One-step (immediate permanent restoration) or stepwise (two-visit, 6-12 month re-entry). HCSC now preferred over hard-setting Ca(OH)2. Swedish registry: 72% survival at 4 years (stepwise), age the only significant factor.

### 4. Direct pulp capping
Mechanical exposure only, hemostasis ≤10 min, HCSC ≥1.5mm. Modern evidence debunks the "1mm exposure size" and "location matters" rules. NaOCl 2.5% pulp-wound irrigation before MTA: 89% vs 55% survival (saline) at 1 year. Outcome by material/time: Biodentine 96%→86%, MTA 91%→81%, Ca(OH)2 74%→56% (6mo→4-5yr). Ca(OH)2 long-term: 45% failure at 5yr, 80% at 10yr (Barthel et al.) — real tension with its own short-term success data.

### 5. Pulpotomy
Partial (Cvek, ~2mm removed, 1.5mm empirical exposure threshold) vs full (entire coronal pulp, to orifice). Partial 95% vs direct capping 80% (young permanent teeth). Full pulpotomy now extended to MATURE teeth with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis + apical radiolucency — a genuine paradigm shift, avoiding pulpectomy. Enhanced protocol: magnification, re-disinfection after excavation, HCSC ≥2-3mm, temporary restoration ≤2 days.

### 6. Apexification vs apexogenesis
Apexification = barrier in NON-VITAL immature tooth (Ca(OH)2, months-years, OR single-visit MTA plug). Apexogenesis = continued development of a VITAL immature tooth after pulpotomy. MTA plug forms barrier faster than Ca(OH)2 (pooled MD −3.58 months) with comparable success.

### 7. Regenerative endodontic procedures (REP)
4 prerequisites: stem cells, disinfection, scaffold, signal. Two-visit protocol: NaOCl→EDTA→Ca(OH)2/dilute TAP (visit 1); EDTA rinse→induce bleeding (pre-bent Hedström file)→collagen matrix→HCSC (visit 2). Cvek stage 1-3 roots benefit most. **Critical nuance**: histology shows repair (ectopic bone/cementum), not true pulp regeneration — "guided endodontic repair" is the more accurate term. Outcome comparable to apexification (83% vs 86%, NS).

### 8. Emerging strategies
Cell-based ETE (ex vivo expansion + scaffold, not yet practical) vs cell-free ETE ("cell homing" — scaffold + signal molecules alone, recruits endogenous stem cells).

### Conclusion
Successful dentine repair/regeneration in VPT rests on matching a graded case-selection framework (caries depth, pulpitis severity, contamination status) to the correct strategy — indirect/direct capping, partial/full pulpotomy, apexification/apexogenesis, or regenerative endodontics — while recognising that "success" in this literature spans a spectrum from symptom resolution to (rarely achieved) true histological regeneration, and that real guideline-level and evidence-level contradictions (AAE vs ESE on caries removal; short- vs long-term Ca(OH)2 outcome) remain genuinely unresolved.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Confusing apexification (non-vital tooth, barrier induction) with apexogenesis (vital tooth, continued physiologic root development).
2. Presenting regenerative endodontic procedures as achieving true "pulp regeneration" rather than correctly identifying the histological evidence for biological repair (ectopic tissue, no true odontoblast layer).
3. Treating dentin-bridge formation as proof of successful healing, omitting the real tunnel-defect incidence data.
4. Citing only short-term Ca(OH)2 success figures without the real long-term failure data (45%/80% at 5/10 years) — a frequently tested "critically discuss" angle.
5. Applying the outdated "1mm exposure size" rule for direct pulp capping eligibility instead of the current duration-of-contamination criterion.

## MNEMONIC
"**C**aries depth decides, **C**ontamination decides, **S**ymptoms don't" — the three-part modern case-selection logic (Caries Profunda stage, Bjørndal contamination class) that has displaced the old symptom-only reversible/irreversible dichotomy.
