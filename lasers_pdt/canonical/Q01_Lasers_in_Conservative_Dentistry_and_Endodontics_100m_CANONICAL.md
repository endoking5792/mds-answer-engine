## 1. Introduction and history

### Explanation

**Definition.** Pandey: LASER is "a technology that can amplify and produce a highly directional, intense, monochromatic and coherent beam" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Pandey adds that "no one device can accomplish all the practical dental uses" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Seltzer and Bender's chapter makes the same point: "there is no one laser wavelength that can be utilized to deliver various modalities of dental care" (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC].

**Physics milestones (the books disagree on the Einstein year).**
| Event | Source and figure |
|---|---|
| Einstein's stimulated emission | "In 1916 Albert Einstein postulated the theory of lasers" (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; Grossman's chronology also gives 1916 (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Pandey's narrative says "In 1917, Einstein also predicted Zur Theorie der Strahlung" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Banday et al say "first established by Albert Einstein in 1917" (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC]; Sulewski reconciles them: "In 1916-1917, Einstein made his third important contribution to laser theory" (Sulewski, 2016) [SRC] |
| Maser | Grossman's chronology: 1953, Charles Townes (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] |
| The word "laser" | Gould's 1957 notebook, "the first time the term laser was used" (Sulewski, 2016) [SRC] |
| First laser | Ruby, Maiman, 1960 (Pandey, 2018; Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; Sulewski gives the day: "On May 16, 1960, Maiman and" D'Haenens (Sulewski, 2016) [SRC] |

**Dental milestones.** Stern and Sognnaes began ruby-laser studies on teeth in 1964; "In 1965, Goldman was the first to irradiate the surface of tooth intraorally"; "CO2 laser was the first laser to receive FDA clearance for oral use in 1976" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri: "The first lasers to be marketed for intraoral use were carbon dioxide lasers" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The first dedicated dental laser is dated differently: "The first laser specifically designed for dentistry was marketed in 1989" (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; Sulewski: "On May 3, 1990, the first laser designed specifically for general dentistry, the dLase 300 Nd:YAG laser" was introduced in the USA (Sulewski, 2016) [SRC]; Pandey has it commercially available in 1989 and "first sold in the UK in 1990" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. The hard-tissue breakthrough: "In 1989, experimental work by Keller and Hibst using a pulsed Er:YAG (2,940 nm) laser, demonstrated its effectiveness in cutting enamel, dentin and bone"; Er:YAG "received FDA clearance in the United States in 1997" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Endodontics: "The first laser used in endodontics was reported by Weichman and Johnson (1971)", an in vitro CO2 attempt to seal the apex (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; "The first laser pulpotomy was performed by Shoji et al in 1985" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. The newest hard-tissue wavelength: "In September 2013, the first CO2 laser emitting at 9300 nm for caries removal" reached the US market (Parker, 2016) [SRC].

**An early verdict worth quoting.** "In 1974, Stern concluded that unless heat-related structural changes and damage to dentinal tissues could be reduced, laser technology could not replace the conventional dental drill" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. The modern history of dental lasers is the story of solving that thermal problem with pulsed, water-absorbed wavelengths. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** LASER is an acronym for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation: a highly directional, intense, monochromatic, coherent beam (Pandey, 2018). In conservative dentistry and endodontics, lasers are used for diagnosis, caries removal and cavity preparation, surface modification, soft-tissue management, pulp therapy, canal disinfection and surgery. No single wavelength performs all tasks (Pandey, 2018; Goodis and Pashley, 2012); the wavelength must be matched to the target tissue.

**History**
- 1916-1917: Einstein described stimulated emission (1916: Coluzzi, 2017; Gopikrishna, 2021; 1917: Pandey, 2018; Banday et al, 2026; Sulewski, 2016 gives 1916-1917).
- 1953: Townes, MASER (Gopikrishna, 2021); 1957: Gould first used the word "laser" (Sulewski, 2016).
- 1960: Maiman, first (ruby) laser (Coluzzi, 2017).
- 1964-1965: Stern and Sognnaes, then Goldman, irradiated teeth; Goldman first lased a tooth intraorally (Pandey, 2018).
- 1971: Weichman and Johnson, first endodontic laser study (CO2, sealing the apex in vitro).
- 1974: Stern: lasers could not replace the drill until thermal damage was solved.
- 1976: CO2 laser first cleared by the FDA for oral use (Pandey, 2018).
- 1985: Shoji, first laser pulpotomy.
- 1989-1990: first dedicated dental laser, the pulsed Nd:YAG (1989: Coluzzi, 2017; 3 May 1990, dLase 300: Sulewski, 2016).
- 1989: Keller and Hibst, Er:YAG cuts enamel, dentine and bone; 1997: Er:YAG FDA clearance for caries removal and cavity preparation (Pandey, 2018).
- 2013: 9300 nm CO2 laser for hard tissue (Parker, 2016).

**Significance in modern dentistry.** Lasers support minimal intervention: selective removal of infected tissue, decontamination of cavities and canals, bloodless soft-tissue work beside restorations, and pain-reduced treatment for anxious patients and children. They also extend diagnosis (fluorescence caries detection, pulp blood-flow measurement) and add non-surgical therapies (photobiomodulation, photodynamic disinfection). Their place is adjunctive: each benefit must be weighed against cost, slower cutting and thermal risk (Banday et al, 2026; Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 2. Laser physics: components, stimulated emission and properties

### Explanation

**Stimulated emission and amplification.** Coluzzi explains Bohr's spontaneous emission (random direction and phase) and Einstein's addition: an extra photon meeting an excited atom releases two identical photons. Pandey: "The energy is emitted, radiated as 2 identical photons, traveling as a coherent wave" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Amplification needs population inversion, "meaning that a majority of atoms are in the elevated rather than the resting state", maintained by pumping (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC].

**Components.** All lasers share "an active medium, a pumping mechanism, and a resonator" (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; Pandey adds that "A cooling system, focusing lens completes the basic components" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. The resonator has a 100% reflecting rear mirror and a partially transmitting front mirror: "And it is this output coupler from which the laser bean [beam] emanates" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Pumping: diodes electrically, solid-state crystals by flash lamp, CO2 by AC/DC or radiofrequency current (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]. The laser is named after its active medium: gas (CO2, argon, He-Ne), solid-state crystal (YAG, YAP, YSGG doped with Nd, Er, Cr) or semiconductor diode (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC].

**Properties.** The books list them differently:
| Source | Properties listed |
|---|---|
| Coluzzi (2017) | "Laser energy is distinguished from ordinary light by two properties": monochromaticity and coherence (spatial and temporal); beam starts collimated but diverges from fibres [SRC] |
| Green, Weiss and Stern (2011) | "A laser light has three distinguishing properties": monochromatic, coherent, directional [SRC] |
| Grossman (Gopikrishna, 2021) | monochromatic, collimated, coherent, intense [SRC] |
| Pandey (2018) | "The most striking features of lasers are their:" coherence, monochromatic, brightness and intensity, directionality, collimation, focusability [SRC] |

Pandey's definitions: "Monochromatic refers to single wavelength (color) of laser beam"; "Collimation refers to parallel nature of laser beam" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. The clinical point of monochromaticity is that a single wavelength is absorbed selectively by a chromophore (Section 4). [Certain]

**Spectrum.** Dental lasers are non-ionising. Range: Coluzzi gives "emission wavelengths of approximately 0.45 microns, or 450 nanometers to 10.6 microns or 10,600 nanometers" (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; Pandey and Grossman give approximately 500 nm ("or 500 nm) to 10.6" μm) (Pandey, 2018; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The difference reflects the later 445 nm blue diode in Coluzzi's table. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** An atom absorbing energy moves to a higher orbit and returns, releasing a photon at random (spontaneous emission, Bohr). If a second photon of the same energy strikes the already excited atom, two identical photons are released, in phase, same direction and wavelength (stimulated emission, Einstein). When pumping keeps more atoms excited than at rest (population inversion), photons bounce between two mirrors, stimulating further emission: amplification (Coluzzi, 2017).

**Components of a laser** (draw the diagram)
1. Active (gain) medium: gas (CO2, argon, He-Ne), solid-state crystal (Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG) or semiconductor (diode). The laser is named after it.
2. Pumping mechanism: flash lamp, electrical current or radiofrequency.
3. Optical resonator: fully reflecting rear mirror and partially transmitting output coupler through which the beam leaves.
4. Cooling system, focusing lens, control panel, delivery system and foot switch (Pandey, 2018; Coluzzi, 2017).

**Properties of laser light**
- Monochromatic: single wavelength, so it is absorbed selectively by one chromophore.
- Coherent: waves in phase in space (spatial) and time (temporal).
- Collimated/directional: parallel, low-divergence beam that can be focused to a small spot.
- Intense (high brightness) (Pandey, 2018; Gopikrishna, 2021). Coluzzi (2017) lists only two defining properties, monochromaticity and coherence.

**Spectrum.** All dental lasers lie between about 450-500 nm and 10,600 nm, in the visible and infrared, non-ionising part of the spectrum (Coluzzi, 2017; Pandey, 2018).

## 3. Delivery systems, emission modes and operating parameters

### Explanation

**Delivery.** Pandey: articulated arm (mirrored knuckles; "Most commonly used with CO2 laser"), hollow waveguide, quartz optical fibre (argon, diode, Nd:YAG), and an air-cooled fibre system that "is unique to the erbium family of lasers", ending in quartz or sapphire tips (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Grossman gives two endodontic delivery types, hollow tube and glass fibre of 200-1000 μm (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Contact versus non-contact: Pandey warns that "The lasers should never be positioned with focal point deep or within the tissue (prefocused)" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Emission modes.** Coluzzi: "There are two natural modes of wavelength emission for dental lasers, based on the excitation source: continuous wave and free-running pulse", with gated pulsing as a subset of continuous wave; "Current dental lasers do not utilize Q switching" or mode-locking (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey lists more modes (chopped, gated, super-pulsed, ultra-pulsed, Q-switched, flash-lamp pulsing) (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Clinically: Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG are free-running pulsed; diode and older CO2 are continuous wave or gated (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC].

**Parameters.** Energy (J); "Power is the measurement of work completed over time and is measured in watts" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; "Fluence is a measurement of energy per area and is expressed as J/cm2" (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; "Fluence or energy density determines the magnitude of laser interaction" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; power density (W/cm2). Peak power = pulse energy / pulse duration: Coluzzi's example of a 100 mJ, 100 μs pulse "would have a peak power of 1000 W" (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]. Hand speed: "A slower speed will increase the power density because of the longer time the energy remains in the tissue" (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]. Poli gives the fall in fluence with tip distance and the energy thresholds for erbium ablation (Section 6) (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Delivery systems**
- Articulated arm: hollow tubes with mirrored joints; bulky, non-contact; CO2.
- Hollow waveguide: flexible tube with reflective lining; CO2, some erbium.
- Optical (quartz) fibre, 200-1000 μm: argon, KTP, diode, Nd:YAG; contact use, reaches pockets and canals.
- Air-cooled fibre/handpiece with sapphire or quartz tips: erbium lasers, used 1-2 mm from tissue (Pandey, 2018; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Contact mode: tactile feedback, focal point at the tip. Non-contact: beam focused at a distance; defocusing spreads energy for haemostasis. Never focus within tissue.

**Emission modes**
- Continuous wave: constant output (diode, KTP, older CO2); heat accumulates unless the operator pauses.
- Gated pulse: continuous beam interrupted by a shutter.
- Free-running pulse: microsecond pulses with long intervals, high peak but low average power (Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG); tissue cools between pulses (thermal relaxation).
- Q-switching and mode-locking produce nano- to femtosecond pulses but are not used in current dental lasers (Coluzzi, 2017).
- Clinical significance: shorter, higher-peak pulses with longer rest intervals ablate with less heat spread; continuous wave suits haemostasis but needs operator pauses to let tissue cool.

**Parameters**
- Energy (J, mJ per pulse); power (W = J per second); repetition rate (Hz).
- Peak power = pulse energy / pulse duration; average power is what the tissue experiences.
- Fluence (energy density, J/cm2) and power density (irradiance, W/cm2) decide the tissue effect; smaller spot or tip in contact increases them.
- Pulse duration, spot size, tip-to-tissue distance, water spray, exposure time and hand speed (Coluzzi, 2017; Pandey, 2018).

## 4. Laser–tissue interaction

### Explanation

**Four interactions.** Parker: "Incident photonic irradiation directed onto target tissue will behave in one of four ways: transmission, reflection, scatter, and absorption" (Parker, 2017) [SRC]; Pandey: "Laser light can have four different interactions with the target tissue", and the useful effect is "caused only by that portion of the light that is absorbed" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Grossman and Seltzer and Bender repeat the four (Gopikrishna, 2021; Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC]. Reflection is used by caries-detection devices but endangers the eye; transmission explains why diode and Nd:YAG "are transmitted through the lens, iris and cornea of eye and absorbed on the retina" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; scatter in deep tissue led Fisher to coin the acronym WYDSCHY ("What You Don't See Can Harm You"), "coined by Fisher in his 1993 paper" (Parker, 2017) [SRC].

**Chromophores and absorption.** Pandey: "The main absorbing components or chromophores (tissue compounds) of tissue are:" water, haemoglobin, melanin, protein (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Short wavelengths (argon, diode, Nd:YAG) are absorbed by pigment; long wavelengths by water and hydroxyapatite. "The largest absorption peak for water is just below 3000 nm which is at Er:YAG wavelength" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Parker: water absorption peaks "at approximately 3000 and 10,600 nm" (Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Erbium "acts mainly on the surface, with an absorption depth of approximately 0.01 mm" whereas 800 nm diodes penetrate far deeper (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. The 9.3-9.6 μm CO2 lines target the phosphate of apatite; at 9.3 and 9.6 μm "the enamel absorption coefficient is ten times higher" than at 10.6 μm (Rechmann, 2017) [SRC].

**Tissue effects.**
- Photothermal (dominant): Parker calls the destructive thermal interaction "a process termed photothermolysis" and the non-destructive stimulatory one photobiomodulation (Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey's Table 4.1 grades heating: about 60 °C protein denatures and "The tissue whitens or blanches"; at 100 °C "vaporization of water occurs, also known as ablation"; above about 200 °C carbonisation, when "the surface carbonized layers absorb the incident beam, becoming a heat sink" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- In hard tissue, interstitial water explodes: "This micro-explosion of apatite crystal is termed" spallation (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; erbium cutting is photomechanical, a fast shock wave from water expansion: "This process is called cavitation" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Photochemical: "Photochemical effects cause target cells to start light-induced chemical reactions" (composite curing, photo-activated disinfection, bleaching) (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Photoacoustic: shock waves that pulverise tissue, "This is an example of photo-acoustic effect of laser light" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Photobiomodulation (sub-surgical) and fluorescence (diagnostic) (Pandey, 2018; Parker, 2017) [SRC].

**Thermal relaxation.** Thermal relaxation time is "the time taken for the irradiated tissue to dissipate about 63% of the incident thermal energy" (Parker, 2017) [SRC]; it is available with free-running pulses and "impossible to deliver with continuous wave emission modes" (Parker, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Four interactions of laser light with tissue** (Parker, 2017; Pandey, 2018)
1. Reflection: no tissue effect; used in caries detection; hazard to eyes.
2. Transmission: passes through without effect (e.g. near-infrared through water and the lens of the eye to the retina).
3. Scattering: weakens the beam and spreads heat beyond the target, causing hidden deep damage.
4. Absorption: the only interaction that produces a therapeutic effect.

**Chromophores.** Water, haemoglobin, melanin, protein and hydroxyapatite. Short wavelengths (argon, KTP, diode, Nd:YAG) are absorbed by pigment and penetrate deeply; long wavelengths (erbium, CO2) are absorbed by water and hydroxyapatite at the surface. Water absorption peaks near 3000 nm (Er:YAG) and 10,600 nm (CO2); the 9300-9600 nm CO2 lines target the phosphate of enamel apatite (Pandey, 2018; Parker, 2017; Rechmann, 2017).

**Effects of absorbed energy**
- Photothermal (photothermolysis): about 37-50 °C hyperthermia; about 60 °C protein denaturation, coagulation and haemostasis; 100 °C vaporisation (ablation); above about 200 °C carbonisation, after which char absorbs all energy and overheats tissue (Pandey, 2018).
- Photomechanical/photoacoustic: in enamel and dentine, water in the crystals boils explosively and ejects apatite (spallation); shock waves and cavitation in fluids, the basis of erbium cutting and laser-activated irrigation.
- Photochemical: light starts chemical reactions: composite curing, photo-activated disinfection, bleaching.
- Photobiomodulation: low-level energy alters cell metabolism (healing, analgesia).
- Fluorescence: tissue re-emits light; used to detect caries and bacteria.

**Variables governing the interaction** (Pandey, 2018): wavelength; tissue composition (water, pigment, mineral; enamel is the driest tissue, caries and soft tissue the wettest); power, pulse energy and emission mode; spot size and contact or non-contact delivery; exposure time and hand speed; water spray; and operator experience. The interaction is maximised by matching wavelength and power to the chromophore.

**Thermal relaxation.** The time for tissue to lose about 63% of its heat; pulsed emission with intervals longer than this, plus water spray, prevents heat build-up; continuous wave cannot provide it (Parker, 2017).

**Hypersensitivity and bleaching**: answered in their own topics; do not expand here.

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Caries detection.** QLF (blue-violet light; demineralised enamel loses fluorescence and looks dark), DIAGNOdent (655 nm red diode; bacterial porphyrins in caries fluoresce more; numerical reading 0-99), argon illumination (caries appears dark orange-red), and research tools such as OCT and terahertz imaging (Pandey, 2018). Adjuncts to visual-radiographic examination.

**2. Caries removal and cavity preparation** (Er:YAG 2940 nm, Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm, 9300 nm CO2).
- Mechanism: laser energy absorbed by water causes micro-explosions that eject apatite (photomechanical ablation) under water spray.
- Selective: carious dentine contains more water and ablates at lower energy than sound tissue (Poli, 2017; Parker, 2016).
- Surface: no smear layer, open tubules, no cracks or charring, bacteria killed below the surface (Poli, 2017).
- Comfort: no vibration, less noise, often no injected anaesthesia (68% of patients felt no pain in one report) (Pandey, 2018).
- Technique: non-contact sapphire/quartz tip about 1 mm from tooth under air-water spray (at least 8 ml/min); start with low energy for analgesia, raise for enamel, lower for dentine and caries (roughly 100-150 mJ per pulse for caries and primary dentine, more for permanent enamel); the sound of ablation changes as caries is removed; finish margins by hand or fine bur (Poli, 2017).
- Limits: slower in enamel (Poli, 2017: under 30% of bur speed; Parker, 2016: 80% slower in enamel, comparable in dentine); best for small cavities; cannot remove amalgam, gold or ceramic; amalgam must not be lased (Parker, 2016; Banday et al, 2026).

**3. Laser conditioning ("etching").** Sub-ablative erbium irradiation roughens enamel and dentine, but bond strength to lased tooth is controversial; phosphoric acid etching after lasing is recommended (Poli, 2017).

**4. Caries prevention.** CO2 (9.3-9.6 μm) heats enamel, drives out carbonate and makes it more acid resistant; in vivo, 46% less demineralisation around orthodontic brackets and fewer fissure lesions with fluoride varnish (Rechmann, 2017).

**5. Laser analgesia.** Pulsed Nd:YAG and erbium irradiation at sub-ablative settings blocks A-fibre transmission; lasts about 15 minutes (Poli, 2017); results inconsistent and partly placebo (Parker, 2016).

**6. Curing of composites.** Argon 488 nm activates camphorquinone; faster cure, but bulky, costly units and conflicting marginal-seal data (Pandey, 2018).

**7. Soft-tissue management.** Diode, Nd:YAG or erbium for gingival troughing before impressions, gingivoplasty and crown lengthening in the same visit as the restoration, and haemostasis (Poli, 2017; Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011).

**8. Dentin hypersensitivity and bleaching.** Nd:YAG, diode, erbium and CO2 seal tubules or reduce nerve response; KTP and diode activate bleaching gels (detailed in their own topics).

**9. Photobiomodulation (low-level laser therapy).** Red (about 630-660 nm) and near-infrared (about 800-900 nm) low-power diodes, below the surgical threshold, are absorbed by cytochrome c oxidase in mitochondria; nitric oxide is released, ATP rises and cell activity changes. Effects: analgesia, reduced inflammation, faster healing and dentinogenesis. The response is biphasic (Arndt-Schulz law): low doses stimulate, high doses inhibit. Uses: dentin hypersensitivity, post-operative pain after restorative and endodontic treatment, healing after pulpotomy and periapical surgery. It has no bactericidal effect, and the clinical evidence is still limited (Romagnoli and Cafaro, 2017; Tunér et al, 2016).

**10. Others.** Removal of composite, femtosecond (crack-free) ablation and dye-assisted ablation are experimental (Pandey, 2018).

## 7. Applications in endodontics

### Explanation

Pandey's list: "Pulpal diagnosis Pulp capping/pulpotomy Cleaning and disinfecting of root-canal system", obturation, retreatment and apical surgery (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

Fluorescence diagnosis of infected canals: "Fluorescence readings were significantly higher in infected canals" than in sound dentine, in extracted teeth (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Context: conventional pulp capping "ranges from 44% to 97%" (Stabholz, Sahar-Helft and Moshonov, 2016) [SRC].

**Canal cleaning, shaping and disinfection.** Rationale: after NiTi preparation "more than 35%" of canal "surface area remained unchanged after instrumentation" (Stabholz, Sahar-Helft and Moshonov, 2016) [SRC]; NaOCl "penetrates only 130" μm into tubules (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Shaping with lasers: "it is difficult to attain all of the mechanical objectives of root canal preparation when laser energy is delivered with conventional optical fibers" (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; dry erbium use can cause "over-enlarging of the coronal section, apical transportation, perforation, and root canal ledging" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Direct (photothermal) disinfection: fibre "positioned into the canal, 1 mm shorter than the working length, irradiating while withdrawing the fiber" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Near-infrared lasers "penetrate to a large extent through dentinal tubules and are absorbed by the bacteria pigments", whereas erbium's "bactericidal effect, via photothermal energy, is more superficial" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; George and Walsh: near-infrared "may allow a bactericidal effect beyond 1 mm of dentin"; "Middle-infrared lasers will show the lowest penetration" (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Numbers (all laboratory): Nd:YAG "a bacterial reduction of three log steps at 1 mm"; erbium (Moritz) "an almost total eradication of 99.64% of bacteria at 1.5 W" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. "Gram-negative bacteria, due to the structural characteristics of the different cell walls, are more easily destroyed" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Smear layer: Takeda: "All lasers achieved better smear layer removal than EDTA, and the Er:YAG laser was the most effective of the three lasers used" (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] (in vitro).
- Photo-activated disinfection (Q06): photosensitiser plus red light "leads to the production of singlet oxygen, which causes the bacterial cell wall to rupture killing the bacteria"; dye "left in situ for a fixed period of time (60 seconds)" then "irradiation carried out for 120 seconds"; Pandey notes "There is a consistent lack of in vivo research" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. George and Walsh: ROS "damage the microbial cell membrane"; "To date, 12 studies have reported PAD as being effective in eliminating Enterococcus faecalis"; ICG with 810 nm "is a type of photothermal disinfection process and is not a photodynamic process" (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Tunér et al: "PBM does not have a bactericidal effect" (Tunér et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Pulp diagnosis.** Laser Doppler flowmetry: a low-power He-Ne or diode beam is reflected by moving red cells, giving an objective, painless measure of pulpal blood flow; useful in traumatised and immature teeth. Limits: cost, need for a stabilising splint, signal from gingival blood, light blocked by amalgam and crowns (Pandey, 2018; George and Walsh, 2017; Banday et al, 2026). Laser fluorescence can detect bacteria in canals (laboratory stage).

**2. Pulp capping and pulpotomy.** CO2, Nd:YAG, erbium and diode lasers give haemostasis, a sterile surface and a thin coagulated layer under the capping agent, supporting dentine bridging (Pandey, 2018). Laser is an adjunct: the capping material and an immediate seal are still essential (Banday et al, 2026). Clinical reports are favourable (97% clinical success with Nd:YAG pulpotomy of primary molars; about 90% versus 60% for laser-assisted versus conventional capping), but long-term trials are lacking (Gopikrishna, 2021; Alshahrani, Beebe and Chogle, 2016).

**3. Access and canal preparation.** Er:YAG and Er,Cr:YSGG can cut access and enlarge canals but cannot reliably shape curved canals: ledges, transportation and perforation, so rotary/hand shaping remains standard (George and Walsh, 2017; Pandey, 2018).

**4. Canal disinfection** (after conventional shaping to about ISO 30-40)
- Photothermal: diode or Nd:YAG fibre (200 μm) inserted 1 mm short of working length and withdrawn in a helical motion; near-infrared penetrates and kills bacteria deep in tubules, beyond 1 mm; erbium acts superficially (Pandey, 2018; George and Walsh, 2017).
- Photo-activated disinfection (PAD/aPDT): tolonium chloride or methylene blue left for about 60 seconds, then activated by red light for about 120 seconds; singlet oxygen ruptures bacterial membranes, including E. faecalis (Pandey, 2018).
- Laser-activated irrigation: erbium pulses in NaOCl or EDTA create cavitation bubbles and streaming that remove smear layer and biofilm. PIPS uses under 20 mJ, 50 μs pulses with a stationary tip in the pulp chamber; SWEEPS pairs the pulses (George and Walsh, 2023). Extrusion is no greater than with needle irrigation.

**5. Smear layer removal.** Er:YAG removes smear layer better than EDTA alone in vitro (George and Walsh, 2017).

**6. Obturation.** Argon, CO2 or Nd:YAG can soften gutta-percha, but root-surface temperature rises (Nd:YAG about 14.4 °C) threaten the PDL; clinical evidence is insufficient (Stabholz, Sahar-Helft and Moshonov, 2016).

**7. Retreatment.** Nd:YAG/Nd:YAP soften gutta-percha and loosen files; erbium activation of irrigants clears remnants, but lasers alone cannot clean the canal and can ledge it (Stabholz, Sahar-Helft and Moshonov, 2016; Banday et al, 2026).

**8. Endodontic surgery.** Er:YAG/Er,Cr:YSGG osteotomy, root resection and root-end preparation without charring; CO2/Nd:YAG seal resected dentine; haemostasis and sterilisation of the field; diode PBM for healing (Pandey, 2018); laser ablation of invasive cervical resorption (George and Walsh, 2017).

**9. Analgesia and PBM.** Pre-emptive analgesia and faster periapical healing (George and Walsh, 2017).

| Technique | Laser | Tip position | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct irradiation | diode, Nd:YAG, erbium | 1 mm short of WL, withdrawn helically in a dry or wet canal | photothermal kill; near-IR deep, erbium superficial |

## 8. Effects on pulp and dentine; laser safety

### Explanation

**Pulp temperature thresholds (contested, see synthesis claim 3).**
| Source | Threshold |
|---|---|
| George and Walsh (2017, and unchanged 2023) citing Zach and Cohen | "intra-pulpal temperature increase of approximately 5.5 °C can promote necrosis of the dental pulp in 15% of cases", 11 and 17 °C in 60% and 100% [SRC] |
| Parker (2017) | "temperature increments above 5.6 °C can be considered potentially threatening"; "increments in excess of 16 °C can result in complete pulpal necrosis" [SRC] |
| Goodis and Pashley (2012), Seltzer and Bender | Zach and Cohen "found that a pulpal temperature of 40.5°C produced pulpal necrosis in 60% of the tested teeth"; "the rate of delivery of heat is probably more important than the absolute rise in pulpal temperature" [SRC] |
| Pandey (2018), CO2 caries-prevention work | pulp kept "at a safe level of less than 4°C" [SRC] |
| Poli (2017) / Parker (2016) | erbium with spray: "below 5 °C", usually "1–2 °C"; "less than 5° C" [SRC] |
| Sikri (2019) | "Temperature elevation of 21°C above normal body temperature can produce cell destruction" [SRC] |
| Root surface/PDL: George and Walsh (2017); Banday et al (2026) | "5.5–7 °C" limit; "A threshold temperature increase of 7 °C is commonly considered as the highest thermal change which is biologically acceptable"; Banday: up to 5 °C safe, "with a maximum permissible rise of 7" °C [SRC] |

Pandey has an internal discrepancy: one passage gives a pulp-chamber rise "about 4.3°C (Rechmann et al, 1988)", another, for apparently the same early Er:YAG work, "the mean rise in temperature in the pulpal cavity was 43°C" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; 43 is almost certainly a dropped decimal point. [Likely] Write 4.3 °C.

Water spray: "the use of an air-water spray provides pulpal protection equivalent to that of the common dental drill" (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC]; Glockner: with erbium and spray "a temperature reduction occur after a few seconds from 37 to 25 °C" (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; Poli: at least 8 ml/min water ("The minimum amount of water which should be used is of at least 8 ml/min") (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Seltzer and Bender's comparison: a 4.7 °C pulpal rise with a high-speed bur across 1 mm dentine was similar to 0.7 W Nd:YAG ("They obtained a 4.7°C rise in pulpal temperature using a high-speed bur across 1.0 mm of RDT") (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC]. Erbium pulp histology: Parker: "pulpal temperature rise is minimal when erbium laser wavelengths are employed in cavity preparation" (Parker, 2017) [SRC]; 9.6 μm CO2 "can be safely used ... without permanently damaging the dental pulp" in third molars examined histologically (Rechmann, 2017) [SRC]; 9300 nm "has been shown to be safe for pulpal tissues" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Conventional CO2 "creates severe thermal damage to dentin" (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC].

**Dentine.** Erbium: no smear layer, open tubules, micro-rough surface; collagen may melt (Section 6). Near-infrared in dry canals melts and recrystallises dentine and partly closes tubules (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Safety.** Retinal hazard: "Retina is approximately 100,000 times more vulnerable to injury than skin" in the 400-1400 nm range (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Eyewear: wavelength specific, rated by "optical density (OD)"; "Eye protection for all persons within the controlled area is mandatory with all Class IV lasers" (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; under the microscope, filters are needed except for wavelengths over 2000 nm ("With wavelengths longer than 2000 nm, this is not needed") (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Plume: "The plume is composed of 95% water and 5% particulate matter", with viruses and chemicals; high-volume evacuation and fine-filtration masks (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Reflection: avoid metal matrices ("It would be better instead not to use metal matrixes for" protecting adjacent teeth) (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Laser safety officer: "An LSO is a designated trained person who directs laser safety practices" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Fire: Class 4 (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; oxygen-enriched atmosphere ignition (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Effect on the pulp**
- Heat is the main risk. Commonly quoted thresholds (Zach and Cohen): a rise of about 5.5 °C causes necrosis in 15% of pulps; 11 °C in 60%; 17 °C in 100% (George and Walsh, 2017). Parker (2017) quotes 5.6 °C and 16 °C. The rate of heating may matter more than the absolute rise (Goodis and Pashley, 2012).
- Erbium lasers with adequate water spray keep the pulp rise below 5 °C, usually 1-2 °C, and the spray can even cool the pulp (Poli, 2017; George and Walsh, 2017). Histology after erbium and short-pulsed CO2 preparation shows no lasting pulp injury (Rechmann, 2017).
- Nd:YAG and diode penetrate dentine and can denature pulp through the crown or root; conventional CW CO2 carbonises and cracks dentine (Pandey, 2018; Goodis and Pashley, 2012).
- Root surface/PDL: keep the rise below 5-7 °C; pulsed modes, rest intervals and fresh irrigant reduce heating (George and Walsh, 2017; Banday et al, 2026).

- Pulp healing: accidental exposure during erbium preparation heals with a dentine bridge and reparative dentine (Pandey, 2018).

**Effect on dentine.** Erbium: no smear layer, open tubules, micro-rough surface, superficial collagen denaturation. Nd:YAG/diode in a dry canal: melting and recrystallisation, partial tubule sealing, smear layer only partly removed (Pandey, 2018).

**Laser safety**
1. Eye: wavelength-specific glasses of correct optical density for patient, operator and assistant; retina at risk from 400-1400 nm lasers, cornea from erbium and CO2; microscope filters.
2. Controlled area: warning signs, closed door, key switch, standby mode, laser safety officer.
3. Reflection: avoid shiny and metal instruments (e.g. metal matrices), use cellulose strips.
4. Plume: high-volume evacuation, high-filtration masks (plume carries bacteria, viruses and chemicals).
5. Fire: no oxygen-rich atmosphere or flammable materials near Class 4 beams.
6. Tissue: lowest effective energy, pulsed mode, water spray, rubber dam, never lase amalgam or metal (Pandey, 2018; Parker and Parker, 2017; Poli, 2017).

## 9. Advantages, limitations and future

### Explanation

**Advantages claimed.** Poli's Table 8.1 lists minimal invasion, "Reduced or no need for local anesthesia", selective caries removal, decontamination, no smear layer and soft-tissue procedures at the same visit (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; Pandey's comparison table and Banday et al echo these (Pandey, 2018; Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

**Limitations.** Learning curve: "the operator has to go through a learning curve because the use of these wavelengths is neither intuitive nor immediate" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; cost and multiple wavelengths: "This could be attributed to the high cost of technology and the need for more than one laser frequency" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; erbium lased surfaces are not suitable for porcelain veneers ("it is considered impractical to use such a surface to place indirect ceramic veneers") (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; uneven canal irradiation: "It is almost impossible to obtain uniform coverage of the canal surface using a laser" (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC]; Banday et al: the tip's directional emission "makes achieving uniform 360-degree coverage of canal walls challenging" (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

**Future.** Pandey predicts "Ultimately, laser technology will replace the air turbine for composite restorations" and that "within the next 5 years a small hand-held blue diode laser will replace all current curing systems" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. These are predictions, not evidence; for endodontics Pandey concedes "no system has been built to meet all the previously stated requirements" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Banday et al note "no significant technological innovations or new laser device inventions have been identified beyond the year 1999" within the scope of their review (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC], a curious statement given the 9300 nm CO2 laser (2013) and SWEEPS described in the laser texts. [Likely: their statement reflects the narrow scope of their history section] Directions in the books: 9300 nm CO2 for cutting and caries prevention (Rechmann, 2017) [SRC], ultrashort pulses (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], SWEEPS (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC], fluorescence-guided disinfection (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages**
- Minimally invasive, selective removal of caries; conservation of tooth structure.
- Little vibration and noise; often no injected anaesthesia; well accepted by children and anxious patients.
- Sterilises as it cuts; no smear layer; reaches tubules and canal irregularities that files miss.
- Haemostasis and soft-tissue surgery in the same visit; less postoperative pain.
- Multiple uses: diagnosis, PBM, PDT (Poli, 2017; Banday et al, 2026).

**Limitations**
- High cost; one wavelength cannot do everything; learning curve (Gopikrishna, 2021; Poli, 2017).
- Slow on enamel; no defined margins for indirect restorations; cannot remove amalgam, gold or ceramic.
- Bonding to lased surfaces unpredictable without acid etching.
- Heat risk to pulp and PDL; no tactile feedback in non-contact mode.
- In canals: straight fibres, forward emission, uneven irradiation, ledging and perforation; risk near the apex.
- Eye, plume and fire hazards; most endodontic evidence is laboratory based.

**Bur versus erbium laser** (Poli, 2017; Pandey, 2018)
| Feature | Bur | Erbium laser |
|---|---|---|
| Selective caries removal | no | yes |
| Smear layer | produced | none |
| Pulp temperature rise | can exceed 15 °C | under 5 °C with spray |
| Vibration/noise | present, about 120 dB | no vibration, quieter |
| Enamel cutting speed | fast | much slower |
| Defined margins, removing metal | yes | no |
| Bactericidal action | none | surface decontamination |

**Future.** Short-pulsed 9300 nm CO2 lasers for hard and soft tissue and caries prevention; ultrashort (femtosecond) pulses for crack-free cutting; side-firing and conical tips; SWEEPS; fluorescence-guided endodontic disinfection; smaller diode-pumped and combined-wavelength units (Rechmann, 2017; George and Walsh, 2023; Pandey, 2018).

## 10. Conclusion

### Explanation

Grossman's balanced view: laser irradiation kills microorganisms and "can act as an adjunct to the traditional means of shaping and cleaning" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. George and Walsh are more enthusiastic ("laser-based methods can provide not only equivalent but now superior results") (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC], as are Green, Weiss and Stern, who list "decrease in number of endodontic failures" without trial data (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011) [SRC]. An examiner rewards the adjunct position backed by the evidence grading below. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** Lasers have moved from Stern's 1974 verdict that heat made them unusable to pulsed erbium and short-pulsed CO2 systems that cut teeth safely under water spray. In conservative dentistry and endodontics they are valuable adjuncts for selective caries removal, soft-tissue management, pulp therapy, disinfection and irrigant activation, but not replacements for burs, files and irrigants. Correct wavelength, minimum effective energy, water cooling and strict eye safety are essential, and long-term clinical trials are still needed (Gopikrishna, 2021; Banday et al, 2026).

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim 1: "Composites bond as well, or better, to laser-prepared (laser-'etched') tooth."**
*Textbook says:* Parker: the rough lased surface "together with an acid-etch technique, permits a strong bond" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Banday et al: "The integration of Er:YAG laser etching with conventional acid etching has been shown to improve tooth-resin adhesion and bond strength compared to acid etching alone" (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Poli: "adhesion values came out much lower than those obtainable with the acid", attributed to "the collapse and/or melting of collagen fiber network during laser excision"; he advises phosphoric acid etching after lasing, not over 30 s on enamel and 15 s on lased dentine (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. The 2nd ed: "most of the literature shows a poor bond obtained after laser conditioning as compared to acid etching", there was "no value-added benefit of laser conditioning of the enamel after laser preparation", and the dentine protocol "is still inconclusive" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].
*Reconciliation:* all sources agree that laser alone is inferior and acid etching must follow; they disagree on whether laser plus acid beats acid alone. [Likely]
*Strength:* bond-strength and microleakage data are in vitro; no clinical survival trial was read. [Certain about type]
*Clinical implication:* after laser preparation, remove unsupported enamel, etch with phosphoric acid and bond conventionally; do not rely on laser "etching".

**Claim 2: "Laser disinfection gives superior endodontic results."**
*Textbook says:* George and Walsh: "laser-based methods can provide not only equivalent but now superior results" (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; Green, Weiss and Stern: lasers are superior in removing microorganisms and smear layer and decrease endodontic failures (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011) [SRC]; Stabholz reports Peters' study "with the laser treatment the most effective modality" (Stabholz, Sahar-Helft and Moshonov, 2016) [SRC].
*Challenge:* the kill figures (three-log Nd:YAG reduction, 99.64% erbium, 99.5% PIPS with NaOCl) come from extracted teeth and inoculated canals (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; activation of water alone gave only 73% reduction, so NaOCl does most of the work (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Pedulla's group found no difference between PIPS and needle NaOCl (Alshahrani, Beebe and Chogle, 2016) [SRC]; Pandey's PIPS-versus-ultrasonic fluid data are "as-yet-unpublished" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Grossman cites Goodis's limitations of forward-firing fibres and calls lasers an adjunct (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; for PAD "There is a consistent lack of in vivo research" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
*Strength:* predominantly in vitro and ex vivo; no healing-outcome trial was read in any source. [Certain about type]
*Conclusion:* laser activation enhances irrigant action in the laboratory; claims of fewer clinical failures are unproven. Write "adjunct".

**Claim 3: "The pulp tolerates up to 5.5 °C."**
*Textbook says:* 5.5 °C (George and Walsh, 2017, 2023) [SRC]; 5.6 °C (Parker, 2017) [SRC]; below 5 °C as the safe working target (Poli, 2017; Parker, 2016) [SRC]; less than 4 °C in Featherstone's CO2 design (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
*Challenge:* these all trace to Zach and Cohen's monkey study, which Seltzer and Bender's chapter calls crude, adding that "the rate of delivery of heat is probably more important than the absolute rise in pulpal temperature" (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC]; their own rendering of the same data ("a pulpal temperature of 40.5°C produced pulpal necrosis in 60%") does not match the 11 °C/60% figure in the laser texts. [Guessing: different expression of the same data or a transcription difference; verify against the printed book] Most laser temperature data are thermocouple studies in extracted teeth without pulpal blood flow.
*Conclusion:* quote 5.5 °C as a conservative ceiling, not a biological constant; protection comes from pulsed emission, water spray and low energy. [Likely]

**Claim 4: "Low-level laser therapy / PBM reliably heals and relieves pain."**
*Textbook says:* mechanism via cytochrome c oxidase and NO dissociation (Romagnoli and Cafaro, 2017; Tunér et al, 2016) [SRC]; Banday et al list RCT and review support for pain modulation (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Tunér et al: "most of the literature is still not at an evidence-based level, except for a few indications" (Tunér et al, 2016) [SRC]; Pandey: "few represent good evidence-based research" and soft laser "as a therapeutic tool is thus a contentious issue", with placebo responses in hypersensitivity trials (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; doses are inconsistent: Convissar's "Therapeutic window is therefore between 0.01 and 10 J/cm2" (Tunér et al, 2016) [SRC], while Pandey writes clinical fluences of "2–10 mJ/cm2" in one line and J/cm2 in the next (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], a unit error that shows how hard dosimetry is.
*Conclusion:* biologically plausible, biphasic and dose dependent; clinical efficacy for any single dental indication remains uncertain. [Likely]

## High-yield points
- Dental lasers: argon 488/514, KTP 532, diode 810-980, Nd:YAG 1064, Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 (Grossman 2790), Er:YAG 2940, CO2 9300/10,600 nm.
- Only three cut hard tissue: Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG, 9300 nm CO2 (Poli, 2017).
- Four interactions (reflection, transmission, scatter, absorption); only absorption acts; chromophore matching.
- Einstein 1916 (Coluzzi, Grossman) or 1917 (Pandey, Banday); Maiman ruby 1960; first dental laser 1989 or 1990 (dLase 300).
- Pulp: 5.5 °C (Zach and Cohen) / 5.6 °C (Parker); erbium with spray 1-2 °C; PDL limit 7 °C.
- Laser "etching" is laser conditioning; always acid etch after.
- PIPS: under 20 mJ, 50 μs, stationary tip in chamber; SWEEPS paired pulses (2nd ed).
- Endodontic laser evidence is mostly in vitro: adjunct, not replacement.

## Common exam mistakes
- Saying Nd:YAG or 10,600 nm CO2 lasers are used for cavity preparation; they melt and crack enamel and heat the pulp.
- Writing that lasers remove amalgam or that lased enamel needs no acid etching.
- Presenting in vitro bacterial-kill percentages as clinical success rates.
- Giving one pulp threshold as fact without its source (5.5 °C Zach and Cohen) or confusing pulp and PDL limits.
- Omitting laser safety (eyewear, plume, reflection, fire) from a 100-mark answer.
