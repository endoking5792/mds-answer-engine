## 1. Introduction and history

### Explanation

Banday and co-workers trace the theory to Einstein: it was "first established by Albert Einstein in 1917", and the first "functional laser was developed in 1960 by Theodore H. Maiman" using ruby (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC]. Parker's restorative chapter frames the clinical problem that lasers try to solve: removing caries selectively while preserving sound tissue and pulp vitality, without the fear of the drill; he notes that the first laser marketed for the general dentist (the Nd:YAG) "did not become commercially available until 1989", and that this wavelength had little action on hard tissue (Parker, 2016) [SRC].

The history of erbium cutting is reported differently in each book, and the student should know the disagreement rather than pick one date:
- Mount: the Er:YAG with water mist "was first characterized in detail by Raimund Hibst at the University of Ulm in Germany in the mid-1980s" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC].
- Pandey: "In 1988, Paghdiwala in the United States tested for the first time the ability of the Er:YAG laser to ablate dental hard tissues", and "Cavity preparation by Er:YAG laser was first reported by Keller et al in 1988" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Parker in Convissar: "In the mid-1990s, the search for a more suitable laser wavelength by Keller and Hibst" led to the Er:YAG (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. His own reference list cites Keller and Hibst papers from 1991 and 1993, so "mid-1990s" probably refers to commercial development rather than first laboratory work [Likely].
- Regulatory dates (Pandey): "In May 1997, the Er:YAG (2.94 μm) laser was cleared for marketing by the US Food and Drug Administration", and the Er,Cr:YSGG "was approved for ablation of hard as well as soft tissues by FDA in 1998" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** LASER stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. The concept of stimulated emission was proposed by Einstein in 1917 and Maiman built the first working (ruby) laser in 1960 (Banday et al, 2026). Conservative dentistry needs a tool that removes caries selectively, spares sound tissue and pulp, and reduces the fear, noise, vibration and injections associated with the drill. Lasers address these aims for diagnosis, prevention, cavity preparation, adhesion, curing, soft tissue management and therapy.

**Milestones**
- 1960s-1970s: ruby, Nd:YAG and CO2 lasers on teeth caused cracking, charring and pulpal heating (Pandey, 2018).
- 1989: first dental laser (Nd:YAG) marketed for general dentists; poor hard tissue action (Parker, 2016).
- Erbium cutting with water spray: Hibst, Ulm, mid-1980s (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016); Keller et al and Paghdiwala, 1988 (Pandey, 2018); Parker (2016) places Keller and Hibst's work in the mid-1990s.
- 1997: FDA clearance of Er:YAG; 1998: Er,Cr:YSGG (Pandey, 2018).
- 2013: 9300 nm CO2 laser for caries removal marketed in the USA (Parker, 2016).

**Applications in conservative dentistry (scope of the answer)**
1. Diagnostic: laser fluorescence (DIAGNOdent), QLF, optical coherence tomography.
2. Preventive: increasing acid resistance of enamel and fissures.
3. Operative: caries removal, cavity preparation, removal of composite and sealants, conditioning of enamel and dentine, bevelling of margins.
4. Restorative adjuncts: curing of composite (argon), troughing and gingival management, crown lengthening.
5. Therapeutic: laser analgesia, dentin hypersensitivity, bleaching, photobiomodulation, pulp capping and haemostasis of exposures.

## 2. Laser basics: properties, components, classification and emission

### Explanation

Green, Weiss and Stern state that "A laser light has three distinguishing properties": it is monochromatic, coherent and directional (collimated), and that a laser has three components: "These components consist of an energy source, an active medium, and a resonant chamber", the chamber holding one fully and one partially reflecting mirror (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011) [SRC]. Banday and co-workers summarise that, because all photons arise by stimulated emission, "the light produced will have 2 principal characteristics: monochromatic and coherent" (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC]; collimation is listed by Green but not by Banday, which is a difference in emphasis rather than a contradiction [Certain].

Banday's classification is by wavelength (UV, visible, infrared), "By Lasing Medium: Gas (CO2), Solid-state" (Nd:YAG, Er:YAG) and semiconductor (diode), by mode (continuous or pulsed) and by hazard class I-IV (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC]. On emission mode, Green notes that pulsing "prevents deeper tissue penetration and minimizes heat buildup" (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011) [SRC]. Poli explains the erbium mode: "Erbium lasers operate by free-running pulse (FRP)", with power given by "W = J × Hz", and the shorter the pulse, the less energy is converted into heat (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Properties of laser light:** monochromatic (one wavelength), coherent (photons in phase), collimated/directional (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011).

**Components:** energy (pumping) source; active (lasing) medium; optical resonator with a fully reflecting and a partially reflecting mirror; delivery system (fibre, waveguide or articulated arm) (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011).

**Classification (Banday et al, 2026)**
- By wavelength: ultraviolet, visible (argon 488 nm), near-infrared (diode, Nd:YAG), mid-infrared (Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm, Er:YAG 2940 nm), far-infrared (CO2 9300 and 10,600 nm).
- By medium: gas (CO2, argon), solid state (Nd:YAG, erbium), semiconductor (diode).
- By mode: continuous wave, gated, pulsed; erbium lasers are free-running pulsed, power = energy per pulse × frequency (Poli, 2017).
- By hazard: class I to IV; surgical dental lasers are class 4 (Parker and Parker, 2017).
- By use: hard tissue (erbium, 9300 nm CO2), soft tissue (diode, Nd:YAG, 10,600 nm CO2), low-level/PBM and diagnostic (diode 655 nm).

**Operating parameters the clinician controls (Poli, 2017)**
- Energy per pulse (mJ) and fluence or energy density (J/cm2): ablation occurs only above the tissue threshold; below it only structural change occurs.
- Pulse repetition rate (Hz) and average power (W = J × Hz).
- Pulse duration (µs): shorter pulses give higher peak power and less heat.
- Spot size and tip diameter: a smaller spot concentrates energy.
- Tip-to-tissue distance: fluence falls steeply as the tip is withdrawn, so a defocused beam does not cut.
- Air-water spray ratio: water cools, removes debris and couples energy; too little water causes heating, cracking and carbonisation.
- Contact or non-contact delivery; hand speed (slower than with a bur).

## 3. Laser–tissue interaction and the hard-tissue mechanism

### Explanation

Parker explains that light striking tissue will "behave in one of four ways: transmission, reflection, scatter, and absorption", and that predictable effects require absorption by target molecules "termed chromophores" (water, hydroxyapatite, collagen, haemoglobin, melanin) (Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Convissar's chapter gives the tissue composition that makes this work: enamel "is 85% mineral (predominantly carbonated hydroxyapatite), 12% water, and 3% organic proteins"; dentin is "47% mineral (carbonated hydroxyapatite), 33% protein (mostly collagen), and 20% water"; and "Water content in carious dentin may be as high as 54%" (Parker, 2016) [SRC].

**Where the mineral absorbs: the books contradict each other.** Mount lists "hydroxyl (2800 nm), water (2940 nm), carbonate (7000 nm), and phosphate" at 9000-11,000 nm (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Parker in Convissar places absorption "by the phosphate (PO4) radical of carbonated hydroxyapatite (near 7.0 μm); and by the carbonate" radical near 9.6 μm (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Poli says the 9.3-9.6 μm CO2 wavelength "is the peak of absorption for the molecule of phosphate in hydroxyapatite" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Mount and Poli agree (phosphate at 9-11 μm); Convissar reverses the two radicals. The exam-safe statement is that 9.3-9.6 μm is strongly absorbed by hydroxyapatite mineral; naming the radical should carry the author [Likely].

**Erbium ablation.** Mount: "an explosive subsurface expansion of interstitially trapped water" ejects apatite crystals, and "A wavelength of 2940 nm will match exactly the maximal absorption of water"; the absorption of Er:YAG in water is "13,000 cm-1", higher than the YSGG lasers (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Gonçalves and co-workers call this "photomechanical ablation" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]; Parker calls it spallation: "A common term for this effect is spallation", and adds the rule that "the ablation front always precedes the thermal front" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Penetration is shallow: for 2940 nm "it is 7 μm into the enamel and 5 μm into the dentin", and 21 μm and 15 μm for 2780 nm (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

**Why other wavelengths failed.** The Nd:YAG was of marginal benefit "because of its very low absorption in sound enamel or dentin" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson note that "the tooth has to be painted with a chromophore to allow absorption to occur" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. The CW 10,600 nm CO2 laser gave "reports of charring, cracking, and damaging heat buildup in tooth and bone structure" (Parker, 2016) [SRC], "which has made its use in restorative dentistry impossible" (Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Other non-erbium systems have "not led to clinical application due to concerns about effectiveness, safety, or cost" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Hence Poli: of current wavelengths "only three can be used for hard tissue", the two erbium lasers and a CO2 laser whose "emission wavelength is 9300 nm" (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Laser–tissue interaction.** Light falling on tissue is reflected, transmitted, scattered or absorbed; only absorbed energy produces a predictable effect, so the wavelength is matched to a chromophore (Parker, 2017). Dental chromophores: water, hydroxyapatite, collagen, haemoglobin and melanin. Effects: photothermal (coagulation, vaporisation, carbonisation), photomechanical/photoacoustic (erbium ablation), photochemical (photodynamic therapy) and photobiomodulation.

**Why erbium lasers cut teeth**
- Enamel contains about 12% water, dentine 20% and carious dentine up to 54% (Parker, 2016); 2940 nm coincides with the absorption peak of water (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
- Energy is absorbed in a few micrometres (Er:YAG about 7 µm in enamel, 5 µm in dentine; Er,Cr:YSGG 21 and 15 µm) (Poli, 2017).
- Interstitial water flashes to steam, the subsurface explodes and mineral fragments are ejected (photomechanical ablation, "spallation"); the ejected debris carries heat away and a coaxial water spray cools (Parker, 2016; Gonçalves et al, 2020).
- Carious tissue has more water and is ablated at lower energy: the basis of selective ablation (Poli, 2017).

**Thermal effects on soft tissue with rising temperature (Parker, 2017):** about 45 °C destructive heating begins; 50 °C bacterial inactivation; about 60 °C protein denaturation and coagulation (haemostasis); 100 °C vaporisation of water (ablation); about 200 °C carbonisation (char, to be avoided).

**Unsuitable wavelengths:** Nd:YAG is poorly absorbed by sound enamel and needs a dark chromophore; continuous 10,600 nm CO2 chars and cracks enamel (Parker, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Only Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG and the pulsed 9300 nm CO2 laser are used for hard tissue (Poli, 2017).

## 4. Caries detection (laser fluorescence)

### Explanation

**QLF.** Parker describes the origin: an argon-ion laser at 488 nm was later replaced by an arc lamp, and "This became known as quantitative light-induced fluorescence (QLF)"; he gives the QLF excitation as about 405 nm (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Pandey and Muruppel give a 370 nm peak (Pandey, 2018; Muruppel, 2017) [SRC], and Sikri describes the original argon method ("blue green light of 488 nm wavelengths") (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. In QLF the lesion loses autofluorescence and "appears as a dark area in a QLF image" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; QLF "does not differentiate between active and arrested carious lesion" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], and Muruppel says it is "not able to differentiate or detect lesions extending into dentin" (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC].

**DIAGNOdent.** "The first such unit was manufactured by KaVo (Biberach, Germany) in 1998, with an emission wavelength of 655 nm" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. "The DIAGNOdent unit contains a laser diode (655 nm, modulated 1 mW power)"; the source of fluorescence is uncertain, "possibly porphyrins (Hibst et al, 2001)" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Muruppel states it as fact: "the reflected fluorescence from bacterially produced porphyrins is detected and measured" (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC]. Readings run 0-99.

**Cut-off values differ between books** (this is the commonest exam trap):

| Source | Sound | Enamel caries | Dentine caries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pandey, 2018 | 0-10 | 10-20 surface; 20-30 deep | >30 |
| Muruppel, 2017 | not given | 5-25 | >35 |
| Sikri, 2020 | -9 to 0 (initiation 0-9) | 10-17 | above 17 |
| Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019, in vivo, original device, occlusal | 0-13 | 14-20 | >20 |
| Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019, pen, occlusal / approximal | 0-12 / 0-7 | 13-25 / 8-16 | >25 / >16 |

Neuhaus and Lussi warn that "the cut-off levels for the occlusal and approximal measurements are different" (Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019) [SRC].

**Accuracy.** Sikri lists "0.92 sensitivity (more than electronic caries monitor)" as an advantage (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Braga and co-workers report occlusal sensitivity of about 0.80-0.90 but "The specificity values obtained in different studies have been between 0.60 and 0.70", and note that values vary "due to different cut-off points used in the different studies" (Braga, Mendes and Ekstrand, 2010) [SRC]. Muruppel cites a clinical study with sensitivity 0.89 in primary molars "but only 0.40 in permanent molar teeth", and Lussi's finding that it "was not able to clearly differentiate between superficial and deep dentinal caries" (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC]. Rochlen and Wolff: "These devices have been found to have high sensitivity of detection, but have only moderate specificity", so they "have the potential of overdiagnosis" (Rochlen and Wolff, 2011) [SRC]. Neuhaus and Lussi: "DIAGNOdent has been intended to act as a second opinion device"; "For secondary caries detection, there is evidence that DIAGNOdent is not helpful"; root caries findings "are discouraging"; it gives a "Punctual measurement (no images)" and "No detection of cavitation"; and "Remnants of several prophylaxis pastes, but also calculus give a false positive fluorescence signal" (Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019) [SRC]. Maltz and co-workers: using DIAGNOdent "to assess the endpoint of caries excavation is doubtful" (Maltz, Arthur and Fatturi-Parolo, 2019) [SRC]. The position statement quoted by Muruppel is that laser fluorescence is "an adjunct to traditional caries detection methods and not used as a primary diagnostic tool" (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC], and Braga adds that "visual examination is the only effective method available to assess caries lesion activity" (Braga, Mendes and Ekstrand, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Caries detection**
1. **QLF (quantitative light-induced fluorescence):** originally argon laser 488 nm, now arc-lamp violet-blue light (370 nm per Pandey 2018 and Muruppel 2017; about 405 nm per Parker 2016). Sound enamel fluoresces green; demineralised enamel scatters light and appears dark; fluorescence loss is quantified as mineral loss. Good for monitoring smooth-surface enamel lesions; cannot separate active from arrested lesions and does not assess dentine lesions (Pandey, 2018; Muruppel, 2017).
2. **Laser fluorescence (DIAGNOdent, KaVo, 1998):** 655 nm diode; red light excites bacterial porphyrins in caries, emitted near-infrared fluorescence is read as 0-99 (Parker, 2016; Pandey, 2018). Two tips (occlusal, approximal); DIAGNOdent pen for proximal surfaces. Technique: clean the tooth, calibrate against the ceramic standard and a sound reference, tilt the tip around the fissure, record the peak (Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019).
   - Cut-offs differ between sources (for example dentine caries >30 in Pandey 2018, >35 in Muruppel 2017, >20 occlusal in vivo in Neuhaus and Lussi 2019); quote the source.
   - Limitations: false positives from stain, calculus, prophylaxis paste and composite; useless for secondary and root caries; no image and no cavitation information (Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019); high sensitivity, moderate specificity (0.60-0.70), risk of overtreatment (Braga, Mendes and Ekstrand, 2010; Rochlen and Wolff, 2011).
   - Role: a second-opinion adjunct to visual-tactile and radiographic examination, not a primary diagnostic tool (Muruppel, 2017).
**QLF versus DIAGNOdent (Muruppel, 2017):** QLF uses short-wavelength violet-blue light, measures loss of the tooth's own green autofluorescence and gives an image; it suits early smooth-surface enamel lesions. DIAGNOdent uses 655 nm red light, measures red/near-infrared fluorescence of bacterial porphyrins as a single number, and relates to dentine caries depth.

3. **Newer optical methods:** polarisation-sensitive optical coherence tomography (1310 nm) can detect caries under sealants and composites at research level (Parker, 2016).

## 5. Caries prevention: acid resistance of enamel

### Explanation

Pandey explains the mechanism: heating enamel releases carbonate, "the most widely accepted theory is based on the fact that bound carbonate is released when dental enamel is heated", leaving a less soluble mineral (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Laboratory pH-cycling work showed CO2 laser treatment "can inhibit subsequent caries-like progression in the laboratory by up to 85%", and Featherstone's group reported "The threshold pH for enamel dissolution was reportedly lowered from 5.5 to 4.8"; but "these promising results have not been tested in human mouths" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. These are laboratory data, not clinical evidence [Certain]. Pandey adds that "Use of an air/water cooling has a negative impact on caries preventive effect of erbium laser irradiation" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], which conflicts with the cooling that erbium cutting needs; the preventive and the ablative settings are therefore different [Likely].

Parker describes thermal conversion of enamel "to a more acid-resistant amorphous" glass-like state (Parker, 2017) [SRC]; in Convissar he traces this to early Nd:YAG work and the "quasi–fissure sealant technique" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. The 9300 nm CO2 laser is the current focus: "removal of the carbonate group from the enamel mineral molecule results in a much more highly acid-resistant compound" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; Poli: irradiation "thus decreasing the solubility to acids with an enhanced resistance to secondary caries", and suggests irradiating fissures "prior to placing sealants" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Gonçalves and co-workers report Nd:YAG "with simultaneous application of topical fluoride" reducing hydroxyapatite solubility (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]. Banday: "CO2 and Er:YAG lasers have the ability to change the enamel structure", with synergy with fluoride (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Caries prevention.** Sub-ablative heating of enamel (CO2 9300-9600 nm, Nd:YAG, erbium) drives off carbonate from carbonated hydroxyapatite, leaving a less soluble, more acid-resistant, partly fused surface with reduced permeability (Pandey, 2018; Parker, 2016).
- Laboratory pH-cycling: up to 85% inhibition of caries-like progression with CO2 laser; critical pH lowered from 5.5 to 4.8 (Pandey, 2018). These results have not been tested in human mouths (Pandey, 2018).
- Combined with fluoride the effect is greater (Gonçalves et al, 2020; Banday et al, 2026).
- Clinical ideas: fissure irradiation before sealing; making cavity walls acid resistant after preparation with the 9300 nm CO2 laser (Poli, 2017).
- Water cooling reduces the preventive effect of erbium irradiation (Pandey, 2018), so preventive and cutting settings differ.
- Status: promising laboratory concept, not an established clinical preventive method.

## 6. Caries removal and cavity preparation

### Explanation

**Selectivity and energy.** Poli: high-water tissues ablate at lower energy, and "This phenomenon is at the basis of selective ablation" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey reports the threshold of healthy dentin as "2 times higher than the corresponding threshold of carious dentin", yet Richman "found that selective ablation of carious dentin is difficult with Er:YAG laser", and Aoki found the laser "removed infected and softened carious dentin to the same degree as bur treatment" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Selectivity is therefore relative, not absolute [Likely].

**Parameters differ between books (show both):**

| Item | Poli, 2017 (Coluzzi and Parker 1st ed; same text 2023) | Parker, 2016 (Convissar) |
|---|---|---|
| Ablation threshold | "about 8–11 J/cm2 for the Er:YAG laser and about 10–14 J/cm2 for the Er,Cr:YSGG laser" | enamel 12-20 J/cm2, dentin 8-14 J/cm2 |
| Clinical energy per pulse | "About 125 mJ (100–150 mJ) for primary dentin and decayed tissues"; about 150 mJ permanent dentin; "About 225 mJ (200–250 mJ) for permanent enamel"; up to about 350 mJ for hard enamel | "150 to 250 mJ/pulse" for an average spot; 400-700 mJ at 10-20 pps with good water cooling |
(Poli, 2017; Parker, 2016) [SRC]

Poli's rule: "It is always good practice to use minimum efficient value to obtain adequate excision" (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

**Technique.** The tip is used near-contact: "it is indispensable to place the laser tip at about 1 mm in order to maximize ablation"; fluence falls "by 52% at 1 mm"; the tip angle to the wall "should not exceed 45° otherwise ablation becomes ineffective" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Cutting is end-on: "this mode of action is at variance with that of the rotary bur, which predominantly uses a side-cutting action" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; Flax: "Since the laser tip is only end cutting", undermined enamel is removed with spoons or a slow round bur, and on occlusal surfaces the tip is "pointed at a buccal or lingual angle parallel to the enamel rods to improve cutting efficiency" (Flax, 2011) [SRC]. "It is imperative that the laser beam be kept moving at all times" and "Tactile feedback is intrinsically lacking in laser use"; "When gross caries is present, it is advisable to use an excavator to remove bulk volume" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Poli suggests opening thick occlusal enamel with a small diamond bur first and using the laser after; "the operator has to go through a learning curve" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson found that in tunnels "the slower the pulse rate, the faster the tissue is removed" because debris must escape (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Pandey reports Er:YAG with Carisolv as an alternative caries-removal technique (in vitro) (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Feedback.** "This audible clue is a lower-level sound with healthy tooth tissue but becomes louder with caries ablation" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; Mount: "These sounds assist the user in determining when caries removal is complete" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Poli calls it a «popcorn» noise (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. "At least one Er:YAG laser has a coaxial low-level laser beam to determine the fluorescence of the target tissue" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. "Caries-detecting dyes are unsuitable for use with lasers" because there is no smear layer (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC].

**Cavity form.** "sharp cavosurface angles are absent, and the cavity outline may appear distinctly irregular"; retention relies on micro-roughness and acid etching, "often with reduced need for physical undercuts in the cavity margin"; "calcium hydroxide, or glass ionomer lining is recommended on open tubules exposed by the ablation process" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Laser veneer and crown preparation is used by some "despite the lack of peer-reviewed literature to validate this application" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Poli 2023 warns that lased surfaces reach "up to seven times the roughness index of sound enamel" and, if left uncovered, "they can significantly promote plaque retention and cariogenic bacteria adhesion" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].

**Speed: the books disagree.**
- Poli's Table 8.2: enamel "<30% bur speed", dentine comparable (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; Poli: "high- or low-speed burs used with the dental drill are still more efficient and fast in removing dental tissues" (Poli, 2017) [SRC].
- Parker: "reports of 80% slower speed in enamel and comparable speed in dentin"; on fluoridated occlusal enamel "the ablation rate is approximately 20% of that achievable with a turbine" because "Fluoridated enamel presents greater resistance"; in class III-V and primary teeth "the ablation rate is comparable to that for slow-speed rotary instrumentation" (Parker, 2016) [SRC].
- Pandey: Keller's preparation time "was approximately twice that of the dental handpiece"; Matsumoto's times were class I 10-15 min, class II 13-20 min, class III 1-3 min, class IV 2-5 min, class V 30 s to 3 min (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Sikri's Table 25.6 lists the hard tissue laser as "Quicker" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. This is the outlier and conflicts with every laser textbook read [Likely].

**Class-wise approach (Poli, 2017) [SRC]:** class I is slow because of thick enamel (bur-assisted entry helps); class II takes longer than with a bur and access to the walls is limited by the 45° rule; class III/IV are quick and can be more conservative, protecting the neighbour with a cellulose strip rather than a metal matrix ("it is advisable to place a cellulose strip interproximally"); class V is quick because enamel is thin.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Caries removal and cavity preparation (erbium lasers: Er:YAG 2940 nm, Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm; also 9300 nm CO2)**

*Principle:* water-mediated photomechanical ablation; carious dentine (more water, threshold about half that of sound dentine) ablates at lower energy, giving relative selectivity (Poli, 2017; Pandey, 2018).

*Parameters (vary by book; quote both):* ablation threshold Er:YAG 8-11 and Er,Cr:YSGG 10-14 J/cm2 (Poli, 2017) versus enamel 12-20 and dentine 8-14 J/cm2 (Parker, 2016). Working energies about 125 mJ for caries and primary dentine, 150 mJ permanent dentine, 225 mJ permanent enamel, up to 350 mJ for dense enamel (Poli, 2017); 150-250 mJ per pulse (Parker, 2016). Use the minimum effective energy.

*Technique*
1. Laser analgesia phase (section 7), then rubber dam.
2. Tip about 1 mm from the surface (fluence halves at 1 mm), perpendicular, moved slowly and continuously; angle to wall not more than 45° (Poli, 2017).
3. Enamel entry: tip parallel to enamel rods; on thick or fluoridated occlusal enamel open with a small bur first (Flax, 2011; Parker, 2016).
4. Reduce energy in dentine; remove bulk soft caries with excavators; never let debris collect; adequate water spray throughout (Parker, 2016).
5. End point: change of the popping sound (loud low "thud" in caries, sharp "click" in sound tissue), visual and tactile check; caries-detector dyes give false positives on lased dentine (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
6. Finish: remove unsupported enamel with hand instruments or fine burs, or bevel with low-energy laser (40-80 mJ, 25-50 Hz); line deep cavities with glass ionomer or flowable composite; acid etch; incremental composite (Poli, 2017).

*Cavity features:* no smear layer, open tubules, rough micro-retentive surface, rounded irregular outline without sharp line angles; retention from adhesion rather than undercuts (Parker, 2016). Uncovered lased enamel is up to seven times rougher and retains plaque, so do not irradiate sound surrounding enamel (Poli et al, 2023).

*Speed:* slower in enamel (under 30% of bur speed, Poli 2017; 80% slower, and about 20% of turbine on fluoridated occlusal enamel, Parker 2016), comparable in dentine, class III-V and primary teeth. Class I 10-15 min and class II 13-20 min versus class V 30 s-3 min (Pandey, 2018).

*Class-wise:* class I slow (thick enamel); class II slow, poor access; class III/IV quick, protect adjacent tooth with a cellulose strip, not a reflective metal matrix; class V quick (Poli, 2017).

**Bur versus erbium laser (Poli, 2017, Table 8.2; Parker, 2016, Table 10-1)**

| Feature | High-speed bur | Erbium laser |
|---|---|---|
| Selective caries removal | No | Yes (relative) |
| Precision | 1-2 mm | under 300 µm |
| Smear layer | Produced | None |
| Thermal rise | Above 15 °C | Below 5 °C (2017 table) |
| Noise / vibration | 120 dB, vibration | Less noise, no vibration |
| Bactericidal action | No | Surface decontamination |
| Enamel cutting speed | Fast | Under 30% of bur |
| Dentine cutting speed | Fast | Comparable |
| Tissue contact | Required | Non-contact possible |
| Pain | High | Less or none |

**Indications:** small and minimal cavities, pit and fissure lesions, class III and V lesions, primary teeth, children, anxious or needle-phobic patients and those allergic to local anaesthetics, multiple small cavities in one visit, cases needing simultaneous gingival management (Poli, 2017; Banday et al, 2026). **Not suitable:** removal of amalgam, gold or ceramic; large cavities and preparations for indirect restorations; where defined margins and line angles are needed (Parker, 2016; Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011).

## 7. Pulp safety and laser analgesia

### Explanation

**Thermal thresholds.** Parker cites "temperature increments above 5.6 °C can be considered potentially threatening to the vitality of the pulp" and "increments in excess of 16 °C can result in complete pulpal necrosis" (Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Banday quotes a safe rise of up to 5 °C "with a maximum permissible rise of 7◦C beyond which irreversible thermal damage may occur" for periodontal tissues (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

**Laser versus bur.** Parker: rotary instruments "can cause conductive thermal elevation in excess of 20° C above 37.4° C", while erbium ablation "allows much of the heat to escape from the cavity, carried in the ablated particles, resulting in pulpal thermal increases of less than 5° C" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Poli: pulp temperature rise below 5 °C with spray, and "there is a temperature increase by 1–2 °C in the pulp chamber" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; the 2023 edition adds a study showing "its reduction by −2 °C in the Er:YAG laser group" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]. Pandey reports Glockner's finding of a fall "from 37°C to 25°C to 30°C" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], also cited by Flax (Flax, 2011) [SRC]. Mount: in teeth later extracted for orthodontics, "The studies showed no histological or biochemical injury to the dental pulp arising from the laser cutting procedure" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Pandey reports that accidental exposures healed with dentine bridge formation (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Edition and internal contradictions.**
- Poli's comparison table gives the laser thermal rise as "Thermal rise <5 °C" in 2017 (Poli, 2017) [SRC] and "Thermal rise <15 °C" in 2023 (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]. The 2023 text still says below 5 °C, so the table change is probably a typographical error [Likely]; write both with edition.
- Pandey gives Paghdiwala's uncooled pulpal rise as "43°C" on one page and "about 4.3°C" (attributed to Rechmann) on another (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. The second is more plausible for a vital-tooth claim of no damage [Guessing]; verify against the printed book.

**Conditions for safety.** Water is essential: Poli advises that the operator "personally measures how much water per minute is delivered by the handpiece" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; excess energy causes "cracking in tooth dental tissues", melting and collagen damage (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. By contrast bur-cut enamel shows vibration cracks: "Note cracking caused by vibration" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Bacteria are killed "to a depth of 300–400 μ" (Poli, 2017) [SRC], though "absolute" sterilisation is not possible (Parker, 2016) [SRC].

**Analgesia.** Mount: large trials show "only 2–5% of patients will request local anaesthesia"; animal work shows suppressed nerve firing "with a duration of 10–15 minutes"; pre-emptive settings: "Typical parameters are 70–120 mJ/pulse, at a frequency between 10–30 Hz, applied for 30 seconds" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Poli: "The mechanism by which the laser analgesia can take place is not completely known" (gate control, Na-K pump suppression, PBM preconditioning); the "Rabbit technique (also called hare technique)" starts defocused at 6-10 mm and moves closer, while the "Turtle technique (also called tortoise T.)" starts at 1 mm at low power and increases; with his protocol, 24 of 30 patients (80%) were treated without local anaesthesia; the effect lasts "approximately 15 min" (Moritz) whereas Nd:YAG pain thresholds "returned to baseline approximately after 60 min" (Whitters) (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey: 68% had no pain during Er:YAG preparation (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Parker is more cautious: placebo has "rendered Er:YAG laser application inconsistent"; "pain-free" claims "are still controversial"; he uses 20% tetracaine gel; a laser setting produced "400 times less vibration than that from a rotary bur" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Poli concedes that lasers "are not completely able to replace traditional bur, and it is not always possible to avoid injected anesthesia" (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pulp response**
- Pulp threat: rise above 5.6 °C; necrosis above 16 °C (Parker, 2017).
- Rotary cutting can raise temperature by more than 20 °C; erbium ablation with water spray raises it by less than 5 °C, typically 1-2 °C, because heat leaves with the ejected particles and spray (Parker, 2016; Poli, 2017); some studies even record cooling.
- Histology of teeth cut with Er:YAG before orthodontic extraction showed no pulpal injury (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
- Safety depends on adequate water, minimum energy, short pulses and keeping the beam moving; without water the surface carbonises and cracks.
- Edition difference: Poli's table gives laser thermal rise <5 °C (2017) but <15 °C (2023).

**Laser analgesia**
- Many patients need no injection: 2-5% request anaesthesia in large trials (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016); 80% treated without anaesthesia in Poli's protocol (Poli, 2017); 68% pain-free (Pandey, 2018).
- Proposed mechanisms (not proven): gate control from microsecond pulses, Na-K pump block in A-delta and C fibres, photobiomodulation; duration about 10-15 min (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Poli, 2017).
- Techniques: pre-emptive sub-ablative irradiation of the crown and cervical area (70-120 mJ, 10-30 Hz, 30 s); "rabbit" (defocused high energy brought closer) and "turtle" (1 mm, low energy increased gradually) techniques (Poli, 2017).
- Limits: placebo and anxiety influence results; posterior teeth, long preparations, anxious adults and clamp/matrix placement may still need anaesthesia (Parker, 2016; Poli, 2017).

## 8. Bonding to lased tooth: laser etching or laser conditioning?

### Explanation

Poli explains that sub-ablative irradiation produces a surface resembling acid-etched enamel and that "Improperly, this effect has been long defined «laser etching» in literature"; "it would be appropriate to use the term «laser conditioning»" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. In the 2023 edition conditioning is defined as energy just above threshold ("15 J/cm2 for Er:YAG and 20 J/cm2 for Er;Cr:YSGG") (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].

**Why bonding may be weaker.** "Ablation of dentin melts collagen fibrils together, resulting in a lack of interfibrillar space" that restricts resin diffusion; Dunn's study showed fused enamel prisms and lower enamel bond; "adhesion values came out much lower than those obtainable with the acid" (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

**Recommended protocol.** "it is however preferable to perform enamel acid etching by orthophosphoric acid"; etching should "not exceed 30 s of contact time on the enamel and 15 s on irradiated dentin"; pretreatments (NaOCl, CHX and so on) have "no certain clinical proof"; and "the most advised therapeutic attitude with regard to the adhesive system is to adopt the same procedures that are normally used for tooth tissues treated by diamond burs" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Caprioglio and co-workers: etch-and-rinse bond strengths in Er:YAG "lased cavities are higher than those of the newer" self-etch systems, and "acid etching of lased surfaces is still to be preferred" (Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018) [SRC]. Flax: "it is still prudent to continue chemical etching until definitive studies are conclusive" (Flax, 2011) [SRC]. Gonçalves: Nd:YAG before bonding is "still extremely controversial and should not yet be indicated for clinical use" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC].

The contested evidence is treated in the critical synthesis (claims 1 and 2).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Adhesion to lased tooth**
- Sub-ablative erbium irradiation leaves a clean, smear-free, micro-rough, etched-looking surface; the correct term is "laser conditioning", not "laser etching" (Poli, 2017).
- Problems: fused collagen with no interfibrillar space, fused enamel prisms, subsurface microcracks and denatured layers reduce resin infiltration and hybrid layer quality; most studies show lower bond strength with laser conditioning alone than with phosphoric acid (Poli, 2017; Poli et al, 2023).
- Some sources disagree: little difference in bond strength (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016); laser etching similar to acid etching (Sikri, 2020); laser plus acid better than acid alone (Banday et al, 2026).
- **Recommended protocol:** remove unsupported enamel and smooth margins; phosphoric acid on enamel 15-30 s and on lased dentine about 15 s or less; etch-and-rinse or two-step self-etch adhesive as for bur-cut cavities; liner in deep cavities; incremental placement (Poli, 2017; Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018).
- Do not rely on laser conditioning as a substitute for acid etching (Poli et al, 2023). Nd:YAG before bonding is not recommended (Gonçalves et al, 2020).

## 9. Laser curing of composite resin (argon laser)

### Explanation

Pandey: "use of Argon laser for photo-polymerization of composite resin restorative material started" in the early 1980s, because "the wavelength (488 nm) of light emitted by the argon laser is optimal" for camphorquinone; it gives depth of cure faster, "10 seconds for 2 mm depth of cure compared to 40 seconds", and better properties, but "There are conflicting reports about the marginal seal obtained with argon laser curing", it is bulky, hot and noisy with a warm-up lag, and "Cost of the argon laser curing unit is a deterrent to its acquisition" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri lists argon lasers as "Used for curing of composite restorations and bonding cements" and for transillumination (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The claims of improved properties come from the period before modern LED units; no source read compares argon with current high-irradiance LEDs [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Composite curing (argon laser, 488 nm)**
- 488 nm lies in the camphorquinone absorption band (400-500 nm, peak about 480 nm); monochromatic, collimated beam keeps power density over distance (Pandey, 2018).
- Advantages: one quarter of the exposure time (10 s versus 40 s for 2 mm), greater depth and degree of cure, better compressive, tensile and flexural properties, possibly better shear bond (Pandey, 2018).
- Disadvantages: conflicting reports on marginal seal; increased shrinkage and brittleness of some resins; bulky, heat and fan noise, warm-up lag, high cost; blue-light hazard (Pandey, 2018).
- Also used for transillumination and bonding of cements (Sikri, 2020). Largely replaced by LED curing units in practice.

## 10. Removal of restorations: what lasers can and cannot remove

### Explanation

Parker: "No laser is capable of removing amalgam or gold restorations"; reflection, heat and mercury fumes are the dangers, and "thermally fused indirect ceramic restorations are subject to rapid spot-heat buildup, leading to carbonization and crazing" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Poli: "amalgam melting may occur with subsequent damages to reconstruction and release of mercury vapor"; ceramic crowns "can be fractured following quick thermal expansion" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Banday: "Lasers do not ablate metal restorations and tend to prepare cavities more slowly than high-speed handpieces" (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

**Composite: the books disagree.**
- Removable: Parker figures "Composite ablation using two different erbium laser wavelengths" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; Parker (2017) notes "the potential for easy disruption and ablation of composite restorative materials" (Parker, 2017) [SRC]; Green: Er:YAG removes "enamel, dentin, and nonmetallic restorations" (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011) [SRC]; Pandey's FDA list includes removal of composite and glass ionomer (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; the 9300 nm CO2 laser allows "selective ablation of composite when replacing failed restorations" (Poli, 2017) [SRC].
- Avoid: Poli says melted resin solidifies on and damages the tip, so "it is absolutely advisable to avoid resin-based materials ablation during conservative therapy" and "it is preferable to remove the old material by diamond bur" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; Pandey's note says "dental amalgam should not be lased. The same is true for composite restorations" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
Pandey therefore contradicts himself (FDA list versus his note) [Certain]. Reconciliation: composite can be ablated, but with erbium lasers it is inefficient and damages tips, so bur removal is preferred; the 9300 nm CO2 laser is better suited [Likely].

Ultrashort-pulse lasers "can remove any kind of restorative material including amalgam", but "Size, the sensitivity to environment and high cost are their biggest limitations" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Removal of restorations**
- **Cannot:** amalgam, gold and other metals (reflection, heat to pulp and periodontium, amalgam melting with mercury vapour); fused ceramic (spot heating, crazing, fracture) (Parker, 2016; Poli, 2017; Banday et al, 2026). Remove these with burs, then use the laser for the caries beneath.
- **Can, with limits:** composite, glass ionomer and sealants: erbium lasers ablate them but slowly, and molten resin damages the tip, so many authors prefer a diamond bur followed by laser removal of caries (Poli, 2017); the 9300 nm CO2 laser can selectively remove composite and failed sealants and orthodontic adhesive remnants (Poli, 2017).
- **Future:** ultrashort-pulse (femtosecond/picosecond) lasers can remove any material including amalgam but are large and expensive (Pandey, 2018).
- Protect adjacent crowns and restorations from the beam.

## 11. Soft tissue management in restorative dentistry

### Explanation

Poli: during cavity preparation "it is very easy to perform a light gingivoplasty in order to remove the superfluous keratinized tissue", and erbium lasers used with long pulses and little water give "moderate control over bleeding" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Downs and co-workers: "laser gingival troughing with a laser gingivectomy can be performed at the same appointment as impression taking"; "Retraction cord is not necessary for reflection around the tooth or teeth"; "Laser treatment causes no recession or repositioning of the gingival margin"; procedures must respect biologic width, which "should be fully understood" (Downs et al, 2016) [SRC]. Green: "The laser eliminates the need for the retraction cord", and "Aesthetic crown lengthening is usually done with the use of a diode laser set at a wavelength of 810 nm", which is not absorbed by tooth or restorations (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011) [SRC]. Flax cites a study in which "osseous crown lengthening with ErCr:YSGG laser could be completed without laying a flap, suturing, or damaging the bone", while keeping "a 3-mm DGC" (dentogingival complex) (Flax, 2011) [SRC]; the bovine-bone study he cites is in vitro [Certain].

Depigmentation: "All of the current dental lasers can accomplish gingival depigmentation" (Downs et al, 2016) [SRC]; it requires ablation "to the basal layer where the melanocytes are located", and "Patients should be aware of the recurrence of pigmentation, but this can be retreated readily" (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2017) [SRC]. Parker relates diode depigmentation to selective photothermolysis of melanin (Parker, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Soft tissue procedures that support restorations**
- **Gingival troughing/retraction:** diode, Nd:YAG, CO2 or erbium laser removes the sulcular lining around the finish line with haemostasis; impression the same day, no retraction cord, little recession (Downs et al, 2016; Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011).
- **Gingivoplasty/gingivectomy during cavity preparation:** expose subgingival margins of class II and V cavities in the same visit (Poli, 2017).
- **Crown lengthening:** soft tissue (diode 810 nm or erbium) and closed-flap osseous recontouring with Er,Cr:YSGG, preserving about 3 mm biologic width (Flax, 2011; Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011).
- **Frenectomy, operculectomy, removal of hyperplastic tissue, exposure of fractured margins.**
- **Gingival depigmentation:** ablation to the basal melanocyte layer (erbium, CO2) or photothermal targeting of melanin (diode, Nd:YAG); recurrence possible (Luk and Anagnostaki, 2017).
- **Pulp exposures:** decontamination and haemostasis before capping (Poli, 2017).
- Advantages: haemostasis, clear dry field, less swelling and pain, often no sutures.

## 12. Dentin hypersensitivity, bleaching and photobiomodulation

### Explanation

These are answered fully elsewhere (cross-references in the header). Banday: "the Nd:YAG laser appears to be most effective in the treatment of dentin hypersensitivity" (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC]; Poli describes low-water erbium "welding" that may reduce sensitivity by tubule occlusion "and it may allow to reduce dentinal sensitivity" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; PBM acts by modifying pain transmission and "the laser would promote the production of sclerotic dentin, obliterating the dentinal tubules from the inside", usually with diodes "between 650 and 870 nm" (Romagnoli and Cafaro, 2017) [SRC].

Bleaching: "The laser is used in conjunction with a hydrogen peroxide gel" and heat accelerates the reaction (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011) [SRC]; Downs lists "Laser bleaching" among cosmetic procedures (Downs et al, 2016) [SRC].

PBM: "Low-level laser therapy (LLLT), nowadays also known as photobiomodulation"; "The clinical effects of LLLT are anti-inflammatory, analgesic, biostimulating, and also bactericidal", though "there is no universal consensus about the managing parameters" (Romagnoli and Cafaro, 2017) [SRC]. Banday: "Modulation of cytochrome c oxidase activity is proposed to enhance ATP production", and the dose follows the "hormesis" principle (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Dentin hypersensitivity** (see the dentin hypersensitivity answer): Nd:YAG and CO2 melt and occlude tubules; erbium at low water ("welding") seals the surface; low-level diode lasers (650-870 nm) reduce nerve transmission and stimulate sclerotic/secondary dentine (Banday et al, 2026; Poli, 2017; Romagnoli and Cafaro, 2017).

**Bleaching** (see the bleaching answer): laser (argon, diode, CO2, erbium) energy activates hydrogen peroxide gel and speeds in-office bleaching; heat and sensitivity are the risks (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011).

**Photobiomodulation (LLLT):** red or near-infrared light absorbed by mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase raises ATP and modulates inflammation; effects are analgesic, anti-inflammatory and biostimulatory; biphasic dose response (Romagnoli and Cafaro, 2017; Banday et al, 2026). Restorative uses: post-operative sensitivity, pulpal inflammation after deep preparation, analgesia before cavity preparation, healing after crown lengthening. Parameters are not standardised.

## 13. Laser safety in the operatory

### Explanation

Parker and Parker define the nominal hazard zone as the "Area within which the level of the direct, reflected or scattered radiation during normal operation exceeds the applicable MPE", and state that "Class 4 is the highest and most dangerous class of laser", capable of eye and skin injury and fire (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Plume "represents a significant hazard from breathing airborne contaminants produced during the vaporization of tissues" (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Restorative specifics: Poli advises "not to use metal matrixes" because they "could reflect the laser beam" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; Parker shows "a metal matrix band has been dulled to prevent reflection of the laser beam" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; Bonsor and Pearson warn of collateral damage "as a result of reflection or passage through tissue" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Banday: "wavelength-specific protective eyewear must be worn"; "Laser sparks can ignite patient fires" in oxygen-rich settings (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC]. A full answer is in P2 (predicted).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Safety**
- Dental surgical lasers are class 4: eye, skin and fire hazard; define the nominal hazard zone, appoint a laser safety officer, warning signs, key switch (Parker and Parker, 2017).
- Wavelength-specific eyewear (optical density marked) for patient, operator and assistant (Banday et al, 2026).
- Avoid reflective instruments; use non-reflective or dulled matrices, cellulose strips for neighbours (Poli, 2017; Parker, 2016).
- High-volume evacuation for plume; masks (Parker and Parker, 2017).
- No firing near oxygen or flammable materials (Banday et al, 2026).
- Water spray and minimum effective energy protect the pulp; test-fire and check delivered power before use.

## 14. Advantages and limitations

### Explanation

Poli's Table 8.1 lists advantages: soft and hard tissue in one session, minimal invasion, "Reduced or no need for local anesthesia", precision, selectivity, micro-retentive surface, "Tissue decontamination", "Biostimulation effect", no pulp heating, no cracking, "No smear layer in hard tissues"; his Table 8.2 compares bur and laser ("Comparison bur vs erbium laser") (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Parker: to most dentists the drill remains "the “gold standard.”" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson: hard-tissue cutting "still remains in its infancy" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Green: use "is limited to smaller cavity preparations" (Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011) [SRC]. The AAPD recognises benefits for children (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages (erbium hard tissue lasers)**
- Minimally invasive, relatively selective removal of caries; precision under 300 µm versus 1-2 mm for burs (Poli, 2017).
- Often no injection; no vibration, less noise; good acceptance in children and anxious patients (Poli, 2017; Banday et al, 2026).
- Less pulpal heating and no vibration cracks when used correctly.
- No smear layer; micro-retentive surface; surface decontamination of dentine.
- Hard and soft tissue in one visit; haemostasis; photobiomodulation effect.

**Limitations**
- Slow on enamel, especially fluoridated occlusal enamel; time-consuming for large cavities and indirect preparations (Parker, 2016; Green, Weiss and Stern, 2011).
- End-cutting, no tactile feedback, no sharp line angles or defined margins; undermined enamel needs hand instruments or burs; learning curve.
- Cannot remove metal or ceramic restorations.
- Bonding to lased surfaces unpredictable without acid etching; rough uncovered enamel retains plaque.
- Risk of thermal damage, cracking and pulpal injury with wrong settings or no water.
- High cost, safety measures, eyewear, plume.
- Parameters not standardised and differ between books; much evidence is in vitro.

**Cautions:** metal restorations, crowns and matrices near the beam (reflection and heat transfer to pulp and periodontium); oxygen or flammable materials in the field (fire); patients or staff without wavelength-specific eyewear; deep cavities close to the pulp without adequate water spray; an operator without training in the specific device and its parameters (Poli, 2017; Banday et al, 2026; Parker and Parker, 2017).

## 15. Future directions and conclusion

### Explanation

Pandey describes ultrashort pulses: "Serbin et al in 2000 showed that crack free generation of cavities can be accomplished using a femtosecond laser", and dye-assisted laser ablation with indocyanine green, where "The dye exhibits a strong absorption centered at 800 nm" so an 800 nm diode removes stained caries selectively (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Parker cites a "thin-disc femtosecond laser (wavelength 1025 nm, pulse width 400 fs)" ablating enamel and dentine (Parker, 2017) [SRC]. The 9300 nm CO2 laser is short pulsed (10-15 µs): "the CO2 allows to efficiently ablate enamel and dentin with laser pulses of 10–15 μs"; thermocouple readings were 3.3 ± 1.4 °C without water versus 1.7 ± 1.6 °C with water; repeated pulses on one spot cause "cessation of ablation after penetration of 2–3 mm", so scanning is needed (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. PS-OCT detects caries "under composite restorations and sealants" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Banday mentions antimicrobial PDT adhesives: "Antimicrobial PDT using photosensitizer-doped dentine adhesives has been investigated" (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Future**
- 9300 nm short-pulsed, scanned CO2 lasers: hydroxyapatite-absorbed, fast enamel cutting, simultaneous acid-resistance of cavity walls, composite removal, soft tissue surgery (Poli, 2017; Parker, 2016).
- Ultrashort-pulse (femtosecond) lasers: crack-free, heat-free ablation of any tissue or material; still large and costly (Pandey, 2018).
- Feedback-controlled lasers (fluorescence or plasma spectroscopy) that stop at sound tissue; dye-assisted ablation of caries with an 800 nm diode (Pandey, 2018).
- Optical coherence tomography for caries under restorations; antimicrobial photodynamic adhesives (Parker, 2016; Banday et al, 2026).

**Conclusion.** Lasers have a real but adjunctive role in conservative dentistry. Erbium lasers prepare small and medium cavities selectively, with less pain, vibration and pulpal heat, but they are slower than burs on enamel, cannot remove metal or ceramic, and need acid etching for reliable bonding. Laser fluorescence is a second-opinion diagnostic aid, laser caries prevention is still a laboratory concept, and soft-tissue and photobiomodulation uses add value around restorations. The bur remains the main instrument; the laser is a complementary one whose success depends on correct wavelength, parameters, water cooling and operator training.
For the postgraduate, the critical points are to quote parameters with their source, to distinguish laboratory from clinical evidence, and to follow every laser preparation with conventional finishing of margins, acid etching and a proven adhesive protocol.

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim 1: "Laser etching can replace acid etching."**
*Textbook says:* Sikri: "CO2 and Nd:YAG are the most effective lasers for etching" and changes in surface morphology "and bond strength after laser etching are similar to acid etching" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Poli 2023 reports a study that "proposed laser etching at 1.5 W/20 Hz" as giving similar roughness and shear bond strength (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Poli 2017: values "came out much lower than those obtainable with the acid" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; Poli 2023 quotes De Moor and Delmé: acid etching "cannot be ignored on laser-irradiated enamel or dentin with respect to bond strength and marginal seal", and "There was no value-added benefit of laser conditioning of the enamel after laser preparation" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]; Gonçalves: Nd:YAG before bonding is controversial and not for clinical use (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC]; Caprioglio: "acid etching of lased surfaces is still to be preferred" (Caprioglio, Caprioglio and Caprioglio, 2018) [SRC].
*Strength:* nearly all data are in vitro bond strength and microleakage studies; no clinical trial was read. Sikri's statement is unreferenced in the passage read. [Certain about source type]
*Conclusion:* laser "etching" is better called conditioning and is not a substitute for phosphoric acid. Banday's claim that laser plus acid improves bond over acid alone (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC] is the only positive statement and rests on a narrative review. [Likely]

**Claim 2: "Restorations in laser-prepared cavities bond and last as well as in bur-prepared cavities."**
*For:* Mount: "most of these show little difference in terms of bond strengths", and "clinical studies have shown that the integrity of a margin created with the laser is identical to that of a high speed turbine" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Poli 2023: long pulses (400-500 µs) gave bond strength "and marginal integrity in laser prepared cavities" comparable or better (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].
*Against:* Parker: "Studies on the marginal integrity of early laser-created restorations reflect poor stability", improved when acid etching is added: it "improves the longevity of the restoration" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; Poli 2023: dentine protocols are "still inconclusive" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].
*Reconciliation:* outcomes depend on wavelength, pulse duration, energy, adhesive pH and whether enamel is acid etched; results are contradictory because protocols are not standardised. With acid etching and an etch-and-rinse or two-step self-etch adhesive, short-term clinical margins appear similar. [Likely] Long-term survival data were not found in the sources read. [Certain]

**Claim 3: "DIAGNOdent accurately detects caries."**
*For:* Sikri: "0.92 sensitivity (more than electronic caries monitor)" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Neuhaus and Lussi: enough clinical evidence for primary care use (Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019) [SRC].
*Against:* specificity 0.60-0.70 (Braga, Mendes and Ekstrand, 2010) [SRC]; sensitivity only 0.40 in permanent molars in one clinical study; most validity studies in vitro (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC]; overdiagnosis potential (Rochlen and Wolff, 2011) [SRC]; not useful for secondary or root caries (Neuhaus and Lussi, 2019) [SRC]; doubtful for the end point of excavation (Maltz, Arthur and Fatturi-Parolo, 2019) [SRC].
*Reason for variation:* different cut-offs (table in section 4) and confounders (stain, plaque, paste, drying) (Braga, Mendes and Ekstrand, 2010) [SRC].
*Conclusion:* a reproducible monitoring and second-opinion aid for occlusal and approximal primary caries, never the basis for a restorative decision alone. [Certain]

**Claim 4: "Erbium lasers do not heat the pulp."**
*For:* rise under 5 °C, average 1-2 °C, sometimes a fall (Poli, 2017; Poli et al, 2023; Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; no histological injury in human teeth before orthodontic extraction (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC].
*Against:* without water, Pandey records a pulpal rise reported as 43 °C (or 4.3 °C on another page) in early work (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; excess energy cracks and melts tissue (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; Banday warns that "lasers can create excessive heat" unless high-flow water is used (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC]. Poli's 2023 table changed "<5 °C" to "<15 °C" (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC].
*Strength:* temperature figures are mostly in vitro thermocouple data; the pulp histology data are human but short-term. [Certain about type]
*Conclusion:* the claim holds only with adequate water spray, minimum effective energy, short pulses and a moving beam; it is a property of correct technique, not of the wavelength. [Likely]

## High-yield points
- Hard tissue lasers: Er:YAG 2940 nm, Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm, CO2 9300 nm (Poli, 2017); Nd:YAG and 10,600 nm CO2 are unsuitable for cutting teeth.
- Mechanism: water-mediated photomechanical ablation ("spallation"); carious dentine ablates at lower energy.
- Thresholds differ: 8-11/10-14 J/cm2 (Poli) versus enamel 12-20, dentine 8-14 J/cm2 (Parker 2016).
- Pulp: <5 °C rise with water (Poli 2017 table; 2023 table says <15 °C); 5.6 °C threat, 16 °C necrosis (Parker 2017).
- Analgesia: 2-5% need LA (Mount); rabbit and turtle techniques; mechanism unproven.
- Bonding: "laser conditioning"; always acid etch enamel (15-30 s).
- DIAGNOdent: 655 nm, porphyrins, 0-99; cut-offs differ by source; adjunct only.
- Cannot remove amalgam, gold or ceramic; argon 488 nm for curing.

## Common exam mistakes
- Saying lasers can remove amalgam, gold or porcelain.
- Writing that laser etching replaces acid etching; omitting phosphoric acid after laser preparation.
- Giving one DIAGNOdent cut-off or one ablation threshold as fact without naming the source.
- Claiming lasers are faster than burs on enamel (only Sikri's table says "quicker").
- Presenting in vitro caries-prevention or temperature data as clinical proof.
