## 1. Laser physics: principle and components

### Explanation

LASER is the acronym for light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; Einstein postulated the theory in 1916 (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC].

**From spontaneous to stimulated emission.** An electron that absorbs a photon jumps to a higher, unstable orbit and, on returning, releases a photon of random direction and phase (spontaneous emission). Einstein proposed that a further photon striking the already excited atom would stimulate the release of two quanta identical in phase, direction and wavelength (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]. If pumping continues, a population inversion develops, i.e. a majority of atoms are in the elevated rather than the resting state (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; the Glossary defines it as the state in which excited species exceed unexcited species (Coluzzi and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Only then does stimulated emission outrun absorption and amplification occur. Coluzzi adds that three- and four-level systems sustain the inversion more easily than a two-level system, because absorption back to ground state cannot then match stimulated emission [Likely].

**Components.** The books agree on three core parts but count and name them slightly differently:

| Source | Core components named |
|---|---|
| Coluzzi, 2017 | active medium, pumping mechanism, resonator (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; plus cooling system, controls and delivery system |
| Coluzzi et al, 2016 (Convissar) | three components make up the laser cavity (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]: active medium, pumping mechanism, optical resonator |
| Green et al, 2011 | energy source, active medium and resonant chamber (Green et al, 2011) [SRC] |

- **Active medium** (names the laser): a container of gas, a solid-state crystal, or a semiconductor (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]. Convissar's chapter adds a fourth group, a liquid, used in some medical lasers (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]. Crystals (YAG, YAP, YSGG) are doped with neodymium, erbium or chromium, hence Nd:YAG.
- **Pumping (excitation) mechanism:** diode lasers are pumped electronically and solid-state crystal lasers by high-powered strobes or flash lamps (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; CO2 lasers by AC/DC current or radiofrequency.
- **Optical resonator:** two parallel mirrors, one totally reflective and the other partially transmissive (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; the distal, partially transmissive mirror lets the beam out once there is enough energy (Coluzzi and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. In a diode the polished ends of the wafer act as mirrors and collimation is done by an external lens.
- **Delivery system:** optical fibre (KTP, diode, Nd:YAG), hollow waveguide or articulated arm (erbium, CO2), with a coaxial aiming beam for invisible wavelengths.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** LASER = light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation; theory by Einstein, 1916 (Coluzzi, 2017).
- Pumping raises electrons to a higher energy level; a photon striking an excited atom releases two identical photons (same phase, direction, wavelength) = stimulated emission.
- **Population inversion:** more atoms excited than at rest; needed for amplification (Coluzzi and Parker, 2017).

**Components** (Coluzzi et al, 2016; item 4 from Coluzzi, 2017):
1. Active medium: gas (CO2), solid crystal (Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG), semiconductor (diode).
2. Pumping source: flash lamp or electric current.
3. Optical resonator: one fully reflective and one partially transmissive mirror.
4. Cooling, controls, delivery (fibre, waveguide, articulated arm), aiming beam.

## 2. Properties of laser light, emission modes and parameters

### Explanation

**Properties.** The books list different numbers of properties:
- Coluzzi (2017): two properties distinguish laser energy from ordinary light (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC], monochromaticity and coherence (spatial and temporal); the beam also begins in collimated form (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC] but diverges from fibre tips.
- Green et al (2011): three distinguishing properties (Green et al, 2011) [SRC]: monochromatic, coherent, directional.
- Banday et al (2026): two principal characteristics, monochromatic and coherent (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].
- Pandey (2018): six "striking features" (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]: coherence, monochromaticity, brightness and intensity, directionality, collimation, focusability.
The underlying physics is the same; the lists differ in whether collimation and directionality are counted separately [Certain].

**Emission modes.** This is the point on which the books differ most:

| Source | Modes |
|---|---|
| Coluzzi, 2017 and 2023 | two natural modes, based on the excitation source: continuous wave and free-running pulse (Coluzzi, 2017; Coluzzi, 2023) [SRC]; gated pulse is a subset of continuous wave (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC] |
| Coluzzi et al, 2016 (Convissar) | three modes (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]: continuous wave, gated pulse (a mechanical shutter in front of the beam path, Coluzzi et al, 2016 [SRC]) and free-running pulse; free-running devices cannot give CW or gated output (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC] |
| Pandey, 2018 | a longer list: continuous wave, chopped (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], gated, pulsed, super-pulsed, ultra-pulsed, Q-switched (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] and flash-lamp pulsing |

Coluzzi states that current dental lasers do not use Q-switching or mode-locking (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC], so Pandey's Q-switched entry describes laser physics in general rather than dental devices [Likely]. The editions also differ on which lasers are free-running: the 2017 text includes "some carbon dioxide devices" (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC], whereas the 2023 text lists only Nd:YAG, Nd:YAP, Er:YAG and Er,Cr:YSGG (Coluzzi, 2023) [SRC].

**Parameters.** Selting divides parameters into intrinsic ones fixed by design (Selting, 2017) [SRC] (wavelength, delivery system) and adjustable ones (power, pulse energy, pulse width, repetition rate, tip diameter, distance, water and air, hand speed). Key definitions:
- Energy (J, mJ); power (W = J/s).
- Fluence = energy per area, J/cm² (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; power density = power per unit area, W/cm² (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC].
- Peak power = pulse energy / pulse duration: a 100 mJ pulse of 100 μs gives 1000 W (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; average power is far lower because of the long pulse interval.
- Spot size: a smaller spot raises fluence at the same setting. Hand speed: slower movement raises the effective power density (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Properties:** monochromatic, coherent, collimated/directional. Books differ: two (Coluzzi, 2017; Banday et al, 2026), three (Green et al, 2011), six including brightness and focusability (Pandey, 2018).

**Emission modes** (books differ):
- Coluzzi (2017, 2023): two natural modes, continuous wave (CW) and free-running pulse (FRP); gated pulse is a subset of CW.
- Convissar (Coluzzi et al, 2016): three modes: CW, gated (shutter), FRP (flash-lamp, microsecond pulses, high peak power).
- Pandey (2018): also chopped, superpulsed, ultrapulsed, Q-switched.

**Parameters:** power (W), pulse energy, pulse duration, repetition rate, peak power, fluence (J/cm²), power density (W/cm²), spot size, hand speed (Coluzzi, 2017).

## 3. Laser–tissue interaction

### Explanation

**Four optical events.** Light striking tissue is transmitted, reflected, scattered or absorbed (Parker, 2017) [SRC]; Convissar's chapter gives the mnemonic RATS (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC].
- **Reflection:** no effect on the target but a safety hazard; for example a CO2 beam reflected off a titanium implant (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC].
- **Transmission:** passage through tissue without effect, e.g. diode and Nd:YAG through water.
- **Scatter:** the predominant event with near-infrared lasers in healthy soft tissue (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]; it spreads heat sideways.
- **Absorption:** the desired event, by a chromophore, i.e. a chemical group capable of selective light absorption (Parker, 2017) [SRC].

**Chromophores.**
- Water absorbs the two erbium wavelengths most, followed by the two CO2 wavelengths (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]; Parker places water's peaks near 3000 and 10,600 nm (Parker, 2017) [SRC].
- Enamel apatite readily absorbs CO2 and, to a lesser degree, erbium wavelengths (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC].
- Pigments (haemoglobin, melanin) absorb the short wavelengths, 532-1064 nm (Parker, 2017) [SRC].
- Water content governs hard-tissue absorption: enamel is lowest at 2-3% (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], soft tissue highest.
Penetration follows absorption: CO2 extinction length in mucosa is 0.03 mm (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC], against 1 to 3 mm for Nd:YAG (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Tissue effects.** Parker separates two regimes: destructive photothermolysis (Parker, 2017) [SRC] and sub-ablative photobiomodulation (Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Absorbed energy most commonly becomes heat by internal conversion (Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Individual photons of dental lasers carry far too little energy to break hard-tissue bonds (Parker, 2017) [SRC], so ablation is thermal, not direct bond-breaking [Certain].
1. **Photothermal** (principal interaction, Coluzzi et al, 2016 [SRC]): temperature ladder per Convissar, protein denaturation without vaporisation between about 60 and 100 °C (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC], vaporisation at 100 °C, called ablation (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC], carbonisation near 200 °C (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]; Parker gives the same 100 °C and around 200 °C points (Parker, 2017) [SRC] and bacterial deactivation near 50 °C (Parker, 2017) [SRC].
2. **Photomechanical/photoacoustic:** in hard tissue, water turns to steam and blasts the mineral apart, called spallation (Coluzzi et al, 2016; Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; the volume change causes cavitation and a shock wave (Parker, 2017) [SRC].
3. **Photochemical:** a photosensitiser transfers energy to oxygen to form singlet oxygen (Parker, 2017) [SRC], the basis of photodynamic therapy (Q06).
4. **Photobiomodulation:** low-level energy absorbed by cytochrome-c-oxidase (Parker, 2017) [SRC].
5. **Photodisruption/plasma:** at ultrashort exposures and peak power density above 10⁸ W/cm² (Parker, 2017) [SRC], electromagnetic fields tear molecules apart (Parker, 2017) [SRC]; not used by current dental lasers.
6. **Fluorescence:** re-emission at a longer wavelength, used in caries detection.

**Thermal relaxation.** Inherent in free-running pulsed modes and impossible in continuous wave (Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Parker defines thermal relaxation time as the time to dissipate about 63% of the heat (Parker, 2017) [SRC]; the Glossary phrases it as cooling to 37% of the peak (Coluzzi and Parker, 2017) [SRC], which is the same quantity.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Laser–tissue interaction** (RATS: reflection, absorption, transmission, scatter) (Coluzzi et al, 2016; Parker, 2017).
- Absorption by chromophores: water (peaks near erbium 2780/2940 nm and CO2), hydroxyapatite (CO2 > erbium), haemoglobin and melanin (532-1064 nm).
- Penetration: CO2 0.03 mm in mucosa; Nd:YAG 1-3 mm (Coluzzi et al, 2016).

**Effects:**
1. Photothermal: >60 °C coagulation, 100 °C vaporisation/ablation, ~200 °C carbonisation.
2. Photomechanical: subsurface water explodes = spallation (hard tissue).
3. Photochemical: photosensitiser, singlet oxygen (PDT).
4. Photobiomodulation: cytochrome-c-oxidase.
5. Photodisruption (plasma) and fluorescence.
Pulsing allows thermal relaxation; continuous wave does not (Parker, 2017).

## 4. Classification of dental lasers

### Explanation

Pandey lists four traditional bases (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]: wavelength, construction/active medium, tissue, and the degree of hazard to skin or eyes (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Banday et al add mode of operation, continuous wave versus pulsed (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC], and give risk level as Class I to Class IV (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

**A. By wavelength (spectral region).** Band limits differ between sources:
- Pandey: ultraviolet/excimer approximately 150-350 nm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], visible 350-750 nm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], infrared 730-12,000 nm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Banday et al: UV 140-400 nm (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC], visible 400-700 nm (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC], infrared above 700 nm.
Dental lasers sit in the non-ionising range; the ionising boundary is at the junction of ultraviolet and visible-violet light (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]. The span of current dental devices is given differently:
- approximately 450 to 10,600 nm (Coluzzi, 2017; Coluzzi, 2023) [SRC];
- approximately 500 to 10,600 nm (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC].
The newer 445 nm indium gallium nitride diode (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC] explains the lower 450 nm figure [Likely].

**B. By active medium** (wavelengths per Convissar, Coluzzi et al, 2016 [SRC] unless stated):

| Medium | Laser | Wavelength |
|---|---|---|
| Gas | CO2 | 9300 and 10,600 nm (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC] |
| Gas | argon, He-Ne | visible; argon is no longer manufactured as a dental surgical laser (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC] |
| Solid-state crystal | KTP (frequency-doubled Nd:YAG) | 532 nm (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC] |
| Solid-state crystal | Nd:YAG | 1064 nm (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]; Green et al give 1064-1320 nm (Green et al, 2011) [SRC] |
| Solid-state crystal | Er,Cr:YSGG | 2780 nm (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC] |
| Solid-state crystal | Er:YAG | 2940 nm (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC] |
| Semiconductor | diode (InGaN, AlGaAs, InGaAs, InGaAsP) | 445 nm (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; 800-1064 nm (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC] |

**C. By tissue.** Soft-tissue lasers for incision, excision and ablation (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] (diode, Nd:YAG, KTP, CO2 10,600 nm); hard-tissue lasers (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] (Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG, 9300 nm CO2).

**D. By hazard (safety class).** The books use different systems:
- Pandey (older ANSI form): Class I, II, IIIa, IIIb (up to 0.5 W, Pandey, 2018 [SRC]) and IV.
- Parker and Parker (IEC, post-2002): 1, 1M, 1C, 2, 2M, 3R, 3B, 4, with Class 4 above 500 mW CW (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].
All surgical dental lasers are Class IV; details are in P2.

**E. By emission mode:** CW, gated, free-running pulsed (Section 2).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**
1. **Wavelength:** UV, visible, infrared. Band limits differ: visible 350-750 nm (Pandey, 2018) or 400-700 nm (Banday et al, 2026). Dental lasers span about 450-10,600 nm (Coluzzi, 2017) or 500-10,600 nm (Coluzzi et al, 2016).
2. **Active medium:** gas (CO2 9300/10,600 nm); solid-state (KTP 532, Nd:YAG 1064, Er,Cr:YSGG 2780, Er:YAG 2940 nm); semiconductor diode (445, 800-1064 nm).
3. **Tissue:** soft-tissue (diode, Nd:YAG) and hard-tissue (erbium, 9300 nm CO2).
4. **Emission mode:** CW, gated, FRP.
5. **Hazard class:** I-IV (ANSI); IEC 1, 1M, 2, 2M, 3R, 3B, 4; surgical lasers Class 4, above 500 mW (Parker and Parker, 2017).

## High-yield points
- Stimulated emission needs population inversion; resonator = one full and one partial mirror.
- Core components: active medium, pumping source, optical resonator (+ delivery, cooling).
- Emission modes: two natural modes plus gated subset (Coluzzi) versus three modes (Convissar); free-running pulsing gives high peak, low average power and thermal relaxation.
- RATS; absorption needs a matching chromophore.
- Water: erbium > CO2; enamel apatite: CO2 > erbium; pigment: 532-1064 nm.
- Photothermal is the main effect; spallation in hard tissue.
- Peak power = pulse energy / pulse duration.

## Common exam mistakes
- Calling every laser "pulsed": diodes and KTP are continuous wave, gated by a shutter; Nd:YAG and erbium are free-running pulsed.
- Giving one fixed number of emission modes or properties without saying books differ.
- Saying laser photons break hard-tissue bonds directly; ablation is thermal (water vaporisation).
- Listing Nd:YAG as a hard-tissue cutting laser.
- Writing old ANSI classes as the IEC 3R/3B scheme without distinguishing them.

## Mnemonic
RATS: Reflection, Absorption, Transmission, Scatter (Coluzzi et al, 2016).
