## 1. Introduction, rationale and wavelengths used in endodontics

### Explanation

**Why lasers in endodontics.** George and Walsh state the primary goal of root canal treatment as eliminating microorganisms from the root canal system and radicular dentin (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC], and point out that with conventional instrumentation large parts of the root canal system are untouched (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Stabholz et al cite Peters' finding that more than 35% of canal surface area remained unchanged after nickel-titanium preparation (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. Light reaches where a file does not, which is the whole case for lasers in this field [Certain].

**History.** The first endodontic laser report was Weichman and Johnson in 1971, who tried to seal the apical foramen in vitro with a CO2 laser (Pandey, 2018; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Wavelengths used in endodontics.** George and Walsh's table of lasers and their reported endodontic uses (both editions):

| Laser | Wavelength | Reported endodontic uses |
|---|---|---|
| Argon | 488-514.5 nm | disinfection (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] |
| He-Ne / diode | 633 / 635 nm | Doppler flowmetry, photoactivated disinfection (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] |
| Diode | 810-980 nm | soft tissue surgery, disinfection, analgesia, photobiomodulation (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] |
| Nd:YAG | 1064 nm | soft tissue surgery, disinfection, biomechanical preparation (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] |
| Ho:YAG | 2100 nm | listed in 2017 edition (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] |
| Er,Cr:YSGG | 2780 nm | tooth preparation, hard and soft tissue surgery, disinfection, preparation (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] |
| Er:YAG | 2940 nm | the same uses (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] |
| CO2 | 10,600 nm | pulp capping, soft tissue surgery (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] |

The 2023 edition drops Ho:YAG, replaces the argon row with an unnamed 445-514.5 nm entry, and footnotes He-Ne (and that entry) as kept "purely for historical reasons" now that 635 nm diodes have replaced it (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC]. **Books differ on the Er,Cr:YSGG wavelength:** 2780 nm (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; 2790 nm (Gopikrishna, 2021; Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. Both are recorded, not reconciled.

**The two families that matter inside the canal.** Pandey's scientific basis is the clearest summary: near-infrared lasers (810-1340 nm) have negligible affinity for water and hydroxyapatite, so they penetrate dentin and are absorbed by bacterial pigments (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; mid-infrared erbium lasers (2780, 2940 nm) are absorbed by water, so their bactericidal effect is more superficial (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. CO2 cannot be delivered through a fibre, which limits it inside the canal (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Emission modes: diode and KTP are continuous wave (gated) lasers (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC]; Nd:YAG, Er:YAG and Er,Cr:YSGG are free-running pulsed lasers (Coluzzi, 2017) [SRC].

**Delivery: the books disagree for Er:YAG.** Stabholz et al place Nd:YAG, KTP, Er:YSGG, argon and diode on thin optical fibres (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC] and CO2 and Er:YAG on a hollow tube (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]; Fouad and Levin say Er:YAG is delivered by a solid optic fibre (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. Current Er:YAG units use both, depending on the tip [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** LASER = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. The aim of root canal treatment is to eliminate microbes from the canal system and radicular dentin; files leave large areas (over 35% of canal surface) untouched, and laser light and laser-activated fluids can reach them (George and Walsh, 2017; Stabholz et al, 2016). First endodontic use: Weichman and Johnson, 1971, CO2 laser to seal the apex in vitro (Pandey, 2018).

**Lasers used in endodontics** (George and Walsh, 2017)

| Laser | Wavelength | Main endodontic use |
|---|---|---|
| He-Ne / diode | 633-635 nm | LDF, photoactivated disinfection |
| Diode | 810-980 nm | disinfection, PBM, analgesia |
| Nd:YAG | 1064 nm | disinfection, retreatment |
| Er,Cr:YSGG | 2780 nm (2790 nm, Gopikrishna, 2021) | access, preparation, LAI, surgery |
| Er:YAG | 2940 nm | smear layer, LAI/PIPS, surgery |
| CO2 | 10,600 nm | pulp capping, soft tissue |

Near-infrared lasers pass through water and penetrate dentin; erbium lasers are absorbed by water and act superficially (Pandey, 2018).

## 2. Diagnostic applications

### Explanation

**Laser Doppler flowmetry (detail in Q07).** Light scattered by moving erythrocytes is frequency-shifted while light from static tissue is unshifted, and this shift measures pulpal blood flow (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. George and Walsh call LDF the closest to a "gold standard" (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] but warn of signals from periodontal blood flow (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; Stabholz et al report that up to 80% of the signal from intact teeth without rubber dam may be non-pulpal (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. LDF cannot be used through an opaque full-coverage restoration (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Banday et al note that cost and procedural complexity limit routine use (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

**The books give different LDF sources**, recorded side by side:
- 1-2 mW He-Ne or 810 nm diode (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC];
- He-Ne 632.8 nm or diode 780-820 nm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC];
- He-Ne 632.8 nm only (Sikri, 2019) [SRC];
- "visible red and near-infrared" in general (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

**Fluorescence detection of canal bacteria.** The DIAGNOdent uses 655 nm light to elicit near-infrared fluorescence (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; sound dentin and healthy pulp read about 5 on a scale of 100 (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Oxidants quench the signal (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] and tetracycline medicaments give false positives (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. This is a laboratory-based system built on extracted teeth [Certain].

**Crack detection (new in the 2023 edition).** An in vitro study found more laser energy passes through cracked teeth (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC]; clinically, an 810 nm diode at 1 W CW was scanned across suspect teeth and a transient sharp pain located the crack (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC].

**Laser stimulation for pulpitis.** Pandey describes pulsed Nd:YAG stimulation: in acute pulpitis pain starts immediately and persists more than 30 seconds after the laser stops (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. This is a single-source claim [Guessing about its clinical use].

**Dentin hypersensitivity** is answered in dentin_hypersensitivity/Q06 and is only named here.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Diagnostic uses**
1. **Laser Doppler flowmetry:** low-power He-Ne or diode light; moving RBCs Doppler-shift the reflected light, giving an objective, painless measure of pulpal blood flow (vascular, not neural) (Stabholz et al, 2016). Useful in traumatised and immature teeth. Limits: periodontal signal (up to 80% non-pulpal without rubber dam), no reading through full crowns, cost (Stabholz et al, 2016; Banday et al, 2026). Sources vary: He-Ne or 810 nm diode (Stabholz et al, 2016); diode 780-820 nm (Pandey, 2018).
2. **Fluorescence detection of bacteria:** 655 nm light makes bacterial porphyrins fluoresce; guides the end-point of disinfection (George and Walsh, 2017).
3. **Crack detection:** 810 nm diode scanned over the tooth provokes sharp pain at the crack (George and Walsh, 2023).
4. Dentin hypersensitivity (Nd:YAG, diode, erbium).

## 3. Vital pulp therapy: pulp capping and pulpotomy

### Explanation

Lasers used for direct capping include CO2, 808 nm diode, Er:YAG and Er,Cr:YSGG, with clinical trial support (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; pulpotomy lasers include Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, CO2 and 632 or 980 nm diode (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey lists the mechanisms: bactericidal effect, sealing, and coagulation giving a dry field (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

Clinical figures:
- Moritz et al (CO2 then calcium hydroxide versus calcium hydroxide alone): 89% symptom-free with normal vitality responses (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC] versus 68% in controls (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC].
- Liu's Nd:YAG pulpotomy trial in primary molars: 97% clinical success (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

Reported advantages over ferric sulphate, MTA or electrosurgery include shorter operating time, a simpler procedure and less postoperative pain (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Banday et al add that laser alone may not seal reliably, so a capping agent is still needed (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC]. The laser is an adjunct to the capping material, not a substitute [Certain].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pulp capping and pulpotomy:** CO2, Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG and diode lasers are used (George and Walsh, 2017). Actions: haemostasis and dry field, sterilisation of the wound surface, superficial coagulation zone, photobiomodulation of pulp cells (Pandey, 2018; George and Walsh, 2017). Moritz (CO2 + Ca(OH)2): 89% success versus 68% with Ca(OH)2 alone; Nd:YAG pulpotomy in primary molars: 97% clinical success (Stabholz et al, 2016; Gopikrishna, 2021). A capping agent (MTA/Ca(OH)2) must still be placed (Banday et al, 2026).

## 4. Canal preparation, smear layer removal and disinfection

### Explanation

**Laser shaping.** Levy widened canals from ISO #20 to #35 with Nd:YAG and water spray (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. With Er,Cr:YSGG the risk of ledging, zipping, perforation and over-instrumentation was noted (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. George and Walsh conclude that conventional fibres cannot meet all the mechanical objectives of preparation (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC], and Pandey that traditional files are still required (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Q04 covers this in detail.

**Smear layer.** Nd:YAG disinfects better but removes smear layer less well than erbium lasers (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; in Takeda's comparison all lasers beat EDTA and Er:YAG was best (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. The procedure should never be done dry (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Stabholz et al describe the deeper smear layer as packed into tubules up to 40 μm (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Photothermal disinfection.** Laser light reaches fins, deltas and lateral canals that irrigants miss (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Water-absorbing lasers rupture microbes by expanding their water (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; visible and near-infrared lasers act through porphyrins, melanin and other pigments (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

**Depth of penetration, which the sources state differently:**
- near-infrared may act beyond 1 mm of dentin (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC];
- diode and Nd:YAG penetrate up to 500-1000 μm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC];
- mid-infrared about 0.5 mm (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

The comparison point is the irrigant: NaOCl penetrates only 130 μm into tubules (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], while bacteria reach 1100 μm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Stabholz et al report bacteria about halfway to the cementodentinal junction (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. Laboratory kill data: Nd:YAG three log steps at 1 mm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; 810 nm diode at 0.6 W, 74% reduction in a 500 μm dentin slice (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. These are ex vivo dentin-slice data, not clinical outcomes [Certain].

**Photodynamic (photoactivated) disinfection (detail in Q06).** A photosensitiser binds bacteria and laser light generates reactive oxygen species that damage the cell membrane (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; dye or light alone kills far less than the combination (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Visible red lasers (633-670 nm) are used with tolonium chloride or methylene blue (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. George and Walsh say indocyanine green is a fluorescent dye, not a photosensitiser, so ICG-810 nm "PDT" is really photothermal (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Cleaning, shaping and disinfection**
- **Shaping:** Nd:YAG, Er:YAG and Er,Cr:YSGG can widen canals, but straight-firing fibres cause ledges, zips and perforations and cannot meet the mechanical goals; files are still needed (George and Walsh, 2017; Pandey, 2018).
- **Smear layer:** erbium lasers with water/EDTA remove it best (Er:YAG better than EDTA alone); never lase dry (George and Walsh, 2017).
- **Photothermal disinfection:** near-infrared (diode, Nd:YAG) passes through dentin and kills pigmented bacteria; erbium acts by explosive vaporisation of water. Penetration: near-infrared beyond 1 mm (George and Walsh, 2017) or 500-1000 μm (Pandey, 2018); erbium about 0.5 mm. NaOCl reaches only 130 μm (Pandey, 2018). Technique: 200-300 μm fibre 1 mm short of WL, withdrawn coronally in a helical motion, several passes (Pandey, 2018).
- **Photodynamic disinfection:** dye (tolonium chloride/methylene blue) + red light (633-670 nm) → reactive oxygen species (George and Walsh, 2017).

## 5. Laser-activated irrigation: LAI, PIPS and SWEEPS

### Explanation

Laser fluid activation was described in 2008 (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Er:YAG and Er,Cr:YSGG are ideally suited because water absorbs them strongly (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]: bubbles expand and implode, creating rapid fluid motion (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC], and unlike a needle the tip need not enter the apical third (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

**PIPS (detail in Q05).** Pandey gives the parameters: under 20 mJ, 50 μs pulses at 2940 nm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], with the tip held stationary in the coronal pulp chamber (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. **SWEEPS** delivers pulses in pairs instead of single pulses (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC], and R-SWEEPS aims at more wall pressure without more apical extrusion (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC].

**Effects.** LAI with Er,Cr:YSGG for 60 s made 0.5% NaOCl as effective as 2.5% NaOCl (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC]. Apical extrusion with erbium fibres 5-10 mm from the apex was no greater than with a 25-gauge needle (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Stabholz et al stress that the PIPS data are in vitro proof of principle awaiting longitudinal in vivo studies (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. Device details belong to irrigants/Q05.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Laser-activated irrigation (LAI):** erbium energy absorbed by the irrigant forms vapour bubbles that expand and implode (cavitation), producing streaming and shock waves that remove smear layer and biofilm; the tip need not reach the apical third (George and Walsh, 2017).
- **PIPS:** Er:YAG, under 20 mJ, 50 μs pulses, tapered stripped tip held still in the pulp chamber; photoacoustic, not thermal (Pandey, 2018).
- **SWEEPS:** paired pulses that speed bubble collapse and add shock waves (George and Walsh, 2023).
- LAI made 0.5% NaOCl as effective as 2.5% (George and Walsh, 2023); extrusion no greater than needle irrigation in vitro (George and Walsh, 2017).

## 6. Obturation and retreatment

### Explanation

**Obturation.** The rationale is softening gutta-percha or conditioning canal walls (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. Outer root surface rise was 14.4 °C with Nd:YAG and 12.9 °C with argon (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC], above periodontal safety limits (section 8). Stabholz et al conclude that procedural simplification is the only proven advantage (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC], and Gopikrishna that more clinical evidence is needed (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Retreatment.** Nd:YAG removed gutta-percha in 70% and broken files in 55% of samples (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. Nd:YAP alone could not completely remove filling materials (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]; Er:YAG in curved canals had to be stopped for fear of perforation (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. A possible gain is avoiding toxic solvents (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC], but the decisive advantage remains unconfirmed (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. Banday et al report Er:YAG-activated NaOCl/EDTA as considerably more effective at clearing residual gutta-percha after NiTi removal (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

**Mishaps.** Pandey describes Nd:YAG smear removal over pulp-floor perforations before sealing (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] and Nd:YAG fusion of tricalcium phosphate into vertical root fractures (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; both are experimental [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Obturation:** laser (argon, Nd:YAG, CO2, Er:YAG) used to soften gutta-percha or condition canal walls; root surface rises of 14.4 °C (Nd:YAG) and 12.9 °C (argon) limit it; only advantage proven is simplicity (Stabholz et al, 2016).
**Retreatment:** Nd:YAG removed gutta-percha in 70% and broken files in 55% of samples; laser alone does not clear all material, risk of perforation in curves; may avoid solvents (Stabholz et al, 2016). Er:YAG-activated irrigants clear residual gutta-percha (Banday et al, 2026).

## 7. Endodontic surgery, photobiomodulation and analgesia

### Explanation

**Apical surgery.** Erbium lasers ablate bone with water spray, giving deep, sharp, char-free cuts (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC], and Er:YAG or Er,Cr:YSGG with a microscope gave better postoperative healing than conventional surgery (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Gouw-Soares used Er:YAG for osteotomy and resection and Nd:YAG to seal tubules (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. Er:YAG root-end cavities showed less microleakage (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]; Er,Cr:YSGG retropreparation produced fewer cracks than ultrasonics in vitro (Mortman, 2011) [SRC]. Against this, CO2 in 320 cases did not improve healing (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC], Er:YAG does not melt or seal tubules (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC], and it cuts slightly slower than burs (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. Invasive cervical resorption can be ablated instead of using trichloracetic acid (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

**Photobiomodulation.** Red (633-635 nm) or near-infrared (810-1100 nm) light (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] acts on the mitochondrial electron transport chain (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; bystander effects in the PDL and periapical bone aid healing after intracanal lasing (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

**Analgesia.** Short exposures block Aδ fibres more than C fibres (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; pulsed Nd:YAG has been used for analgesia (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri also lists instrument sterilisation with CO2, argon and Nd:YAG (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Endodontic surgery:** erbium lasers for osteotomy, granulation tissue removal and root resection (char-free cuts with water spray); Nd:YAG/CO2 to glaze and seal resected dentin; haemostasis and sterile field; Er:YAG root-end cavities leak less (George and Walsh, 2017; Stabholz et al, 2016). CO2 apicectomy did not improve healing (Stabholz et al, 2016). Laser ablation of invasive cervical resorption.
**PBM and analgesia:** red/near-infrared light stimulates mitochondria, speeding healing after pulpotomy and surgery; laser blocks Aδ fibres (George and Walsh, 2017).

## 8. Effects on pulp, root dentin and periodontium: thermal limits

### Explanation

**Thresholds as each source states them:**
- 5.5-7 °C root-surface limits in laboratory safety studies (George and Walsh, 2017; George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC];
- PDL sensitive at 47 °C, and 60 °C and above stops blood flow and causes bone necrosis (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC];
- 7 °C rise commonly taken as the biological limit (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC];
- up to 5 °C safe, 7 °C maximum (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC];
- above 10 °C detrimental to the attachment apparatus (Mortman, 2011) [SRC].
For the pulp, Zach and Cohen's 5.5 °C rise causes necrosis in 15% of cases (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

**What intracanal lasers actually produce.** Nd:YAG can raise root-surface temperature by 17-27 °C (Mortman, 2011) [SRC]. In dogs, Nd:YAG intracanal treatment caused PDL necrosis and bone remodelling (Alshahrani et al, 2016) [SRC] leading to ankylosis and replacement resorption (Alshahrani et al, 2016) [SRC]. Fouad and Levin report charring and pulp inflammation with Nd:YAG more than with Er:YAG (Fouad and Levin, 2021) [SRC]. Goodis and Pashley caution that in vitro pulp temperatures may overestimate in vivo values because blood flow is absent (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC].

**Dentin morphology.** Used dry, near-infrared lasers melt and close tubules with partial smear removal (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; mid-infrared lasers can over-enlarge, transport, perforate and ledge (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Banday et al list overheating and melting of canal walls in dry canals (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

**A contradiction on "safe" parameters.** Stabholz et al attribute the under-1 W, 10 Hz, 10 s safety parameters to pulsed Nd:YAG (White et al) (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]; Alshahrani et al give the same numbers for a CO2 laser (Alshahrani et al, 2016) [SRC]. Goodis and Pashley's account of White's work names Nd:YAG (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC], so the Current Therapy attribution is probably an error [Likely].

**Control measures.** Short pulses below the thermal relaxation time (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; refreshing the irrigant between cycles (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; avoiding dry canals, which conduct heat like a solid (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; microscope filters matched to the wavelength (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Thermal effects and safety**
- Periodontal ligament: 47 °C harmful, 60 °C causes bone necrosis; a 7 °C root-surface rise is the accepted limit (George and Walsh, 2017); Banday et al: 5 °C safe, 7 °C maximum; Mortman: above 10 °C harmful.
- Pulp: 5.5 °C rise → necrosis in 15% (Zach and Cohen).
- Nd:YAG can raise root-surface temperature 17-27 °C (Mortman, 2011); dog study showed PDL necrosis, ankylosis and replacement resorption (Alshahrani et al, 2016).
- Dry lasing: melting, carbonisation, ledging, transportation, perforation (Pandey, 2018).
- Prevention: pulsed mode, low energy, water/irrigant cooling, fibre kept moving, irrigant refreshed, eye protection (George and Walsh, 2017).

## 9. Limitations and conclusion

### Explanation

Straight-ahead emission means energy is directed along the canal, not onto the walls (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC], so uniform 360° coverage is almost impossible (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC], and energy beyond the foramen risks the mental foramen or mandibular nerve (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. The fibre must be about 0.2 mm, stiff but not brittle (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC], and bacteria can survive within tubules (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC]. There is no consensus on settings (Alshahrani et al, 2016) [SRC]; cost and the need for more than one wavelength limit uptake (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

The evidence base is the main limitation. Stabholz et al note the lack of well-designed clinical studies showing an advantage over conventional methods (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]; Mortman found no case-controlled studies showing higher success (Mortman, 2011) [SRC]. George and Walsh, the most enthusiastic authors, describe laser debridement as now "superior" (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. The field has shifted from thermal ablation to subablative activation of irrigants (Alshahrani et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Limitations:** forward-firing fibres cannot irradiate all walls (no 360° coverage); risk of energy beyond the apex (near mental foramen/IAN); thermal damage, ledges and perforation; fibres stiff, cannot be precurved; no agreed settings; high cost and more than one wavelength needed; few well-designed clinical trials (Stabholz et al, 2016; Gopikrishna, 2021; Alshahrani et al, 2016).

**Conclusion:** lasers are adjuncts, not replacements, for chemomechanical preparation. Their best-supported uses are erbium laser-activated irrigation, adjunctive disinfection, vital pulp therapy and surgery; clinical outcome trials are still needed (Mortman, 2011; Alshahrani et al, 2016).

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim: laser disinfection improves endodontic outcome.** The laboratory case is strong: ex vivo dentin-slice and extracted-tooth studies show log-level bacterial kills with Nd:YAG and diode and near-complete smear and biofilm removal with erbium LAI (Pandey, 2018; George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC], but extracted teeth have no blood flow, no periapical tissue and usually straight, enlarged canals, so they may overstate both effect and safety: Goodis and Pashley make this point for pulpal temperature (Goodis and Pashley, 2012) [SRC], and Mortman notes that two conflicting JADA studies simulated straight canals opened to a large diameter (Mortman, 2011) [SRC]. Clinically, Mortman found no case-controlled studies showing higher success (Mortman, 2011) [SRC]; a 2026 meta-analysis of five RCTs (451 teeth) found activated irrigation improved periapical healing over syringe irrigation, with a wide-interval LAI subgroup (OR 6.77, 95% CI 1.63-28.12) and moderate certainty (Fernández-Moreno et al, 2026) [LIT]. A 2025 meta-analysis of seven RCTs found LAI reduced postoperative pain more than ultrasonic activation, with the effect seen for pulsed Er:YAG (PIPS, SWEEPS) and not for diode lasers (Sabeti et al, 2025) [LIT]. The reasonable reading is that erbium laser-activated irrigation now has early clinical support as an irrigant activator, while thermal intracanal disinfection by itself still rests mainly on laboratory evidence [Likely].

## High-yield points
- Near-infrared (diode, Nd:YAG) penetrates dentin and kills pigmented bacteria; erbium is absorbed by water and acts superficially but activates irrigants.
- Er,Cr:YSGG is 2780 nm (George and Walsh; Pandey) or 2790 nm (Gopikrishna; Cohen's): name both.
- LDF measures blood flow (vascular), not nerve response.
- PIPS: Er:YAG, under 20 mJ, 50 μs pulses, tip in pulp chamber; SWEEPS: paired pulses.
- Root-surface temperature limit about 7 °C (books range 5-10 °C); Nd:YAG can exceed it.
- Laser shaping causes ledges and zips: files remain essential.
- Lasers are adjuncts; clinical outcome evidence is still limited.

## Common exam mistakes
- Saying lasers can replace files for shaping: they cause ledges and transportation.
- Calling LDF a "nerve" or sensibility test: it measures blood flow.
- Giving one wavelength for Er,Cr:YSGG or one LDF light source without noting that books differ.
- Quoting in vitro bacterial kill percentages as clinical success rates.
- Writing PIPS as a thermal technique: it is subablative and photoacoustic.

## Mnemonic
