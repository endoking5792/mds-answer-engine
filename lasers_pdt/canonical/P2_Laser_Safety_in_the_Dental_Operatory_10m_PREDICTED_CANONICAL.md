## 1. Hazards of dental lasers

### Explanation

Pandey frames laser safety in three facets (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]: manufacture of the instrument, its proper operation, and personal protection of the team and patient. The risk is real: failure to wear the available eye protection is one of the most frequent contributing factors to laser injuries (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC], and in one medical series operator error was at fault in 67% of incidents (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].

**A. Beam hazards**
1. **Eye (the organ at greatest risk).** The injured structure depends on wavelength (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Parker and Parker give dental wavelengths as 370 to 10,600 nm (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].
   - 400-1400 nm (visible and near-infrared: KTP, diode, Nd:YAG) is particularly dangerous (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] and is called the retinal hazard region (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. The lens focuses the beam so that 1 W/cm² at the cornea becomes 100 kW/cm² on the retina (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Convissar's chapter notes that diode and Nd:YAG pass through cornea, lens and humours to be absorbed on the retina (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC].
   - Above 1400 nm (erbium, CO2): anterior-eye injury. Mid-IR causes aqueous flare, cataract and corneal burn (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; corneal injury is normally very superficial (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] and heals, unless the stroma is involved. Pandey names Er:YAG, CO2 and Ho:YAG as causes of corneal burn (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
   - The two "100,000" figures in the books mean different things: Parker and Parker describe a concentration of light on the retina (above); Pandey says the retina is about 100,000 times more vulnerable than skin in the retinal hazard range (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
   - The blink reflex takes about 0.2-0.25 s (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] and gives no protection outside the visible spectrum (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. The maximum permissible exposure is substantially higher near 3.0 μm (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] because the cornea's surface water absorbs it.
2. **Skin:** risk considered low in intraoral dentistry (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; near-IR penetrates deepest.
3. **Non-target oral tissue and pulp:** metal instruments and mouth mirrors reflect the beam (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; near-IR and visible wavelengths can be transmitted through tooth to the pulp (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; metal tips heat up without water spray (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. A CO2 beam reflects off titanium implants (Coluzzi et al, 2016; Green et al, 2011) [SRC], and some beams keep adequate energy beyond 3 m (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]. Banday et al cite a periodontal temperature rise of up to 5 °C as safe (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC], with 7 °C as the maximum (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].

**B. Non-beam hazards**
1. **Plume (aerosol, infection):** water vapour, hydrocarbons, carbon monoxide and dioxide, metal fumes (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC], particulates, bacteria and viral bodies; effects include eye irritation, nausea and breathing difficulties (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey cites a report that CO2 laser smoke is probably mutagenic (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
2. **Fire and explosion:** flammables in the surgery include clothing, paper, plastics, waxes and resins (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] and solvents (ethanol, acetone); risk rises in an oxygen-enriched atmosphere above 20% (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Ignition can burn with a blue flame that is hard to see under operating lights (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Rubber endotracheal tubes should be avoided (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Banday et al make the same point about oxidiser-rich atmospheres (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC].
3. **Electrical:** the most serious laser accidents reported have been electrocutions (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].
4. **Mechanical and other:** heavy articulated arms, quartz fibre needlestick, pressurised lines; Pandey adds explosion hazards from high-pressure lamps (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Convissar's chapter warns of air embolism from excessive air/water spray pressure (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Hazards** (Parker and Parker, 2017)
1. **Ocular (greatest risk), wavelength-dependent:**
   - 400-1400 nm (KTP, diode, Nd:YAG): retinal burn; lens focuses beam about 100,000-fold.
   - Above 1400 nm (erbium, CO2): cornea, aqueous flare, cataract.
   - Blink reflex (0.2-0.25 s) protects only against visible beams.
2. **Skin and non-target tissue:** reflection from metal instruments and mirrors; transmission to pulp; hot metal tips.
3. **Plume:** toxic gases, particles, bacteria, viruses; eye irritation, nausea, breathing difficulty.
4. **Fire/explosion:** clothing, paper, plastics, waxes, resins; oxygen-enriched atmosphere (above 20%).
5. **Electrical** (electrocution, the most serious reported accidents) and **mechanical** (fibre needlestick).

## 2. Standards, laser classes, MPE and nominal hazard zone

### Explanation

**Standards.** IEC Technical Committee 76 developed a four-class system that was modified in 2002 (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. ANSI Z136.3 covers the safe use of lasers in healthcare (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. IEC 60825-1 classifies products emitting from 180 nm to 1 mm (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; its revisions are dated to 2014 in the 1st edition (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] and to 2017 in the 2nd (Parker and Parker, 2023) [SRC]. Pandey cites US federal law, 21 CFR Part 1000, requiring manufacturers to classify and label lasers (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Key terms** (Parker and Parker, 2017, Table 5.1):
- **MPE (maximum permissible exposure):** the level to which a person may be exposed without hazardous effect (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].
- **NHZ (nominal hazard zone, also NOHZ):** the area within which direct, reflected or scattered radiation exceeds the MPE (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].
- **Aversion response:** blinking or head movement away from bright light (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].
- **AEL (accessible emission limit):** the maximum radiation permitted within a class.

**Classes.** The books use different schemes; they are shown side by side, not reconciled:

| Class | Parker and Parker (IEC, post-2002) | Pandey (ANSI form) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 / I | safe under all conditions; 1M hazardous only with magnifying optics (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; 1C for contact products (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; Class I and IM up to 40 μW blue or 400 μW red (Parker and Parker, 2023) [SRC] | totally enclosed, safe |
| 2 / II | visible, up to 1 mW CW, protected by blink (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; 2M hazardous with optics | visible, below 1 mW |
| 3R / IIIA | visible CW up to 5 mW (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] | IIIA: 1-5 mW; momentary viewing harmless (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] |
| 3B / IIIB | up to 500 mW CW or 30 mJ pulsed (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; direct viewing hazardous, diffuse reflection generally safe (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] | 5-500 mW CW (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] |
| 4 / IV | above 500 mW CW (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; eye and skin injury from direct, diffuse or indirect viewing; fire hazard; key switch and interlock mandatory (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] | 500 mW and above (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] |

Contradictions recorded:
- Pandey states that under IEC 60825-1 Class IIIB "is renamed" Class 3R (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Parker and Parker list 3R and 3B as separate IEC classes (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].
- The 1st edition of Parker and Parker writes the IEC classes in Arabic numerals (1, 1M, 3R, 3B, 4); the 2nd edition uses Roman numerals (I, IM, II, IIM, IIIR) (Parker and Parker, 2023) [SRC].
- All surgical dental lasers are Class IV. Oliveira and Rocha add that even dental light-curing lasers above 500 mW are high-power Class 4 visible-beam lasers (Oliveira and Rocha, 2022) [SRC].

**NHZ in practice.** The NOHZ is a complex calculation done by a medical physicist (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC], using wavelength, output, emission mode, beam divergence and focal length. In practice a surgery enclosed by intact, non-transparent walls and doors satisfies the requirement, and an open-plan clinic must mark and control adjacent areas (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Standards:** ANSI Z136.1/Z136.3 (lasers in healthcare); IEC 60825-1 (Parker and Parker, 2017).
- **MPE:** highest exposure without injury to eye or skin.
- **NHZ:** area where direct, reflected or scattered radiation exceeds the MPE; in practice, the closed operatory (Parker and Parker, 2017).

**Classes** (books differ):
- IEC (Parker and Parker, 2017): 1/1M/1C (safe), 2/2M (visible, ≤1 mW, blink), 3R (≤5 mW), 3B (≤500 mW; direct viewing hazardous), 4 (>500 mW; eye, skin and fire hazard).
- Pandey (2018), ANSI form: I, II, IIIA, IIIB (5-500 mW), IV; says IIIB was renamed 3R, which Parker and Parker do not.
- All surgical dental lasers are Class IV.

## 3. Control measures in the operatory

### Explanation

**Administrative.** Practices offering Class IIIB and IV treatment must appoint a laser protection advisor (LPA) and/or laser safety officer (LSO) (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; the LSO can be a suitably trained dental surgery assistant (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. The 2nd edition adds the alternative title laser safety supervisor (Parker and Parker, 2023) [SRC]. ANSI Z136.1 and Z136.3 define the LSO post (Roshkind and Convissar, 2016) [SRC]. Duties across the books:
- define the hazard and non-hazard zone (Roshkind and Convissar, 2016) [SRC]; post warning signs at all access points (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC];
- train staff, oversee maintenance, keep a log of every procedure with patient, procedure and parameters (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC];
- interrupt the procedure if any safety measure is infringed (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; Pandey adds stopping laser use if necessary (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] and investigating all accidents and incidents (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- All staff should read and sign written local rules (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Serious injury is reported on Form FDA 3500A within 10 work days in the US (Roshkind and Convissar, 2016) [SRC], and some jurisdictions require laser registration (Roshkind and Convissar, 2016) [SRC].

**Engineering and environment.** Controlled area marked by warning signs; all surfaces non-reflective (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; a fire extinguisher sited for easy access (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; key or password, remote interlock, covered foot switch (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC], emergency stop; an audible or visual emission warning is mandatory for Class IV (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey adds a sign outside the door telling entrants to wear protective glasses (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Test fire** before the patient enters, at minimum settings, into pigmented articulating paper for visible and near-IR wavelengths (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] and into water for erbium and CO2.

**Eye protection.**
- "Patient on first, off last" is the maxim that keeps the patient's safety paramount (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; everyone in the controlled area wears eyewear.
- Wavelength-specific material (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC], side panels against diffuse entry (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC], marked with the protected wavelength (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC] and the optical density (OD, a log10 attenuation scale).
- OD threshold, phrased differently by edition: OD 5.0 and above "explicitly safe" (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; OD 5.0 a "minimum safe level" (Parker and Parker, 2023) [SRC].
- Further markings: DIR, the emission mode the eyewear is rated for (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC], DIN, L-grade, CE.
- Loupes require a wavelength-specific insert (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. In a laboratory study through a dental microscope, mismatched filters failed, especially for Nd:YAG and diode (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey says a surgeon working with a CO2 laser through an operating microscope need not wear glasses (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Parker and Parker instead require appropriate filters to be fitted to the microscope (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC], although the study they quote detected little or no laser energy through the microscope even without a filter (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Eyewear may not withstand ultrashort-pulse irradiance (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].
- Pandey describes Nd:YAG glasses as usually blue-green (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] and argon/KTP glasses as amber (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Orange curing-light shields are not laser eyewear: three direct exposures to a Class 4 curing laser compromised one (Oliveira and Rocha, 2022) [SRC], so laser-certified goggles are needed (Oliveira and Rocha, 2022) [SRC].

**Patient and tissue protection.** Retraction, non-reflective instruments and wet gauze (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]; damp gauze over skin near the vermilion (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey (from a medical-surgery source) specifies double layers of saline-soaked towels (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], instruments with low specular and high diffuse reflectance (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], and no plastic instruments, which melt (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Use the minimum power that achieves the result (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].

**Plume control.** Masks filtering 0.1 μm particles (Parker and Parker, 2017; Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]; ordinary cup masks are insufficient (Coluzzi et al, 2016) [SRC]; high-volume evacuation (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC] with closed-circuit filtration. Pandey recommends two separate suction set-ups, one for smoke and one for blood and mucus (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Fire precautions:** remove or wet flammables, avoid oxygen-rich atmospheres, use closed-circuit sedation and scavenging.

**Infection control:** parts that contact tissue or blood must be heat-sterilised or disposed of as single-use (Parker and Parker, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Control measures**
1. **Administrative:** laser safety officer (LSO; may be a trained assistant) and laser protection advisor; LSO defines the hazard zone, posts signs, trains staff, keeps a procedure log, stops unsafe use (Parker and Parker, 2017; Roshkind and Convissar, 2016). Written local rules signed by staff; incident reporting.
2. **Controlled area:** warning signs at doors, closed door, non-reflective surfaces, key switch, interlock, covered foot switch, emergency stop, fire extinguisher.
3. **Test fire** at minimum settings into dark articulating paper (visible/near-IR) or water (erbium, CO2).
4. **Eyewear** for patient, operator and assistant ("patient on first, off last"): wavelength-specific, side shields, marked with wavelength and optical density; OD ≥5 (Parker and Parker, 2017, 2023). Loupe inserts; curing-light orange shields are not laser-safe (Oliveira and Rocha, 2022).
5. **Tissue protection:** retraction, wet gauze, matt instruments, minimum power.
6. **Plume:** high-volume evacuation, 0.1 μm filtration masks (Coluzzi et al, 2016).
7. **Fire and infection control:** remove flammables, avoid oxygen enrichment, sterilise tips.

## High-yield points
- Eye is the organ at greatest risk; 400-1400 nm is the retinal hazard region.
- Blink reflex protects only against visible beams.
- Every surgical dental laser is Class IV (above 500 mW).
- MPE and NHZ: define the controlled area; in practice a closed operatory.
- Eyewear: wavelength-specific, side shields, OD ≥5, patient on first and off last.
- LSO enforces; LPA (medical physicist) advises.
- Plume: high-volume evacuation and 0.1 μm masks.
- Fire risk rises in oxygen-enriched atmospheres.

## Common exam mistakes
- Writing generic "goggles" without wavelength specificity and optical density.
- Saying the blink reflex protects against diode or Nd:YAG beams (invisible near-IR).
- Mixing ANSI IIIA/IIIB with IEC 3R/3B as if one scheme.
- Forgetting non-beam hazards (plume, fire, electrical).
- Using orange curing-light shields as laser protection.

## Mnemonic
"PEEP": Personnel (LSO, training), Environment (controlled area, signs), Eyewear (wavelength, OD), Plume (evacuation, masks).
