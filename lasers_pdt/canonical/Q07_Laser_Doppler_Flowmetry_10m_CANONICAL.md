## 1. Introduction: vitality versus sensibility

### Explanation

Thermal and electric tests stimulate pulpal nerves; they report the sensory supply, not the blood supply (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Andreasen makes the same distinction: most tests assess the nerve supply, whereas LDF assesses the presence of a functioning vascular supply (Andreasen et al, 2007) [SRC]. Stabholz et al add that LDF reflects vascular rather than neural responsiveness (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. The distinction matters most after trauma, when nerves may stop conducting for months while the blood supply is intact or returning, so a sensibility test can be falsely negative [Certain].

Pandey dates LDF to the 1970s, first used for retina, gut and skin blood flow (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Pandey credits Gazelius et al (1986) with the first human study separating vital from nonvital pulps (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Laser Doppler flowmetry (LDF) is a noninvasive, objective, painless method of assessing pulpal blood flow. Thermal and electric tests measure nerve response (sensibility); LDF measures the vascular supply (true vitality) (Andreasen et al, 2007; George and Walsh, 2017). First used in dentistry by Gazelius et al, 1986 (Pandey, 2018).

## 2. Principle

### Explanation

Low-power laser light is carried by a fibre-optic probe to the tooth surface and passes through enamel and dentine to the pulp. Light that strikes moving red blood cells is frequency-shifted (the Doppler shift), while light reflected from static tissue is unshifted (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Part of the scattered light returns to a photodetector, usually through a second fibre in the same probe (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The instrument extracts the shifted fraction; the output is proportional to the number and velocity of RBCs (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]. Sikri defines the signal as red-cell flux, the volume of cells illuminated times mean cell velocity (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], sampled from about 10 mm³ of tissue (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Flux is given in arbitrary perfusion units, which is why George and Walsh call LDF semi-quantitative (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]: readings are compared over time in the same tooth or against a control tooth, not against a fixed normal value [Likely].

Muruppel notes that in dentistry the hard tissues are static and the only movement is that of red cells in the pulp (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC]; he reports light penetration of 6-13 mm in teeth depending on density (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle (Doppler shift)**
1. Probe transmits low-power laser light through the crown to the pulp.
2. Light scattered by moving RBCs is frequency-shifted; light from static tissue is unshifted (George and Walsh, 2017).
3. A second fibre returns backscattered light to a photodetector.
4. Output (flux) = number × velocity of RBCs, in arbitrary perfusion units (Stabholz et al, 2016; Sikri, 2019).

## 3. Instrumentation

### Explanation

**Lasers.** He-Ne at 632.8 nm or GaAlAs diode at 780-820 nm, at 1 or 2 mW (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Stabholz et al give 1 to 2 mW He-Ne or 810 nm diode (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]; Muruppel gives 632 and 780-820 nm (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC]. George and Walsh state that visible red and near-infrared wavelengths are the most suitable (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Kahler's chapter in Cohen's describes the beam as "infrared (780 to 820 nm) or near-infrared (632.8 nm)" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; 632.8 nm is visible red, so that label appears to be a slip and should not be copied [Likely].

**Probe.** Probe design and fibre separation (250-500 μm) affect reliability (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC]. Odor et al (reported by Pandey) found 810 nm with a 3.1 kHz bandwidth gave the best sensitivity and specificity for separating vital from root-filled teeth (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Instrumentation:** He-Ne laser 632.8 nm or diode 780-820 nm, 1-2 mW (Pandey, 2018); 810 nm in Stabholz et al (2016). Fibre-optic probe with emitting and receiving fibres (separation 250-500 μm) (Muruppel, 2017), signal processor and display/recorder; 810 nm with 3.1 kHz bandwidth performed best (Pandey, 2018).

## 4. Technique

### Explanation

The sources agree that the critical problem is signal from outside the pulp:
- Up to 80% of the signal from an intact tooth without rubber dam may be of nonpulpal origin; rubber dam with a rigid splint is recommended (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC].
- The probe cannot be hand-held because of motion; an individual stabilising stent is made, and the gingiva is covered with dark dam or aluminium foil (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri lists the need for a custom jig (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
- Probe position should be reproducible for repeat readings (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; Andreasen recommends 2-3 mm from the facial gingiva (Andreasen et al, 2007) [SRC].
- George and Walsh suggest placing the probe on dentine in the floor of a cavity rather than on enamel, to improve signal-to-noise (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].
- Patient posture and heart rate also alter readings (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].
- A control (contralateral or adjacent) tooth is recorded for comparison; Andreasen's figure shows a nonvital tooth trace beside an adjacent normal control (Andreasen et al, 2007) [SRC].

Each tooth took 10-15 min at the time Andreasen wrote (Andreasen et al, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Technique**
1. Isolate with opaque (dark) rubber dam or foil over the gingiva (Pandey, 2018).
2. Fix the probe in a custom splint/stent, 2-3 mm from the gingival margin, labial surface (Andreasen et al, 2007).
3. Record the test tooth and a control tooth; repeat in the same position over time.
4. Patient still; about 10-15 min per tooth.

## 5. Uses

### Explanation

- **Traumatised teeth.** LDF diagnosed pulp revascularisation about 3-4 months before EPT (Andreasen et al, 2007) [SRC]. In luxated teeth, readings over time help identify "at-risk" teeth and trigger treatment (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Kahler reports detection of blood flow as early as 2-3 weeks after avulsion of immature dog teeth (Kahler, 2021) [SRC].
- **Children and patients who cannot communicate**, whose responses to sensibility tests are unreliable (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC].
- **Orthodontics and orthognathic surgery**: monitoring pulpal blood flow during tooth movement (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC].
- **Objective record**: data are objective rather than subjective (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Uses**
- Traumatised and luxated teeth: detects revascularisation about 3-4 months before EPT (Andreasen et al, 2007); identifies "at-risk" teeth.
- Replanted and immature teeth.
- Young children and uncommunicative patients (Stabholz et al, 2016).
- Pulp monitoring in orthodontics and orthognathic surgery (Muruppel, 2017).

## 6. Limitations

### Explanation

- **Nonpulpal signal**: periodontal and gingival blood flow (Stabholz et al, 2016; George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].
- **Tooth factors**: molars, with thicker enamel and dentine, give variable readings (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]; light does not pass through amalgam or gold crowns (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; blood pigment in a discoloured crown interferes (Andreasen et al, 2007) [SRC].
- **Artefacts**: movement and changes in ambient light (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; systemic medications alter pulpal flow (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
- **Practical**: high setup cost (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; cost has kept it from becoming routine (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC]; Cohen's states it is not used routinely (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC]; Banday et al cite procedural complexity, cost and clinical constraints (Banday et al, 2026) [SRC]; time per tooth (Andreasen et al, 2007) [SRC].

George and Walsh describe LDF as coming closest to a "gold standard" (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC], yet the same paragraph lists its interferences. "Most accurate available test" is defensible; "gold standard" overstates it given that it is not in routine use [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Limitations**
- Signal contamination from gingiva/periodontium (up to 80% without dam) (Stabholz et al, 2016).
- Poor in molars, under amalgam or crowns, and in discoloured teeth (Andreasen et al, 2007).
- Movement and ambient-light artefacts; drugs alter flow.
- Expensive, slow, needs splint; not routine (Berman and Rotstein, 2021).

## 7. Accuracy and comparison with pulse oximetry

### Explanation

**Accuracy figures differ by study design and are shown side by side:**
- Andreasen: in traumatised incisors without electrometric response, sensitivity 97% (Andreasen et al, 2007) [SRC] and specificity 100% (Andreasen et al, 2007) [SRC].
- Muruppel: 80-90% accuracy for vitality assessment (Muruppel, 2017) [SRC].
- A 2018 meta-analysis of 28 studies gave pooled sensitivity 0.98 and specificity 0.95 for LDF, similar to pulse oximetry and higher than cold, heat or electric tests (Mainkar and Kim, 2018) [LIT]. A 2022 commentary on a later review found too few LDF data for meta-analysis (Adam, 2022) [LIT].

**Pulse oximetry** measures oxygen saturation and pulse rate (Berman and Rotstein, 2021) [SRC], using red (660 nm) and infrared (800 nm) light (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], usually in transmission across the tooth. Its pulpal drawback is that pulp blood flow may not be pulsatile enough to detect (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. LDF instead measures red-cell movement directly, in reflection. Transmitted-light approaches avoid periodontal signal (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Accuracy:** sensitivity 97%, specificity 100% in traumatised incisors (Andreasen et al, 2007); pooled 0.98 and 0.95 (Mainkar and Kim, 2018).

| Measures | RBC flux | O2 saturation, pulse |
| Light | 632.8 or 780-820 nm | 660 and 800 nm |

## High-yield points
- LDF = vitality (blood flow); thermal/EPT = sensibility (nerves).
- Doppler shift from moving RBCs; output = flux (number × velocity), arbitrary units.
- He-Ne 632.8 nm or diode 780-820 nm, 1-2 mW.
- Rubber dam (opaque) + splint; up to 80% nonpulpal signal without dam.
- Best use: trauma, detecting revascularisation 3-4 months before EPT.
- Limits: cost, time, molars, restorations, discoloration; not routine.

## Common exam mistakes
- Calling LDF a sensibility test.
- Forgetting the rubber dam and splint (and why: gingival signal).
- Giving absolute "normal" flow values; readings are relative.
- Calling 632.8 nm infrared.
- Claiming LDF is in routine clinical use.
