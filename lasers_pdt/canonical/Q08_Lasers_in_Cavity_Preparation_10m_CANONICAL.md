## 1. Introduction and lasers used

### Explanation

Poli: of the wavelengths available "only three can be used for hard tissue": Er:YAG 2940 nm, Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm and a CO2 laser whose "emission wavelength is 9300 nm" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Earlier lasers failed: the Nd:YAG was of little use "because of its very low absorption in sound enamel or dentin", and the continuous 10,600 nm CO2 laser gave "charring, cracking, and damaging heat buildup" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Erbium cutting was characterised by Hibst "in the mid-1980s" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Pandey dates the first Er:YAG cavity preparation report to Keller et al in 1988 and FDA clearance to May 1997 (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. The two dates for first description differ by book; give both.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Laser cavity preparation uses pulsed mid-infrared lasers to ablate caries and tooth tissue without a rotating bur. Lasers used: Er:YAG (2940 nm), Er,Cr:YSGG (2780 nm) and short-pulsed CO2 (9300 nm) (Poli, 2017). Nd:YAG and continuous 10,600 nm CO2 lasers are unsuitable because they are poorly absorbed or cause charring, cracking and pulpal heating (Parker, 2016). Er:YAG was cleared by the FDA in 1997 (Pandey, 2018).

## 2. Mechanism

### Explanation

Mount: the erbium beam causes "an explosive subsurface expansion of interstitially trapped water", and "A wavelength of 2940 nm will match exactly the maximal absorption of water" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; Gonçalves calls the process "photomechanical ablation" (Gonçalves et al, 2020) [SRC] and Parker "spallation" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Penetration is 7 µm in enamel and 5 µm in dentine for Er:YAG (Poli, 2017) [SRC]. Carious dentine contains more water: "This phenomenon is at the basis of selective ablation" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; healthy dentine's threshold is "2 times higher" than carious dentine (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Mechanism:** the wavelength is absorbed by water within a few micrometres; interstitial water flashes to steam and the micro-explosion ejects mineral (photomechanical ablation). Carious dentine, with more water, ablates at lower energy, giving relative selectivity (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016; Poli, 2017).

## 3. Parameters and technique

### Explanation

Parameters differ between books, so both are shown:

| Item | Poli, 2017 | Parker, 2016 |
|---|---|---|
| Ablation threshold | Er:YAG 8-11 J/cm2; Er,Cr:YSGG 10-14 J/cm2 | enamel 12-20, dentine 8-14 J/cm2 |
| Energy per pulse | about 125 mJ caries/primary dentine; 225 mJ permanent enamel | 150-250 mJ |

(Poli, 2017; Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Technique points: the tip "at about 1 mm", angle not more than 45° (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; end-on cutting is "at variance with that of the rotary bur" (Parker, 2016) [SRC], so undermined enamel is removed with hand instruments (Flax, 2011) [SRC]; "It is imperative that the laser beam be kept moving at all times" and "Tactile feedback is intrinsically lacking" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. The popping sound helps judge the end point (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]; "Caries-detecting dyes are unsuitable for use with lasers" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. After preparation: bevel at 40-80 mJ, acid-etch enamel, liner in deep cavities (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Technique**
1. Laser analgesia (sub-ablative pre-irradiation), rubber dam.
2. Energy: enamel about 225 mJ, dentine 150 mJ, caries 125 mJ per pulse (Poli, 2017); thresholds and settings differ between books.
3. Tip about 1 mm from surface, angle to wall under 45°, slow continuous movement, ample water spray (Poli, 2017).
4. Bulk soft caries removed with excavators; undermined enamel with hand instruments or a fine bur, as the laser cuts end-on (Parker, 2016).
5. End point by change in popping sound and tactile check; dyes give false positives (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016).
6. Bevel margins at low energy, phosphoric acid etching, liner in deep cavities, incremental composite (Poli, 2017).

## 4. Advantages

### Explanation

Pulp: heat "carried in the ablated particles" keeps pulpal rise "less than 5° C" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; average 1-2 °C (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; no histological injury (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Poli's table gives "Thermal rise <5 °C" in 2017 and "<15 °C" in 2023 (Poli, 2017; Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]; the 2023 text still says below 5 °C, so the table change is probably an error [Likely]. Anaesthesia: "only 2–5% of patients will request local anaesthesia" (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016) [SRC]. Vibration "400 times less" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; bacteria killed to "300–400 μ" (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages:** conservative, relatively selective caries removal; often no injection (2-5% request anaesthesia) (Mount, Walsh and Brostek, 2016); no vibration, less noise; pulpal rise below 5 °C with water spray (Parker, 2016); no smear layer, micro-rough surface; decontamination of dentine; soft tissue can be treated in the same visit (Poli, 2017).

## 5. Limitations

### Explanation

Speed: enamel "<30% bur speed" (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; "80% slower speed in enamel", and about 20% of turbine speed on fluoridated occlusal enamel (Parker, 2016) [SRC]; class I 10-15 min, class II 13-20 min (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. "No laser is capable of removing amalgam or gold restorations" (Parker, 2016) [SRC]. Bond values with laser conditioning alone "came out much lower" than with acid (Poli, 2017) [SRC]; uncovered lased enamel is up to seven times rougher (Poli et al, 2023) [SRC]. Lasers "are not completely able to replace traditional bur" (Poli, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Limitations:** slow on enamel (under 30% of bur speed; class II 13-20 min) (Poli, 2017; Pandey, 2018); no tactile feedback, end-cutting, no sharp line angles or defined margins; cannot remove amalgam, gold or ceramic (Parker, 2016); bonding needs acid etching; risk of cracking and pulpal heating without water; costly, learning curve, eye protection required.

**Conclusion:** erbium lasers are a useful adjunct for small cavities, children and anxious patients, but do not replace the bur.

## High-yield points
- Er:YAG 2940 nm, Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm, CO2 9300 nm.
- Water-mediated photomechanical ablation; caries ablates at lower energy.
- Tip 1 mm, angle under 45°, keep moving, water spray.
- Pulpal rise under 5 °C; 2-5% need local anaesthesia.
- Always acid-etch after laser preparation; cannot remove metal restorations.

## Common exam mistakes
- Naming Nd:YAG or diode lasers as cavity preparation lasers.
- Claiming lasers remove amalgam or are faster than burs on enamel.
- Omitting acid etching after laser preparation.
- Giving one set of energy values as universal without naming the source.
