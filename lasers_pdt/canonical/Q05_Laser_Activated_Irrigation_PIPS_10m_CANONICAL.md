## 1. Definition and principle

### Explanation

Laser-activated irrigation (LAI) uses laser energy to agitate an irrigant already in the canal rather than to irradiate the canal wall. George and Walsh date the first description of laser fluid activation to 2008 (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC] and describe PIPS as one variant of LAI, typically used with sodium hypochlorite (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. DeVito and co-workers patented the technique (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC]; Sikri calls it a cleaning method using Er:YAG energy at subablative power levels (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Why an erbium laser.** Unlike Nd:YAG, Er:YAG output (2940 nm) is strongly absorbed by water (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC], so the pulse is spent in the irrigant rather than in the dentine [Likely]. The sequence in the fluid is:
1. Absorption heats a minute volume of water, which flashes into a vapour bubble.
2. The bubble expands and then implodes; this is cavitation (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey describes a primary expansion and a secondary cavitation effect on the intracanal fluid (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
3. Bubble collapse produces shock waves and streaming of the fluid along the canal; the fluid motion also breaks the apical vapour lock that defeats needle irrigation (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

**What makes PIPS different from earlier LAI.** PIPS deliberately uses very low pulse energy with very short pulses. Jaramillo describes subablative energies of 20 mJ at 15 Hz, 0.3 W average power and 50 μs pulses, which produce peak powers of 400 W in the water (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC]. The effect is therefore **photoacoustic and photomechanical rather than photothermal** (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]: PIPS works indirectly, by activating the irrigant, without thermal effects (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC]. The name records this: a photon is absorbed, an acoustic (pressure) wave is generated, and the fluid streams.

#### ✎ Exam core
**Definition.** PIPS (photon-induced photoacoustic streaming) is laser-activated irrigation in which an Er:YAG laser (2940 nm) at subablative energy activates irrigant in the pulp chamber, streaming it three-dimensionally through the canal system (Jaramillo, 2015).

**Principle.** Water absorbs the pulse → vapour bubble → expansion and implosion (cavitation) → shock wave → streaming; the vapour lock is broken (George and Walsh, 2017). Short pulses give high peak power: mainly photoacoustic rather than photothermal (Pandey, 2018).

## 2. Tip, parameters and technique

### Explanation

**Tip.** The books give two tip descriptions, and both are recorded as printed:
- Pandey: a 9 mm long, 600 μm quartz tapered tip with the polyamide sheath stripped 3 mm from its end (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Stripping lets energy leave laterally rather than only from the end. Pandey's figure shows two tips, 400/12 and 600/9 (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- Jaramillo reports DiVito's smear-layer study as using a 12 mm, 400 μm quartz tip (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC].

**Parameters.** These differ between sources, so each is shown with its source and not reconciled:

| Parameter | Jaramillo, 2015 | Pandey, 2018 (PIPS) | Pandey, 2018 (Twinlight protocol, PIPS step) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pulse energy | 20 mJ | 50 down to 20 mJ | 10-20 mJ |
| Repetition rate | 15 Hz | 10-15 Hz | 10-50 Hz |
| Pulse duration | 50 μs | 50 μs | not stated |
| Average power | 0.3 W | 0.3 W or less | not stated |
| Peak power | 400 W | 400 W | not stated |

(Jaramillo, 2015; Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Pandey states the PIPS energy range as 50 to 20 mJ at 10 to 15 Hz with 50 μs pulses (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] and gives 10-20 mJ at 10-50 Hz with EDTA for the PIPS step of the Twinlight protocol (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Tip position.** Traditional laser endodontics places the fibre 1 mm short of the working length; earlier LAI used 5 mm short of the apex (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. The PIPS tip is not placed in the canal at all, only in the pulp chamber (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC], held stationary in the coronal chamber with the canal orifices open and the chamber flooded. Jaramillo argues this reduces the need for larger files and rotary instruments to create larger canal shapes (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC]; that is the minimally invasive claim, and it rests on the laboratory fluid data below [Likely].

**Manufacturer protocol** as reported by Jaramillo (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC]:
1. NaOCl with PIPS in 30 s activation cycles separated by 30 s rest periods (the chapter text reads "six 30 s cycle[s]" of activation with three 30 s rests, then says "3–30 s cycles" with NaOCl; this may mean six 30 s periods, three on and three off [Guessing]; Fig. 13.6, which shows the protocol, is not reproduced in the file, so check the cycle count against the printed figure).
2. 30 s of PIPS with water.
3. Chamber emptied; 17% EDTA activated with continuous flow for 30 s (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC].
4. A final 30 s of water with PIPS; the canal is then ready for obturation.

Irrigant must be replenished during activation; refreshing the fluid between lasing cycles also removes the heat of each cycle (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Tip:** tapered quartz, terminal 3 mm sheath stripped for lateral emission; 9 mm/600 μm (Pandey, 2018) or 12 mm/400 μm (Jaramillo, 2015).

**Parameters** (books differ): 20 mJ, 15 Hz, 0.3 W, 50 μs, about 400 W peak (Jaramillo, 2015); 20-50 mJ, 10-15 Hz (Pandey, 2018).

**Technique**
1. Minimal shaping; flood the chamber.
2. Tip held stationary in the pulp chamber, never in the canal.
3. NaOCl in 30 s cycles with rests, replenished.
4. Water, then 17% EDTA, then water, each activated (Jaramillo, 2015).

## 3. Comparison with needle and ultrasonic irrigation

### Explanation

**Fluid dynamics.** Jaramillo reports Fincham's velocimetry: PIPS gave over 1.2 m/s at 3 mm from the tip and about 0.3 m/s at 5, 10 and 15 mm, with no further fall with distance after the initial drop (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC]. Pandey reports that at 21 mm PIPS velocities were 4 times those of an ultrasonic device (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], but these came from as-yet-unpublished fluid dynamics studies (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], so that figure is weaker evidence than a published study [Certain]. With needle irrigation, computational models always showed laminar flow, with maximum velocity near the needle end (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC]. Passive ultrasonic streaming stays close to the file; Pandey describes ultrasonic fluid movement as linear, standing-wave type and limited to 0.5-1.0 mm of the tip (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], whereas PIPS showed more "turbulent" flow, including far from the tip (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Debris, smear layer and bacteria (laboratory and ex vivo).**
- Lloyd et al: PIPS removed debris better than single-needle irrigation in extracted molars (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC].
- Ordinola et al: PIPS cleaned infected dentine better than passive ultrasonic irrigation even though the PIPS tip was 22 mm from the target (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC].
- Jaramillo et al, in vitro model of infected single-rooted teeth (E. faecalis): 83% disinfection after 20 min of conventional irrigation versus 100% with 1 min of PIPS (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC].
- Peters et al (reported by Pandey): 99.5% reduction of a 3-week biofilm with PIPS and NaOCl (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Jaramillo's account of the Peters study adds that PIPS did not remove bacteria from infected tubules completely (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC].
- In Pedulla et al's E. faecalis study (reported by Pandey), Er:YAG activation of distilled water alone gave only 73% reduction (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], so the chemistry of NaOCl is still required; PIPS improves delivery, it does not replace the irrigant [Certain].

A 2025 systematic review of 14 in vitro SEM studies found PIPS generally better than syringe irrigation for smear-layer removal, better in the coronal and middle thirds than the apical third, with most studies at medium or high risk of bias (Brum et al, 2025) [LIT]. A network meta-analysis of micro-CT studies ranked SWEEPS, then PIPS, highest for removing accumulated hard-tissue debris from mandibular mesial roots, but no technique left canals debris-free and all included studies were laboratory studies (Natanasabapathy et al, 2021) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Versus needle and ultrasonic irrigation**
- Needle: laminar flow, fastest near the needle end (Jaramillo, 2015); ultrasonic: movement within 0.5-1.0 mm of the tip; PIPS: turbulent flow far from the tip (Pandey, 2018).
- Velocity >1.2 m/s at 3 mm, no further loss with distance (Jaramillo, 2015).
- Better debris removal than single-needle irrigation in extracted molars (Stabholz et al, 2016).
- E. faecalis: 100% versus 83% disinfection, in vitro (Jaramillo, 2015); water alone only 73%, so NaOCl remains essential (Pandey, 2018).

## 4. SWEEPS, the successor

### Explanation

The 2nd edition of Coluzzi and Parker adds SWEEPS (shock wave-enhanced emission photoacoustic streaming), which the 1st edition does not mention. PIPS uses a single short pulse, while SWEEPS delivers pulses in pairs (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC]; the second pulse accelerates the collapse of the first bubble and generates new bubbles (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC], so more shock waves are produced in the canal. A further evolution, Resonant SWEEPS (R-SWEEPS), aims at more wall pressure without more apical extrusion (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC].

Clinically relevant claim: SWEEPS allows NaOCl to be used at a lower concentration without loss of antimicrobial effect (Guruswamy Pandian et al, 2026) [SRC]. The 2nd edition gives a related figure for a different laser: Er,Cr:YSGG LAI for 60 s made 0.5% NaOCl as effective as 2.5% (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC]. Both statements come from laboratory work [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core
**SWEEPS** (shock wave-enhanced emission photoacoustic streaming): paired pulses, the second accelerating bubble collapse, giving more shock waves (George and Walsh, 2023); may allow lower NaOCl concentration (Guruswamy Pandian et al, 2026).

## 5. Safety: heat and apical extrusion

### Explanation

**Heat.** In DiVito's study the outer root surface rose 1.4 °C after 40 s of continuous activation (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC]. The threshold usually quoted for bone injury is more than 10 °C for more than 1 min (Jaramillo, 2015) [SRC]. The 1.4 °C figure is an in vitro measurement.

**Extrusion.** This is where the sources disagree:
- George and Walsh: with bare or conical tips 5 or 10 mm from the apex, extruded droplets were no greater than with a conventional 25-gauge non-side-vented needle (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. PIPS aims to move fluid along the walls rather than toward the foramen (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC], although several studies show small volumes are extruded (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC]. Their key points warn that excessively high pulse energies can push fluid through the foramen (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].
- Guruswamy Pandian et al: light-activated techniques show negligible apical extrusion (Guruswamy Pandian et al, 2026) [SRC]. The cited support is a dye-extrusion study.
- Mortman, writing in 2011 before PIPS had been published on, cautioned that pressure waves from pulsed lasers can extrude fluid (Mortman, 2011) [SRC].
- A 2026 systematic review of 12 studies found that PIPS and high-power lasers extruded more debris than conventional irrigation, with very low certainty of evidence (de Assis et al, 2026) [LIT].

The difference may partly be one of outcome: fluid (irrigant) versus debris extrusion, and dye models versus weighed debris [Guessing]. The safe position for the exam is that PIPS is not extrusion-free, that energy must stay low, and that the tip must stay in the chamber [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Safety**
- Heat: 1.4 °C root-surface rise after 40 s in vitro, below the 10 °C threshold (Jaramillo, 2015).
- Extrusion conflicting: erbium laser tips 5-10 mm from the apex extruded no more than a 25-gauge needle (George and Walsh, 2017), but a 2026 systematic review found more debris extrusion (de Assis et al, 2026).

## 6. Evidence, advantages and limitations

### Explanation

Convissar's chapter states the position plainly: in vitro studies have established proof of principle for this technique (Stabholz et al, 2016) [SRC], and long-term clinical comparisons are still needed. George and Walsh (2nd ed) hope future research will include not only laboratory studies but also outcome studies (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC]. Siqueira and Rôças cite one clinical study in which PIPS improved disinfection and did not change postoperative pain (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC], while also noting that no clinical studies consistently show the safety and effectiveness of intracanal laser devices (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC].

**Postoperative pain.** Guruswamy Pandian et al report less pain with laser activation than with manual dynamic activation (Guruswamy Pandian et al, 2026) [SRC]. A 2024 systematic review of six RCTs (five at high risk of bias) found no difference between PIPS and needle irrigation, but less pain with PIPS than with manual dynamic, sonic or ultrasonic activation (McGillivray and Dutta, 2024) [LIT]. No study in this set reports periapical healing outcomes for PIPS [Likely].

**Advantages:** tip stays out of the canal (no ledging, no apical laser exposure); minimal heat; less need for apical enlargement; activates NaOCl and EDTA throughout complex anatomy (Jaramillo, 2015; Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
**Limitations:** cost of an Er:YAG unit; only erbium lasers can do it; fluid must be replenished continuously; extrusion question unresolved; evidence mainly laboratory (Stabholz et al, 2016; de Assis et al, 2026).

#### ✎ Exam core
**Advantages:** tip outside canal; minimal heat; less apical enlargement; possibly less early pain than ultrasonic activation (McGillivray and Dutta, 2024).

**Limitations:** costly erbium laser; continuous replenishment; extrusion unsettled; evidence is in vitro "proof of principle" without healing-outcome trials (Stabholz et al, 2016).

**Conclusion:** PIPS improves irrigant delivery; it does not replace shaping or NaOCl.

## High-yield points
- PIPS = Er:YAG 2940 nm, subablative, about 20 mJ, 15 Hz, 50 μs, 0.3 W average, about 400 W peak (Jaramillo); Pandey gives 20-50 mJ, 10-15 Hz.
- Tip: tapered, sheath stripped 3 mm, stays in the pulp chamber.
- Mechanism: water absorption → bubble → cavitation → shock wave → streaming; photoacoustic, not photothermal.
- Lab: 100% vs 83% E. faecalis disinfection; water alone 73%.
- SWEEPS = paired pulses → more shock waves (2nd ed Coluzzi and Parker only).
- Extrusion: books say little; a 2026 systematic review says more debris than needle irrigation.
- Evidence is laboratory "proof of principle"; no outcome trials.

## Common exam mistakes
- Placing the PIPS tip in the canal near working length (that is traditional laser endodontics or older LAI).
- Calling PIPS a thermal disinfection method.
- Writing that PIPS replaces NaOCl; water-only PIPS kills far fewer bacteria.
- Quoting ex vivo bacterial kill as clinical success.
- Claiming PIPS causes no extrusion.
