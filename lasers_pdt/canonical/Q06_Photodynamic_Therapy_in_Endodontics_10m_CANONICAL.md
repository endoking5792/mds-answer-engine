## 1. Definition and terminology

### Explanation

Kishen and Shrestha define PDT as the use of a nontoxic dye or photosensitiser with visible light which, in the presence of molecular oxygen, produces cytotoxic oxygen species such as singlet oxygen (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Three things are therefore needed together: dye, light of the matching wavelength, and oxygen. Remove any one and killing falls sharply; George and Walsh stress that dye alone or laser alone kills much less than the combination (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

The phenomenon was discovered by chance; Oscar Raab used acridine dyes and light to kill paramecia in the early 1900s (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Several names are used for the same procedure: PACT (photodynamic antimicrobial chemotherapy), LAD (light-activated disinfection) and PAD (photo-activated disinfection) (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; George and Walsh add lethal laser photosensitisation (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Mortman lists the same synonyms (Mortman, 2011) [SRC]. So "light activated disinfection" in the 2012 paper is the same topic.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is the use of a nontoxic photosensitising dye activated by light of matching wavelength, in the presence of oxygen, to generate reactive oxygen species (chiefly singlet oxygen) that kill microorganisms (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015).

**Synonyms:** PAD, LAD (light-activated disinfection), PACT (Pandey, 2018).

## 2. Components

### Explanation

**Photosensitisers.** Kishen and Shrestha list halogenated xanthenes (rose bengal), phenothiazines (methylene blue, toluidine blue O) and perylenequinones (hypericin) as the antibacterial photosensitisers in use (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Pandey gives peak absorptions: toluidine blue O 633 nm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], methylene blue 670 nm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], rose bengal 550 nm (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Cationic phenothiazines bind the negatively charged bacterial wall, which gives the selectivity. Newer forms conjugate the dye to chitosan, poly-L-lysine or polyethylenimine, or load it into nanoparticles (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]; this is the research direction rather than routine practice.

**Indocyanine green: the books disagree.**
- Kishen and Shrestha's table of positive studies includes indocyanine green with an 805 nm laser (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC], and Pandey describes the commercial system as using tolonium chloride and indocyanine green (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].
- George and Walsh say ICG with 800-830 nm light acts by heating, a photothermal process and not a photodynamic one (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC], and that ICG is a fluorescence dye and not a photosensitiser (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]; the 2nd edition keeps this sentence unchanged (George and Walsh, 2023) [SRC]. They list ICG and curcumin among photosensitisers "of interest", activated at 808 and 470 nm (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC], so their own chapter is not fully consistent.
In the exam, name methylene blue and toluidine blue as the standard dyes and mention ICG only with this caveat [Likely].

**Light source.** Siqueira and Rôças state that most photosensitisers are activated by red light at 630 to 700 nm (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]. George and Walsh prefer visible red lasers at 633, 635, 660 or 670 nm for blue dyes (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Lasers (He-Ne, GaAlAs, diode, Nd:YAG, KTP), LEDs and lamps have all been used, and no light source has been shown to be superior (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]; LEDs work as well as lasers for dye activation (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Diode lasers dominate because they are portable, easy to handle and cost-effective (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]. Pandey names commercial units, for example Aseptim PAD at 635 nm and 50-100 mW with TBO (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

**Oxygen.** Molecular oxygen in the canal is the substrate for singlet oxygen; low oxygen tension in the canal is one of the constraints (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Components ("dye, light, oxygen")**
1. **Photosensitiser:** methylene blue (peak 670 nm), toluidine blue O (633 nm), rose bengal (550 nm) (Pandey, 2018); newer chitosan- or nanoparticle-conjugated dyes. Indocyanine green is disputed: George and Walsh (2017) call it photothermal, not photodynamic.
2. **Light:** red light 630-700 nm (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022); diode laser or LED with a fibre; e.g. 635 nm, 50-100 mW (Pandey, 2018).
3. **Oxygen** in the tissue.

## 3. Mechanism: Type I and Type II reactions

### Explanation

Kishen (Endodontic Microbiology) describes two steps: photosensitisation, in which the dye binds to bacteria, and photoactivation by light (Kishen, 2017) [SRC]. On absorbing a photon the dye moves to an excited singlet state and then to a longer-lived triplet state. From the triplet state it reacts in one of two ways (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]:
- **Type I:** electron transfer to a substrate produces radical ions (superoxide, hydroxyl, lipid-derived radicals) (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC].
- **Type II:** energy transfer to ground-state oxygen produces excited singlet oxygen (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Kishen and Shrestha state that the antimicrobial effect of PDT is mainly due to the Type II reaction (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC].

Singlet oxygen diffuses only about 50 nm (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC], so the dye must be on or in the bacterial cell; this explains both the selectivity and the poor action in thick biofilm. Targets are the cell wall and membrane, DNA, proteins and enzymes (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]; Sikri describes rupture of the bacterial wall by singlet oxygen (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Because the attack is on many targets at once, bacterial resistance is considered almost impossible (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Pandey states that Gram-positive organisms are more sensitive than Gram-negative ones, a difference he says is marginal with cationic TBO (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Mechanism.** Dye binds bacteria (photosensitisation) → light excites dye to singlet then triplet state (photoactivation) (Kishen, 2017).
- **Type I:** electron transfer → superoxide, hydroxyl radicals.
- **Type II:** energy transfer to O2 → singlet oxygen (main pathway).
Singlet oxygen diffuses only about 50 nm, so killing is localised to dye-bound cells; it damages membrane, proteins and DNA, and resistance is unlikely (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015).

## 4. Clinical technique

### Explanation

PDT does not shape or debride, so it is used after chemomechanical preparation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]. The sources differ on timings; each is shown with its source:

| Step | Pandey, 2018 (PAD system) | Siqueira and Rôças, 2022 | George and Walsh, 2017 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dye | Tolonium chloride, 12.7 mg/L | MB or TBO, low concentration | TBO or MB |
| Pre-irradiation time | 60 s | 2 min, agitated with a #15 K-file | dye placed before laser |
| Irradiation | 120 s | usually 3-5 min, diode laser, optical fibre inserted to working length | typical 60-90 s (figure legend) |

(Pandey, 2018; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022; George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Supporting statements: Pandey's 60 s dwell (Pandey, 2018) [SRC] and 120 s irradiation (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]; Siqueira and Rôças's K-file agitation and 2 min pre-irradiation time (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC], fibre to working length (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC], 3-5 min irradiation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC] and low dye concentration to avoid staining (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]; George and Walsh's figure legend (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Pandey also gives a concentration range of "12–100 mg/mL" [sic] for TBO (Pandey, 2018) [SRC], which conflicts by three orders of magnitude with his 12.7 mg/L and is probably a units error; verify against the printed book [Likely].

**Delivery.** A diffusing fibre inside the canal reduced biofilm by 2 logs more than a tip at the orifice (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]; George and Walsh prefer lateral-emitting tips (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Mortman's sequence is to fill the canal with dye, illuminate it, then dry and obturate (Mortman, 2011) [SRC]; the dye must not stain the tooth permanently (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Technique** (Pandey, 2018; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
1. Complete cleaning, shaping and NaOCl/EDTA irrigation.
2. Fill canal with photosensitiser (e.g. toluidine blue 12.7 mg/L); agitate; dwell 60 s to 2 min.
3. Insert the fibre/emitter to working length; irradiate 120 s (Pandey) to 3-5 min (Siqueira and Rôças) with a red diode laser.
4. Dry and obturate.

## 5. Factors affecting efficacy

### Explanation

Pandey lists dye type, dye concentration, radiation dose and microbial species (Pandey, 2018) [SRC]. Kishen lists five tissue-specific constraints: light penetration, photosensitiser penetration, oxygen availability, dentine discoloration, and inactivation by tissue and serum (Kishen, 2017) [SRC]; the same chapter in Basrani names low oxygen tension and dentine discoloration (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Further points:
- Biofilm: only about 30% of biofilm mass is bacteria (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]; the matrix limits dye diffusion, so biofilm needs more energy than planktonic cells.
- Efflux pumps expel phenothiazine dyes; adding an efflux pump inhibitor gave better biofilm elimination at much lower PDT dosage (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC].
- Pulp remnants and serum reduce PDT efficacy just as they reduce irrigants (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC].
- Formulation: methylene blue in a glycerol-ethanol-water mixture penetrated tubules better and produced more singlet oxygen than in water (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC].
- Light delivery (fibre versus tip) as above.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Factors affecting efficacy:** dye type and concentration; light dose and wavelength match (Pandey, 2018); light, dye and oxygen penetration; biofilm matrix and efflux pumps; inactivation by tissue and serum (Kishen, 2017); intracanal diffusing fibre better than orifice tip.

## 6. Advantages and limitations

### Explanation

**Advantages.** Selective killing: methylene blue PDT killed 97.7% of E. faecalis while causing 30% fibroblast dysfunction in vitro (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Kills within minutes, unlike antibiotics; resistance unlikely (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Thermal effects are minimal (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. A suggested role is the persistent infection that has resisted conventional treatment (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC].

**Limitations.** Dentine discoloration, limited light and oxygen in the canal, inactivation by tissue fluids (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]; no standard protocol exists (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]; equipment cost [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages:** selective for bacteria (97.7% E. faecalis kill versus 30% fibroblast dysfunction in vitro); rapid; no resistance; no heat; useful for persistent infections (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015; George and Walsh, 2017).

**Limitations:** dentine staining; poor light/oxygen penetration; inactivated by tissue fluids; no standard protocol; cost.

## 7. Evidence: an adjunct to NaOCl, not a replacement

### Explanation

Guruswamy Pandian et al group PDT with PIPS as methods that enhance NaOCl penetration into dentinal tubules (Guruswamy Pandian et al, 2026) [SRC]. All sources agree that PDT is an adjunct after conventional disinfection, not a replacement (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Evidence by type:

**Laboratory.** Soukos et al eliminated all tested endodontic pathogens except E. faecalis, of which 53% were killed (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. A review of 17 studies found 70% reporting a benefit against E. faecalis, with wide variation in parameters (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. George and Walsh count 12 studies reporting PAD effective against E. faecalis (George and Walsh, 2017) [SRC]. Souza et al found no significant enhancement when PDT with MB or TBO followed NaOCl preparation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]. Ex vivo, Ng et al found 86.5% negative cultures with PDT after 6% NaOCl versus 49% without (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]. A 2026 meta-analysis of seven ex vivo studies favoured NaOCl plus PDT over NaOCl alone, but only with methylene blue; toluidine blue showed no clear benefit, and heterogeneity was high (Oliveira-Aguiar et al, 2026) [LIT].

**Clinical.** Bonsor et al: complete killing in 86.7% of canals after cleaning and shaping and 96.7% after adding PDT (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. Garcez et al: in retreatment of teeth with antibiotic-resistant flora, PDT plus endodontic therapy left all teeth bacteria-free (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015) [SRC]. A 2023 meta-analysis of clinical trials in primary infections found that aPDT after chemomechanical disinfection gave a further, statistically significant reduction in microbial load, with certainty of evidence still needing improvement (Quintana et al, 2023) [LIT]. These are microbial counts, not healing outcomes; a systematic review cited by Siqueira and Rôças concluded that information supporting clinical use is insufficient (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC], and Pandey notes a lack of in vivo research (Pandey, 2018) [SRC].

The books are therefore split: the Kishen chapters and George and Walsh lean positive; Siqueira and Rôças are sceptical until outcome trials exist. Both positions rest mainly on bacterial counts [Certain].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Evidence.** PDT is an adjunct, not a replacement (Kishen and Shrestha, 2015).
- Clinical: complete killing rose from 86.7% after cleaning and shaping to 96.7% with PDT (Bonsor, in Kishen and Shrestha, 2015); a 2023 meta-analysis found extra microbial reduction (Quintana et al, 2023).
- Ex vivo: benefit with methylene blue, not clearly with toluidine blue (Oliveira-Aguiar et al, 2026); one study showed no gain after NaOCl (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- No healing-outcome trials yet.

## High-yield points
- Dye + matching light + oxygen; dye or light alone kills little.
- Type I = radicals by electron transfer; Type II = singlet oxygen by energy transfer.
- Singlet oxygen range about 50 nm: dye must sit on the bacterium.
- MB 670 nm, TBO 633 nm, RB 550 nm; red light 630-700 nm; diode laser or LED.
- Use after chemomechanical preparation; diffusing fibre in the canal.
- ICG is disputed (photothermal per George and Walsh).
- Evidence = microbial counts; adjunct to NaOCl, not a substitute.

## Common exam mistakes
- Treating PDT as a high-power thermal laser method.
- Leaving out oxygen as the third component.
- Writing that PDT replaces NaOCl or shaping.
- Presenting ex vivo E. faecalis kill rates as clinical success.
- Naming ICG as a standard photosensitiser without the caveat.
