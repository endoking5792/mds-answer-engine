## 1. Definition and rationale

### Explanation

Postoperative endodontic pain is common, especially with preoperative pain, pulp necrosis and symptomatic apical periodontitis, and has traditionally been managed with paracetamol, NSAIDs, opioids or corticosteroids (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Cryotherapy, the therapeutic use of cold borrowed from sports and surgical medicine, was proposed as a drug-free alternative.

**Mechanism.** The general effects of local cold are vasoconstriction, reduced oedema, slower nerve conduction and reduced release of inflammatory mediators [RECALL]. The endodontic rationale is that cold saline in the canal cools the periradicular tissues: in extracted teeth (ex vivo), a final irrigation with 2.5 °C saline lowered the external root surface temperature by more than 10 °C and kept it there for 4 minutes, which the authors suggested may be enough for a local anti-inflammatory effect (Vera et al, 2015) [LIT]. A clinical trial of an intraoral ice-gel pack found lower postoperative pain and lower substance P in apical fluid than controls (Hamza et al, 2024) [LIT], which gives a biochemical correlate, not proof of mechanism [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** cryotherapy is the therapeutic application of cold to reduce pain and inflammation. In endodontics it most often means a final irrigation of the canal with cold (2.5 °C) sterile saline after cleaning and shaping (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021).

**Rationale/mechanism**
- Cold causes vasoconstriction, less oedema, slower nerve conduction and fewer inflammatory mediators.
- 2.5 °C saline lowered the external root surface temperature by more than 10 °C for 4 minutes in extracted teeth (Vera et al, 2015), enough for a local anti-inflammatory effect in periradicular tissue.
- Intraoral cold packs reduced substance P in apical fluid (Hamza et al, 2024).

## 2. Types

### Explanation

Peters, Arias and Shabahang list intracanal, intraoral and extraoral applications, all reported to lower postoperative pain (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. Intraoral use has been tested as an ice pack on the vestibule after obturation (Ajeesh et al, 2023) [LIT] and as a custom ice-gel pack for 30 minutes after canal preparation (Hamza et al, 2024) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Types** (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021)
1. Intracanal: cold saline final irrigation.
2. Intraoral: ice pack on the buccal vestibule of the treated tooth.
3. Extraoral: cold pack on the face.

## 3. Intracanal protocol

### Explanation

The protocol described by Vera et al and reproduced in both textbooks: after cleaning and shaping, a final irrigation with cold (2.5 °C) sterile saline, delivered through a cold (2.5 °C) sterile microcannula attached to the EndoVac negative-pressure system (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC] for 5 minutes (Peters, Arias and Shabahang, 2021) [SRC]. The trial used 20 mL of saline delivered to working length (Vera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Negative-pressure delivery matters because the cold irrigant has to reach the apical third without extrusion [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Intracanal protocol** (Vera et al, 2018)
1. Complete cleaning, shaping and the usual NaOCl/EDTA irrigation.
2. Final irrigation: 20 mL sterile saline at 2.5 °C.
3. Deliver to working length through a cold sterile EndoVac microcannula for 5 minutes.
4. Dry and obturate or dress as usual.

## 4. Clinical evidence

### Explanation

**Landmark RCT.** In a multicentre RCT of 210 patients with necrotic single-rooted teeth and symptomatic apical periodontitis, controls irrigated with room-temperature saline had significantly more postoperative pain, higher intensity and more analgesic intake than the cold-saline group (Vera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Teixeira and Cheung report the same conclusion (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021) [SRC].

**Systematic reviews** (all abstracts only):
- 8 RCTs, 810 patients: pain lower at 6 and 24 h, moderate-quality evidence; no effect at 48 h, 72 h or 7 days (Sadaf et al, 2020) [LIT].
- Lower pain at 6 and 24 h with very low certainty; similar at 48 and 72 h (Monteiro et al, 2021) [LIT].
- 16 studies in qualitative and 9 in quantitative analysis: reductions to 72 h, all low-quality evidence with high heterogeneity (Almohaimede and Al-Madi, 2021) [LIT].
- Benefit at 24 h in symptomatic apical periodontitis; no difference in teeth with normal periapical tissue (Hespanhol et al, 2022) [LIT].
- 16 RCTs, 1479 participants: fewer patients with pain (risk ratio 0.72) and lower intensity at 6, 24 and 48 h, less at 72 h (Huang et al, 2026) [LIT].
- Irreversible pulpitis, 12 RCTs: reduction at 6 and 12 h only (Mohamed et al, 2026) [LIT].
- Intraoral versus intracanal: both lowered pain at 6 and 24 h with no difference between them (Ajeesh et al, 2023) [LIT].

The reviews agree on an early, short-lived benefit, strongest in symptomatic apical periodontitis; certainty ranges from very low to moderate because trials are small and heterogeneous [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Evidence**
- RCT (Vera et al, 2018), 210 patients with necrotic teeth and symptomatic apical periodontitis: cold saline reduced incidence and intensity of postoperative pain and analgesic use.
- Meta-analyses: pain reduced at 6 and 24 h, not at 48-72 h (Sadaf et al, 2020: 8 RCTs, moderate quality); 16 RCTs, risk ratio 0.72 (Huang et al, 2026).
- Benefit seen in symptomatic apical periodontitis, not in teeth with normal periapex (Hespanhol et al, 2022); in irreversible pulpitis only at 6-12 h (Mohamed et al, 2026).
- Intraoral and intracanal cryotherapy equally effective (Ajeesh et al, 2023).
- Certainty is low to moderate; heterogeneous small trials.

## 5. Advantages, limitations and indications

### Explanation

Its attractions are low cost, simplicity and no systemic drug effects (Huang et al, 2026; Mohamed et al, 2026) [LIT]. Limitations: the effect is confined to the first day or two; a negative-pressure system and a cold chain for saline are needed; the in vitro temperature data come from extracted teeth without periodontal blood flow, so the true periradicular temperature in vivo is unknown [Likely]. It is an adjunct and does not replace thorough debridement or analgesics when indicated [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Advantages:** simple, cheap, non-pharmacological, no systemic side effects, may reduce analgesic need.

**Limitations:** effect lasts only 6-24 h; needs negative-pressure delivery; temperature data are ex vivo; protocols vary; low-certainty evidence.

**Indication:** best supported as an adjunct in necrotic teeth with symptomatic apical periodontitis.

**Conclusion:** intracanal cryotherapy is a promising, inexpensive adjunct for early postoperative pain, but larger, standardised RCTs are needed.

## High-yield points
- Cold (2.5 °C) saline, 20 mL, 5 minutes, EndoVac microcannula (Vera et al, 2018).
- Root surface cooled by more than 10 °C for 4 minutes, ex vivo (Vera et al, 2015).
- Pain reduced at 6 and 24 h, not 48-72 h (Sadaf et al, 2020).
- Benefit mainly in symptomatic apical periodontitis.
- Three types: intracanal, intraoral, extraoral.

## Common exam mistakes
- Confusing cryotherapy with cryosurgery or with cold pulp testing.
- Claiming a long-lasting effect; benefit is mostly within 24 h.
- Presenting the root-surface temperature drop as a clinical measurement; it was in extracted teeth.
- Omitting the negative-pressure delivery that the protocol uses.
