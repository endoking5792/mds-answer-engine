## STUDY MODE

### S1. What it is [basic science]
Alkasite materials are bulk-fill, resin-based restoratives with an alkaline glass filler that releases hydroxide, calcium and fluoride ions; the hydroxyl release may raise pH and neutralise acid, and the ions "may contribute" to remineralisation (Bonchev et al, 2025). Bonchev et al (2025) describe them as resin-based, whereas a DCNA review describes the commercial ion-releasing restoratives (Activa BioActive Restorative, Cention N, Surefil One, on the market from 2013) as glass-ionomer-type materials with more resin and acid-reactive fillers, and says their short- and long-term behaviour remains unclear (Melo et al, 2022); the two sources group the material differently. The composition and ion-release statements are as reported in abstracts [abstract only; not textbook-verified].

### S2. Properties [treatment relevance]
Alkasite materials are suited to Class I, II and V cavities in primary and permanent teeth, and show comparable or superior physical and mechanical properties to common restoratives (Bonchev et al, 2025). A network meta-analysis of in-vitro studies found a representative alkasite (Cention N) had physical and mechanical properties similar to composite and stronger than glass ionomer, though glass ionomers "may still" show the greatest fluoride release of the direct materials in vitro (Justen et al, 2024).

### S3. Clinical evidence [prognosis]
A meta-analysis of clinical studies found comparable clinical performance of alkasite against resin composite, and superior outcomes against glass ionomer, but gave no pooled numbers in the abstract and reported moderate risk of bias in several studies (Laporte et al, 2026). A small non-randomised pilot in 16 children (36 restorations, 6 months) found 3.6% dislodgement for an alkasite against 75% for Fuji IX, with unequal groups, so it generates a hypothesis only (Padilla-Ocampo et al, 2025). A meta-analysis of 40 RCTs found bioactive materials reduced secondary caries risk by 45% (RR 0.55), but the reported subgroup benefit was in glass ionomers (RR 0.36) and not giomers (RR 1.09); the abstract gives no alkasite-specific result (Zailai et al, 2026).

### S4. Conclusion and caution
Alkasite materials look like a clinically acceptable alternative with composite-like strength and some ion release, but follow-up is short and the remineralisation claim is mostly laboratory-based. The exam point is that they are described as resin-based and not as conventional glass ionomers (Bonchev et al, 2025), although Melo et al (2022) group such products with glass-ionomer-type materials.

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Alkasite restoratives are a newer class of bulk-fill, ion-releasing, resin-based direct restorative materials; Cention N is the commonly cited example (Justen et al, 2024; Laporte et al, 2026).

### 2. Composition and mechanism
- **Filler:** alkaline (alkasite) glass in a resin matrix (Bonchev et al, 2025).
- **Ions released:** hydroxide, calcium and fluoride; hydroxyl release may raise pH and neutralise acid (Bonchev et al, 2025).
- **Remineralisation:** claimed contribution ("may contribute"), not demonstrated clinically (Bonchev et al, 2025).
- **Setting:** resin-based, bulk fill; curing mode not stated in the abstracts (Bonchev et al, 2025).

### 3. Properties
| Property | Finding |
|---|---|
| Strength | similar to composite, stronger than GIC in vitro (Justen et al, 2024) |
| Fluoride release | lower than GIC in vitro; GICs "may still" release most (Justen et al, 2024) |
| Placement | bulk fill (Bonchev et al, 2025) |
| Aesthetics | tooth-coloured [inference] |

### 3A. Where alkasite sits among the options
| Feature | Glass ionomer | Alkasite | Composite |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matrix | water-based polysalt (Mount et al, 2016) | resin (Bonchev et al, 2025) | resin |
| Adhesion | ion exchange, never acid-etch (Mount et al, 2016); micromechanical plus chemical (Sikri, 2020) | bonding system [Likely, inference] | bonding system |
| Fluoride release | "may still" be highest in vitro (Justen et al, 2024) | lower than GIC in vitro (Justen et al, 2024) | lowest, least recharged (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| Strength | lower than alkasite in vitro (Justen et al, 2024) | composite-like (Justen et al, 2024) | high |
| Evidence | long history [inference] | short-term only (Laporte et al, 2026) | long history [inference] |

### 3B. Context
Commercial ion-releasing restoratives came to market from 2013, and a DCNA review says their behaviour over time remains unclear and clinical evidence was not yet available (Melo et al, 2022). Stressing short follow-up is the answer-writer's own advice, not Melo et al's (2022).

### 4. Indications
Class I, II and V cavities in primary and permanent teeth, placed as a bulk fill (Bonchev et al, 2025).

### 5. Clinical evidence
- Meta-analysis of clinical studies: comparable to composite, superior to GIC; moderate risk of bias; no pooled numbers (Laporte et al, 2026).
- Small pilot in children: fewer dislodgements than Fuji IX at 6 months; not randomised (Padilla-Ocampo et al, 2025).
- Bioactive materials overall reduced secondary caries (RR 0.55), with the reported subgroup benefit in glass ionomers (RR 0.36) and no alkasite-specific result in the abstract (Zailai et al, 2026).

### 6. Advantages
Composite-like strength, bulk placement, ion release, short-term performance at least comparable to composite (Justen et al, 2024; Laporte et al, 2026).

### 7. Limitations
Short follow-up; remineralisation unproven clinically; lower fluoride release than GIC in vitro (Justen et al, 2024); resin-based (Bonchev et al, 2025), although Melo et al (2022) group such products as glass-ionomer-type; ion-exchange adhesion is a GIC feature (Mount et al, 2016), absent here by inference.

### 8. Conclusion
Alkasite materials are promising ion-releasing alternatives to composite and glass ionomer in moderate cavities, but are resin-based (Bonchev et al, 2025; Melo et al, 2022 group them as glass-ionomer-type) and short on long-term evidence.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Bulk-fill, resin-based, alkaline glass filler; releases hydroxide, calcium, fluoride (Bonchev et al, 2025).
- Strength like composite, stronger than GIC; fluoride release lower than GIC in vitro (Justen et al, 2024).
- Clinical: comparable to composite, better than GIC, short term (Laporte et al, 2026).
- Example: Cention N (Justen et al, 2024).
