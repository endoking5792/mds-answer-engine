## STUDY MODE

### S1. What ART is [basic science]
ART removes caries with hand instruments only, without anaesthesia or expensive equipment, and restores the cavity with a chemically adhesive glass ionomer, which bonds and releases fluoride (Hilton et al, 2013). It was first tried in the mid 1980s, and single-surface restorations have done better than multi-surface ones, with marked operator effects (Sikri, 2020).

### S2. Why high-viscosity GIC [treatment relevance]
High-viscosity (condensable) GIC has polyacrylic acid in the powder and finer glass (about 3 micrometres), giving amalgam-like handling and better abrasion resistance, though strength is little changed (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Bhat lists easy packing, less early moisture sensitivity, non-stickiness, rapid immediate finishing, better wear resistance and low solubility (Bhat et al, 2019). Examples are Fuji IX and Ketac Molar (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### S3. Evidence [prognosis]
A systematic review of 14 RCTs found no significant difference in survival between ART/high-viscosity GIC and traditional restorations for primary molars (single and multiple surfaces) and single-surface permanent restorations at 1, 2, 3 and 5 years; only 2 of 14 reports were at low risk of bias (Frencken et al, 2021). A different review (67 articles, including non-randomised studies) found the conventional technique better than ART for single-surface occlusal and multi-surface GIC restorations (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018). An umbrella review graded the evidence as conflicting and long-term performance questionable (Panetta et al, 2024). The honest reading is that ART is best supported for single-surface cavities and primary teeth.

## EXAM MODE

### 1. Introduction
Atraumatic restorative treatment (ART) is a minimal intervention technique in which caries is removed with hand instruments and the cavity is restored with high-viscosity glass ionomer cement (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013).

### 2. High-viscosity GIC
- **Composition:** polyacrylic acid in the powder, finer glass (about 3 micrometres) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Properties:** packable, less early moisture sensitivity, non-sticky, immediate finishing, better wear resistance, low solubility (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Examples:** Fuji IX, Ketac Molar, Chemflex, Hi-Fi (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Limit:** strength little different from conventional GIC (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### 3. Indications
Single-surface cavities, primary teeth, patients and settings without equipment or electricity, rampant caries stabilisation, and anxious children (Sikri, 2020; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 4. Contraindications
Heavy occlusal load in multi-surface permanent restorations; pulp involvement; inaccessible cavities (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020).

### 5. Technique
1. Examine; isolate with cotton rolls (Sikri, 2020).
2. Access with hatchet or sharp excavator; break undermined enamel (Sikri, 2020).
3. Excavate soft dentine; clean (Sikri, 2020).
4. Condition with weak polyacrylic acid (Sikri, 2020).
5. Fill the cavity and confluent fissures with GIC (Sikri, 2020).
6. Press with a petroleum-jelly-coated finger; cover with varnish or petroleum jelly (Sikri, 2020).
Routine ART needs no lining; undermined dentine becomes supported by GIC (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Advantages and disadvantages
- **Advantages:** no equipment or electricity, minimal discomfort, little training, low cost, less time, fluoride effect, a more radical approach remains possible (Sikri, 2020).
- **Disadvantages:** operator dependence, poorer success in multi-surface restorations, wear, need for maintenance (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013).

### 7. Evidence
No significant difference from conventional restorations in primary molars and single-surface permanent cavities up to 5 years; evidence base partly at high risk of bias (Frencken et al, 2021). A second review found conventional technique better for single-surface occlusal and multi-surface GIC restorations (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018). Overall evidence conflicting (Panetta et al, 2024).

### 8. Modifications
Single-step ART (one-stage partial removal): a better seal, with arrested caries-affected dentine sealed from nutrients (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Occlusal resurfacing of GIC with composite after 2-3 years if worn (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 9. Conclusion
ART with high-viscosity GIC is an effective, low-cost approach for single-surface cavities and primary teeth; it needs maintenance and is not a substitute for conventional restorations in heavily loaded multi-surface permanent teeth.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- ART = hand excavation plus high-viscosity GIC; first tried in the mid 1980s (Sikri, 2020).
- High-viscosity: finer glass (about 3 micrometres), packable, better wear (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019).
- Best for single-surface cavities and primary teeth (Frencken et al, 2021).
- Needs maintenance and fluoride (Hilton et al, 2013).
