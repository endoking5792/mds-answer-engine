## STUDY MODE

### S1. What ART is [basic science]
ART removes caries with hand instruments only, without anaesthesia or expensive equipment, and restores the cavity with a chemically adhesive glass ionomer, which bonds and releases fluoride (Hilton et al, 2013). It was first tried in the mid 1980s, and single-surface restorations have done better than multi-surface ones, with marked operator effects, although later authors observed no difference in survival between children and adults (Sikri, 2020). Summitt recommends ART as a definitive restoration for children and fearful adults, with maintenance care (Hilton et al, 2013). The closely related interim therapeutic restoration (ITR) is applicable mainly to single-surface or small two-surface restorations (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S2. Why high-viscosity GIC [treatment relevance]
High-viscosity (condensable) GIC has polyacrylic acid in the powder and finer glass (about 3 micrometres); van Noort and Barbour attribute the higher viscosity to these, and describe amalgam-like handling, abrasion resistance that appears superior, and strength little changed from conventional GIC (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). They add that GIC is not recommended for Class II restorations in adults but has been used in deciduous posterior teeth (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Summitt says these materials are still inferior to resin composites in wear and should not be used to restore load-bearing areas in the permanent dentition (Hilton et al, 2013). Bhat lists easy packing, less early moisture sensitivity, non-stickiness, rapid immediate finishing, better wear resistance and low solubility (Bhat et al, 2019). Examples are Fuji IX and Ketac Molar (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### S3. Evidence [prognosis]
The journal reviews below were read at abstract level only (abstract only). They are set side by side, and the answer does not choose between them.
- **Frencken et al (2021), systematic review of 14 RCTs (abstract only):** no significant difference in survival between ART/high-viscosity GIC and traditional restorations (amalgam, composite) for primary molars (single and multiple surfaces) and single-surface permanent restorations at 1, 2, 3 and 5 years; at 4.3 and 6.3 years the difference was significant, favouring ART/high-viscosity GIC; no significant difference for multi-surface posterior permanent restorations; only 2 of 14 reports were at low risk of bias. The authors conclude ART can replace traditional restorations in those cavities, "particularly for amalgam" (Frencken et al, 2021).
- **Ruengrungsom et al (2018), systematic review of 67 articles, including non-randomised studies (abstract only):** the conventional technique showed better survival than ART for single-surface occlusal or multi-surface GIC restorations in both dentitions; the authors call GIC-ART an alternative for single-surface occlusal restorations and say it should be carefully considered for load-bearing approximal restorations (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018).
- **Panetta et al (2024), umbrella review of 13 reviews (abstract only):** the evidence was graded conflicting (Class II, Level B); GICs are described as a good choice in both dentitions with more evidence in the primary dentition (especially ART), and medium and long-term performance is called questionable (Panetta et al, 2024).
- **Textbook survival figures (different studies, not directly comparable):** two-year survival of single-surface and multiple-surface ART restorations in primary teeth 93% and 62%, five-year survival of single-surface ART restorations in permanent teeth 80% (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Single-surface permanent ART restorations 99% at 1 year and 88% at 3 years; ART versus amalgam in children 66.1% versus 57.0% at 6.3 years; one 2-year study found no significant difference between ART and conventional preparation for Class I, and a higher survival rate with conventional preparation for Class II (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Related comparison:** a meta-analysis of primary-tooth trials found GIC more effective than silver diamine fluoride at 12 months, but not significantly so (Oliveira et al, 2019, as quoted by Hill and Gillam, 2024).

### S4. Where the textbooks differ (side by side, not ranked)
- **Where ART is used:** Sikri lists rural areas and absence of electricity among its advantages and reports better success for single-surface than multi-surface restorations (Sikri, 2020). Summitt recommends it for children, fearful adults and special groups (Hilton et al, 2013). Banerjee and Watson refer to high-viscosity GIC and resin-modified GIC transitional restorations in rampant caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Where it is not used:** Summitt says high-viscosity GIC should not restore load-bearing areas in the permanent dentition (Hilton et al, 2013); van Noort and Barbour do not recommend GIC for Class II restorations in adults (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Sikri gives no contraindication list in the ART section (Sikri, 2020).
- **Routine use:** Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed (Ritter et al, 2019) describes the ART material as made for rural regions of developing countries and not recommended in routine clinical use, restricted to areas with limited access to dental treatment (Ritter et al, 2019); Summitt, Banerjee and Watson, and Sikri describe ART as a definitive or stabilising restoration (Hilton et al, 2013; Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Sikri, 2020).
- **Conditioning:** Sikri uses a weak polyacrylic acid, or the liquid of the GIC itself (not of a water-settable GIC) (Sikri, 2020).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Atraumatic restorative treatment (ART) is a minimal intervention technique in which caries is removed with hand instruments and the cavity is restored with high-viscosity glass ionomer cement (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013).

### 2. High-viscosity GIC
- **Composition:** polyacrylic acid in the powder, finer glass (about 3 micrometres), to which the higher viscosity is attributed (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Properties:** packable, less early moisture sensitivity, non-sticky, immediate finishing, better wear resistance, low solubility (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Examples:** Fuji IX, Ketac Molar, Chemflex, Hi-Fi (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Limit:** strength little different from conventional GIC (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### 3. Indications
Single-surface cavities and settings without equipment or electricity (Sikri, 2020); primary teeth (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); children and fearful adults (Hilton et al, 2013); stabilising restorations in rampant caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 4. Contraindications
Load-bearing areas of permanent teeth (Hilton et al, 2013); Class II in adults (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); multi-surface cavities do less well (Sikri, 2020).

### 5. Technique
1. Examine; isolate with cotton rolls (Sikri, 2020).
2. Access with hatchet or sharp excavator; break undermined enamel (Sikri, 2020).
3. Excavate soft dentine; clean (Sikri, 2020).
4. Condition with weak polyacrylic acid or the GIC liquid (Sikri, 2020).
5. Fill the cavity and confluent fissures with GIC (Sikri, 2020).
6. Press with a petroleum-jelly-coated finger; cover with varnish or petroleum jelly (Sikri, 2020).
Routine ART needs no lining; undermined dentine becomes supported by GIC (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Advantages and disadvantages
- **Advantages:** no equipment or electricity, minimal discomfort, little training, low cost, less time, fluoride effect, a more radical approach remains possible (Sikri, 2020).
- **Disadvantages:** operator dependence, poorer multi-surface success (Sikri, 2020); wear (Hilton et al, 2013; Banerjee and Watson, 2015); maintenance (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 7. Evidence
Abstracts: Frencken et al (2021): no significant difference from traditional restorations in primary molars and single-surface permanent cavities at 1-5 years, significant difference favouring ART at 4.3 and 6.3 years; 2 of 14 trials low risk of bias. Ruengrungsom et al (2018): conventional technique better for single-surface occlusal and multi-surface GIC restorations. Panetta et al (2024): evidence conflicting. Survival: 93% single-surface and 62% multi-surface at 2 years in primary teeth; 80% single-surface permanent at 5 years (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 8. Modifications
Single-step ART (one-stage partial removal): a better seal, with arrested caries-affected dentine sealed from nutrients (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Occlusal resurfacing of GIC with composite after 2-3 years if worn (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 9. Conclusion
ART with high-viscosity GIC is a low-cost approach with trial support in primary teeth and single-surface permanent cavities (Frencken et al, 2021); the reviews differ on single-surface occlusal cavities (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018); maintenance is needed (Hilton et al, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- ART = hand excavation plus high-viscosity GIC; first tried in the mid 1980s (Sikri, 2020).
- High-viscosity: finer glass (about 3 micrometres), packable, better wear (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019).
- Frencken 2021: no significant difference at 1-5 years in primary molars and single-surface permanent cavities; Ruengrungsom 2018: conventional better for single-surface occlusal (abstracts).
- Needs maintenance and fluoride (Hilton et al, 2013).
