## STUDY MODE
**Definition and origin.** When enamel or dentin is cut with a bur or hand instrument a film of debris is smeared over the surface. Summitt's calls it tenacious grinding debris that adheres to the surface and partially plugs the tubular orifices (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's gives the composition as hydroxyapatite and altered, denatured collagen, which can be gelatinised by the friction and heat of preparation (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Thickness is about 0.5-2 μm (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998; Craig, 2002 gives 1-2 μm) [SRC] and depends on the instrument: a medium-grit diamond bur leaves a thicker (about 5 μm) denser layer than tungsten carbide (about 2 μm) (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Protective role.** The layer blocks tubule entrances. Sturdevant's reports it lowers dentin permeability by nearly 90% in vitro (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and the dentin hypersensitivity literature agrees that losing it increases fluid flow and sensitivity (Dentine Hypersensitivity, ch.5) [SRC]. It is not a seal: it is 25-30% porous and cannot prevent slow long-term diffusion (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Craig (2002) says it reduces hydrostatic pressure well but diffusion less, and molecules as large as albumin (66 kDa) diffuse through it [SRC]. Its protection is temporary, as it dissolves in oral fluids in about 7 days (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998) [SRC]. Air-drying a cut surface still dries dentin locally (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Harmful role.** It is a weak substrate. Phillips gives its cohesive strength as about 5-10 MPa, too low to resist daily forces, so debonding and leakage were a recurring problem; composite has no corrosion-type self-sealing as amalgam does (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Resins bonded to it fail within the layer: second-generation adhesives bonded to calcium in the smear layer and failed because it was the weakest link (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. It is also usually contaminated with microorganisms (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998) [SRC]. The preparation method matters: diamond-bur-created layers gave lower bond strength (5 MPa) than carbide-bur ones (8 MPa), but after acid etching there was no difference (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998) [SRC].

**How adhesives deal with it.** Three strategies. *Remove:* etch-and-rinse systems dissolve it and demineralise dentin beneath (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. *Modify:* self-etch systems leave it in place and infiltrate through it; the third generation (1984) used acidic primers to keep a modified layer (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. *Strong self-etch* dissolve it, mild and ultramild cannot, so with them a thicker layer lowers bond strength and clinicians should lightly abrade cavity walls with fine diamonds, scrub the adhesive, and etch enamel selectively with phosphoric acid (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. *Incorporate (GIC):* 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds removes it but leaves tubules relatively closed, and an alternative is a mineralising solution (ITS or tannic acid) that includes it in the ion-exchange layer (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]. Air abrasion does not remove it and creates its own (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Costs of removing it.** Acid removal increases local dentinal permeability and outward fluid movement, an adverse condition for bonding (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]; hydrophobic resins do not adhere to a wet hydrophilic substrate (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Craig (2002) lists three pulp risks: loss of a barrier to diffusion of material components, microleakage becoming more significant, and the acid itself as an irritant; yet removal is routine for bond strength [SRC]. A dentin thickness of 0.5 mm buffers most acid (Craig, 2002) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
*Claim: smear layer should be removed or dissolved for bonding.* Textbook: Phillips says longer-lasting bonds result when it is removed; Craig (2002) says this is true of contemporary bonding agents, "although earlier research with older bonding agents showed the opposite" [SRC]. Agreement across Phillips, Nakabayashi and Pashley and Sturdevant's. *Strength:* mostly bench bond-strength tests. *Refinement:* modern self-etch systems work through a modified layer, so complete removal is not a requirement of all adhesives. *Limitation:* laboratory bond strength does not equal clinical retention. *Implication:* match the layer's handling to the adhesive pH, not a single rule [Likely].

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: ~34 minutes)*

### 1. Definition and composition
When enamel or dentin is cut, a film of debris is smeared over the surface. It is tenacious grinding debris that partly plugs the tubular orifices. Composition: hydroxyapatite and altered, denatured collagen, which friction and heat may gelatinise. It is usually contaminated with microorganisms. Thickness about 0.5-2 μm.

### 2. Variation with the instrument
- Medium-grit diamond bur: rough enamel with loose fragments; on dentin a thicker (about 5 μm), denser layer and compact plugs.
- Tungsten carbide bur: about 2 μm.
- Hand instruments and air abrasion give different layers; air abrasion does not remove the layer but creates one.

### 3. Functions: the protective view
- Occludes tubule entrances; reduces dentin permeability by nearly 90% in vitro.
- Reduces hydrostatic pressure effectively; reduces diffusion less (albumin-sized molecules pass; 25-30% porous).
- Loss of it increases fluid flow and so dentin hypersensitivity.
- Dentin under it is moderately protected from drying by air, though local desiccation can still occur.
- Temporary: dissolves in oral fluids in about 7 days.

### 4. The harmful view
- Cohesive strength only 5-10 MPa, too low for daily forces; before acid etching restorative materials sat on it and debonded and leaked. Composite has no corrosion product to self-seal margins as amalgam has.
- A weak, loosely attached layer: bond failures occur within it (second-generation adhesives).
- Diffusion barrier to primers and monomers; contaminated with microorganisms.
- Studies cited by Nakabayashi and Pashley indicate it is necessary to remove the layer for high bond strengths.

### 5. Management by adhesive type
| System | Smear layer | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Etch-and-rinse | Removed (phosphoric acid) and dentin demineralised | Keep dentin moist; collagen collapses if over-dried |
| Strong self-etch | Dissolved | Works through the layer |
| Mild/ultramild self-etch | Modified only | Thicker layer lowers bond; abrade with fine diamond, scrub, longer application; etch enamel selectively |
| One-step self-etch | Incorporated | Remnants may reduce in vitro bond |
| Glass-ionomer | 10% polyacrylic acid 10 s removes it, tubules stay relatively closed; or mineralising solution incorporates it | |
| Amalgam | Layer should be sealed where enamel margins may leak (chemical protection) | |

### 6. Pulpal considerations of removal
- Increases permeability and outward fluid flow; hydrophobic resins do not adhere to wet hydrophilic dentin.
- Craig's three risks: loss of a barrier to diffusion of material components; microleakage becomes more significant; the acid is itself an irritant.
- 0.5 mm of dentin buffers most acid; acids penetrate less than 100 μm in most cases.
- Despite these, removal is routine because of the bond strengths obtained.

### 7. Critical analysis (for the "critically analyse" wording)
Earlier work with older bonding agents suggested the opposite of today's finding; contemporary data show higher bond strength after removal. The evidence is bench bond strength, and modern self-etch systems succeed through a modified layer, so complete removal is not universal. Pulpal protection and bonding pull in opposite directions; the clinician balances them by system choice and remaining dentin thickness.

### Additional points
- **Acidic materials act on the layer:** zinc phosphate has pH about 1.6 just after mixing and can etch through the layer into dentin; the layer is a good buffer but it can be overwhelmed by large amounts of acid.
- **Acid effect on dentin:** acid removes the layer, exposes collagen, increases permeability and chemically modifies the surface; the bonding substrate becomes the collagen matrix.
- **Reasoning for the critical wording:** a layer that protects the pulp (low permeability) and a layer that weakens the bond cannot both be left alone; adhesive choice and remaining dentin thickness decide the balance.

### 8. Conclusion
The layer is a short-lived biological shield and a weak bonding substrate. Remove, modify or incorporate it according to the adhesive; protect the pulp when dentin is thin.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
