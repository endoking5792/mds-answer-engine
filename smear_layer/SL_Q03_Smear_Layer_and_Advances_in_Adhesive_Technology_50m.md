## STUDY MODE
**Smear layer, short form.** See Q06. The points this answer needs: the layer is weak (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; protective but temporary and porous (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; and it is the substrate on which each adhesive generation was designed to act.

**Adhesive technology as a history of handling the smear layer.** Early (second-generation, 1978) phosphate-ester systems bonded to calcium in the layer and failed because the layer was the weakest link (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The third generation (1984) used acidic primers to keep a modified layer (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The field then split: etch-and-rinse systems dissolve the layer and hybridise the demineralised collagen; self-etch systems modify it and hybridise through it; strong ones dissolve it, mild and ultramild ones cannot, and are sensitive to layer thickness (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Nakabayashi's early work already concluded that removing the layer gives more reliable, higher bond strengths (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998) [SRC].

**Recent advances.**
1. *Universal (multimode) adhesives.* Used in etch-and-rinse, self-etch or selective-etch mode. Sturdevant's lists examples with pH values from 2.3 to 3.2 (Clearfil Universal 2.3, Scotchbond Universal 2.7, All-Bond Universal 3.1-3.2) and a very low-pH one not recommended for indirect restorations (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Their listed pH values are in the mild range, so the mild/ultramild thickness rule above may apply to them in self-etch mode; this is my inference, not a statement in the source [Guessing]. The 10-MDP monomer binds residual hydroxyapatite and gives more hydrolysis-resistant interfaces (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC].
2. *Selective enamel etching* with self-etch is recommended because a thick layer neutralises weak acids at enamel (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC].
3. *Surface preparation to control the layer.* Fine-grit abrasion of walls (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. In one 2026 in vitro study, 600-grit finishing gave the highest dentin shear bond for self-etch and universal systems, above 120-grit and air abrasion (Verma et al, 2026) [LIT, abstract]. A 2026 narrative review found alumina air abrasion improved bonding with etch-and-rinse but often reduced dentin bond with self-etch, "likely" through smear layer removal without adequate demineralisation (Polak et al, 2026) [LIT, abstract]. Treat both as bench data. Mount et al (2016) note air abrasion leaves its own smear layer [SRC].
4. *GIC with polyacrylic conditioning or mineralising pretreatment* (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC].
5. *Matrix metalloproteinase inhibitors, bioactive and remineralising adhesives, nano-filled adhesives.* [RECALL] Not retrieved this session; mention by name only.

**Limitation.** Evidence retrieved here is largely bench bond strength; do not claim one adhesive or mode is best.

### Part A. Smear layer in conservative dentistry

**1. Definition and origin:** tenacious grinding debris smeared on cut enamel and dentin; composition hydroxyapatite and altered, denatured collagen; plugs the tubular orifices partly.

**2. Thickness:** about 0.5-2 μm. A medium-grit diamond bur leaves a thicker (about 5 μm), denser layer and a compact plug; tungsten carbide about 2 μm.

**3. Protective functions**
- Reduces dentin permeability by nearly 90% in vitro; lowers hydrostatic pressure; reduces fluid flow and sensitivity.
- Limits: 25-30% porous, does not prevent slow diffusion (albumin-sized molecules pass), and dissolves in about 7 days.

**4. Disadvantages for bonding**
- Cohesive strength 5-10 MPa, much less than needed; bonds fail within it.
- Contaminated with microorganisms.
- Diffusion barrier slowing primer and monomer infiltration.
- Layer type affects results: diamond-bur layers gave lower bond (5 MPa) than carbide (8 MPa), but after acid etching there was no difference.

**5. Pulpal effects of removing it:** more permeability, outward fluid flow, microleakage becomes more significant, acid irritation; 0.5 mm dentin buffers acid.

**6. Clinical methods of handling:** remove (etch-and-rinse), modify (self-etch), incorporate (one-step self-etch; GIC with mineralising solution), or leave and seal it (Sturdevant's advises sealing it under amalgam whose enamel margins may leak).

### Part B. Evolution of adhesives viewed through the smear layer
| Stage | What happens to the smear layer | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Second generation (1978) | Phosphate-ester bonds to calcium in the layer | Layer was the weakest link; 1-5 MPa against a 10 MPa threshold; peeled |
| Third generation (1984) | Acidic primer (2.5% maleic acid, 55% HEMA) modifies layer, slightly demineralises dentin | Mixed clinical results |
| Etch-and-rinse (three-step, two-step) | Phosphoric acid removes layer, opens tubules, exposes collagen | Strong, but technique sensitive (wet bonding) |
| Two-step self-etch | Primer fixes layer, exposes 0.5-1 μm collagen, pH 1.2-2 | Simple, less technique sensitive; remnants may lower bond |
| One-step self-etch | Layer incorporated into the interface | Thin layer, lower bond; extra hydrophobic coat helps |
| Universal (multimode) | Operator chooses mode | See Part C |

**pH classes of self-etch adhesives:** mild, moderate, aggressive. Mild ones bond dentin well and enamel poorly; aggressive ones the reverse.

### Part C. Recent advances in adhesive technology
**1. Universal (multimode) adhesives**
- Applied etch-and-rinse, self-etch or selective-etch mode.
- Examples and pH in Sturdevant's: Clearfil Universal 2.3, Scotchbond Universal 2.7, All-Bond Universal 3.1-3.2; a pH 1.6 product not recommended for indirect restorations.
- Some need a chemical activator with dual-cure materials; others do not.
- **10-MDP** functional monomer bonds chemically to residual hydroxyapatite (nanolayering) and makes the interface more resistant to biodegradation; also binds zirconia and metal.
- Some contain silane for ceramics.
- Their listed pH values are in the mild range, so the mild/ultramild thickness rule may apply in self-etch mode (my inference).

**2. Selective enamel etching** with phosphoric acid before a self-etch adhesive: a thick smear layer neutralises the acidic monomers at enamel; selective etching improved marginal adaptation and discolouration in a study cited in Sturdevant's.

**3. Controlling the smear layer before bonding**
- Fine-grit diamond finishing; scrubbing; prolonged application.
- In vitro (2026): 600-grit finishing gave the highest dentin bond for self-etch and universal adhesives; 120-grit lowest.
- Alumina air abrasion: improves etch-and-rinse bonding but often lowers dentin bond with self-etch systems (2026 review).

**4. Glass-ionomer conditioning:** 10% polyacrylic acid, or mineralising solutions (ITS, tannic acid) to incorporate the layer.

**5. Desensitising bonding:** resin tags and hybrid layers reduce sensitivity; glutaraldehyde-HEMA primers (Gluma) denature proteins and occlude tubules.

**6. Others (names only, not retrieved):** MMP inhibitors to protect collagen in the hybrid layer, bioactive and remineralising adhesives, nanofilled adhesives.

### Part D. Limitations and appraisal
- Evidence is mostly in vitro bond strength; clinical durability is not established.
- Hydrolytic instability of water-containing primers; technique sensitivity persists.
- No mode or adhesive can be called best on the evidence retrieved.

### Further detail: etch-and-rinse and self-etch mechanisms
- **Three-step etch-and-rinse:** etchant removes the layer, exposes intertubular and peritubular collagen and opens tubules in a funnel shape; the primer (bifunctional molecules) restores surface energy; the bonding resin copolymerises and forms the structural backbone of the hybrid layer. Dentin is demineralised by up to about 7.5 μm depending on acid, time and concentration. Resin tags form in open tubules.
- **Two-step etch-and-rinse:** primer and bond combined in one coat after etching.
- **Two-step self-etch:** the primer fixes the layer, exposes 0.5-1 μm collagen at pH 1.2-2; bonding agent follows; tags form in the impregnated plug.
- **One-step self-etch:** layer incorporated; thin adhesive layer and low bond; extra hydrophobic coat or multiple coats help; incompatible with self-cure composites unless coated.
- **Self-etch interface:** partial dissolution and incorporation of the layer gives a thin, irregular hybrid layer; remnants may interfere with in vitro bonds.
- **Advantages of self-etch:** no rinse or dry, less overwet/overdry error, simultaneous demineralisation and infiltration; **drawbacks:** hydrolytic instability of water-containing primers, poorer enamel etch.

### Practical points for adhesive technology
- Enamel: always phosphoric acid etch, whatever bonding agent system is used (Alani and Patel); re-etch if contaminated.
- Bond strengths to enamel and dentin of 10-30 MPa are typical.
- For deep cervical or sensitive dentin, glutaraldehyde-HEMA desensitiser may be used under adhesives.
- Dentin hypersensitivity link: removing the layer increases fluid flow and hence sensitivity, so desensitising adhesives reseal tubules.

### Viva one-liners
- Remove (etch-and-rinse), modify (two-step self-etch), incorporate (one-step), choose (universal).
- Mild self-etch: great on dentin, poorer on enamel; selective enamel etch fixes it.
- Fine-diamond finishing and scrubbing thin the layer for mild systems.
- Air abrasion: caution with self-etch on dentin.

### Chemistry and limitations of self-etch systems (Stangel et al; Bedran-Russo and Leme-Kraus)
- Adhesive "generations" are commercial categories reflecting handling or formulation, not new mechanisms; only two concepts exist (remove the layer, or incorporate it).
- **Enamel standard:** about 35% phosphoric acid (pH below 1.0); in vitro joint strength about 27 MPa; etch-and-rinse on enamel generally gives greater in vitro strength than self-etching primers.
- **Primer pH:** two-step self-etch primers pH 1.0-2.5; one-step below 1.0. Esters such as HEMA, TEGDMA and UDMA are hydrolytically unstable at this acidity and can degrade up to 90% in storage; raising pH above 2 reduces this but the primer is then mild and may not etch enamel adequately or form deep hybrid layers.
- **Solvent:** self-etch primers use water to ionise the acid; water in dentin dilutes the primer and inhibits polymerisation, so water-based adhesives bond less than acetone-based in vitro.
- **Wet bonding:** with etch-and-rinse, leave the cavity visibly wet without pooling; this remains the most predictable dentin adhesion.
- **10-MDP:** chemical bonding of certain acidic primers to dentin mineral adds retention; clinical performance of self-etch improves with selective enamel etching.
- **Variability:** large variability between self-etch brands and in universal systems according to application mode; average bond strengths vary greatly between brands and studies.

### Historical background: why the layer shaped the field (Nakabayashi and Pashley)
- The layer was once thought to protect the pulp; studies of bond strength before and after removal indicated it must be removed for high bond strength, and it is usually contaminated with microorganisms.
- Acid removal also demineralises the dentin beneath, so the true substrate is the collagen matrix, which collapses if air-dried; wet bonding (Kanca, Gwinnett) was developed to prevent collapse and keep monomer permeability.
- Nakabayashi's early system (1982): conditioner of 3% ferric chloride in 10% citric acid to remove the layer, followed by 4-META/MMA-TBB resin.
- Acidic cements act on the layer first: zinc phosphate has pH about 1.6 just after mixing, which can etch through the layer and leave demineralised, sometimes sensitive, dentin.

### Chemical bonding and indirect use of universals (Summitt's; Sturdevant's; Alani and Patel)
- 10-MDP has a strong affinity for dentin, enamel and metal and forms a chemical bond with zirconia (Zr-O-P); MDP-containing adhesives make the interface more resistant to biodegradation because of the bond to residual hydroxyapatite after self-etch.
- Some universals contain silane for ceramics; they can also lute indirect restorations with self- or dual-cured composites and cements, with a self-cure activator.
- Manufacturers differ on the activator: some require it with dual-cure materials, others do not because of the adhesive's higher pH; a pH 1.6 product is not recommended for indirect restorations.
- In sandblasted zirconia, universal adhesives used light-cured with an amine-free dual-cure cement gave high bond strengths.
- Alani and Patel call universals the eighth generation, used in total-etch, selective-etch or self-etch mode, with the choice of etching left to operator preference; phosphoric acid on enamel is always advised.
- Take-home: a modern adhesive answer must say what the system does to the smear layer in each mode.

### Part E. Conclusion
Each step in adhesive development has been a different answer to one question: what to do with a weak smear layer. Current practice favours modifying or removing it according to adhesive pH and substrate, with selective enamel etching and careful surface preparation.
