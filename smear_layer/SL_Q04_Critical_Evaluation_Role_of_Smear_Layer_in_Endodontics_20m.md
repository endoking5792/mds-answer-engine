## STUDY MODE
See Q01 for definition, structure and removal methods. This answer is the argument.

**The two positions.** Retain: tubule blocking, lowered permeability, partial bacterial barrier (Sikri, 2019; Berman et al, 2021) [SRC]. Remove: infected substrate, blocks irrigant, medicament and sealer penetration, dissolves and leaves a void (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**What each side's evidence actually is.** Almost all of it is in vitro: SEM scoring of cleanliness, dye or bacterial leakage, tubular penetration of sealer. Shahravan et al (2007) pooled 26 leakage studies: most comparisons (53.8%) showed no difference, 41.5% favoured removal, 4.7% favoured retention; pooled dye-leakage tests favoured removal [LIT, abstract]. Torabinejad et al (2002) reported a lack of agreement and conflicting results [LIT, abstract]. Violich and Chandler (2010) concluded that investigations are still required to determine the role of the smear layer in treatment outcome [LIT, abstract]. Cohen's states no consensus, with growing evidence for removal (Berman et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Strength and limits.** Leakage and dye tests are surrogate laboratory endpoints; they do not report periapical healing [Certain]. SEM scoring is observer-dependent [RECALL]. Studies differ in canal region, irrigant, time and obturation technique, so a pooled "no difference" may reflect noise as much as equivalence [Guessing].

**Points that cut both ways.** Retention argument: removal exposes tubules to reinfection if the coronal seal leaks (Berman et al, 2021) [SRC]. But one study cited in the same chapter found bacteria present before obturation were not viable afterwards (Berman et al, 2021) [SRC], and the smear layer is not a complete barrier (Berman et al, 2021) [SRC]. Removal adds its own harm: excessive chelator exposure erodes peritubular and intratubular dentin (10 minutes in the cited study; Berman et al, 2021), and NaOCl after chelator erodes exposed collagen (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
*Claim: removal improves seal.* Textbook: probably, no consensus. Evidence: pooled laboratory data modest and mixed (Shahravan et al, 2007). Interpretation: removal is more likely to help than hurt under test conditions. Limitation: no clinical outcome study; effect on healing unknown. Clinical implication: remove with short, correct chelator contact, as the majority of investigators advise (Sikri, 2019) [Likely].

*Claim: removal improves disinfection.* Textbook and Violich and Chandler (2010) both say it should; this is mechanistic (access to tubules), not measured as better healing [Likely].

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: ~34 minutes)*

### 1. Introduction
Instrumentation leaves a smear layer on the canal wall. Whether it should be kept or removed has been debated for decades, and the honest answer depends on the quality of the evidence behind each side.

### 2. Definition
AAE: a surface film of debris retained on dentin after instrumentation, consisting of dentin particles, remnants of vital or necrotic pulp tissue, bacterial components and retained irrigants. Superficial layer about 1-2.5 μm; plugs in tubules to about 40 μm.

### 3. Arguments for retaining it
1. Blocks tubule orifices and lowers permeability.
2. Acts as a barrier to bacterial migration into tubules; it is not a complete barrier but delays penetration.
3. Bacteria remaining after preparation may be sealed in by the layer and the filling.
4. Removal opens tubules, so a coronal leak can reinfect them.
5. One study found removal allowed significantly higher tubule colonisation.
6. Removal agents themselves cause damage: 10 minutes of EDTA caused excessive loss of peritubular and intratubular dentin; NaOCl after EDTA erodes exposed collagen and may weaken root dentin.

### 4. Arguments for removing it
1. It may be infected and can protect bacteria already in tubules.
2. It blocks irrigants and medicaments from entering tubules; bacteria within tubules are killed more easily once it is removed.
3. Sealer penetration into tubules does not occur with the layer present; removal increased sealer penetration by 10-80 μm in studies cited.
4. It is loosely adherent, disintegrates and leaves a void between wall and filling, a path for leakage.
5. Removal improved gutta-percha adaptation and reduced coronal leakage in studies cited.
6. Most investigators favour removal, and Cohen's reports growing evidence for removal before obturation.

### 5. Critical appraisal of the evidence
| Question | Evidence | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Does removal improve sealing? | Meta-analysis of 26 studies (65 comparisons): 53.8% no difference, 41.5% for removal, 4.7% for retention; dye-leakage subset favoured removal | In vitro; dye leakage is a surrogate; over half of comparisons showed nothing |
| Does it improve disinfection? | Mechanistic reasoning, tubule access; Violich and Chandler (2010) say it should | No clinical measurement of healing |
| Does it help healing? | Not established; investigations needed | The key gap |
| Are the studies consistent? | Torabinejad et al (2002): conflicting results | Studies differ in canal level, irrigant, time, obturation |
| Does the retained layer hurt? | Cohen's: bacteria pre-obturation not viable after filling in one study | Single laboratory study |
- SEM scoring of cleanliness is observer dependent [RECALL].
- Apical-third results are worse because chelators act less there.

### 6. Interpretation and limitation
**Evidence** shows removal helps seal in a minority of comparisons and rarely harms. **Interpretation:** removal is more likely to help than hurt in infected canals, mainly through access of disinfectants and sealers. **Limitation:** none of this shows that teeth heal better.

### 7. Clinical implication
Remove it in infected canals with 17% EDTA (about 1 minute, high volume) alternating with NaOCl; activate; avoid prolonged chelator and prolonged NaOCl afterwards; maintain a good coronal seal so tubules are not re-contaminated.

### Methodological cautions (for the critical appraisal)
- SEM that shows a clean wall does not prove permeability rose: during EDTA treatment high calcium and phosphate in tubular fluid can precipitate in tubules and lower permeability, so SEM misses subsurface change.
- The sources used here are silent on blinding or standardisation of the leakage and SEM studies; I would treat their conclusions as hypothesis-generating [Guessing].
- Only one study (retreatment cases) was reported by Siqueira and Rocas as linking removal with outcome.

### 8. Conclusion
The role of the smear layer is the best studied "unproven" question in cleaning and shaping: biologically plausible, supported by laboratory data, but not proven to change clinical outcome. Removal is the reasoned default; certainty is not warranted.
