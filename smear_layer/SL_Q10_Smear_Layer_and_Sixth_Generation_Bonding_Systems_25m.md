## STUDY MODE
**What "sixth generation" means.** In Pickard's classification the 4th and 5th generations remove the smear layer, while the 6th and 7th generations dissolve it using self-etching primers (weak primers for the earlier, strong for the later group), with examples such as Clearfil SE and Prompt L-Pop (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC]. In the common Indian exam sense the sixth generation is the two-step self-etch system: a self-etching primer then a bonding resin. Sturdevant's corresponds to this as the "two-step self-etch" [EP] + B strategy (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Smear layer handling.** The two-step self-etching primer does not remove the smear layer but fixes it and exposes about 0.5-1 μm of intertubular collagen (pH 1.2-2); the smear plug is impregnated with acidic monomers and resin tags form in the primer-impregnated plug (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. This is the "modify" strategy, descended from the third generation's modified layer (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and the opposite of the second generation, which bonded to a layer that was the weakest link (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Why it was developed.** It removes the rinse and dry steps, lowers technique sensitivity and avoids a mismatch between demineralisation depth and resin infiltration, since both occur together; Sturdevant's notes this concept has been challenged by evidence of partly demineralised dentin below the hybrid layer in one system (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Limits.** SEAs do not remove the smear layer completely, and remnants can interfere with in vitro bond strengths (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; mild ones bond dentin well and enamel poorly, aggressive ones the reverse (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; with mild and ultramild systems a thicker layer lowers bond strength, so abrade the walls with fine diamonds and etch enamel selectively (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Water is in the primer, so these systems are prone to hydrolytic instability (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
*Claim: sixth-generation systems are simpler and as good.* Textbooks agree they are simpler and less technique sensitive. Bond quality is system-specific and bench-based; no head-to-head clinical data were retrieved. Do not state equivalence [Likely].

## EXAM MODE

### 1. Introduction
Each generation of dentin adhesive is a different answer to one problem: what to do with the smear layer. The sixth-generation systems answer "modify it and bond through it, without rinsing."

### 2. The smear layer
- Debris film from cutting; hydroxyapatite and altered, denatured collagen; about 0.5-2 μm; plugs tubule orifices.
- Protective: lowers permeability (about 90% in vitro) and hypersensitivity; temporary and porous.
- Weak: cohesive strength about 5-10 MPa; a diffusion barrier to monomers.

### 3. Evolution by smear layer strategy
| Generation (Pickard's numbering) | Strategy | Problem or feature |
|---|---|---|
| 2nd (1978) | Phosphate-ester bonds to calcium in the layer | Layer weakest link; 1-5 MPa against 10 MPa needed; peeled |
| 3rd (1984) | Acidic primer modifies layer (2.5% maleic acid, 55% HEMA) | Slight demineralisation; mixed results |
| 4th (three-step), 5th (two-step) | Layer removed by etch-and-rinse | Strong; wet bonding; technique sensitive |
| 6th | Self-etching primer dissolves/modifies the layer; primer then bond (two-bottle, e.g. Clearfil SE) | No rinse or dry |
| 7th | Strong self-etch; one-step systems incorporate the layer into the interface | Thin layer, lower bond |
Note: numbering differs between authors. Sturdevant's prefers strategy names (etch-and-rinse three-step or two-step, two-step self-etch, one-step self-etch). The two-step self-etch corresponds to the commonly taught sixth generation.

### 4. Sixth-generation (two-step self-etch) systems
**Composition:** an acidic, water-containing self-etching primer followed by a hydrophobic bonding resin like that of the three-step etch-and-rinse systems.

**Mechanism and smear layer**
1. The primer (pH about 1.2-2 for some; mild ones about 1.9-2.0 e.g. Clearfil SE Primer) does not remove the smear layer but fixes it.
2. It exposes about 0.5-1 μm of intertubular collagen.
3. The smear plug is impregnated with acidic monomers but not removed; some monomers bond chemically to dentin (10-MDP to hydroxyapatite).
4. The primer opens microchannels through the plug; the bonding resin follows and forms resin tags in the primer-impregnated plug.
5. Demineralisation and infiltration are simultaneous, so there is less mismatch between depth of etch and depth of infiltration (a concept partly challenged by partly demineralised dentin found beneath the hybrid layer of one system).
6. Residual hydroxyapatite crystals and smear remnants are embedded in the hybrid layer.

**Classification by pH:** mild, moderate, aggressive. Mild: excellent dentin bond, poorer enamel bond; aggressive: the reverse.

### 5. Advantages
- No rinsing or drying: simpler technique, less risk of over-wetting or over-drying dentin.
- Less technique sensitive than etch-and-rinse.
- Less discrepancy between etch depth and infiltration depth.
- Less post-operative sensitivity potential because dentin is not stripped of its plugs [RECALL].

### 6. Disadvantages and limitations
- Do not remove the smear layer completely; remnants may interfere with in vitro bond strength.
- Mild and ultramild systems are sensitive to smear-layer thickness: a thicker layer gives lower bond. Remedy: finish with fine diamond, scrub, longer application.
- Do not etch uninstrumented enamel as well as phosphoric acid; marginal seal at enamel may be compromised. Remedy: selective enamel etching, which improved marginal adaptation and discolouration in a study cited.
- Water-containing primers: hydrolytic degradation.
- Some early systems deteriorated more than etch-and-rinse under thermal cycling.

### 7. Critical comment
Textbooks agree that these systems simplify technique. Bond quality is product specific and based mostly on laboratory data; head-to-head clinical equivalence with etch-and-rinse is not established by the sources used here.

### Chemistry and limits of self-etch primers (Stangel et al; Bedran-Russo and Leme-Kraus)
- Two-step self-etch primers have pH 1.0-2.5; one-step below 1.0. They must dissolve smear layers and aprismatic enamel to form hybrid layers.
- Water ionises the acid but dilutes primer in wet dentin and inhibits polymerisation; water-based adhesives bond less than acetone-based in vitro.
- At this acidity esters such as HEMA, TEGDMA and UDMA can degrade by up to 90% on storage; raising pH above 2 lessens this but gives a mild primer that may not etch enamel or form deep hybrid layers.
- Hybrid layer in self-etch systems is thin and irregular because the smear layer is incorporated or partly demineralised; 10-MDP adds chemical bonding to dentin mineral.
- Enamel: etch-and-rinse generally gives greater in vitro joint strength than self-etching primers, hence selective enamel etching.
- Dentin: conventional three-step total-etch adhesives generally give significantly higher joint strengths than one-step systems in vitro.
- Terminology note: Alani and Patel number universals as the eighth generation; the sixth generation of Pickard's scheme is a two-step self-etch.

### 8. Conclusion
Sixth-generation systems turn the smear layer from an obstacle to be removed into a substrate to be modified. Success depends on pH, layer thickness and enamel handling.
