## STUDY MODE

### S1. Where the fluoride comes from [basic science]
Fluoride is used as a flux in making the glass and is held in the glass as extremely fine droplets; some is released from the powder, and considerable amounts after mixing with the polyacid (Mount, 1994). It is not part of the matrix, so release does not damage properties (Mount, 1994). Ions released early lie in the matrix but are free to migrate, depending on concentration gradient and pH, with an initial washout phase (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### S2. How release and recharge work [mechanism]
Fluoride is about the same size and mobility as hydroxyl, so exchange depends on the fluoride gradient; uptake during high-fluoride exposure turns the cement into a reservoir (Mount et al, 2016). Exposure to topical fluoride, rinses or toothpaste temporarily reverses the gradient so fluoride diffuses in, then diffuses out again (Roberson et al, 2006). Recharge depends on the agent, its concentration and frequency; gels recharge more than low-fluoride toothpastes, and the raised release is short-lived, so recharging is daily (Hilton et al, 2013). A two-part recharge material exists in which a charge gel replenishes the restoration by ion exchange (Sikri, 2020).

### S3. How fluoride acts on tooth and plaque [treatment relevance]
Fluoride replaces hydroxyl in apatite making it acid-resistant, catalyses calcium and phosphate uptake, and enters acidogenic bacteria where it slows enzyme activity and acid production (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Bhat et al, 2019). Concentrations of 0.03-0.08 ppm shift the balance from net demineralisation to net remineralisation (Hilton et al, 2013). Summitt says enhancing remineralisation is probably the most important anticaries mechanism (Hilton et al, 2013); a direct bactericidal effect is unlikely because the released levels are far lower than bactericidal ones, and the effect is very local, within about 1 mm of the margin (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S4. The critical point [prognosis]
Sturdevant notes that discharging occurs as fast as recharging, so recharge is unlikely to improve secondary caries prevention much compared with other materials (Roberson et al, 2006). McCabe adds that the therapeutic fluoride level required is unknown (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Laboratory release is highest in GIC (Justen et al, 2024), but the RCT meta-analysis found less secondary caries than amalgam and not than composite (Ge et al, 2023).

## EXAM MODE

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement releases fluoride for years and can take it up again from the environment, acting as a rechargeable reservoir (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Source of fluoride
Fluoride used as a flux in the glass is incorporated as very fine droplets; considerable release follows mixing with the polyacid (Mount, 1994). It is not part of the matrix (Mount, 1994).

### 3. Release pattern
Initial burst, falling over 2-3 months to a low steady level monitored for at least 5 years (Mount, 1994). More is released at low pH (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 4. Mechanism
- Release is by diffusion and ion exchange down a concentration gradient (Mount et al, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- Fluoride is about the size of hydroxyl, so exchange is easy (Mount et al, 2016).
- Surface washout in the early phase, then slow release from the bulk (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Hilton et al, 2013).

### 5. Recharge
- High external fluoride (toothpaste, gel, rinse, varnish) reverses the gradient; fluoride enters the cement and later leaves it (Roberson et al, 2006; Mount et al, 2016).
- Gels recharge more than low-fluoride toothpastes; the effect is short-lived, so recharge daily (Hilton et al, 2013).
- GIC and resin-modified GIC recharge most; composites least (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Fluoride-rich variants (Fuji VII, Fuji Triage) release more (Bhat et al, 2019; Ngo, 2010).

### 6. Anticaries mechanisms
| Mechanism | Detail |
|---|---|
| Fluorapatite | F replaces OH, acid-resistant crystals (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Bhat et al, 2019) |
| Remineralisation | 0.03-0.08 ppm shifts balance; probably the most important effect (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| Bacterial effect | F inhibits acid-producing enzymes; almost complete S. mutans inhibition on the cement surface (Bhat et al, 2019; Mount, 1994) |
| Local action | within about 1 mm of the margin (Hilton et al, 2013) |

### 7. Factors affecting release
Powder:liquid ratio, mixing, temperature, solubility, finishing, storage medium; fluoride release is higher in more open (weaker) materials (Sikri, 2020).

### 8. Critical appraisal
Laboratory release is highest for GIC (Justen et al, 2024). Clinically, secondary caries are lower than with amalgam but not than composite (Ge et al, 2023); discharge is as fast as recharge (Roberson et al, 2006); the therapeutic level is unknown (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### 8A. Clinical advice
Advise high-risk patients on daily neutral fluoride toothpaste or gel to recharge GIC restorations; acidulated phosphate fluoride products (pH about 2-3) can erode GIC and are avoided (Mount et al, 2016).

### 9. Conclusion
GIC is a fluoride reservoir that releases and recharges by ion exchange; the mechanism is clear in the laboratory, but the clinical benefit over composite is not proven.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Release: burst, then low steady level for years; recharge from toothpaste, gel, rinse (Mount, 1994; Roberson et al, 2006).
- Mechanism: concentration-gradient ion exchange; F similar in size to OH (Mount et al, 2016).
- Action: fluorapatite, remineralisation, enzyme inhibition (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Bhat et al, 2019).
- Benefit shown against amalgam, not composite (Ge et al, 2023).
