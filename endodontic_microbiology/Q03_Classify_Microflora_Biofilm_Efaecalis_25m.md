## STUDY MODE

### 1. Terminology
Cohen (Berman et al, 2021) prefers *microbiota* (all the microorganisms in a site) to "flora" or "microflora", which preserve an outdated plant-based classification. The exam question uses "microflora", so use the word the question uses and mention the modern term once. *Diversity* means richness (number of species) plus relative abundance (Berman et al, 2021). Endodontic infections develop in a previously sterile place, so any species found could be a pathogen or at least play a part in the community's ecology; culture and molecular studies are cross-sectional, so only association can be inferred (Berman et al, 2021). [Certain]

### 2. Classification
**By location and time of entry** (Cohen; Grossman; Ingle): intraradicular or extraradicular.
- *Primary* intraradicular: microorganisms that first invade and colonise the necrotic pulp ("virgin" infection).
- *Secondary*: microorganisms not present in the primary infection, introduced after professional intervention.
- *Persistent*: members of the primary or secondary infection that resisted intracanal antimicrobial procedures and endured nutrient deprivation in the treated canal (Berman et al, 2021).
- *Extraradicular*: microbial invasion of the inflamed periradicular tissues, dependent on or independent of the intraradicular infection (Berman et al, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Persistent and secondary infections are for the most part clinically indistinguishable (Berman et al, 2021).

**By phylum** (Berman et al, 2021): nine phyla in endodontic infections: Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Spirochaetes, Fusobacteria, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Synergistetes, TM7 and SR1; high-throughput sequencing has also found Tenericutes, Acidobacteria, Deinococcus-Thermus, Chloroflexi and Cyanobacteria at low abundance. Fungi and archaea are only occasionally found.

**By oxygen need and Gram stain** (Grossman, Box 2.1, Gopikrishna, 2021; the column layout of this box is garbled in the converted file, so check the genus placement against the printed book): Gram-positive cocci (Finegoldia, Peptoniphilus, Peptostreptococcus obligate; Enterococcus, Staphylococcus, Streptococcus facultative), Gram-negative cocci (Veillonella obligate; Neisseria facultative), Gram-positive rods (Actinomyces, Eggerthella, Eubacterium, Filifactor, Propionibacterium, Lactobacillus), Gram-negative rods (Alloprevotella, Dialister, Fusobacterium, Porphyromonas, Prevotella, Tannerella, Treponema; Capnocytophaga, Eikenella, Haemophilus facultative).

**Taxonomic changes** (Grossman): black-pigmented Bacteroides split into Prevotella (saccharolytic) and Porphyromonas (asaccharolytic).

### 3. Microbiota in each infection
- **Primary:** mixed community dominated by anaerobes; 10³–10⁸ cells per canal; mean 10–20 species per canal by molecular studies; lesion size proportional to richness and load (small lesions about 12 taxa, 5–10 mm about 16, over 10 mm about 20; some over 40) (Berman et al, 2021). Prevalent genera: Gram-negative Fusobacterium, Dialister, Porphyromonas, Prevotella, Tannerella, Treponema, Pyramidobacter, Campylobacter, Veillonella; Gram-positive Parvimonas, Filifactor, Pseudoramibacter, Streptococcus, Propionibacterium, Olsenella, Actinomyces (Berman et al, 2021). Early oxygen-rich phase is facultative-dominated, later anaerobes dominate as oxygen falls and proteins from tissue fluid become the nutrient source (Gopikrishna, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- **Secondary:** Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus, E. coli and other enteric rods, Candida and E. faecalis within the canal strongly suggest a secondary infection, as none is normally found in primary infections (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Persistent / root-filled teeth:** decreased diversity; canals with apparently good treatment harbour 1–5 species while inadequately treated canals can reach 10–20; 10³–10⁷ cells per canal; Gram-positive facultatives or anaerobes (streptococci, P. micra, Propionibacterium, P. alactolyticus, Actinomyces, lactobacilli, E. faecalis, Olsenella uli); Candida in up to 18% of treated canals (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- **Extraradicular:** most common is the acute apical abscess; Actinomyces and Propionibacterium propionicum are the typical independent extraradicular species (Berman et al, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 4. Biofilm in this context
Defined and described in Q1: a sessile community in a self-produced EPS matrix (about 15% cells, 85% matrix), and the usual way bacteria are organised in infected canals; biofilms in about 80% of apical canals with apical periodontitis (Berman et al, 2021).

### 5. Enterococcus faecalis and treatment
- **What it is:** Gram-positive facultative anaerobic coccus (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Where it is found:** the most frequent species in root-canal-treated teeth, prevalence up to 90% in some studies; root-filled teeth about nine times more likely to harbour it than primary infections; about 1% (range 0.1–100%) of total bacterial counts in treated canals (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Grossman gives 40% in primary and 24–77% in persistent infections (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Why it matters for treatment:** it is commonly recovered from teeth treated over multiple visits or left open for drainage, so it behaves as a secondary invader that can later become persistent; it is a transient oral species whose source may be food (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022; Berman et al, 2021).
- **Why it survives:** invades dentinal tubules and escapes instruments and irrigants; forms biofilms on canal walls; resists calcium hydroxide, possibly through a proton pump that acidifies the cytoplasm at high pH; enters a viable-but-non-cultivable state; survives nutrient scarcity and flourishes when nutrients return; can colonise as a single species unlike most anaerobes (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). Grossman adds extreme alkaline pH, salt, temperature range 10–45°C (survives 60°C for 30 min), virulence factors (hemolysin, gelatinase, cytolysin, aggregation substance, lipoteichoic acid, extracellular superoxide), serum as nutrient and suppressing lymphocytes (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Treatment implications:**
  - Mechanical preparation alone does not predictably clear it: all canals in an ex vivo E. faecalis study still had viable bacteria; larger apical size (30 to 40) reduced counts (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
  - Calcium hydroxide alone could not kill it inside dentinal tubules, but combined with iodine potassium iodide or chlorhexidine it disinfected dentine in vitro (Berman et al, 2021).
  - 2% chlorhexidine was very effective in eliminating an E. faecalis biofilm in vitro (Berman et al, 2021); 6% NaOCl was most effective against 3-week-old E. faecalis biofilm in dentine canals (Berman et al, 2021).
  - A final chlorhexidine rinse reduced culture-positive canals from 67% to 8% in one clinical study, but this was total cultivable bacteria, not E. faecalis specifically (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Is it the cause of failure? Contested.** Prevalence and attributes "do not suffice to prove a causative role" (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). Reasons for doubt: not detected in all studies; rarely among the most dominant species; as prevalent in treated teeth without lesions as with lesions; streptococci often exceed it (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Possible reconciling factors: spatial distribution, load, clonal virulence (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim)
**Claim — E. faecalis is the principal cause of endodontic treatment failure.** Grossman describes it as a persistent organism that "plays a major role" in persistent lesions and quotes prevalence of 24–77% in persistent infections (Gopikrishna, 2021). Cohen, Ingle and Siqueira and Rôças report a higher maximum prevalence (up to 90%) and the nine-fold association with root-filled teeth, but explicitly state that a causative role is unproven, because it is often not dominant and is equally prevalent in treated teeth with and without lesions (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). The evidence is cross-sectional (prevalence), so it can show association, not cause. What can be concluded: E. faecalis is a marker of secondary or persistent infection and a species that survives standard treatment well; what cannot be concluded is that eliminating it would remove most failures. Clinical implication: treat the whole community with thorough disinfection and seal the canal. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Estimated writing time: ~39 minutes at 22 words/min; ~853 words)**

### 1. Introduction
Endodontic microbiota (microflora) are the microorganisms in the root canal system and periradicular tissues. Infection develops in a previously sterile site after pulp necrosis from caries, trauma or leakage, so any species found may be a pathogen or at least part of the community's ecology; studies are cross-sectional and show association only (Berman et al, 2021). Bacteria enter through dentinal tubules, traumatic exposure, coronal leakage, resorption, periodontal tissues and anachoresis (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 2. Classification
**A. By location and time of entry**

```
Endodontic infections
├── Intraradicular
│   ├── Primary    – first invaders of the necrotic pulp
│   ├── Secondary  – introduced after professional intervention
│   └── Persistent – survived treatment and starvation
└── Extraradicular – dependent on / independent of the intraradicular infection
```
(Berman et al, 2021). Persistent and secondary infections are mostly clinically indistinguishable.

**B. By phylum:** Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Spirochaetes, Fusobacteria, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Synergistetes, TM7 and SR1; sequencing also finds low-abundance Tenericutes, Acidobacteria and others. Fungi and archaea are occasional (Berman et al, 2021).
**C. By Gram stain and oxygen need:** Gram-positive and Gram-negative cocci and rods, each as obligate or facultative anaerobes (Gopikrishna, 2021).
**D. Taxonomy:** black-pigmented Bacteroides are now Prevotella (saccharolytic) and Porphyromonas (asaccharolytic) (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 3. Microbiota by type of infection
| Type | Features | Typical organisms |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Mixed, anaerobe-dominated; 10³–10⁸ cells; mean 10–20 species | Fusobacterium, Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Tannerella, Treponema, Dialister, Parvimonas, Filifactor, Streptococcus |
| Secondary | From intervention or leakage | Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, E. coli, Candida, E. faecalis |
| Persistent | Fewer species (1–5 if well treated); Gram-positive | Streptococci, E. faecalis, Actinomyces, lactobacilli, Olsenella; Candida in up to 18% |
| Extraradicular | Periradicular invasion; abscess | Actinomyces, Propionibacterium propionicum |

- Lesion size rises with richness and load: about 12 taxa in lesions under 5 mm, about 16 in 5–10 mm, about 20 over 10 mm (Berman et al, 2021).
- Early infection is facultative-dominated; as oxygen falls and tissue-fluid proteins become the main nutrient, obligate anaerobes replace them (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- E. faecalis, Candida, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus and enteric rods are not normal in primary infection, so their presence suggests secondary infection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 4. Biofilm
A biofilm is a sessile multicellular community attached to a surface and enmeshed in self-produced EPS: about 15% microcolonies and 85% matrix, separated by water channels (Berman et al, 2021). It is the usual form of canal infection, found in the apical canal of about 80% of cases. Resistance comes from the matrix barrier, slow growth and dormancy, persister cells and quorum sensing. Categories: intracanal, extraradicular, periapical and biomaterial-centred (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 5. Role of E. faecalis in treatment
**Features:** a Gram-positive facultative anaerobic coccus (Gopikrishna, 2021).
**Prevalence:** the most frequently detected species in root-filled teeth, up to 90%, and about nine times likelier than in primary infections; about 1% of total bacterial counts (range 0.1–100%) (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
**Behaviour:** commonly recovered from multi-visit or open-drainage teeth, so it acts as a secondary invader that later becomes persistent; its source may be food (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
**Survival factors:**
- Invades dentinal tubules and escapes instruments and irrigants.
- Forms biofilm on canal walls.
- Resists calcium hydroxide, possibly through a proton pump that acidifies the cytoplasm (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- Enters a viable-but-non-cultivable state and survives nutrient scarcity.
- Can colonise as a single species.
- Tolerates extreme alkaline pH, salt, 10–45°C and 60°C for 30 minutes; produces lytic enzymes, gelatinase, aggregation substance and lipoteichoic acid; suppresses lymphocytes (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Treatment implications:**
- Instrumentation alone does not clear it, but a larger apical preparation (no. 30 to no. 40) reduced counts (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Use NaOCl in large volume with activation; 6% NaOCl was most effective against old E. faecalis biofilm in dentine, and 2% CHX eliminated an E. faecalis biofilm in vitro (Berman et al, 2021).
- Calcium hydroxide alone could not kill it inside tubules, but combined with chlorhexidine or iodine potassium iodide it disinfected dentine in vitro (Berman et al, 2021).
- Prevent re-entry: aseptic technique, rubber dam, and a bacteria-tight coronal seal.

**Caution:** prevalence does not prove cause. E. faecalis is missing from some studies of failed cases, is rarely dominant, is as common in treated teeth without lesions, and streptococci often exceed it (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Grossman, in contrast, assigns it a major role in persistent lesions (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 5a. Bacteria left at obturation (Berman et al, 2021)
- Persisting bacteria are mostly in areas inaccessible to treatment; one to five species and 10²–10⁵ cells per sample remain after good treatment. Gram-negative bacteria are usually eliminated, so Gram-positive species predominate.
- Healing can occur despite residual bacteria if they die after filling (toxic filling material, no nutrients, disrupted ecology), are subcritical in number or virulence, or are sealed where the periradicular tissues cannot reach them.
- Residual bacteria influence outcome only if they withstand starvation (including a dormant state), resist disruption of quorum sensing, food webs and biofilm structure, reach a critical load, reach the periradicular tissues through foramina or perforations, and express virulence in the changed environment; host resistance also matters.

### 6. Conclusion
The microbiota differ by type of infection. After treatment the community is smaller and mainly Gram-positive and biofilm-based, and E. faecalis is the best-known survivor, though its role as the sole cause of failure is not proven.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Merging secondary and persistent infection; they differ in when the organisms entered.
2. Saying E. faecalis is common in primary infections; it is not, and is about nine times likelier in treated teeth.
3. Stating E. faecalis as the proven cause of failure.
4. Quoting "flora" figures without a source; numbers differ between books (see below).
5. Listing Bacteroides as a current genus for black-pigmented anaerobes (now Prevotella and Porphyromonas).
