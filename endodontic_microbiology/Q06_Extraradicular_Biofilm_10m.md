## STUDY MODE
**Definition.** An extraradicular biofilm is a biofilm adhered to the external root surface (cementum) around the apical foramen of an infected tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021; Gomes and Pinheiro in Fouad, 2017). It is one form of extraradicular infection, which also includes acute apical abscess and actinomycosis (Berman et al, 2021).

**Origin.** Intraradicular biofilm bacteria can reach the external apical surface through the dentinal tubules or apical foramen (Fouad, 2017). Cohen describes the infection as a continuum of intraradicular and extraradicular segments in which the intraradicular part usually fosters the extraradicular part, although the extraradicular part can sometimes become independent (Berman et al, 2021). Routes: advance of bacteria that overcome host defences at or beyond the foramen; persistence in the lesion after an acute abscess; and apical extrusion of dentinal debris and bacterial flocs during instrumentation, especially over-instrumentation (Berman et al, 2021).

**Structure.** Cocci, rods and filaments in an extracellular matrix, often on areas of cementum resorption, coated by a smooth material that protects from host cells; fungal forms and mineralised, calculus-like areas have been seen (Fouad, 2017).

**Prevalence.** Rare in untreated teeth: 6 of 100 teeth with apical periodontitis, usually dependent on an intraradicular biofilm and associated with symptoms (Ricucci and Siqueira, reported in Berman et al, 2021 and Fouad, 2017). Findings vary: some SEM studies saw extraradicular microorganisms in most root tips while another found them in 1 of 27 (Fouad, 2017). They are more frequent in post-treatment disease: in one study they were found in all 10 persistent cases and 3 of primary cases (Fouad, 2017).

**Significance.** Possible cause of post-treatment apical periodontitis (with periapical actinomycosis), possibly through persistent inflammation and endotoxin from Gram-negative bacteria, but most failures still come from persistent intraradicular infection in inaccessible apical areas; in 24 treated teeth all had intraradicular bacteria while only 5 had extraradicular biofilms (Fouad, 2017). Cohen links extraradicular biofilm and complex apical canal arrangements with chronic abscesses with sinus tract and "wet canals" (Berman et al, 2021).

**Management.** Bacteria in an acute abscess are usually handled by host defences after the intraradicular infection is controlled and drainage is established; persistent independent extraradicular infection may need apical surgery [RECALL; not retrieved from the sources read]. Prevention: avoid apical extrusion, maintain apical patency and disinfect thoroughly (Berman et al, 2021).

## EXAM MODE
**(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes at 22 words/min; ~346 words)**

### Definition
A biofilm adhered to the external (cemental) root surface around the apical foramen of an infected tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021). It is one form of extraradicular infection, alongside acute apical abscess and periapical actinomycosis (Berman et al, 2021).

### Development
- Intraradicular biofilm bacteria reach the external apical surface through the dentinal tubules or apical foramen (Fouad, 2017).
- Advance of bacteria that overcome host defences at or beyond the foramen; bacteria persisting in the lesion after an acute abscess; apical extrusion of infected dentinal chips or bacterial flocs during instrumentation, especially over-instrumentation (Berman et al, 2021).
- The infection is a continuum in which the intraradicular part usually fosters the extraradicular part; occasionally the extraradicular part becomes independent (Berman et al, 2021).

### Structure
Cocci, rods and filaments embedded in a matrix on the apical cementum, especially at resorption areas, coated by a smooth protective material; may be mineralised (calculus-like) and may contain fungal forms (Fouad, 2017).

### Prevalence
- Rare: 6 of 100 teeth with apical periodontitis, usually dependent on intraradicular biofilm and associated with symptoms (Berman et al, 2021; Fouad, 2017).
- Reports vary: some SEM studies found organisms on most root tips, another found them in 1 of 27 teeth.
- More frequent in post-treatment disease (all 10 persistent cases versus 3 primary cases in one study) (Fouad, 2017).

### Clinical significance
- A possible cause of post-treatment apical periodontitis, draining sinus tracts and wet canals; protected from host cells and from root canal disinfection (Berman et al, 2021).
- Gram-negative members release endotoxin, adding to inflammation (Fouad, 2017).
- Most failures still arise from persistent intraradicular infection in inaccessible apical areas: in 24 treated teeth all had intraradicular bacteria and only 5 had extraradicular biofilm (Fouad, 2017).

### Related extraradicular infections
Acute apical abscess, the most common, which usually subsides once the canal infection is controlled and pus drained; periapical actinomycosis (Actinomyces, Propionibacterium propionicum) (Berman et al, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).

### Prevention
Avoid apical extrusion and over-instrumentation, keep apical patency, and disinfect the canal thoroughly (Berman et al, 2021).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- External root surface biofilm; 6% of teeth with apical periodontitis.
- Usually depends on intraradicular biofilm; symptomatic cases.
- More frequent in persistent disease.
- Origins: spread, post-abscess persistence, apical extrusion.
- Actinomyces and P. propionicum for periapical (independent) infection.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling extraradicular biofilm common; it is rare.
2. Treating extraradicular and periapical biofilm as the same thing (Grossman separates them).
3. Overstating its role in failure; intraradicular infection is the main cause.
4. Omitting apical extrusion as a route.
