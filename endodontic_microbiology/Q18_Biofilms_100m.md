### 1. Introduction
In nature most bacteria live as members of surface-attached communities called biofilms rather than as free-floating cells. In the mouth this is dental plaque, and in the root canal it is the usual form of infection (Chávez de Paz, 2015; Berman et al, 2021). Endodontic biofilms are central to the aetiology of apical periodontitis, to the failure of treatment and to the difficulty of disinfection.

### 2. Definition
A biofilm is a sessile multicellular microbial community whose cells are firmly attached to a surface and enmeshed in a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substance (EPS), mostly polysaccharide with proteins and nucleic acids (Berman et al, 2021). Grossman defines it as a community of microcolonies surrounded by a glycocalyx matrix that attaches the cells to a solid substratum (Gopikrishna, 2021). Infected root canals are multispecies biofilms, and the community, not a single species, is the unit of pathogenicity: composition, abundance, synergy and virulence factors act together, so the whole is more than the sum of its parts (Berman et al, 2021).

### 3. Characteristics
- **Altered phenotype:** cells in a biofilm express 20–70% different genes from planktonic cells of the same species (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Organised community:** populations are spatially arranged for metabolic interaction, with food chains, local environment modification, collective protection, coaggregation, cell-cell signalling and horizontal gene transfer (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- **Heterogeneity:** gradients of oxygen, pH, nutrients and cell density create microenvironments; cells at the base may be dead or lysing while surface cells grow actively (Chávez de Paz, 2015).
- **Resistance:** antibiotic concentrations needed to kill biofilm bacteria are about 100–1000 times those for planktonic cells (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Disease behaviour:** biofilms provoke chronic, persistent inflammation proportional to cell density and species composition, and release planktonic cells that can start acute flare-ups (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Advantages to bacteria:** wider habitat range, greater metabolic diversity, protection from competitors, host defences and antimicrobials, facilitated genetic exchange and enhanced pathogenicity (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 4. Ultrastructure
- Microcolonies make up about 15% and the EPS matrix about 85% of volume; the microcolonies are separated by **water channels**; dental biofilms may reach 300 or more cell layers (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- Matrix composition: polysaccharide, protein, DNA, RNA and lipid, with water up to 97% of the biofilm (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Water channels circulate fluid carrying nutrients, wastes and signalling molecules (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- EPS functions: adhesion ("biologic glue"), mechanical stability, store of enzymes and nutrients, water retention, protection from phagocytes and from antimicrobials (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Caution: tower or mushroom microcolonies need shear forces that do not normally occur in canals, so the endodontic structure is still not fully described (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Draw:** tooth wall, conditioning film, microcolonies inside matrix, water channels, detaching planktonic cells.

### 5. Formation of a biofilm
Conditioning film (water, salts, albumin, fibronectin) forms on the surface; a planktonic cell makes weak reversible contact; adhesion becomes irreversible through pili, flagella and sticky exopolymers; other species co-adhere; the biofilm grows, matures and sheds cells (Chávez de Paz, 2015). Grossman lists four stages: conditioning layer, planktonic attachment, detachment (seeding dispersal), and growth and expansion (Gopikrishna, 2021). In canals the biofilm builds gradually behind the infection front as the pulp becomes necrotic (Berman et al, 2021).

### 6. Formation on tooth and restorative surfaces
1. Salivary pellicle (phosphoproteins, glycoproteins) forms within minutes.
2. Planktonic bacteria attach to the pellicle; streptococci are 60–90% of the first flora.
3. Microcolonies and coaggregation of later colonisers (Fusobacterium as a bridge).
4. Mature multispecies biofilm; shift to Gram-negative anaerobes by about 4 days.
In caries-prone conditions the biofilm is rich in acidogenic and aciduric streptococci and lactobacilli.

### 7. Categories of endodontic biofilm
1. **Intracanal:** on radicular dentine; E. faecalis forms one of the most therapy-resistant and prevalent examples.
2. **Extraradicular:** on the cemental surface around the apex; found in only about 6% of cases, nearly always with an intraradicular biofilm and with symptoms (Berman et al, 2021).
3. **Periapical:** independent of canal surfaces; Actinomyces and Propionibacterium propionicum form lesions resistant to endodontic therapy.
4. **Biomaterial-centred:** on an artificial surface (foreign-body biofilm).

Intraradicular biofilm is found in the apical segment of about 80% of canals with apical periodontitis, more often in large lesions and cysts (Berman et al, 2021).

### 8. Mechanisms of antimicrobial resistance
1. **Slow penetration:** the EPS matrix retards diffusion of agents (Gopikrishna, 2021).
2. **Slow growth and dormancy:** starved, stationary-phase cells are less susceptible; dormancy is likely in nutrient-poor treated canals, and dormant biofilm cells reactivated slowly (Berman et al, 2021; Chávez de Paz, 2015).
3. **Persister cells:** a survivor subpopulation that restarts growth once the agent is gone.
4. **Phenotypic subpopulations:** gradients produce low-metabolism and stress-responding cells (Chávez de Paz, 2015).
5. **Arrangement of cells:** outer cells shield inner cells from agents acting on the periphery (Gopikrishna, 2021).
6. **Density and quorum sensing:** enhanced tolerance at high cell density.
7. **Starvation response:** in E. faecalis the (p)ppGpp stringent response supports survival and biofilm formation (Chávez de Paz, 2015).

### 9. Quorum sensing
Quorum sensing is cell-to-cell communication through released chemical signals called **autoinducers**. As the population grows the extracellular concentration rises, and at a threshold (the quorum) the population changes gene expression together (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Gram-negative bacteria: acyl homoserine lactones (AI-1, LuxI/LuxR), mostly species-specific.
- Gram-positive bacteria: modified peptides, for example competence-stimulating peptide in streptococci, linked to DNA uptake.
- AI-2 (LuxS pathway): a cross-species signal (Fouad, 2017).
- It regulates virulence, secondary metabolites and **biofilm formation**; pathogens may delay virulence factors until numbers are high enough to avoid alerting the host (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Quorum-sensing inhibitors are potential antibiofilm agents (Fouad, 2017).

### 10. Endodontic infections in which biofilms occur
**A. By location and time of entry**

```
Endodontic infections
├── Intraradicular
│   ├── Primary    – first invaders of the necrotic pulp
│   ├── Secondary  – introduced after professional intervention
│   └── Persistent – survived treatment and starvation
└── Extraradicular – dependent on / independent of the intraradicular infection
```
(Berman et al, 2021). Persistent and secondary infections are mostly clinically indistinguishable.

**B. By phylum:** Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Spirochaetes, Fusobacteria, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Synergistetes, TM7 and SR1; sequencing also finds low-abundance Tenericutes, Acidobacteria and others. Fungi and archaea are occasional (Berman et al, 2021).
**C. By Gram stain and oxygen need:** Gram-positive and Gram-negative cocci and rods, each as obligate or facultative anaerobes (Gopikrishna, 2021).
**D. Taxonomy:** black-pigmented Bacteroides are now Prevotella (saccharolytic) and Porphyromonas (asaccharolytic) (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 11. Microbiota of the biofilm by type of infection
| Type | Features | Typical organisms |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Mixed, anaerobe-dominated; 10³–10⁸ cells; mean 10–20 species | Fusobacterium, Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Tannerella, Treponema, Dialister, Parvimonas, Filifactor, Streptococcus |
| Secondary | From intervention or leakage | Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, E. coli, Candida, E. faecalis |
| Persistent | Fewer species (1–5 if well treated); Gram-positive | Streptococci, E. faecalis, Actinomyces, lactobacilli, Olsenella; Candida in up to 18% |
| Extraradicular | Periradicular invasion; abscess | Actinomyces, Propionibacterium propionicum |

- Lesion size rises with richness and load: about 12 taxa in lesions under 5 mm, about 16 in 5–10 mm, about 20 over 10 mm (Berman et al, 2021).
- Early infection is facultative-dominated; as oxygen falls and tissue-fluid proteins become the main nutrient, obligate anaerobes replace them (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- E. faecalis, Candida, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus and enteric rods are not normal in primary infection, so their presence suggests secondary infection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 12. How biofilm bacteria survive treatment
1. Biofilm adherence and matrix.
2. Colonising areas away from the main canal.
3. Protection by tissue remnants, dentine, serum, dead cells.
4. Intrinsic resistance (for example E. faecalis to calcium hydroxide).
5. Adaptation to nutrient loss, including viable-but-non-cultivable state.
6. Niches in the apical canal where nutrients remain.

### 13. Biofilms and apical periodontitis: the evidence
- Intraradicular biofilm in the apical canal in about 80% of cases, in primary and post-treatment disease.
- More frequent in large lesions and cysts: cysts 95%, abscesses 83%, granulomas 69.5%.
- Biofilm morphology varies between teeth; bacteria penetrate dentinal tubules from the base of the biofilm.
- Biofilm on walls of ramifications, lateral canals and isthmuses.
- Extraradicular biofilm in only 6%, nearly always dependent on intraradicular biofilm and symptomatic.
- Flocs ("planktonic biofilms") also occur in the lumen.

### 14. Criteria for a biofilm disease
Adherence to a surface; matrix-encased colonies; confinement to a site; antibiotic resistance despite sensitivity in planktonic form; ineffective host clearance (neutrophils and macrophages around colonies); remission after biofilm disruption. Apical periodontitis fulfils four: criteria 1, 2, 3 and 5; the others are supported indirectly.

### 15. Acute versus chronic disease
- **Acute:** planktonic cells, high counts, virulent community.
- **Chronic:** low-virulence biofilm on canal walls, protected from host defences, causing persistent inflammation.
- Biofilms release planktonic cells that can trigger flare-ups.

### 16. Biofilm persistence and treatment failure
1. Biopsies of failed teeth show bacteria in the apical third, rarely along the whole canal; coronal leakage would colonise the entire canal.
2. Bacteria-positive samples at filling predict a poor outcome, and bacteria can survive a root filling for years (monkey study).
3. Failure is more frequent in teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis than in vital teeth; if leakage were the main cause, rates in vital, necrotic and retreatment cases would be similar.
4. Well-prepared and sealed canals resisted coronal bacterial leakage.

### 17. Extraradicular biofilm: development
- Intraradicular biofilm bacteria reach the external apical surface through the dentinal tubules or apical foramen (Fouad, 2017).
- Advance of bacteria that overcome host defences at or beyond the foramen; bacteria persisting in the lesion after an acute abscess; apical extrusion of infected dentinal chips or bacterial flocs during instrumentation, especially over-instrumentation (Berman et al, 2021).
- The infection is a continuum in which the intraradicular part usually fosters the extraradicular part; occasionally the extraradicular part becomes independent (Berman et al, 2021).

### 18. Extraradicular biofilm: structure
Cocci, rods and filaments embedded in a matrix on the apical cementum, especially at resorption areas, coated by a smooth protective material; may be mineralised (calculus-like) and may contain fungal forms (Fouad, 2017).

### 19. Extraradicular biofilm: prevalence
- Rare: 6 of 100 teeth with apical periodontitis, usually dependent on intraradicular biofilm and associated with symptoms (Berman et al, 2021; Fouad, 2017).
- Reports vary: some SEM studies found organisms on most root tips, another found them in 1 of 27 teeth.
- More frequent in post-treatment disease (all 10 persistent cases versus 3 primary cases in one study) (Fouad, 2017).

### 20. Extraradicular biofilm: significance
- A possible cause of post-treatment apical periodontitis, draining sinus tracts and wet canals; protected from host cells and from root canal disinfection (Berman et al, 2021).
- Gram-negative members release endotoxin, adding to inflammation (Fouad, 2017).
- Most failures still arise from persistent intraradicular infection in inaccessible apical areas: in 24 treated teeth all had intraradicular bacteria and only 5 had extraradicular biofilm (Fouad, 2017).

### 21. E. faecalis and biofilm
**Features:** a Gram-positive facultative anaerobic coccus (Gopikrishna, 2021).
**Prevalence:** the most frequently detected species in root-filled teeth, up to 90%, and about nine times likelier than in primary infections; about 1% of total bacterial counts (range 0.1–100%) (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
**Behaviour:** commonly recovered from multi-visit or open-drainage teeth, so it acts as a secondary invader that later becomes persistent; its source may be food (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
**Survival factors:**
- Invades dentinal tubules and escapes instruments and irrigants.
- Forms biofilm on canal walls.
- Resists calcium hydroxide, possibly through a proton pump that acidifies the cytoplasm (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- Enters a viable-but-non-cultivable state and survives nutrient scarcity.
- Can colonise as a single species.
- Tolerates extreme alkaline pH, salt, 10–45°C and 60°C for 30 minutes; produces lytic enzymes, gelatinase, aggregation substance and lipoteichoic acid; suppresses lymphocytes (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Treatment implications:**
- Instrumentation alone does not clear it, but a larger apical preparation (no. 30 to no. 40) reduced counts (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Use NaOCl in large volume with activation; 6% NaOCl was most effective against old E. faecalis biofilm in dentine, and 2% CHX eliminated an E. faecalis biofilm in vitro (Berman et al, 2021).
- Calcium hydroxide alone could not kill it inside tubules, but combined with chlorhexidine or iodine potassium iodide it disinfected dentine in vitro (Berman et al, 2021).
- Prevent re-entry: aseptic technique, rubber dam, and a bacteria-tight coronal seal.

**Caution:** prevalence does not prove cause. E. faecalis is missing from some studies of failed cases, is rarely dominant, is as common in treated teeth without lesions, and streptococci often exceed it (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Grossman, in contrast, assigns it a major role in persistent lesions (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 22. Biofilm models in research: need
Untouched canal areas harbour biofilm that only irrigation can reach; models test irrigants and techniques against biofilm rather than planktonic cells, which give an optimistic picture (Basrani, 2015).

### 23. Biofilm models in research: types
1. **Membrane models** (membrane filter, nitrocellulose): single species; standardised, reproducible; no dentine substrate.
2. **Hydroxyapatite / dentine disc models** (open biofilm models): mono- or multispecies; biofilm age can be varied; collagen coating improves relevance.
3. **Dentine models:** powder, block, and dentine canal infection by centrifugation (high bacterial numbers in tubules; CLSM quantification) (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
4. **Tooth models:** bovine tooth biofilm; infected extracted teeth or root halves (for example E. faecalis, 72 h).
5. **In vivo animal models:** rat oral cavity, mouse skin wound (Chávez de Paz, 2015).

### 24. Biofilm models in research: variables to control
Biofilm age (3-week biofilms resist NaOCl, CHX and iodine), single versus multispecies, substrate, growth phase, contact time (Basrani, 2015).

### 25. Biofilm models in research: limitations
No standard model; short-incubation biofilms overestimate susceptibility; results cannot be extrapolated directly to clinical performance (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

### 26. Biofilm and restorative materials: adhesion
- **Surface roughness:** Ra above about 0.2 µm increases accumulation; caused by wear, poor finishing, ultrasonic scaling or air polishing.
- **Surface free energy and hydrophobicity:** higher energy surfaces retain more; materials have higher energy than tooth, so margins collect plaque.
- **Surface chemistry and charge, physical forces** (van der Waals, electrostatic, acid–base).
- **Pellicle** reduces and masks material differences in vivo.

### 27. Biofilm and restorative materials: plaque ecology
| Material | Effect on biofilm |
|---|---|
| Resin composite | More S. mutans beside posterior composite; roughening, hydrolysis and softening of resin; monomer and degradation products modulate bacterial growth |
| Amalgam | Low-viability biofilm (mercury); selects mercury-resistant bacteria |
| Gold | Low viability (bacteriostatic) |
| Glass ionomer / RMGIC / compomer | Fluoride release lowers acid production and S. mutans nearby; rechargeable; but roughness increases with biofilm |
| Ceramic | Less biofilm than tooth; zirconia roughens after scaling |
| Denture resin | Candida after streptococcal adhesion; stomatitis |

- Studies of biofilm on restorative materials are in vivo, in situ or in vitro (mono- or multispecies); results are inconsistent and no trend of accumulation by material has been established; biofilm thickness and viability vary (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 28. Role of biofilms in endodontics
- **Aetiology:** apical periodontitis is a biofilm-related disease; intraradicular biofilm is found in the apical canal of about 80% of cases and more often in large lesions and cysts (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Persistence and failure:** biofilms remain in unprepared areas, lateral canals, tubules and between the end of the filling and the foramen (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Flare-ups:** planktonic cells released from biofilm can start acute infection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Tolerance to treatment:** biofilm is tolerant to irrigants, medicaments and systemic antibiotics, which do not reach the avascular canal (Berman et al, 2021).

### 29. Why complete eradication is difficult
- Instrumentation leaves roughly 10–50% of the canal surface unprepared on micro-CT, and biofilm in isthmuses, recesses and ramifications escapes instruments and the short irrigant contact time (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Biofilms older than three weeks become much more resistant to NaOCl, and clinical biofilms are almost always older (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- Killing of E. faecalis in dentine by NaOCl is rapid in the first minutes and then plateaus, because a biofilm contains sensitive and resistant areas (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- The accepted aim is therefore to eliminate as many members of the community as possible and deny substrate to those that remain (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 30. Eradication (control) strategies
**A. Mechanical**
- Instrumentation with irrigation removes over 90% of cultivable bacteria and reduces counts 100–1,000-fold, but negative cultures are not predictable (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- A larger apical preparation gives greater bacterial reduction, and NaOCl's advantage over saline appears mainly with larger preparations.
- The patency file disorganises apical biofilm and carries irrigant apically; apical patency was linked to higher success.
- Expandable instruments (XP-endo Shaper) and finishers touch more wall and dislodge biofilm, though the Shaper increased apical extrusion of bacteria.

**B. Chemical**
- **NaOCl** dissolves organic components of dentine and biofilm and can dissolve the biofilm matrix; 6% NaOCl completely removed experimental biofilm in 15 minutes and was best against three-week-old E. faecalis biofilm (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). Use large volumes, frequently renewed.
- **Chlorhexidine** kills bacteria but leaves the biofilm structure, and was weaker than NaOCl on older multispecies biofilm.
- **EDTA** may detach biofilm from the walls and removes the smear layer; **QMiX** killed up to 12 times more biofilm bacteria than 1% NaOCl or 2% CHX in vitro; 4% peracetic acid also showed antibiofilm activity in vitro (Berman et al, 2021).

- **Concentration is debated:** laboratory biofilm models favour 6% NaOCl, but clinical studies found no significant difference between 0.5% and 5% NaOCl (bacteria-positive canals), and healing of 72% vs 81% for 1% vs 5%; large volume and frequent exchange compensate for concentration. Antibacterial effectiveness is the most important irrigant property, NaOCl remains the irrigant of choice and CHX a possible substitute of similar potency (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**C. Delivery and activation**
- NaOCl penetrates tubules to about 77–300 µm; irrigation reaches further when the needle is near working length and the canal is larger; curvature reduces effect (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

| Method | Mechanism | Evidence (as stated) |
|---|---|---|
| Passive ultrasonic irrigation | Acoustic streaming and cavitation disrupt biofilm | Inconsistent; systematic reviews weak; healing 95% vs 88%, not significant |
| Finishers (XP-endo) | Mechanical touch plus agitation | Greater bacterial and biofilm reduction ex vivo |
| Multisonic (GentleWave) | Cavitation cloud | Passage read gives no clinical outcome |
| PIPS laser | Shock waves | Laboratory activity; no consistent clinical evidence |
| Photodynamic therapy | Photosensitiser plus red light gives reactive oxygen | Mostly in vitro; used after preparation |

**D. Intracanal medication**
- The aim is to eliminate residual biofilm and organisms that survived preparation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Calcium hydroxide acts poorly in biofilms, tubules and ramifications because of low solubility and buffering by dentine and serum.
- Recommended: calcium hydroxide with CPMC–glycerin or 0.12–2% CHX for about 7 days; remove with the master apical file under irrigation, which improved bacterial reduction.
- Medicament effect on multispecies biofilm remains unclear (Berman et al, 2021).

**E. Ecological approach**
- Removing nutrients and key partner species and sealing the canal can collapse the community; partial elimination may instead reorganise it toward more resistant species, hence single-visit preparation with medication and then obturation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**F. Newer, experimental**
- Chitosan and silver nanoparticles, benzalkonium chloride surface coating (about 70-fold less biofilm in vitro, but the effect is lost as the coating exhausts), matrix-degrading enzymes and quorum-sensing inhibitors; all laboratory-stage (Berman et al, 2021; Chávez de Paz, 2015).

### 31. Clinical protocol
1. Rubber dam and disinfection of the field.
2. Crown-down preparation with 2.5% NaOCl after each instrument, to a large apical size, with apical patency.
3. Small needles near working length for delivery.
4. EDTA or citric acid to remove the smear layer, then NaOCl.
5. Supplementary disinfection: ultrasonic activation, finisher, or final 2% CHX with an intermediate rinse to avoid precipitate.
6. Calcium hydroxide paste for about 7 days, then removal with the master apical file.
7. Obturation with a bacteria-tight coronal seal.

### 32. Conclusion
Biofilms are the principal mode of bacterial organisation in endodontic infections. They cause apical periodontitis, protect bacteria from irrigants, medicaments and antibiotics, and are why persistent and post-treatment disease occurs. Eradication in the strict sense is not achievable; the aim is to disrupt and reduce the biofilm below the level that sustains disease, through adequate enlargement, copious frequently renewed NaOCl with activation, intracanal medication where indicated and a bacteria-tight coronal seal (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Biofilm: 15% cells, 85% EPS; water channels; resistance 100-1000x planktonic.
- Four categories (Grossman); extraradicular biofilm only 6%.
- Biofilm in about 80% of apical canals; cysts 95%, abscesses 83%, granulomas 69.5%.
- Resistance: matrix, dormancy, persisters, phenotypic subpopulations, quorum sensing.
- Quorum sensing: AHL (Gram-negative), peptides (Gram-positive), AI-2 (cross-species).
- Eradication means reduction; instrumentation leaves 10-50% unprepared.
- Models: membrane, hydroxyapatite/dentine disc, dentine block/canal infection, tooth, animal.
