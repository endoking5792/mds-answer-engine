## STUDY MODE
**The concept.** Oral biofilms (dental plaque) form on teeth and on biomaterials in the mouth. The interaction runs in both directions: the material's properties influence what attaches and how the community develops, and the biofilm in turn changes the material and the tissues next to it (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). In caries-prone conditions the adherent biofilm is dominated by acidogenic, aciduric streptococci and lactobacilli (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

**Biofilm formation (Craig).** A salivary pellicle of adsorbed phosphoproteins and glycoproteins coats tooth and material within minutes; planktonic bacteria attach to the pellicle; early colonisers divide into microcolonies; later species join through coaggregation to form a matrix-embedded multispecies biofilm. Streptococci recognise pellicle receptors and make up 60–90% of the initial flora on enamel; in the first 4 hours Gram-positive cocci predominate (mitis-group streptococci); rods appear by 8 hours; by 24–48 hours there are cocci, coccobacilli, rods and filaments; by 4 days Gram-negative anaerobes rise, notably Fusobacterium nucleatum, which coaggregates with early and late colonisers. With maturation the flora shifts toward Gram-negative rods, filaments, vibrios and spirochetes, which correlates with gingivitis.

**How the material influences adhesion.**
- Adhesion of proteins and bacteria 5–100 nm from a surface depends on Lifshitz–van der Waals forces, electrostatic interactions and acid–base bonding (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- *Surface roughness:* smooth surfaces attract less biofilm in vivo. A mean roughness (Ra) above about 0.2 µm, or higher surface free energy, increases biofilm accumulation, and roughness has the greater effect when both act. Roughness arises from wear, erosion, air polishing, ultrasonic instrumentation or poor finishing.
- *Surface free energy and hydrophobicity:* low-energy and hydrophobic surfaces retain less biofilm. Restorative materials generally have higher surface energy than tooth, so margins collect more debris, saliva and bacteria, which may partly explain the relatively frequent secondary caries at margins of composite and amalgam.
- *Pellicle masking:* in vivo adhesion falls considerably once a pellicle forms, regardless of the substrate, and the pellicle masks some surface characteristics.

**How the material influences plaque ecology (by material, Craig).**
- *Resin composite:* S. mutans levels are higher in biofilm next to posterior resin restorations than next to amalgam or glass ionomer. Biofilm is linked to rougher composite, acid degradation, hydrolysis of the resin matrix (esterases of saliva and bacteria) and lower surface microhardness. Residual monomers and degradation products (TEGMA, TEG, methacrylic acid, BisHPPP) modulate growth of bacteria near the restoration, and this feeds a cycle of roughness and attachment that puts adjacent enamel at risk. Bacteria may also enter the adhesive interface, causing secondary caries and pulp pathology.
- *Amalgam and gold:* low-viability biofilms, attributed to mercury(II) in amalgam and a bacteriostatic effect of gold; amalgam promotes mercury-resistant bacteria, with parallel tetracycline resistance (though mercury-resistant bacteria also occur in children without amalgam).
- *Glass ionomer, resin-modified glass ionomer, compomer:* fluoride release can neutralise biofilm acids, may affect bacterial metabolism, and the materials can be recharged by fluoride toothpaste. However, biofilm accumulation on them is linked to increased roughness (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Hilton et al add that fluoride affects bacteria by inhibiting metabolism, growth and, at high concentration, killing; released fluoride is below bactericidal level, so the effect is on acid production and the succession of bacteria. It is local: plaque fluoride was not raised on teeth away from the restoration, and protection was confined to about 1 mm from the margin. Plaque on glass ionomer had more fluoride and less S. mutans nearby than plaque on composite, and early studies found comparable plaque on amalgam, gold, acrylic and cast gold but altered plaque on fluoride-releasing silicate cement (Hilton et al, 2013).
- *Ceramics:* accumulate less biofilm than adjacent tooth; zirconia roughness increases after scaling, which could raise accumulation.
- *Dentures:* Candida biofilms follow initial streptococcal adhesion; linked to denture stomatitis.

**Margins and secondary caries.** Secondary caries at the gingival margin is the commonest reason to replace restorations and has an aetiology like primary caries (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Loss of margin integrity (amalgam ditching and corrosion, composite and bonding shrinkage, glass ionomer dissolution, chipping, overhangs, ledges) allows plaque stagnation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**Control.** Mechanical removal by brushing is more effective than chemical agents, because biofilm bacteria resist antimicrobials and agents are usually tested on planktonic cells; brushing may roughen restorations over time. Prolonged antimicrobial use raises resistance concerns, and the accepted hypothesis is that the flora should be kept from shifting to a disease-favouring ecology rather than eliminated (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). ## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim)
**Claim — fluoride-releasing restorative materials reduce secondary caries by altering plaque.** Hilton et al report laboratory and in vivo plaque findings: lower S. mutans and higher plaque fluoride near glass ionomer than composite, a mechanism through reduced acid production, but an effect confined to about 1 mm and no change in fluoride in plaque on distant teeth (Hilton et al, 2013). Craig states that clinical studies have not clearly shown fewer secondary lesions with fluoride-releasing than non-releasing materials (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Hilton et al also note that some "secondary" caries may be new lesions beside old restorations, not caries left beneath them. So the biological plausibility is supported but the clinical benefit is not established; what can be concluded is a local effect on adjacent plaque, not a proven reduction in restoration failure. ## EXAM MODE
**(Estimated writing time: ~38 minutes at 22 words/min; ~842 words)**

### 1. Concept of biofilm–restoration interaction
Dental plaque is a surface-adherent, spatially organised polymicrobial community in a polysaccharide matrix. It forms on tooth and on restorative materials, and the relationship is two-way: the material influences adhesion and the ecology of the biofilm, and the biofilm degrades the material and threatens the tooth–restoration margin (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

Prolonged biofilm accumulation can also cause periodontal disease and peri-implantitis, depending on where it attaches, and biofilm on denture base resin is hard to remove, needing mechanical or chemical means (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 2. Biofilm formation on a restoration
1. Salivary pellicle (phosphoproteins, glycoproteins) forms within minutes.
2. Planktonic bacteria attach to the pellicle; streptococci are 60–90% of the first flora.
3. Microcolonies and coaggregation of later colonisers (Fusobacterium as a bridge).
4. Mature multispecies biofilm; shift to Gram-negative anaerobes by about 4 days.
In caries-prone conditions the biofilm is rich in acidogenic and aciduric streptococci and lactobacilli.

### 3. How materials influence bacterial adhesion
- **Surface roughness:** Ra above about 0.2 µm increases accumulation; caused by wear, poor finishing, ultrasonic scaling or air polishing.
- **Surface free energy and hydrophobicity:** higher energy surfaces retain more; materials have higher energy than tooth, so margins collect plaque.
- **Surface chemistry and charge, physical forces** (van der Waals, electrostatic, acid–base).
- **Pellicle** reduces and masks material differences in vivo.

### 4. How materials influence plaque ecology
| Material | Effect on biofilm |
|---|---|
| Resin composite | More S. mutans beside posterior composite; roughening, hydrolysis and softening of resin; monomer and degradation products modulate bacterial growth |
| Amalgam | Low-viability biofilm (mercury); selects mercury-resistant bacteria |
| Gold | Low viability (bacteriostatic) |
| Glass ionomer / RMGIC / compomer | Fluoride release lowers acid production and S. mutans nearby; rechargeable; but roughness increases with biofilm |
| Ceramic | Less biofilm than tooth; zirconia roughens after scaling |
| Denture resin | Candida after streptococcal adhesion; stomatitis |

- Studies of biofilm on restorative materials are in vivo, in situ or in vitro (mono- or multispecies); results are inconsistent and no trend of accumulation by material has been established; biofilm thickness and viability vary (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 5. Consequences
- **Secondary caries** at the gingival margin, the commonest reason for replacement, with an aetiology like primary caries (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- **Marginal breakdown** (ditching, shrinkage gaps, overhangs, ledges) causes plaque stagnation and periodontal inflammation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Material degradation:** roughness, matrix hydrolysis, reduced hardness.

### 5a. Mechanisms of adhesion to a material
- Adhesion of salivary proteins and bacteria at 5–100 nm from the surface depends on Lifshitz–van der Waals forces, electrostatic interactions and acid–base bonding.
- Smooth surfaces attract less biofilm in vivo; hydrophobic supragingival surfaces attract less biofilm than hydrophilic ones over 9 days.
- Low surface energy surfaces retained the smallest amount of biofilm over several days, whatever the pellicle.
- The interplay of roughness and energy: roughness has the greater effect when both act.

### 5b. Other materials
- **Titanium implants:** reduction of Ra below 0.2 µm has no major further effect; titanium nitride coating reduced adhesion in vivo; hydrophobicity, surface chemistry and free energy matter (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- **Fluoride-releasing materials:** recharged by fluoride toothpaste; fluoride inhibits metabolism and growth, but is below bactericidal level, so it alters acid production and the succession of bacteria; the protective zone was about 1 mm; early fluoride-releasing silicate cement altered plaque while amalgam, gold, acrylic and cast gold showed comparable plaque (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 5c. Margin failure and plaque stagnation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015)
Amalgam creep, corrosion and ditching; shrinkage at composite and bonding margins; dissolution and shrinkage of glass ionomer margins; chipping under load; overhangs, ledges and poor contour. If the patient can keep the failed margin plaque- and caries-free and there is no aesthetic or functional concern, partial loss of integrity may not justify replacement.

### 6. Fluoride-releasing materials
Fluoride inhibits bacterial metabolism and acid production but not at bactericidal levels; the effect is local (about 1 mm); clinical reduction of secondary caries has not been clearly shown (Hilton et al, 2013; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 6a. Cycle of damage
Rough surface → more bacterial attachment → acid production and resin hydrolysis → more roughness and lower surface hardness → greater risk of secondary caries in adjacent enamel (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 6b. Further points on fluoride-releasing materials (Hilton et al, 2013)
- High fluoride release in the first few days after placement may help because initial microleakage often follows placement.
- Early silicate cements released much fluoride but dissolved and failed; this led to glass ionomer cement.
- Some lesions called secondary caries may in fact be new lesions beside previous restorations, not caries left beneath them.
- Composite is degraded by frequent acidic fluoride solutions, with filler dislodgement at the filler–resin interface.

### 7. Management
- Finish and polish restorations to a smooth surface; avoid overhangs and open margins; check margins at recall.
- Mechanical plaque control (brushing) is more effective than chemical agents; antimicrobials are limited by biofilm resistance and resistant strains.
- Aim: keep the flora from shifting to a disease-favouring ecology, not eliminate it (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Brushing, especially interproximally and on posterior teeth, is crucial for removing tenacious biofilm, though wear can roughen restorations and permit more attachment (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Pellicle → attachment → microcolony → coaggregation → mature biofilm; streptococci 60–90% of early flora.
- Ra above about 0.2 µm and higher surface energy increase biofilm.
- More S. mutans next to posterior composite than amalgam or glass ionomer.
- Fluoride release: local (about 1 mm); clinical benefit on secondary caries not clearly shown.
- Mechanical removal beats chemical control.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Describing only material-to-biofilm effects and forgetting the biofilm-to-material damage.
2. Claiming fluoride-releasing materials are proven to prevent secondary caries.
