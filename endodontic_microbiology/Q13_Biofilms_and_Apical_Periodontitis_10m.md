## STUDY MODE
**Evidence that apical periodontitis is a biofilm disease.** Numerous morphological studies had shown bacterial aggregations on canal walls; the strongest evidence is the histobacteriologic study by Ricucci and Siqueira of untreated teeth with primary apical periodontitis and treated teeth with post-treatment disease (Berman et al, 2021). Its findings:
1. Intraradicular biofilms were seen in the apical segment of about 80% of canals, in both primary and post-treatment disease.
2. Biofilm morphology varied from tooth to tooth (thickness, morphotypes, cell-to-matrix ratio).
3. Bacteria from the base of the biofilm often invaded the dentinal tubules beneath.
4. Biofilms covered the walls of apical ramifications, lateral canals and isthmuses.
5. Biofilms were more frequent with large lesions, which reflect a longstanding process and an older, mature biofilm; this helps explain why outcome relates to lesion size.
6. Prevalence was 95% in cysts, 83% in abscesses and 69.5% in granulomas, and biofilms were significantly associated with epithelialised lesions.
7. Extraradicular biofilms were infrequent (6%), nearly always with an intraradicular biofilm and always in symptomatic cases.
8. Bacterial flocs ("planktonic biofilms") were also seen in the canal lumen, ramifications and isthmuses (Berman et al, 2021).

**Criteria for a biofilm-related disease (Berman et al, 2021).** Six are listed: adherence to a surface; microcolonies in a matrix on direct examination; infection confined to a site; resistance to antibiotics although planktonic cells are susceptible; ineffective host clearance with neutrophils and macrophages around the aggregates; remission when the biofilm is disrupted. Apical periodontitis meets four (adherence, matrix-encased colonies, confinement to the canal system, and neutrophils facing the biofilm). The antibiotic criterion is supported by the lack of efficacy of systemic antibiotics in the avascular canal, and the remission criterion is supported indirectly: biofilms are frequent in treated teeth with disease, successful teeth show no canal biofilm, and negative cultures at filling relate to better outcome (Berman et al, 2021).

**Community as pathogen (TEI).** No single species is specific for a disease form, but community profiles follow patterns related to presentation (asymptomatic, acute abscess, post-treatment); communities differ greatly between individuals and geographical regions, and the apical and coronal canal differ in composition (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**Acute versus chronic disease (TEI).** Biofilm cells express many virulence genes less than planktonic cells; acute infection is linked to planktonic cells, high cell counts and a highly virulent community; chronic apical periodontitis is linked to a lower-virulence community organised as a biofilm on the canal walls, inaccessible to host defences and in close contact with the periapical ligament, which reacts with persistent inflammation. Biofilms are less aggressive in causing immediate damage but cause persistent inflammation, and they release enough planktonic cells to start acute exacerbations. Tissue damage is proportional to cell density and species composition (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**Clinical consequences.** Biofilms on uninstrumented walls, in isthmuses and in tubules explain persistence after treatment; systemic antibiotics fail; treatment aims to disrupt and disinfect the biofilm (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). [Certain]

### Introduction
Apical periodontitis is an inflammatory disease of the periapical tissues caused by infection of the root canal. Bacteria in the canal are organised mainly as biofilms (Berman et al, 2021).

### Evidence for a biofilm aetiology (Ricucci and Siqueira study, in Berman et al, 2021)
- Intraradicular biofilm in the apical canal in about 80% of cases, in both primary and post-treatment disease.
- More frequent in large lesions, which reflect a longstanding process: cysts 95%, abscesses 83%, granulomas 69.5%.
- Morphology varies between teeth (thickness, morphotypes, cell-to-matrix ratio).
- Bacteria from the base of the biofilm invade the dentinal tubules beneath it.
- Biofilm covers the walls of ramifications, lateral canals and isthmuses.
- Extraradicular biofilm in only 6%, nearly always with intraradicular biofilm and with symptoms.
- Flocs ("planktonic biofilms") also occur in the lumen.

### Criteria for a biofilm disease (Berman et al, 2021)
1. Bacteria adhered to a surface.
2. Clusters or microcolonies in an extracellular matrix on direct examination.
3. Infection confined to a site.
4. Difficult to eradicate with antibiotics although the cells are susceptible in planktonic form.
5. Ineffective host clearance, with neutrophils and macrophages around the aggregates.
6. Remission when the biofilm is eliminated or disrupted.
Apical periodontitis fulfils four (1, 2, 3 and 5); the fourth is supported by the failure of systemic antibiotics in the avascular canal, and the sixth indirectly, since successful teeth show no canal biofilm and negative cultures at filling relate to better outcome.

### Community as pathogen (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
Disease results from the composition, abundance, synergy and virulence of the whole community, not a single species; communities differ between individuals but follow patterns for each form of disease.

### Acute versus chronic (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
- Acute: planktonic cells, high counts, virulent community.
- Chronic: lower-virulence biofilm on canal walls, protected from host defences, causing persistent inflammation proportional to cell density and species composition.
- Biofilms release planktonic cells that can trigger acute flare-ups.

### Significance
Biofilms in unprepared areas explain persistence after treatment and the failure of antibiotics; treatment must disrupt biofilm through instrumentation, irrigation with activation and medication.
