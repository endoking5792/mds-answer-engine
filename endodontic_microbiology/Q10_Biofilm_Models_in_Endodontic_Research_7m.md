## STUDY MODE
**Why models are needed.** Biofilms on untouched canal walls and in tissue remnants can only be removed chemically, so models are needed to test how irrigation and instrumentation kill or remove biofilm bacteria (Basrani, 2015). Testing against planktonic bacteria gives an over-optimistic picture, so biofilms are now recommended for direct contact tests (Basrani, 2015). Planktonic bacteria in starvation can be 1,000 times more resistant than exponential-phase cells (Basrani, 2015).

**Types of model.**
1. *Membrane-based, single-species:* biofilms on membrane filter discs or nitrocellulose membranes; standardised and reproducible, allowing many assays, but without a dentine-like substrate and with few species; a 2-day biofilm was less resistant than a 10-day biofilm (Basrani, 2015).
2. *Hydroxyapatite or dentine disc, open biofilm models:* mono- or multispecies biofilms grown from oral, periodontal or root canal bacteria on standardised discs in microaerophilic or anaerobic conditions, exposed to irrigants and read by culture or, preferably, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Biofilms of different age can be tested; collagen-coated hydroxyapatite supports a multispecies model that may serve as a standard (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019; Basrani, 2015).
3. *Dentine-based models:* dentine powder (shows inactivation by dentine, but loses microanatomy and is hard to use with biofilms), dentine block (simulates chemistry and anatomy, allows biofilms, but laborious and not fully standardisable), and the dentine canal infection model in which bacteria are forced deep into tubules by centrifugation so killing can be quantified by CLSM with viability staining (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
4. *Tooth models:* a bovine tooth biofilm (mucin-coated, five-species) and infected extracted human teeth or root halves, often with E. faecalis; short incubation (for example 72 h) gives biofilms less resistant than true older canal biofilms (Basrani, 2015).
5. *In vivo models:* antibiotic-treated rat oral cavity and mouse skin wound infection; the rat model resembles the disease more closely, the wound model is easier to manage (Chávez de Paz, 2015).

**Read-out.** Culture (CFU), CLSM with LIVE/DEAD stain (membrane integrity), SEM for ultrastructure, FISH for species distribution, tetrazolium salts for respiratory activity (Chávez de Paz, 2015).

**Limits.** No standardised method; results vary with growth phase, biofilm age, substrate, species and analysis. Multispecies biofilms became resistant after 3 weeks, and clinical biofilms are older; conclusions from in vitro models must be drawn with caution (Basrani, 2015; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

## EXAM MODE
**(Estimated writing time: ~11 minutes at 22 words/min; ~252 words)**

### Need
Untouched canal areas harbour biofilm that only irrigation can reach; models test irrigants and techniques against biofilm rather than planktonic cells, which give an optimistic picture (Basrani, 2015).

### Types
1. **Membrane models** (membrane filter, nitrocellulose): single species; standardised, reproducible; no dentine substrate.
2. **Hydroxyapatite / dentine disc models** (open biofilm models): mono- or multispecies; biofilm age can be varied; collagen coating improves relevance.
3. **Dentine models:** powder, block, and dentine canal infection by centrifugation (high bacterial numbers in tubules; CLSM quantification) (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
4. **Tooth models:** bovine tooth biofilm; infected extracted teeth or root halves (for example E. faecalis, 72 h).
5. **In vivo animal models:** rat oral cavity, mouse skin wound (Chávez de Paz, 2015).

### Analysis
Culture (CFU), CLSM with LIVE/DEAD staining, SEM, FISH, tetrazolium viability markers.

### Variables to control
Biofilm age (3-week biofilms resist NaOCl, CHX and iodine), single versus multispecies, substrate, growth phase, contact time (Basrani, 2015).

### Advantages and drawbacks of the main models
| Model | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Membrane disc | Standard, reproducible, many assays | No dentine substrate; few species; short incubation |
| Hydroxyapatite / dentine disc | Multispecies, adjustable age, CLSM read-out | Not a canal; substrate differences |
| Dentine block / canal infection | True dentine chemistry and tubules; centrifugation gives high numbers | Laborious; limited standardisation; contamination risk |
| Extracted tooth | Close to clinical anatomy | Variation in canal size and volume; hard to standardise preparation and sampling |
| Animal | In vivo features | Hard to establish a mixed infection and to manage the native microbiota |

### Limitations
No standard model; short-incubation biofilms overestimate susceptibility; results cannot be extrapolated directly to clinical performance (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Biofilm, not planktonic, models for direct-contact tests.
- Models: membrane, hydroxyapatite/dentine disc, dentine block/powder/centrifugation canal infection, tooth, animal.
- CLSM with LIVE/DEAD is the preferred read-out.
- 3-week multispecies biofilms become very resistant.
