## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim 1 — E. faecalis is the principal cause of endodontic treatment failure.** Grossman describes it as a persistent organism that "plays a major role" in persistent lesions and quotes prevalence of 24–77% in persistent infections (Gopikrishna, 2021). Cohen, Ingle and Siqueira and Rôças report a higher maximum prevalence (up to 90%) and the nine-fold association with root-filled teeth, but explicitly state that a causative role is unproven, because it is often not dominant and is equally prevalent in treated teeth with and without lesions (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). The evidence is cross-sectional (prevalence), so it can show association, not cause. What can be concluded: E. faecalis is a marker of secondary or persistent infection and a species that survives standard treatment well; what cannot be concluded is that eliminating it would remove most failures. Clinical implication: treat the whole community with thorough disinfection and seal the canal. [Likely]

**Claim 2 — coronal leakage is the main microbial cause of failure.** The earlier view stressed leakage; the current textbook view is that persistent infection after inadequate disinfection of the apical canal is the more common cause (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). The support is indirect: biopsy distribution of bacteria, outcome by preoperative status, and bacteria at filling predicting outcome. Direct proof is not possible because persistent and secondary infections cannot be told apart clinically (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022; Berman et al, 2021), so both remain possible in a given tooth. Clinical implication: disinfect the apical canal thoroughly and seal coronally, rather than choosing one. [Likely]

### 1. Introduction
Endodontic microbiota (microflora) are the microorganisms in the root canal system and periradicular tissues. Infection develops in a previously sterile site after pulp necrosis from caries, trauma or leakage, so any species found may be a pathogen or at least part of the community's ecology; studies are cross-sectional and show association only (Berman et al, 2021). Bacteria enter through dentinal tubules, traumatic exposure, coronal leakage, resorption, periodontal tissues and anachoresis (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 2. Classification of endodontic infections
**A. By location and time of entry**

```
Endodontic infections
├── Intraradicular
│   ├── Primary    – first invaders of the necrotic pulp
│   ├── Secondary  – introduced after professional intervention
│   └── Persistent – survived treatment and starvation
└── Extraradicular – dependent on / independent of the intraradicular infection
```
(Berman et al, 2021). Persistent and secondary infections are mostly clinically indistinguishable.

**B. By phylum:** Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Spirochaetes, Fusobacteria, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Synergistetes, TM7 and SR1; sequencing also finds low-abundance Tenericutes, Acidobacteria and others. Fungi and archaea are occasional (Berman et al, 2021).
**C. By Gram stain and oxygen need:** Gram-positive and Gram-negative cocci and rods, each as obligate or facultative anaerobes (Gopikrishna, 2021).
**D. Taxonomy:** black-pigmented Bacteroides are now Prevotella (saccharolytic) and Porphyromonas (asaccharolytic) (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 3. Microbiota by type of infection
| Type | Features | Typical organisms |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Mixed, anaerobe-dominated; 10³–10⁸ cells; mean 10–20 species | Fusobacterium, Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Tannerella, Treponema, Dialister, Parvimonas, Filifactor, Streptococcus |
| Secondary | From intervention or leakage | Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, E. coli, Candida, E. faecalis |
| Persistent | Fewer species (1–5 if well treated); Gram-positive | Streptococci, E. faecalis, Actinomyces, lactobacilli, Olsenella; Candida in up to 18% |
| Extraradicular | Periradicular invasion; abscess | Actinomyces, Propionibacterium propionicum |

- Lesion size rises with richness and load: about 12 taxa in lesions under 5 mm, about 16 in 5–10 mm, about 20 over 10 mm (Berman et al, 2021).
- Early infection is facultative-dominated; as oxygen falls and tissue-fluid proteins become the main nutrient, obligate anaerobes replace them (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- E. faecalis, Candida, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus and enteric rods are not normal in primary infection, so their presence suggests secondary infection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 4. Biofilm
A biofilm is a sessile multicellular community attached to a surface and enmeshed in self-produced EPS: about 15% microcolonies and 85% matrix, separated by water channels (Berman et al, 2021). It is the usual form of canal infection, found in the apical canal of about 80% of cases. Resistance comes from the matrix barrier, slow growth and dormancy, persister cells and quorum sensing. Categories: intracanal, extraradicular, periapical and biomaterial-centred (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 5. Role of E. faecalis
**Features:** a Gram-positive facultative anaerobic coccus (Gopikrishna, 2021).
**Prevalence:** the most frequently detected species in root-filled teeth, up to 90%, and about nine times likelier than in primary infections; about 1% of total bacterial counts (range 0.1–100%) (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
**Behaviour:** commonly recovered from multi-visit or open-drainage teeth, so it acts as a secondary invader that later becomes persistent; its source may be food (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
**Survival factors:**
- Invades dentinal tubules and escapes instruments and irrigants.
- Forms biofilm on canal walls.
- Resists calcium hydroxide, possibly through a proton pump that acidifies the cytoplasm (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- Enters a viable-but-non-cultivable state and survives nutrient scarcity.
- Can colonise as a single species.
- Tolerates extreme alkaline pH, salt, 10–45°C and 60°C for 30 minutes; produces lytic enzymes, gelatinase, aggregation substance and lipoteichoic acid; suppresses lymphocytes (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Treatment implications:**
- Instrumentation alone does not clear it, but a larger apical preparation (no. 30 to no. 40) reduced counts (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Use NaOCl in large volume with activation; 6% NaOCl was most effective against old E. faecalis biofilm in dentine, and 2% CHX eliminated an E. faecalis biofilm in vitro (Berman et al, 2021).
- Calcium hydroxide alone could not kill it inside tubules, but combined with chlorhexidine or iodine potassium iodide it disinfected dentine in vitro (Berman et al, 2021).
- Prevent re-entry: aseptic technique, rubber dam, and a bacteria-tight coronal seal.

**Caution:** prevalence does not prove cause. E. faecalis is missing from some studies of failed cases, is rarely dominant, is as common in treated teeth without lesions, and streptococci often exceed it (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Grossman, in contrast, assigns it a major role in persistent lesions (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 6. Bacteria left at obturation
- Persisting bacteria are mostly in areas inaccessible to treatment; one to five species and 10²–10⁵ cells per sample remain after good treatment. Gram-negative bacteria are usually eliminated, so Gram-positive species predominate.
- Healing can occur despite residual bacteria if they die after filling (toxic filling material, no nutrients, disrupted ecology), are subcritical in number or virulence, or are sealed where the periradicular tissues cannot reach them.
- Residual bacteria influence outcome only if they withstand starvation (including a dormant state), resist disruption of quorum sensing, food webs and biofilm structure, reach a critical load, reach the periradicular tissues through foramina or perforations, and express virulence in the changed environment; host resistance also matters.

### 7. Microbial causes of treatment failure
**A. Persistent intraradicular infection** (organisms of the primary infection that resisted treatment)
- Survive in isthmuses, ramifications, lateral canals, dentinal tubules, recesses and unfilled apical areas; about 10–50% of canal surface may be unprepared.
- Bacteria at filling predict a poor outcome.

**B. Secondary intraradicular infection** (new organisms)
- Entry via breach of asepsis, between visits, or coronal leakage after filling.
- Suggested by E. faecalis, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, enteric rods or Candida, which are not found in primary infections.

**C. Extraradicular infection**
- Extraradicular biofilm and periapical actinomycosis; rare and usually dependent on intraradicular infection (Berman et al, 2021).

### 8. How bacteria survive treatment
1. Biofilm adherence and matrix.
2. Colonising areas away from the main canal.
3. Protection by tissue remnants, dentine, serum, dead cells.
4. Intrinsic resistance (for example E. faecalis to calcium hydroxide).
5. Adaptation to nutrient loss, including viable-but-non-cultivable state.
6. Niches in the apical canal where nutrients remain.

### 9. Microbiota of failed cases
Fewer species (1–5 if well treated, up to 10–20 if poorly treated); mainly Gram-positive facultative bacteria and anaerobes: streptococci, Actinomyces, lactobacilli, Olsenella, E. faecalis (up to 90%, causal role unproven); Candida up to 18% (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

### 10. Persistent versus secondary infection
Current evidence favours persistent infection as commoner: bacteria are found in the apical third, failure is higher in teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis, and bacteria at filling predict outcome; coronal leakage in the mid-1990s was thought important, but well-sealed canals resisted leakage in one study (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 11. Evidence that persistent infection is commoner
1. Biopsies of failed teeth show bacteria in the apical third, rarely along the whole canal; coronal leakage would colonise the entire canal.
2. Bacteria-positive samples at filling predict a poor outcome, and bacteria can survive a root filling for years (monkey study).
3. Failure is more frequent in teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis than in vital teeth; if leakage were the main cause, rates in vital, necrotic and retreatment cases would be similar.
4. Well-prepared and sealed canals resisted coronal bacterial leakage.

### 12. When residual bacteria influence outcome
They must withstand starvation or become dormant, resist disruption of quorum sensing, food webs and biofilm structure, reach a critical load, have access to periradicular tissues through foramina or perforations, and express virulence in the changed environment; host resistance also matters.

### 13. Procedural errors and overfilling
- Ledges and fractured instruments prevent instruments and irrigants reaching the apical canal, so organisms persist there.
- A perforation creates an extra exit, usually larger than natural foramina, through which more bacteria can reach the periodontium.
- The apical extent of filling seems unrelated to failure if infection is controlled; lack of apical seal is the main cause when infection is present.

### 14. Non-microbial causes
Cholesterol crystals and true cysts (endogenous); foreign-body reactions to extruded filling material, to cellulose from paper points or cotton, and to food particles (exogenous). The evidence is a few case reports, and concomitant infection cannot be excluded; a bay cyst, unlike a true cyst, has its cavity contiguous with the apical canal.

### 15. Control of the flora to improve outcome
**A. Asepsis (prevention of secondary infection)**
- Rubber dam; remove caries and defective restorations; clean the field with hydrogen peroxide, then 2% iodine, 2% chlorhexidine or 2.5% NaOCl; irrigate the chamber with NaOCl (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**B. Mechanical**
- Instrumentation with irrigation removes over 90% of cultivable bacteria and reduces counts 100–1,000-fold; larger apical size removes more.
- Crown-down preparation; apical patency; small needles near working length.

**C. Chemical**
- 2.5% NaOCl after each instrument; large volumes, frequently renewed; concentration of secondary importance.
- EDTA or citric acid removes the smear layer, which can harbour bacteria.
- Chlorhexidine as an alternative or final rinse.

**D. Supplementary disinfection**
- Ultrasonic or mechanical activation, or final CHX rinse.

**E. Intracanal medication**
- Calcium hydroxide with CPMC–glycerin or chlorhexidine for about 7 days; remove with the master apical file.

**F. Obturation and seal**
- Reduces room for reorganisation and nutrient supply; a bacteria-tight coronal seal prevents re-entry.

**G. Ecological principle**
- Eliminate as many members of the community as possible and deny substrate to the rest; single-visit preparation with medication limits reorganisation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 16. Detecting the microflora
1. **PCR and derivatives:** repeated melting, primer annealing and extension amplify the target exponentially. Variants: species-specific, nested (two rounds, outer then inner primers), multiplex (several species at once), touchdown (higher specificity), real-time (quantitative), reverse-transcriptase, broad-range with cloning and sequencing, and typing (AP-PCR).
2. **DNA–DNA hybridization:** checkerboard (many samples against many probes), reverse-capture checkerboard (oligonucleotide probes, can detect uncultivated species), DNA microarray (HOMIM, about 300 oral taxa).
3. **FISH:** fluorescent rRNA probes detect intact cells in situ and show architecture and spatial relationships.
4. **Fingerprinting:** DGGE and T-RFLP compare whole communities.
5. **Next-generation sequencing and metagenomics:** pyrosequencing and Illumina platforms give far deeper coverage than Sanger sequencing and reveal low-abundance taxa (Fouad, 2017; Berman et al, 2021).

### 17. Conclusion
The microbiota of the root canal determine the course of disease and the outcome of treatment. Primary infections are mixed anaerobic biofilm communities; after treatment the community is smaller and mainly Gram-positive. Failure is primarily a microbial problem, usually from persistent infection in inaccessible areas, sometimes from secondary or extraradicular infection. Outcome depends on how far the community is reduced and whether residual bacteria are sealed in or kept from the periradicular tissues (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Intraradicular (primary, secondary, persistent) and extraradicular infection.
- Primary: mixed, anaerobic, 10-20 species per canal; persistent: 1-5 species, Gram-positive.
- E. faecalis: most frequent in root-filled teeth, causal role unproven.
- Failure is primarily microbial; persistent infection thought commoner than leakage.
- Residual bacteria matter only if they survive starvation, reach critical load and reach the periradicular tissues.
- Procedural errors fail mainly through infection.
