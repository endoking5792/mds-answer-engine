## STUDY MODE

### A. Endodontic microbiology (summary; detail in Q3)
Microorganisms virtually cause all diseases of the pulp and periapical tissues (Gopikrishna, 2021). Infection is intraradicular (primary, secondary, persistent) or extraradicular. Primary infection is a mixed community dominated by anaerobes (10³–10⁸ cells per canal, mean 10–20 species), persistent infection has fewer, mainly Gram-positive species with E. faecalis prominent in root-filled teeth; the usual form of organisation is a biofilm (Berman et al, 2021). Evidence that bacteria are needed: in germ-free rats, exposed pulps did not become necrotic and periradicular inflammation did not occur, and in humans necrotic pulps in traumatised intact teeth showed periapical bone destruction only if bacteria were present (Berman et al, 2021).

### B. Reaction of the pulp to bacteria (Berman et al, 2021)
- **Caries and the pulp.** Caries is a progressive infection of dentine. Bacterial products and the products of dentine dissolution mediate pulp effects. Three basic protective reactions occur together, and their strength depends on how aggressive the lesion is: reduced dentine permeability (dentine sclerosis), tertiary dentine formation, and inflammatory and immune reactions.
- **Buffering.** Dentine and dentinal fluid buffer bacterial products; this protection falls when remaining dentine thickness is small. Once the pulp is exposed, bacterial metabolites, toxins and cell wall components induce inflammation.
- **Dentine sclerosis.** The first defence: more intratubular dentine and crystal precipitation (whitlockite) in the tubules reduce permeability beneath the lesion; TGF-β1 appears central.
- **Tertiary dentine.** Acid dissolution of dentine frees growth factors held in the matrix (TGF-β1, IGFs, PDGF, BMP-2, VEGF) that stimulate dentine formation. *Reactionary dentine* is made by surviving odontoblasts under mild stimuli; *reparative dentine* forms when odontoblasts are killed and progenitors differentiate, giving organised tubular dentine or irregular, more permeable fibrodentin.
- **Innate response.** Odontoblasts, the most peripheral cells, meet antigens first. Pattern recognition receptors (Toll-like receptors) on odontoblasts detect bacterial components: lipoteichoic acid raised TLR3, 5 and 9 expression and lipopolysaccharide raised TLR2 and 4. They then release cytokines, chemokines and antimicrobial peptides that recruit immune cells and kill bacteria directly. Dendritic cells accumulate near the odontoblast layer and the tubule entrances.
- **Adaptive response.** Lymphocytes, macrophages and plasma cells infiltrate, with capillary sprouting and nerve fibre aggregation. The number of T helper cells, B-lineage cells, neutrophils and macrophages rises with lesion depth, and inflammation can appear beneath noncavitated lesions.
- **Outcome.** If the pulp is exposed and the infection advances, inflammation spreads and the pulp becomes necrotic; the infection front then moves apically, and the necrotic canal becomes colonised (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### C. Reaction of the periradicular tissues (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
- **Apical inflammation versus infection.** Apical inflammation is the periapical reaction to irritants emerging from the canal (vasodilation, increased permeability, exudation); apical infection is the physical presence of microbes in the periapical tissue. Most apical periodontitis is inflammation without frank infection of the lesion; bacteria are rarely found in the tissue except in abscesses and a few special cases (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Timing.** Apical periodontitis can start before total necrosis: periapical inflammation and bone loss appear while vital inflamed pulp remains in the apical canal, through diffusion of mediators, cytokines and toxins (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Triggers.** Bacterial toxins (LPS, LTA) and metabolic by-products activate innate immunity through PRRs. TLR4 detects LPS; TLR2 detects LTA, lipoproteins and peptidoglycan. LPS also stimulates sensory nerves to release calcitonin gene-related peptide and substance P, causing vasodilation and permeability, and activates TLRs on dendritic cells to drive T-cell differentiation (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Direct and indirect damage.** Secreted products (enzymes, exotoxins, metabolic end-products) damage tissue directly. Structural components (LPS, peptidoglycan, LTA, fimbriae, outer membrane proteins and vesicles, DNA, exopolysaccharides) act as modulins, stimulating host reactions that defend but also destroy. Indirect damage is considered more significant (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Bone resorption.** Inflammatory and noninflammatory cells release cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, IL-11, IL-17, TNF and others) that enhance proliferation and differentiation of osteoclast precursors and activate mature osteoclasts; the bone loss is mainly by activated osteoclasts (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Pus formation.** In the acute apical abscess, hyperactive or dysregulated neutrophils release oxygen-derived free radicals and lysosomal enzymes that liquefy tissue; the host defence is the main factor in pus formation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Innate cells.** Neutrophils and macrophages phagocytose; neutrophils generate a respiratory burst and neutrophil extracellular traps (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Adaptive immunity.** When infection exceeds innate capacity, T and B lymphocytes give specific responses with memory. Chronic apical periodontitis, a form of adaptive response, is characterised by persisting stimuli, host adaptation, adaptive immunity and initiation of repair. It is a "granuloma"; the host defence holds the infection but is inadequate to eliminate it. Foreign materials can trigger a foreign-body granuloma (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Cyst.** A radicular cyst forms by inflammatory proliferation of epithelial rests in a chronic lesion; it is lined by non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium and may be a pocket cyst (attached to the apical foramen) or a true cyst (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Chronic versus acute.** Acute lesions are linked to planktonic, virulent, high-count communities; chronic lesions to biofilms on canal walls that persist (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim)
**Claim — apical periodontitis begins only after total pulp necrosis.** The textbook states that this is the common belief, based on the pulpal strangulation theory and on studies in which uncontaminated necrotic pulps in traumatised or devitalised teeth caused no periapical inflammation (Berman et al, 2021). Against it, animal studies showed periapical infiltrates, more osteoclasts and bone destruction well before total necrosis, with vital inflamed pulp still in the apical canal, and about one third of teeth with apical periodontitis had a vital but inflamed apical pulp (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). The evidence is histologic and experimental, so it shows that necrosis is not required, not that it never matters; the key variable is infection, not necrosis. Clinical implication: infected, vital apical pulp can already harm the periapical tissue. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Estimated writing time: ~39 minutes at 22 words/min; ~851 words)**

### 1. Introduction
Microorganisms virtually cause all diseases of the pulp and periapical tissues. Kakehashi's germ-free rats showed that exposed pulps do not become necrotic and do not develop periradicular inflammation without bacteria; in humans, necrotic pulps in traumatised intact teeth showed periapical bone destruction only when bacteria were present (Berman et al, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 2. Endodontic microbiology
- **Routes of entry:** caries through dentinal tubules, traumatic exposure, coronal leakage, resorption, periodontal tissues (tubules, lateral and accessory canals, foramina) and anachoresis (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Classification:** intraradicular infection (primary, secondary, persistent) and extraradicular infection (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Primary infection:** a mixed community dominated by anaerobes; 10³–10⁸ cells per canal, mean 10–20 species; Fusobacterium, Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Tannerella, Treponema, Dialister, Parvimonas, Filifactor and streptococci. Lesion size is proportional to richness and load.
- **Secondary and persistent infection:** fewer species, mainly Gram-positive; E. faecalis is the most frequent species in root-filled teeth.
- **Succession:** facultative bacteria first, then obligate anaerobes as oxygen falls and tissue-fluid proteins become the nutrient (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Organisation:** biofilms on canal walls, with invasion of dentinal tubules and ramifications.

### 3. Reaction of the pulp to bacteria (Berman et al, 2021)
Caries is a progressive infection of dentine. Bacterial products and the products of dentine dissolution mediate the pulp's response. Three protective reactions occur together, and their strength depends on how aggressive the lesion is.
**A. Dentine sclerosis (first defence).** Increased intratubular dentine and precipitated mineral crystals (whitlockite) narrow the tubules and reduce permeability beneath the lesion; TGF-β1 is central to the increased deposition.
**B. Tertiary dentine.**
- Acid dissolution of dentine frees growth factors held in the matrix (TGF-β1, IGF-I and II, PDGF, BMP-2, VEGF) that stimulate dentine formation.
- *Reactionary dentine:* surviving odontoblasts are stimulated by mild stimuli, for example under a noncavitated lesion; similar to physiologic dentine.
- *Reparative dentine:* when odontoblasts are killed, progenitors differentiate; the result ranges from organised tubular dentine to irregular fibrodentin, which is more permeable.
**C. Inflammatory and immune response.**
- Odontoblasts, the most peripheral cells, meet antigens first. Their Toll-like receptors detect bacterial components (lipoteichoic acid raised TLR3, 5 and 9; lipopolysaccharide raised TLR2 and 4).
- They then release proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines (for example IL-8) and antimicrobial peptides that recruit immune cells and kill bacteria.
- Dendritic cells gather in the subodontoblastic zone, the odontoblast layer and the tubule entrances; lymphocytes, macrophages and plasma cells follow, with capillary sprouting and nerve fibre aggregation.
- The numbers of T helper cells, B-lineage cells, neutrophils and macrophages rise with lesion depth; inflammation exists beneath noncavitated lesions.
**D. Buffering.** Dentine and dentinal fluid buffer bacterial products, but this protection falls when remaining dentine is thin.
**E. Outcome.** If the pulp is exposed and the infection advances, the pulp becomes necrotic and the infection front moves apically (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 4. Reaction of the periradicular tissues
**A. Triggers (Berman et al, 2021).** Bacterial toxins (LPS, LTA) and metabolic by-products leaving the canal activate innate immunity through pattern recognition receptors. TLR4 detects LPS; TLR2 detects LTA, lipoproteins and peptidoglycan. LPS also stimulates sensory nerves to release calcitonin gene-related peptide and substance P, causing vasodilation and increased permeability.
**B. Inflammation versus infection.** Apical inflammation is the reaction to irritants emerging from the canal (vasodilation, increased permeability, exudation); apical infection is the presence of microbes in the periapical tissue, which is uncommon except in abscesses, sinus tracts, actinomycosis and infected cysts (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
**C. Timing.** Apical periodontitis can begin before total necrosis: periapical inflammation, increased osteoclasts and bone loss are seen while vital inflamed pulp remains in the apical canal, through diffusion of mediators and toxins (Berman et al, 2021).
**D. Mechanisms of damage (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).**
- *Direct:* secreted enzymes, exotoxins and metabolic end-products.
- *Indirect (more significant):* structural components (LPS, peptidoglycan, LTA, fimbriae, outer membrane proteins and vesicles, DNA, exopolysaccharides) act as modulins and stimulate host reactions that defend but also destroy.
- *Bone resorption:* cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, IL-11, IL-17, TNF) from inflammatory and noninflammatory cells enhance osteoclast precursor proliferation and differentiation and activate mature osteoclasts.
- *Pus:* hyperactive neutrophils release oxygen free radicals and lysosomal enzymes that liquefy tissue.
**E. Cellular response (Berman et al, 2021).** Neutrophils and macrophages phagocytose; neutrophils have a respiratory burst and form extracellular traps. When infection exceeds innate capacity, T and B lymphocytes give specific responses with memory.

### 5. Outcomes (Berman et al, 2021)
- **Acute apical abscess:** pus formation by a virulent, often planktonic community.
- **Chronic apical periodontitis (granuloma):** persisting stimuli, host adaptation, adaptive immunity and attempted repair; the host contains but cannot eliminate the infection.
- **Radicular cyst:** inflammatory proliferation of epithelial rests, lined by non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium; pocket cyst (contiguous with the canal) or true cyst.
- Foreign materials can provoke a foreign-body granuloma.

### 6. Conclusion
Bacteria and their products drive pulp and periradicular disease. The pulp first seals and repairs (sclerosis, tertiary dentine) and mounts innate and adaptive immunity; once the canal is necrotic, products leaving it produce the inflammatory and bone-resorbing responses of apical periodontitis, in which the host response causes much of the damage, so treatment must remove the source of the irritants (the canal infection) to allow repair of the periradicular tissues.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Three pulp defences: dentine sclerosis, tertiary dentine, inflammatory-immune response.
- Reactionary vs reparative dentine; TGF-β1 central.
- Odontoblasts act as sentinels through Toll-like receptors; TLR4 detects LPS, TLR2 detects LTA.
- Apical periodontitis can start before total pulp necrosis.
- Bone loss by osteoclasts activated by IL-1, IL-6, IL-11, IL-17, TNF.
- Indirect damage (host response) exceeds direct bacterial damage.
