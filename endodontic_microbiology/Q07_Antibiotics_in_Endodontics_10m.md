## STUDY MODE
**Principle.** Antibiotics are not curative drugs; they help the immune system, and are justified only when signs show the host needs help (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). A necrotic pulp has no blood supply, so systemic antibiotics cannot reach bacteria in the canal and cannot replace removal of the source of infection by debridement, drainage or extraction (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). In teeth with necrotic pulp and apical periodontitis there is no evidence that antibiotics speed healing or reduce postoperative pain (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**When NOT indicated.** Reversible and irreversible pulpitis, pulp necrosis, asymptomatic or symptomatic apical periodontitis, chronic apical abscess, and acute apical abscess in an immunocompetent patient without systemic involvement; also draining sinus tracts, after endodontic surgery, to prevent flare-ups, and after drainage of a localised swelling (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022; Berman et al, 2021).

**When indicated (adjunct to local treatment).**
- Progressive infection (rapid onset in under 24 h, cellulitis, osteomyelitis, spreading infection).
- Systemic involvement: fever above 38°C, malaise, lymphadenopathy, trismus.
- Immunocompromised patients (leukaemia, HIV/AIDS, end-stage renal disease, uncontrolled diabetes, chemotherapy, steroids, transplant drugs).
- Persistent infection after root canal treatment not resolved by regular procedures.
- Acute abscess when treatment or drainage is not immediately available: a delayed prescription (patient takes it only if symptoms and swelling worsen) (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
Drainage before starting antibiotics reduces the bacterial load and the inoculum effect, which makes antibiotics less effective, particularly in slow-growing biofilms (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**Choice and dose.** Most endodontic bacteria, including those of abscesses, are susceptible to penicillins, so amoxicillin is first choice (broader spectrum, better absorption, longer half-life, less resistance than penicillin VK). Oral dose: 1,000 mg loading dose, then 500 mg every 8 hours (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Amoxicillin–clavulanate is reserved for immunosuppressed patients or non-response, because it more easily induces resistance and carries a higher risk of Clostridium difficile infection. Metronidazole may be added if there is no improvement at 48 h. For penicillin allergy: clindamycin (risk of C. difficile; stop and report diarrhoea), clarithromycin or azithromycin; cephalosporins are not good alternatives because about 20% of penicillin-allergic patients are also allergic to them (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Cohen agrees on amoxicillin first, clavulanate or metronidazole in severe cases and clindamycin in allergy or refractory cases (Berman et al, 2021).
Duration: stop 1–2 days after clear clinical resolution (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**Resistance.** Reported: F. nucleatum resistant to penicillin, amoxicillin and metronidazole; beta-lactamase production in 36% of black-pigmented and 32% of non-pigmented Prevotella from oral abscesses (Berman et al, 2021). Indiscriminate use selects resistant strains and risks superinfection.

**Prophylaxis.** No solid scientific basis; transient bacteraemia from endodontic treatment in healthy people clears within minutes to an hour. The ESE position recommends prophylaxis only for immunocompromised patients and in locus minoris resistentiae cases (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**Topical antibiotics.** A ciprofloxacin–metronidazole–minocycline dressing disinfects immature necrotic teeth in regenerative procedures; doxycycline or minocycline topically may aid avulsed teeth; systemic antibiotics are not recommended for most dental injuries (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Evidence against multispecies biofilms is inconsistent and the paste is toxic to stem cells (Berman et al, 2021). Dahlén states local canal antibiotics are not recommended because no single antibiotic covers all species in the canal (Fouad, 2017). [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Estimated writing time: ~15 minutes at 22 words/min; ~323 words)**

### Principles
- Antibiotics are adjuncts, not cures: they assist the immune system (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Systemic antibiotics cannot reach the avascular necrotic canal, so local treatment (debridement, drainage, extraction) comes first (Berman et al, 2021).

### Not indicated
Pulpitis (reversible and irreversible), pulp necrosis, asymptomatic and symptomatic apical periodontitis, chronic apical abscess, localised acute abscess in an immunocompetent patient, draining sinus tracts, after surgery, and to prevent flare-ups (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022; Berman et al, 2021).

### Indicated (with local treatment)
1. Systemic involvement: fever above 38°C, malaise, lymphadenopathy, trismus.
2. Progressive or spreading infection: cellulitis, osteomyelitis, Ludwig's angina, orbital cellulitis.
3. Immunocompromised patients.
4. Persistent infection after root canal treatment.
5. Acute abscess when treatment is not immediately available: delayed prescription.

### Drugs and dose (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
- **First choice: amoxicillin**; 1,000 mg loading dose then 500 mg 8-hourly.
- **Amoxicillin with clavulanic acid:** immunosuppressed or non-responders.
- **Add metronidazole** if no improvement at 48 h.
- **Penicillin allergy:** clindamycin, clarithromycin or azithromycin.
- Stop 1–2 days after resolution.

### Prophylaxis
No firm evidence; ESE recommends it only for immunocompromised patients and locus minoris resistentiae.

### Topical use
Triple antibiotic paste (ciprofloxacin, metronidazole, minocycline) in regenerative endodontics; doxycycline or minocycline in avulsion (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### Drainage first and the inoculum effect
Antibiotic efficacy falls when bacterial density is high, especially in slow-growing biofilms, so drainage should be established before or with antibiotic therapy and treatment should not be delayed (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### Dosing and monitoring
- Loading dose first; amoxicillin three times daily; clarithromycin every 12 hours; azithromycin every 24 hours.
- Review daily; if there is no improvement at 48 hours, reassess the source of infection and consider adding metronidazole.

### Trauma
Systemic antibiotics are not recommended for concussion, subluxation, luxation, avulsion or root fractures (IADT, cited in Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### Problems
Resistance (beta-lactamase-producing Prevotella; resistant F. nucleatum), allergy, C. difficile with clindamycin and clavulanate, and the inoculum effect in biofilms (Berman et al, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Antibiotics are adjuncts; drainage and debridement are primary.
- Indications: systemic involvement, progressive infection, immunocompromised, persistent infection, delayed prescription.
- Amoxicillin 1,000 mg then 500 mg 8-hourly; clindamycin in allergy.
- No benefit in pulpitis or necrosis without systemic signs; no routine prophylaxis.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
Decision flowchart: localised swelling → drain/treat, no antibiotic; systemic signs or immunocompromised or spreading → treat plus amoxicillin (alternatives) (2 min).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Prescribing antibiotics for irreversible pulpitis or localised abscess.
2. Giving clavulanate as first line in healthy patients.
3. Choosing cephalosporins for penicillin-allergic patients.
4. Forgetting the delayed prescription option.
5. Stating antibiotics penetrate the necrotic canal.
