## STUDY MODE

### 1. What "homeostasis of dental hard tissues" means
Mount et al describe a *sialo-microbial-dental complex*: saliva, the oral microbial mix, diet, biofilm and tooth structure evolved together, are closely interrelated physically, functionally and chemically, and until recently were in relative balance. All contribute to health and, under some circumstances, to disease (Mount et al, 2016). Hard tissue homeostasis is therefore the maintenance of tooth mineral by a balance in which remineralisation matches demineralisation at the biofilm–tooth interface. The microbiota are one component of that system, and the key point is that the *structure and metabolism of the biofilm*, not the mere presence of bacteria, decide which way the balance tips. [Certain]

### 2. Microbiota in health
- **Formation.** Tooth surface acquires a salivary pellicle within minutes; streptococci recognise its receptors and make up 60–90% of the initial flora on enamel; early colonisers (Streptococcus, Actinomyces, Veillonella, Neisseria) are oxygen-consuming and make the environment suitable for obligate anaerobes; Fusobacterium nucleatum bridges early and late colonisers by coaggregation; heterogeneity of pH, oxygen and redox potential lets species with different needs coexist (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Fouad, 2017). The flora of health is thin, with a layer of adherent Gram-positive cocci (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- **Biofilm as a semi-permeable membrane.** Biofilm lies between saliva and the tooth and acts as a semi-permeable membrane; interactions between saliva, biofilm and teeth evolved to ensure good oral health (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Closed-system demineralisation–remineralisation cycle.** Acids from food or from bacterial fermentation lower biofilm pH only if the local buffering capacity is exceeded. If pH falls low enough near the enamel crystals, surface dissolution occurs; when acids are buffered and pH rises, it reverses. Falling pH also makes statherin and proline-rich proteins release stabilised calcium within the salivary biofilm. Dissolved mineral is kept within the local environment and reused, a "closed system" that can occur only with an appropriately structured biofilm, and that both protects the tooth and promotes enamel maturation (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Enamel maturation.** Newly erupted apatite is carbonate-rich and soluble. Saliva and biofilm have lower carbonate and higher phosphate than the crystal, so repeated local cycles progressively replace carbonate with phosphate, and fluoride from water, toothpaste or other sources substitutes for hydroxyl, giving a less soluble, more mature surface (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Saliva as partner.** Water for clearance and lubrication, electrolytes for buffering (phosphate system near pH 7, bicarbonate below about pH 6 in stimulated saliva) (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3. Microbiota in imbalance: caries
- **Modern disease.** Caries, erosion and some wear are modern diseases linked to diet and lifestyle change: sucrose and other simple carbohydrates in high concentrations, acidic drinks, softer food and reduced salivary flow; these change biofilm biochemistry and tip the balance toward demineralisation (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Extended ecological plaque hypothesis.** A normal, non-pathogenic biofilm that is frequently exposed to fermentable sugars is acidified repeatedly by facultative non-mutans bacteria. This mild but frequent acidification selects for acid-tolerant (aciduric) and acid-producing (acidogenic) strains, then mutans streptococci, lactobacilli and bifidobacteria, and selects against alkali-producing species, so biodiversity falls. The resting pH drops, little carbohydrate is then needed to reach the critical pH, and recovery between meals is slower, so the cycle tips toward net mineral loss. In very high-risk people the resting pH can sit below critical, so the tooth dissolves continuously (Mount et al, 2016). Banerjee and Watson express the same idea: caries is an imbalanced enrichment of plaque driven by nutrients that favour acidogenic and acid-tolerant bacteria (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Role of sucrose and mutans streptococci.** Mutans streptococci are highly acidogenic and aciduric and are the only species that make extracellular polyglucan from sucrose; the glucan gives a selective advantage and probably changes biofilm thickness and ion diffusion. Their proportion can be kept low by restricting sucrose and eating frequency, and biofilm in such mouths is very unlikely to contribute to caries (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Sites.** Pits and fissures, areas below contacts and the gingival margin have thicker biofilm and are the commonest initiation sites; the first lesion is subsurface because hydrogen ions penetrate deeper than buffers and mineral ions, leaving a more intact surface layer over a porous "white spot" (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Caries microbiota.** Caries is a complex polymicrobial biofilm disease. Older culture studies pointed to S. mutans, lactobacilli and Veillonella; but caries has been seen without these species and absent where they are present, and molecular studies in early childhood caries found 134 to over 200 taxa, mainly Firmicutes (Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Granulicatella) and Actinobacteria (Actinomyces, Bifidobacterium, Scardovia and others). Most individuals carry the caries-associated microbiota, and a shift to a cariogenic biofilm can occur in anyone, for example with a high sugar diet (Fouad, 2017).
- **Proof of bacterial necessity.** In germ-free animals fed a cariogenic diet caries did not develop (Keyes, as cited in Fouad, 2017).

### 4. Microbiota, dentine and pulp
When dentine is exposed by caries, restorative or periodontal procedures, wear, cracks or trauma, bacteria from plaque can invade dentinal tubules and later the pulp (Fouad, 2017). In germ-free rats, exposed pulps remained healthy and began repair by dentine bridging, which shows that the pulp–dentine complex can repair when bacteria are absent (Fouad, 2017). The dentinal-tubule invasion rate and its endodontic significance are covered in the endodontic microbiology answers.

### 5. Maintaining the balance
Restrict sucrose and eating frequency; avoid transmission of S. mutans from carers to infants; fluoride to drive the maturation and remineralisation cycle; mechanical removal of biofilm; antimicrobials only as adjuncts: CHX has little medium- to long-term caries evidence and can alter taste, stain teeth and irritate mucosa, while xylitol gum (about 6.5 g/day in at least three periods) reduced S. mutans load (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sucrose restriction, fluoride and avoiding S. mutans transmission to infants are from Mount et al, 2016. The accepted strategy is to keep the flora from shifting to a disease-favouring ecology, not to eliminate it (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). [Likely]

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, two claims)

**Claim 1 — mutans streptococci are the specific cause of caries.** Mount et al call mutans streptococci of major significance because of their acidogenicity, aciduric nature and sucrose-derived glucan. Endodontic Microbiology reports caries in the absence of these species and no caries in their presence, and a far more diverse set of taxa on molecular analysis (Mount et al, 2016; Fouad, 2017). The sources therefore support an ecological reading: a community under selective pressure from sugar and acid, in which mutans streptococci are an important but not sole contributor. The evidence is cross-sectional microbiology, so association, not proof of cause, except for the germ-free animal work, which shows that bacteria are necessary but not which ones. [Likely]

**Claim 2 — controlling the microbiota by antimicrobials restores balance.** Banerjee and Watson state there is little clinical evidence for chlorhexidine in medium- to long-term caries control, while xylitol reduced S. mutans in short trials; the ecological hypothesis implies that removing the selective pressure (sugar frequency) matters more than killing bacteria (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount et al, 2016). Reduction in S. mutans counts does not by itself show reduced caries. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Estimated writing time: ~75 minutes at 22 words/min; ~1655 words)**

### 1. Introduction
Dental hard tissues (enamel, dentine, cementum) are maintained by a balance between loss and gain of mineral at the interface between tooth, biofilm and saliva. Tooth structure, diet, saliva, the oral microbiota and biofilm have evolved together as one integrated system, the sialo-microbial-dental complex, and all contribute to health and, in some circumstances, to disease (Mount et al, 2016). The microbiota's role in homeostasis depends on the composition and metabolism of the biofilm.

### 2. Components of the complex (Mount et al, 2016)
- **Tooth structure:** carbonated apatite whose crystals are in equilibrium with a local aqueous layer.
- **Saliva:** water for clearance, electrolytes and buffers (phosphate near pH 7; bicarbonate in stimulated saliva below about pH 6), calcium and phosphate, statherin and proline-rich proteins.
- **Diet:** type, amount and frequency of fermentable carbohydrate.
- **Biofilm:** the interface, a semi-permeable membrane between saliva and tooth.

### 2a. Saliva: functions that protect the hard tissues (Mount et al, 2016)
- **Ion reservoir:** calcium, phosphate and hydroxyl ions for remineralisation.
- **Buffer:** the phosphate system works best near pH 7 in resting saliva; at pH below about 6, the bicarbonate system of stimulated parotid saliva takes over and is very effective because carbon dioxide is continuously lost.
- **Antimicrobial action:** enzymes, immunological mediators and other proteins give protection against external pathogens and also influence microbial growth within the biofilm.
- **Clearance and lubrication:** water and mucins; salivary flow is raised by chewing and taste and lowered by sympathetic stimulation and many mood-altering drugs, which is a major contributor to tooth structure loss in patients.

### 2b. The tooth side of the interface (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016)
- **Enamel:** about 96% mineral by weight, with about 3% carbonate in the apatite; each crystal lies in its own hydration shell in ionic balance with the adjacent aqueous phase, and it is in this layer that post-eruption maturation takes place.
- **Dentine:** about 50 vol% carbonate-rich, calcium-deficient apatite, 30 vol% organic matter (mainly type I collagen) and 20 vol% fluid. Its apatite has about 5% carbonate and smaller crystals with a larger surface area, so it is more soluble in acid than enamel.
- **Tubules:** tubule area and diameter fall from about 22% and 2.5 µm near the pulp to about 1% and 0.8 µm at the dentino-enamel junction, which matters for bacterial entry once dentine is exposed.
- Caries can begin on enamel, exposed dentine or root cementum and becomes serious when bacteria reach, infect and kill the pulp and spread through the canal to the facial bones (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3. Formation of the biofilm (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Fouad, 2017)
1. Pellicle forms within minutes from salivary proteins and glycoproteins.
2. Streptococci attach through pellicle receptors; they make up 60–90% of the first flora.
3. Actinomyces, Veillonella and Neisseria follow; early colonisers use oxygen and prepare the environment for anaerobes.
4. Coaggregation: Fusobacterium nucleatum bridges early and late colonisers.
5. Mature multispecies biofilm with gradients of pH, oxygen and redox potential, allowing species with different needs to coexist.

### 4. The microbiota in health
- A thin biofilm of mainly Gram-positive cocci acts as a semi-permeable membrane; balanced interactions between saliva, biofilm and tooth ensure oral health (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Closed-system demineralisation–remineralisation cycle:** acids lower pH only when buffering is exceeded; if pH drops enough, surface crystals dissolve; when pH recovers, mineral redeposits. Falling pH releases calcium stabilised by statherin and proline-rich proteins. The mineral stays in the local environment and is reused. The cycle needs an appropriately structured biofilm (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Enamel maturation:** repeated cycles replace carbonate with phosphate and, with fluoride, hydroxyl with fluoride, producing less soluble enamel.
- Loss of alkali-producing species and of biodiversity accompanies progressive acidification in the diseased state (point 5).

### 4a. What favours remineralisation (Mount et al, 2016)
Increased calcium and phosphate, raised pH and the presence of fluoride favour remineralisation; hydrogen ions are used up or neutralised and dissolved ions are retained, so that apatite, or fluorapatite when fluoride is present, re-forms. Biofilm itself is made of salivary proteins and glycoproteins, bacteria and their products, water and ions.

### 5. The microbiota in imbalance: dysbiosis and caries
**Extended ecological plaque hypothesis (Mount et al, 2016):**
1. Frequent sugar exposure causes repeated acid production by facultative non-mutans bacteria.
2. Repeated acidification selects acid-tolerant and acid-producing strains, then mutans streptococci, lactobacilli and bifidobacteria.
3. Alkali-producing species are selected against, so biodiversity falls.
4. Resting biofilm pH falls; little sugar is needed to reach the critical pH; recovery between meals slows.
5. Net demineralisation: a subsurface white-spot lesion, then cavitation.

**Contributing factors:** frequent sucrose and acidic drinks, reduced salivary flow, soft diet, thick biofilm at pits and fissures, contact areas and the gingival margin, and early transmission of S. mutans from carers.
**Mutans streptococci:** highly acidogenic and aciduric, and the only species that make extracellular polyglucan from sucrose, which gives selective advantage and alters the diffusion properties of the biofilm.

### 6. Caries microbiota (Fouad, 2017)
- Polymicrobial; S. mutans, lactobacilli and Veillonella were identified first, but molecular studies found over 100 to over 200 taxa in early childhood caries, mainly Firmicutes (Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Granulicatella) and Actinobacteria (Actinomyces, Bifidobacterium, Scardovia).
- Caries seen without S. mutans and lactobacilli, and absent in their presence, so no single species is essential.
- Most people carry caries-associated microbiota; a shift to cariogenic biofilm is possible in anyone.
- Germ-free animals did not develop caries on a cariogenic diet: bacteria are necessary.

### 6a. Caries microbiota by site (Fouad, 2017)
- **Early childhood caries:** 134 taxa in 72 children and over 200 taxa in 80 children; Firmicutes and Actinobacteria predominate.
- **Dentinal caries:** S. mutans, Lactobacillus, Actinomyces and Veillonella dominate in culture, with wide diversity on molecular study; species in the middle and deep layers are similar. In deep lesions streptococci were found in 82% but S. mutans in only 44%.
- **Root caries:** progresses rapidly with gingival recession, reduced salivary flow or frequent intake of cariogenic food; Actinomyces and Lactobacillus (acidogenic Gram-positive rods) with S. mutans; Bifidobacteriaceae relate to severity (8% of anaerobes in soft active lesions versus 4% S. mutans and 31% lactobacilli); no single microbiota is consistently seen.
- **Clinical consequence:** screening only for S. mutans is not enough; PCR assays and microarrays for caries-associated taxa could help define endpoints of therapy.

### 7. Microbiota, dentine and pulp (Fouad, 2017)
- When caries, trauma, restorative or periodontal procedures, wear or cracks breach enamel or cementum, dentinal tubules become open to bacteria from plaque; this follows the principles of colonisation (adhesion to the conditioning film, then growth).
- Only a subset of species enters tubules. Streptococci bind to collagen type I in the tubules and to root dentine.
- In exposed non-carious dentine, bacteria penetrate readily within a week; the number of tubules infected and the depth increase with time, and pulpal inflammation is seen within a week. The invading flora is mainly Gram-positive and resembles that of deep dentinal caries.
- Germ-free rats: exposed pulps stayed healthy and began repair by dentine bridging, so the pulp–dentine complex can repair when bacteria are absent.
- Once infection reaches the canal, the problem becomes endodontic (see the endodontic microbiology answers).

### 8. Other hard-tissue conditions
Erosion and some forms of wear are separate diseases, but caries and erosion share changes in biofilm structure and biochemistry that tip the balance toward mineral loss (Mount et al, 2016).

### 9. Interaction with restorations
Biofilm on restorative materials varies with roughness and surface energy; secondary caries at margins has an aetiology like primary caries (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 10. Maintaining homeostasis (clinical)
- Control sucrose and eating frequency; reduce acidic drinks.
- Fluoride to support maturation and remineralisation.
- Mechanical plaque control; avoid S. mutans transmission to infants.
- Antimicrobials as adjuncts only: chlorhexidine has little medium- to long-term caries evidence and can stain and alter taste; xylitol gum about 6.5 g/day in at least three periods lowers S. mutans load (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- The strategy is to prevent a shift to a disease-favouring ecology rather than eliminate the flora (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Active care for high-risk patients adds professional mechanical tooth cleaning and, in rampant caries, early stabilising adhesive restorations (high-viscosity glass ionomers) that remove the carious biomass acting as a bacterial reservoir (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Success depends on patient cooperation: patients need to understand the link between saliva, sugar and caries (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 10a. Why the biofilm is not simply harmful
The closed-system cycle works only in the presence of an appropriately structured biofilm. It both protects the tooth from demineralisation and promotes enamel maturation, so a clean tooth surface is not the aim; the aim is a biofilm that stays in balance with saliva and diet (Mount et al, 2016).

### 10b. Healthy versus dysbiotic biofilm
| Feature | Health | Dysbiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar exposure | Low frequency | Frequent |
| Acid events | Buffered, short | Frequent, prolonged |
| Dominant species | Early colonisers, Gram-positive cocci; diverse | Aciduric, acidogenic species: mutans streptococci, lactobacilli, bifidobacteria |
| Alkali producers | Present | Selected against |
| Resting pH | Near neutral | Lower; may be below critical |
| Mineral balance | Remineralisation matches demineralisation | Net demineralisation |
| Salivary role | Buffers and calcium supply adequate | Reduced flow worsens the balance |

### 10c. Stages of the enamel lesion
Hydrogen ions diffuse deeper and faster than buffers and mineral ions, so the surface is initially intact while a subsurface zone loses mineral and becomes porous and water-filled, appearing as an opaque white spot; the white spot can be reversed by remineralisation if the balance is restored (Mount et al, 2016).

### 10d. Factors that tip the balance
Frequent sucrose and fermentable carbohydrates; acidic and carbonated drinks; reduced salivary flow; softer, less abrasive diet; thick biofilm in pits, fissures and contact areas; early S. mutans acquisition from carers (Mount et al, 2016).

### 11. Conclusion
The microbiota maintain hard tissue health when they form a structured, diverse, buffered biofilm that takes part in the closed demineralisation–remineralisation cycle and enamel maturation. Frequent sugar and low salivary flow select acidogenic, aciduric species, lower the biofilm pH and shift the balance to demineralisation. Management aims to restore the ecological balance rather than eradicate bacteria.
