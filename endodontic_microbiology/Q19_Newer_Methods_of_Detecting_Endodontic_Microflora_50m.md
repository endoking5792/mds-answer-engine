### 1. Introduction
Identifying the microorganisms in infected root canals is needed to understand the aetiology of apical periodontitis, to define the effect of treatment and to find candidate pathogens. Culture was the traditional method; its limits led to molecular methods, and more recently to high-throughput sequencing (Berman et al, 2021). This answer covers culture as the baseline and then the newer, culture-independent methods.

### 2. Culture: the baseline and its limits
1. Sampling and transport in an anaerobic, viability-preserving, non-multiplying medium.
2. Dispersion by sonication or vortex mixing, then dilution.
3. Cultivation on media under aerobic and anaerobic conditions.
4. Isolation of colonies, then identification by colony and cell morphology, Gram stain, oxygen tolerance and biochemical tests (Berman et al, 2021).

**Limitations.** Many species cannot be cultured (40–80% of species at human sites are uncultivated).; Low sensitivity: culture on non-selective media needs about 10⁴–10⁵ cells, against about 10 for PCR.; Contamination and transport dependent; anaerobes need expertise and days to weeks; identification by phenotype can be wrong.; Of more than 400 endodontic species, about 32% were found only by culture, 45% only by molecular methods and 23% by both (Berman et al, 2021).

### 3. Why newer methods were needed
Molecular biology methods identify microorganisms from their nucleic acid markers without cultivation. They were needed because many endodontic bacteria cannot be cultured. They confirmed culture findings and added new candidate pathogens; of more than 400 species reported, about 45% came only from molecular studies (Berman et al, 2021).

The 16S rRNA gene (18S for fungi). Conserved regions take broad-range (universal) primers; variable regions carry genus and species signatures for species-specific primers and probes. A 98.5–99% identity to a database sequence is the usual species criterion (Fouad, 2017).

### 4. PCR: principle and sensitivity
PCR amplifies a target stretch of DNA exponentially through repeated cycles of melting, primer annealing and extension by a thermostable polymerase, so that a few copies become millions in minutes to hours. It can detect as few as 10 bacterial cells, whereas culture on non-selective media needs about 10⁴–10⁵ cells (about 10³ with selective media), and immunological methods and DNA–DNA hybridization need 10³–10⁴, so PCR is at least 10–100 times more sensitive. The product is usually detected by agarose gel electrophoresis, and its identity is best confirmed by sequencing (Fouad, 2017).

### 5. Species-specific, nested, multiplex and touchdown PCR
- **Species-specific PCR:** primers designed on variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene detect a target species directly in the clinical sample, even among non-target species and without cultivation.
- **Nested PCR:** an outer primer pair, then an inner pair on the first product; sensitivity rises because of the larger total number of cycles and the reduction of non-target DNA and inhibitors, and specificity rises because of the internal primers.
- **Multiplex PCR:** several primer pairs in one reaction detect several species together, saving time and cost; primers must have compatible annealing temperatures.
- **Touchdown PCR:** the annealing temperature starts above the primer melting temperature and falls in steps of 0.5–2°C per cycle, avoiding amplification of spurious fragments (Fouad, 2017).

### 6. Real-time (quantitative) PCR
The amplification product is detected and monitored throughout the reaction. SYBR-Green binds double-stranded DNA; the TaqMan assay adds a 20- to 30-base labelled probe with a reporter dye and a quencher; when the polymerase cleaves the probe the reporter is released and fluorescence rises, giving greater specificity. Real-time PCR can quantify all bacteria or particular groups before and after treatment, so it can measure the antibacterial effectiveness of procedures (Fouad, 2017; Berman et al, 2021).

### 7. PCR-based typing
Arbitrarily primed PCR (random amplified polymorphic DNA) uses one random primer of 10–20 bases at low stringency and gives a strain-specific pattern of about 5–15 bands without prior sequence knowledge. Clonal analysis can show whether a root canal strain came from the gingival sulcus or another site (Fouad, 2017).

### 8. Broad-range PCR, cloning and sequencing
Universal primers complementary to conserved regions of the 16S rRNA gene amplify the gene from nearly all bacteria in a sample, so unexpected and uncultivated species are detected. Bulk DNA is extracted, the amplified genes are cloned to separate them and then sequenced (Sanger), and sequences are identified against databases (NCBI, RDP, SILVA, Greengenes, HOMD, CORE). About 98.5–99% identity is accepted as the species-level criterion; lower scores suggest a new species. Uncultivated phylotypes cannot be formally named, so different authors give different names to the same phylotype. Phylogenetic trees add accuracy. Broad-range PCR is prone to contamination, so separate pre- and post-PCR rooms, ultraviolet decontamination, high-quality reagents and no-template controls are needed (Fouad, 2017).

### 9. Community fingerprinting: DGGE and T-RFLP
- **DGGE:** 16S rRNA gene fragments amplified with broad-range primers are run in a polyacrylamide gel with a rising gradient of urea and formamide; fragments of the same length but different sequence stop at different positions as they melt; a GC clamp prevents complete denaturation. Many samples can be compared at once, including changes after antimicrobial treatment, and bands can be excised and sequenced.
- **T-RFLP:** a fluorescently labelled primer amplifies the gene, restriction enzymes cut it, and the sizes and intensities of the terminal fragments on a sequencer give a community profile (Fouad, 2017).

### 10. DNA–DNA hybridization, microarrays and FISH
- **Checkerboard hybridization:** DNA from many samples on a nylon membrane is hybridized at right angles against many labelled whole-genome probes; spot intensity shows the amount of each species.
- **Reverse-capture checkerboard:** oligonucleotide probes (up to 30, 16S rRNA-based) are fixed first and PCR-amplified sample DNA is hybridized; no culture is needed to prepare probes and uncultivated species can be targeted.
- **DNA microarrays:** miniaturised checkerboards; the Human Oral Microbe Identification Microarray uses probes for about 300 oral species and phylotypes after broad-range PCR.
- **FISH:** fluorescent rRNA probes of 15–25 bases detect intact cells in situ and show morphology, number, architecture and spatial relationships, including uncultivated phylotypes (Fouad, 2017).

### 11. Next-generation sequencing and metagenomics
NGS performs massively parallel sequencing; platforms include 454 pyrosequencing (the most used for the oral microbiome), Illumina and SOLiD. Greater sampling depth gives better coverage and detects low-abundance species; greater breadth allows more samples to be compared. High-throughput studies have found phyla missed earlier (Tenericutes, Acidobacteria, Deinococcus-Thermus, Chloroflexi, Cyanobacteria), usually at low abundance (Berman et al, 2021). Sequencing errors may inflate richness estimates. Metagenomics asks what the community is doing as well as who is there, because the 16S rRNA gene is only about 0.05% of the genome and says little about physiology (Fouad, 2017).

### 12. Detecting viable and active bacteria in situ (Chávez de Paz, 2015)
Culture counts only cells that can form colonies, and cells in biofilm may be in a low-activity state that culture misses, so culture-independent viability markers are used with microscopy. The LIVE/DEAD kit uses two nucleic acid stains, SYTO-9 and propidium iodide, so cells with intact membranes fluoresce green and dead or injured cells red; tetrazolium salts (INT, CTC) mark respiratory activity and correlate with colony counts. Confocal laser scanning microscopy gives three-dimensional images of biofilm structure, and combined with FISH it detects and counts subpopulations in their natural 3D setting; ribosome content is a proxy for metabolic activity. Super-resolution microscopy is expected to add single-cell detail (Basrani, 2015).

### 13. Advantages and limitations of molecular methods
**Advantages.** Detects cultivable and uncultivated species.; High specificity and sensitivity; rapid (minutes to hours).; No strict anaerobic handling; usable during antibiotic treatment.; Samples can be frozen and DNA transported.; Detects dead organisms (Berman et al, 2021).

**Limitations.** Mostly qualitative or semiquantitative (real-time PCR excepted).; Few targets per assay (broad-range PCR, checkerboard and microarray excepted); may miss unexpected species.; Costly; bias and contamination risk in broad-range PCR.; DNA of dead cells can persist in canals for up to 2 years, so a positive result does not prove viability (Fouad, 2017).; sequencing errors may inflate richness in NGS; whole-genome hybridization probes detect only cultivable species; assays that target only chosen species miss unexpected ones (Fouad, 2017).

### 14. Choosing a method (Berman et al, 2021)
The choice depends on the question. Broad-range PCR with cloning and Sanger sequencing reveals the breadth of bacterial diversity; high-throughput sequencing gives much deeper coverage and discloses low-abundance members; fingerprinting (DGGE, T-RFLP) compares community structure between samples; FISH measures the abundance of target species and their spatial distribution in tissue; species-specific PCR can survey large numbers of clinical samples for target species; quantitative real-time PCR can test the antibacterial effectiveness of treatment; and PCR variants type strains.

### 15. Interpreting a positive result
The high sensitivity of PCR is a concern: it has been claimed that detecting very few cells of a species may have no significance for disease causation, yet the same sensitivity is a great advantage for diagnosis because paper-point sampling often misses cells in the complex canal, so species prevalence can be underestimated. DNA from dead cells can persist in canals for up to 2 years, which gives false positives for viability (Fouad, 2017).

### 16. Impact on knowledge of endodontic infections
Molecular studies confirmed many culture findings and added candidate pathogens: more than 400 species have been found, about 45% only by molecular methods, 32% by culture alone and 23% by both; nine phyla are represented. Studies are grouped into five generations, culture first and the other four culture-independent; second- to fourth-generation studies found a mean of 10 to 20 species per infected canal, and fifth-generation sequencing studies suggest higher numbers. The communities are mixed, differ between individuals, and no single species is specific for one disease form (Berman et al, 2021).

### 17. Clinical position
- Grossman: culture before obturation is no longer recommended: no outcome benefit, it prevents one-visit treatment, sampling errors give false results (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Cohen and Dahlén: canals negative at filling have a better outcome; a high count of polymicrobial anaerobes during treatment should raise suspicion of leakage; little benefit in sampling primary infections (Berman et al, 2021; Fouad, 2017).

Molecular assays are therefore mainly research tools and potential aids for defining endpoints; Dahlén states that screening only for S. mutans is not enough in caries and that PCR assays and microarrays for caries-associated taxa could help (Fouad, 2017).

### 18. Conclusion
Culture, the first method, remains useful for quantifying viable cultivable bacteria and testing susceptibility, but misses many species. PCR-based assays, hybridization methods, fingerprinting, FISH and sequencing detect cultivable and uncultivated species with greater sensitivity, and have changed the picture of the endodontic microbiota from a few cultivable species to a complex community. Their limits (detection of dead cells, semi-quantitative results, cost, bias) mean results must be interpreted with care (Berman et al, 2021; Fouad, 2017).
