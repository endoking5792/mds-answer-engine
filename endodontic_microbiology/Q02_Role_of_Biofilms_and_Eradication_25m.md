## STUDY MODE

### A. Role of biofilms in endodontics (summary; detail in Q1)
- **Cause of apical periodontitis.** Intraradicular biofilm is present in the apical segment of about 80% of canals with primary or post-treatment apical periodontitis, more often with large lesions and cysts (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Persistent infection.** In many root-filled teeth biofilms sit between the end of the filling and the apical foramen, and bacteria persist in unfilled and uninstrumented areas, dentinal tubules, lateral canals and ramifications (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Treatment failure.** Biofilm survival is why the canal is hard to disinfect; Grossman links endodontic biofilms to endodontic failure (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Acute flare-ups.** Biofilms are less aggressive but are foci for exacerbations by releasing enough planktonic cells to start acute infection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Antibiotic failure.** Systemic antibiotics do not reach the avascular necrotic canal and biofilm cells tolerate them (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Obturation.** Residual biofilm can lie in a lateral canal alongside filling material (histobacteriologic section, Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### B. Why "eradication" is a limited goal
- Instruments reach only part of the canal: micro-CT studies show different systems leave roughly 10–50% of canal surface unprepared (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022), and biofilms on untouched walls and in isthmuses, recesses and ramifications are not affected by instruments or the short irrigant contact time (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Biofilm studies by confocal microscopy suggest that killing all bacteria in a biofilm may not be possible with current substances: after three weeks, multispecies biofilms became much more resistant to NaOCl and other agents, and clinical root-canal biofilms are almost always older than three weeks (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- Even 5–6% NaOCl killed about half of E. faecalis in dentinal tubules in 3 minutes and little more after 10 minutes despite refreshing, interpreted as sensitive and resistant areas within one biofilm (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). The text gives 5% and the figure caption 6%; the discrepancy is in the source.
- The textbook paradigm is therefore "eliminate as many members of the community as possible and deny substrate to those that remain" (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). [Certain]

### C. Strategies (stepwise)
**1. Mechanical removal.**
- Instrumentation plus irrigation removes more than 90% of cultivable bacteria even without antibacterials, reducing counts 100–1,000-fold, but negative cultures were obtained in under half the teeth even after five appointments (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Larger apical preparation reduces bacteria more; NaOCl's benefit over saline appeared mainly with larger preparations (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Instrumenting more canal surface makes biofilm more susceptible to later disinfection (Berman et al, 2021).
- Patency files can disorganise apical biofilms and carry irrigant apically; success rates were higher with apical patency (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- The expandable XP-endo Shaper gave greater bacterial elimination than Reciproc, attributed to greater touch on the walls to dislodge biofilms, but with more apical extrusion of bacteria (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**2. Chemical agents.**
- *Sodium hypochlorite* dissolves organic components of dentine and biofilm (Berman et al, 2021) and, particularly at high concentration, is more effective than chlorhexidine on single- and multispecies biofilms and can dissolve the matrix, which CHX cannot (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). In a 15-minute experimental biofilm test, 6% NaOCl was the only irrigant that completely removed the biofilm and killed all bacteria, while CHX killed bacteria but left the biofilm structure, which may still express antigenic potential and weaken sealing (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). Against 3-week-old E. faecalis biofilm in dentine canals, 6% NaOCl was best, then QMiX, both better than 2% NaOCl and 2% CHX (Berman et al, 2021).
- *Chlorhexidine*: less effective than NaOCl on young and old multispecies biofilm discs; equal to 1–2% NaOCl but weaker than 6% NaOCl in the dentine-canal model (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- *Chelators*: EDTA may detach biofilms from the walls, which may explain why it was far better than saline at reducing canal bacteria despite limited antiseptic power (Berman et al, 2021).
- *Other irrigants*: QMiX killed up to 12 times more biofilm bacteria than 1% NaOCl or 2% CHX in multispecies discs (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019); several antimicrobial-containing chelators lacked antibiofilm activity on intraorally infected dentine, except NaOCl and 4% peracetic acid (Berman et al, 2021).
- Concentration is contested (see Synthesis).

**3. Activation of irrigants.**
- *Passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI)*: acoustic streaming and cavitation may disturb and deagglomerate biofilms, flush out detached cells and make them more susceptible to warmed NaOCl; the instrument needs a canal enlarged to about size 40 to oscillate freely. Evidence is inconsistent: systematic reviews found weak support, and one outcome study found 95% vs 88% satisfactory results with and without PUI, not significant (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). PUI did not effectively disinfect the isthmus in an ex vivo study (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- *Mechanical finishers (XP-endo Finisher)*: agitation plus the instrument's touch gave greater bacterial reduction and biofilm removal from the canal and tubules (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- *Multisonic (GentleWave)*: cavitation cloud said to dissolve tissue and remove biofilm (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022; source gives no clinical outcome in the passage read).
- *Laser (PIPS)*: good laboratory activity including biofilms attached to dentine, but "no clinical studies consistently" show safety and effectiveness of lasers for intracanal disinfection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022); Ingle lists studies where PIPS + 6% NaOCl beat 6% NaOCl alone against E. faecalis biofilm, while others found no difference over NaOCl alone (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). Biofilms are challenging to eradicate even with direct laser exposure (Berman et al, 2021).
- *Photodynamic therapy*: photosensitiser plus red light (630–700 nm) produces singlet oxygen and free radicals; many in vitro studies against biofilm bacteria but mostly as direct effects, not as an add-on; recommended only after chemomechanical preparation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**4. Intracanal medication.** The primary objective of interappointment medication is to eliminate residual biofilms and microorganisms that survived preparation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Calcium hydroxide's low solubility and diffusibility, and buffering by serum and dentine, make it hard to raise pH quickly enough to kill bacteria in biofilms, tubules and anatomical variations (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). CHX has at least as good in vitro activity as Ca(OH)2, and 2% CHX eliminated an E. faecalis biofilm; it remains unclear whether medicaments work against multispecies biofilms (Berman et al, 2021). Recommended protocol: Ca(OH)2 with CPMC–glycerin or with 0.12–2% CHX for about 7 days, then removal with the master apical file under irrigation, which improved bacterial reduction (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**5. Ecological approach.** Removing nutrient sources (necrotic tissue and partners), disturbing key community members and sealing the canal can collapse the community, but partial elimination can instead reorganise it toward more resistant species, hence the recommendation for single-visit preparation with medication and then obturation to deny nutrients (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**6. Emerging.** Chitosan and silver nanoparticles disrupt E. faecalis and multispecies biofilms in vitro (Berman et al, 2021); electrochemically activated solutions have laboratory promise but Cohen advises waiting for safety and efficacy data (Berman et al, 2021); surface coating with benzalkonium chloride reduced biofilm formation about 70-fold in vitro but the coating exhausts over time (Chávez de Paz, 2015); matrix-degrading enzymes and quorum-sensing inhibitors are research ideas (Chávez de Paz, 2015; Fouad, 2017).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim)
**Claim — higher NaOCl concentration is needed to destroy biofilm.** The textbooks disagree. Laboratory biofilm models favour strong NaOCl: 6% was best against old E. faecalis biofilms and the only irrigant removing the biofilm structure (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). Clinical data in Treatment of Endodontic Infections show no difference in bacteria-positive canals between 0.5% and 5% NaOCl, no significant difference between 1%, 2.5% and 5.25% in vitro, and healing of 72% vs 81% with 1% vs 5% (not significant), with the authors concluding that large volumes and frequent exchange compensate for concentration (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Interpretation: the models measure biofilm kill under controlled contact time, while the clinical data measure canal bacterial counts and healing after full preparation. Limitation: the laboratory data are ex vivo or in vitro and the healing difference is a single study; neither supports a claim of superior clinical outcome. Clinical implication: use adequate volume, exchange and activation; concentration is a secondary lever. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Estimated writing time: ~39 minutes at 22 words/min; ~865 words)**

### 1. Role of biofilms in endodontics
- **Aetiology:** apical periodontitis is a biofilm-related disease; intraradicular biofilm is found in the apical canal of about 80% of cases and more often in large lesions and cysts (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Persistence and failure:** biofilms remain in unprepared areas, lateral canals, tubules and between the end of the filling and the foramen (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Flare-ups:** planktonic cells released from biofilm can start acute infection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- **Tolerance to treatment:** biofilm is tolerant to irrigants, medicaments and systemic antibiotics, which do not reach the avascular canal (Berman et al, 2021).

### 2. Why complete eradication is difficult
- Instrumentation leaves roughly 10–50% of the canal surface unprepared on micro-CT, and biofilm in isthmuses, recesses and ramifications escapes instruments and the short irrigant contact time (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Biofilms older than three weeks become much more resistant to NaOCl, and clinical biofilms are almost always older (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- Killing of E. faecalis in dentine by NaOCl is rapid in the first minutes and then plateaus, because a biofilm contains sensitive and resistant areas (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- The accepted aim is therefore to eliminate as many members of the community as possible and deny substrate to those that remain (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 3. Eradication (control) strategies
**A. Mechanical**
- Instrumentation with irrigation removes over 90% of cultivable bacteria and reduces counts 100–1,000-fold, but negative cultures are not predictable (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- A larger apical preparation gives greater bacterial reduction, and NaOCl's advantage over saline appears mainly with larger preparations.
- The patency file disorganises apical biofilm and carries irrigant apically; apical patency was linked to higher success.
- Expandable instruments (XP-endo Shaper) and finishers touch more wall and dislodge biofilm, though the Shaper increased apical extrusion of bacteria.

**B. Chemical**
- **NaOCl** dissolves organic components of dentine and biofilm and can dissolve the biofilm matrix; 6% NaOCl completely removed experimental biofilm in 15 minutes and was best against three-week-old E. faecalis biofilm (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). Use large volumes, frequently renewed.
- **Chlorhexidine** kills bacteria but leaves the biofilm structure, and was weaker than NaOCl on older multispecies biofilm.
- **EDTA** may detach biofilm from the walls and removes the smear layer; **QMiX** killed up to 12 times more biofilm bacteria than 1% NaOCl or 2% CHX in vitro; 4% peracetic acid also showed antibiofilm activity in vitro (Berman et al, 2021).

- **Concentration is debated:** laboratory biofilm models favour 6% NaOCl, but clinical studies found no significant difference between 0.5% and 5% NaOCl (bacteria-positive canals), and healing of 72% vs 81% for 1% vs 5%; large volume and frequent exchange compensate for concentration. Antibacterial effectiveness is the most important irrigant property, NaOCl remains the irrigant of choice and CHX a possible substitute of similar potency (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**C. Delivery and activation**
- NaOCl penetrates tubules to about 77–300 µm; irrigation reaches further when the needle is near working length and the canal is larger; curvature reduces effect (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

| Method | Mechanism | Evidence (as stated) |
|---|---|---|
| Passive ultrasonic irrigation | Acoustic streaming and cavitation disrupt biofilm | Inconsistent; systematic reviews weak; healing 95% vs 88%, not significant |
| Finishers (XP-endo) | Mechanical touch plus agitation | Greater bacterial and biofilm reduction ex vivo |
| Multisonic (GentleWave) | Cavitation cloud | Passage read gives no clinical outcome |
| PIPS laser | Shock waves | Laboratory activity; no consistent clinical evidence |
| Photodynamic therapy | Photosensitiser plus red light gives reactive oxygen | Mostly in vitro; used after preparation |

**D. Intracanal medication**
- The aim is to eliminate residual biofilm and organisms that survived preparation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Calcium hydroxide acts poorly in biofilms, tubules and ramifications because of low solubility and buffering by dentine and serum.
- Recommended: calcium hydroxide with CPMC–glycerin or 0.12–2% CHX for about 7 days; remove with the master apical file under irrigation, which improved bacterial reduction.
- Medicament effect on multispecies biofilm remains unclear (Berman et al, 2021).

**E. Ecological approach**
- Removing nutrients and key partner species and sealing the canal can collapse the community; partial elimination may instead reorganise it toward more resistant species, hence single-visit preparation with medication and then obturation (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**F. Newer, experimental**
- Chitosan and silver nanoparticles, benzalkonium chloride surface coating (about 70-fold less biofilm in vitro, but the effect is lost as the coating exhausts), matrix-degrading enzymes and quorum-sensing inhibitors; all laboratory-stage (Berman et al, 2021; Chávez de Paz, 2015).

### 4. Clinical protocol (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
1. Rubber dam and disinfection of the field.
2. Crown-down preparation with 2.5% NaOCl after each instrument, to a large apical size, with apical patency.
3. Small needles near working length for delivery.
4. EDTA or citric acid to remove the smear layer, then NaOCl.
5. Supplementary disinfection: ultrasonic activation, finisher, or final 2% CHX with an intermediate rinse to avoid precipitate.
6. Calcium hydroxide paste for about 7 days, then removal with the master apical file.
7. Obturation with a bacteria-tight coronal seal.

### 5. Conclusion
Biofilms are reduced, not eliminated. Success rests on adequate enlargement, copious and frequently renewed NaOCl, activation, medication where indicated and a bacteria-tight seal.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Stating that biofilms "can be eradicated" or that any single irrigant does it; the textbooks say reduction.
2. Writing CHX as the best antibiofilm irrigant; it does not remove biofilm structure.
3. Forgetting the unprepared 10–50% of canal wall as the main reason biofilm survives.
4. Giving PUI, laser or PDT as proven clinically superior; evidence is mainly laboratory.
5. Missing medicament removal with the master apical file, which improves disinfection.
