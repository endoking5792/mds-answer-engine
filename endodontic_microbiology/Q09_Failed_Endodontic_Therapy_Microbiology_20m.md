## STUDY MODE
**What failure is.** Signs (radiolucency, sinus tract, swelling) or symptoms in a root-filled tooth mean apical periodontitis has emerged, persisted or recurred: post-treatment apical periodontitis, which can be emergent, persistent or recurrent (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Failure usually follows treatment that did not meet standards for preventing and controlling infection; the larger the unfilled, probably unprepared, apical volume, the higher the bacterial count there (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). But even well-treated teeth fail: post-treatment disease occurs in 5–15% of teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis despite acceptable treatment, and the cause is still bacteria (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**The central statement.** "Endodontic treatment failure is primarily a microbiologic problem": infection is present in virtually all cases. Lesions persist because of a persistent intraradicular infection, a secondary intraradicular infection or an extraradicular infection, and extraradicular infection is nearly always sustained by a concomitant intraradicular one. A lesion that appears after treating a tooth without preoperative disease is very likely a secondary infection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**1. Persistent intraradicular infection.** Organisms from the primary infection that resist treatment. They hide where instruments and irrigants do not reach: isthmuses, ramifications, lateral canals, dentinal tubules, recesses, voids in the filling, and the area between the end of the filling and the foramen (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022); micro-CT shows about 10–50% of the canal surface unprepared (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Mechanisms of evasion (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022): biofilm formation; protection in tissue remnants, dentine, serum and dead cells that inactivate agents; intrinsic resistance; adaptation to the changed ecology by activating survival genes; viable-but-non-cultivable state; and niches where nutrients persist, such as the apical canal near the foramen.
**2. Secondary intraradicular infection.** New organisms introduced during treatment, between visits or after filling through breaks in asepsis or coronal leakage; organisms not found in primary infections (E. faecalis, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, enteric rods, Candida) point to it (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
**3. Extraradicular infection.** Biofilm on the external apical surface and periapical actinomycosis (see Q6); rare (about 6% of apical periodontitis in untreated teeth) and usually dependent on intraradicular infection (Berman et al, 2021).

**Persistent or secondary: which is commoner? The literature shifted.** Interest in coronal leakage grew in the mid-1990s, but recent indirect evidence points to persistent infection as most common (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022): biopsies show bacteria in the apical third, rarely along the whole canal, which leakage would not explain; bacteria-positive samples at filling predict a poor outcome; failure is more frequent in teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis than in vital teeth, which leakage alone would not explain; well-prepared and sealed canals resisted coronal bacterial leakage in one study (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). Cohen notes that the two are for the most part clinically indistinguishable (Berman et al, 2021).

**Microbiota of failed teeth.** Reduced diversity: 1–5 species in apparently well-treated canals, up to 10–20 in inadequately treated ones; 10³–10⁷ cells per canal; mainly Gram-positive facultative and anaerobic bacteria (streptococci, P. micra, Propionibacterium, P. alactolyticus, Actinomyces, lactobacilli, E. faecalis, Olsenella uli); Candida in up to 18%; uncultivated phylotypes up to 55% of taxa (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019). E. faecalis is the most frequent species (up to 90%) but its causal role is unproven (Q3).

**Do residual bacteria always cause failure? No.** Lesions can heal despite bacteria before filling if bacteria die after filling (toxic material, no nutrients, disrupted ecology), are below the critical number or virulence, or lie where the periradicular tissue cannot reach them. They matter if they can withstand starvation, resist ecological disruption (including quorum sensing and biofilm structure), reach a critical load, reach the periradicular tissues, and express virulence (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022; Berman et al, 2021).

**Procedural errors and overfilling.** Fractured instruments, ledges, perforations and over- or underfilling do not usually jeopardise outcome unless infection is present; they block access to the apical canal, letting organisms persist, and a perforation adds an exit for bacteria. The apical level of filling seems unrelated to failure provided infection is controlled (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

**Non-microbial causes.** Cholesterol crystals, true apical cysts, and foreign-body reactions to extruded material, paper-point or cotton cellulose and food particles have been proposed, based on a few case reports with low-sensitivity methods; the book regards the evidence as very low and notes concomitant infection cannot be excluded (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim)
**Claim — coronal leakage is the main microbial cause of failure.** The earlier view stressed leakage; the current textbook view is that persistent infection after inadequate disinfection of the apical canal is the more common cause (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022). The support is indirect: biopsy distribution of bacteria, outcome by preoperative status, and bacteria at filling predicting outcome. Direct proof is not possible because persistent and secondary infections cannot be told apart clinically (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022; Berman et al, 2021), so both remain possible in a given tooth. Clinical implication: disinfect the apical canal thoroughly and seal coronally, rather than choosing one. [Likely]

## EXAM MODE
**(Estimated writing time: ~32 minutes at 22 words/min; ~708 words)**

### 1. Definition
Failure is post-treatment apical periodontitis: signs or symptoms in a root-filled tooth, emergent, persistent or recurrent. It is primarily a microbial problem; infection is present in virtually all cases (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 2. Microbial causes
**A. Persistent intraradicular infection** (organisms of the primary infection that resisted treatment)
- Survive in isthmuses, ramifications, lateral canals, dentinal tubules, recesses and unfilled apical areas; about 10–50% of canal surface may be unprepared.
- Bacteria at filling predict a poor outcome.

**B. Secondary intraradicular infection** (new organisms)
- Entry via breach of asepsis, between visits, or coronal leakage after filling.
- Suggested by E. faecalis, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, enteric rods or Candida, which are not found in primary infections.

**C. Extraradicular infection**
- Extraradicular biofilm and periapical actinomycosis; rare and usually dependent on intraradicular infection (Berman et al, 2021).

### 3. How bacteria survive treatment (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
1. Biofilm adherence and matrix.
2. Colonising areas away from the main canal.
3. Protection by tissue remnants, dentine, serum, dead cells.
4. Intrinsic resistance (for example E. faecalis to calcium hydroxide).
5. Adaptation to nutrient loss, including viable-but-non-cultivable state.
6. Niches in the apical canal where nutrients remain.

### 4. Microbiota of failed cases
Fewer species (1–5 if well treated, up to 10–20 if poorly treated); mainly Gram-positive facultative bacteria and anaerobes: streptococci, Actinomyces, lactobacilli, Olsenella, E. faecalis (up to 90%, causal role unproven); Candida up to 18% (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

### 4a. Bacteria left at filling (Berman et al, 2021)
Residual bacteria are mostly in areas inaccessible to treatment; 1–5 species and 10²–10⁵ cells per sample remain after good treatment, Gram-negatives are usually eliminated, with F. nucleatum and Prevotella among the exceptions, and Gram-positive species predominate. Persistent and secondary infections can cause persistent exudate (wet canals), persistent symptoms, flare-ups and failure.

### 5. Persistent versus secondary
Current evidence favours persistent infection as commoner: bacteria are found in the apical third, failure is higher in teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis, and bacteria at filling predict outcome; coronal leakage in the mid-1990s was thought important, but well-sealed canals resisted leakage in one study (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).

### 5a. Evidence that persistent infection is the commoner cause (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
1. Biopsies of failed teeth show bacteria in the apical third, rarely along the whole canal; coronal leakage would colonise the entire canal.
2. Bacteria-positive samples at filling predict a poor outcome, and bacteria can survive a root filling for years (monkey study).
3. Failure is more frequent in teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis than in vital teeth; if leakage were the main cause, rates in vital, necrotic and retreatment cases would be similar.
4. Well-prepared and sealed canals resisted coronal bacterial leakage.

### 6. Why some residual bacteria do not cause failure
They die after filling, are subcritical in number or virulence, or are sealed away from the periradicular tissues (Berman et al, 2021).

### 6a. When residual bacteria influence outcome (Berman et al, 2021)
They must withstand starvation or become dormant, resist disruption of quorum sensing, food webs and biofilm structure, reach a critical load, have access to periradicular tissues through foramina or perforations, and express virulence in the changed environment; host resistance also matters.

### 7. Related factors
- Procedural errors (ledges, fractured instruments, perforation, over/underfilling) cause failure mainly with concomitant infection.
- Non-microbial causes (cholesterol crystals, true cysts, foreign bodies) are supported only by weak evidence.

### 7a. Procedural errors and overfilling (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
- Ledges and fractured instruments prevent instruments and irrigants reaching the apical canal, so organisms persist there.
- A perforation creates an extra exit, usually larger than natural foramina, through which more bacteria can reach the periodontium.
- The apical extent of filling seems unrelated to failure if infection is controlled; lack of apical seal is the main cause when infection is present.

### 7b. Non-microbial causes proposed (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022)
Cholesterol crystals and true cysts (endogenous); foreign-body reactions to extruded filling material, to cellulose from paper points or cotton, and to food particles (exogenous). The evidence is a few case reports, and concomitant infection cannot be excluded; a bay cyst, unlike a true cyst, has its cavity contiguous with the apical canal.

### 8. Implications
Thorough apical disinfection (adequate enlargement, irrigation with activation, medication), aseptic technique with rubber dam, and a good coronal seal.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Failure = persistent, secondary or extraradicular infection.
- Even well-treated teeth fail in 5–15% of those with preoperative apical periodontitis.
- Persistent infection is thought commoner than leakage-related secondary infection.
- Failed canals have 1–5 species, mainly Gram-positive; E. faecalis most frequent, cause unproven.
- Procedural errors fail mainly through infection.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Blaming procedural errors directly rather than the associated infection.
2. Presenting coronal leakage as the proven main cause.
3. Naming E. faecalis as the sole cause of failure.
4. Omitting extraradicular infection.
5. Giving non-microbial causes as established.
