## STUDY MODE
*(Full reasoning on the pathways, the two-way controversy and classification is in Q01's Study Mode. Only what this question needs in addition is given here.)*

**Framing the answer.** "Interrelationship" asks for mechanism in both directions, not a list of lesions. A strong answer has four parts: the anatomical basis (how the tissues communicate), the effect of pulpal disease and endodontic treatment on the periodontium, the effect of periodontal disease and periodontal treatment on the pulp, and the clinical consequences (combined lesions, classification, sequencing). Weine's line that endodontics may be thought of as periapical periodontics gives the essay its opening idea (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

**Anatomy versus clinical frequency.** The communication channels are common in anatomical studies (accessory canals in 45% of sectioned teeth; 30 to 40% of teeth with lateral or accessory canals) (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC], yet clinically significant spread is uncommon: only 2% of lateral canals were associated with periodontal pockets in 1000 teeth with extensive periodontitis (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC], and Cohen's argues that the rarity of combined lesions suggests periradicular disease has little effect on marginal bone (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]. The examiner rewards this contrast.

**The asymmetry of the two directions.**
- Pulp to periodontium is accepted. The mechanism is direct exit of bacteria and by-products through the foramen and lateral canals; the evidence includes epithelial downgrowth on denuded dentine of infected teeth and greater probing depths at molars with periapical lesions on both roots (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]. The size of the effect is disputed (see Claim 1).
- Periodontium to pulp is controversial. Intact cementum protects the pulp; once tubules or accessory canals are exposed, bacteria can enter, but the pulp usually survives while the apical vessels are intact (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng explain the limited effect by tubule sclerosis and secondary dentine (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

**Treatment effects are part of the interrelationship.** Endodontic medicaments, extruded materials and perforations can injure the attachment (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]; root planing, curettage and flap surgery can expose tubules or cut lateral-canal vessels (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng note that root planing originally aimed to remove "infected" cementum, while modern debridement aims to disrupt biofilm but still removes some cementum (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Endodontic infection promotes periodontitis."**
Textbook: Cohen's calls it a local modifying risk factor if untreated (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]. Evidence: experimental studies show more epithelial downgrowth in infected teeth and clinical studies show deeper pockets at molars with periapical lesions (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]; Walton and Torabinejad counter that the pocket difference (0.5 to 1.0 mm) is within probing error and that endodontic involvement did not predict tooth loss in 2509 teeth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Interpretation: there is a measurable effect, but its clinical size is small; Cohen's itself states that causality has not been established (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]. Clinical point: treat infected canals in periodontally involved teeth before periodontal therapy; do not attribute generalised periodontitis to pulpal disease [Likely].

**Claim 2: "Periodontal disease damages the pulp."**
Evidence: histological change near exposed lateral canals and bacterial recovery from 87% of periodontally involved pulps and dentine (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, 2017) [SRC] against 70% of roots unchanged in an animal model and only 3 to 4% of teeth needing root canal treatment over long-term human follow-up (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Interpretation: these measure different outcomes (contamination, histology, clinical necrosis), so they are compatible: contamination is common, necrosis uncommon until the apex is involved [Likely]. Limitation: the animal and cohort data are old and cannot be compared directly.

### 1. Introduction
The pulp and periodontium form a biological unit linked embryologically, anatomically and through the vascular system (Gopikrishna, 2021; Glickman and Iacono, 2021). Endodontics may be called periapical periodontics, since its aim is health of the periodontal tissues at the apex (Weine, 2004). The relationship was first described by Simring and Goldberg; Bender and Seltzer (1972) called it the "pulpodontic-periodontic syndrome" (Gopikrishna, 2021). Pulpal and periodontal problems together are said to cause more than half of tooth loss (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

**Endo-periodontal lesion (2017):** a pathological communication between the pulpal and periodontal tissues at a given tooth, acute or chronic (Herrera et al, 2018; Papapanou et al, 2018).

### 2. Pathways of communication
**A. Anatomical**
1. **Apical foramen**: the main, most direct pathway (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
2. **Lateral and accessory canals**
   - 30 to 40% of teeth, mostly in the apical third (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
   - Furcation canals: Rubach and Mitchell 45%, Lowman et al 59% maxillary and 55% mandibular molars, Burch and Hulen 76%, Kirkham 23%, Gutmann 28% (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
   - Only 2% were associated with periodontal pockets in a study of 1000 periodontally diseased teeth (Rotstein, 2017).
   - Clinical clues: discrete lateral lesion with a necrotic pulp, a "notch" on the root, sealer extruded on the radiograph; two-view radiographs showed about 30% of lateral canals against 8% on standard films (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
3. **Dentinal tubules**
   - Do not normally communicate; they do when cementum is lost by recession, disease, root debridement or surgery (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
   - Dentine is exposed at the CEJ in about 18% of teeth, 25% of anterior teeth (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
   - Plaque products applied to exposed dentine can inflame the pulp (Rotstein, 2017).
4. **Other routes**: periodontal ligament, alveolar bone, neural (reflex) and vasculolymphatic pathways; the vascular link is the best evidenced (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

**B. Developmental**
- Palatogingival groove: lateral incisors 4.4%, central 0.28%; 0.5% reach the apex; probably an attempt to form an extra root; creates a self-sustaining infrabony pocket (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Cervical enamel projections in molar furcations (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

**C. Pathological (non-physiological)**
- Perforations: iatrogenic, resorptive or carious; middle and apical third perforations heal best, coronal and furcal ones lead to epithelial migration (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Vertical root fracture: "halo" radiolucency, deep narrow pocket (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- External root resorption (Herrera et al, 2018).

### 3. Effect of pulpal disease on the periodontium
- Bacteria, fungi, viruses and their products leave the necrotic pulp through the foramen, lateral and furcation canals and tubules, causing apical, lateral or furcal bone loss (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- When apical inflammation tracks to the gingival margin it forms a retrograde periodontitis, a "reverse pocket" (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Drainage through the ligament gives a narrow sinus-type defect in the sulcus or furcation.
- Experimental intrapulpal infection promoted epithelial downgrowth on denuded dentine after 20 weeks; infected defects had 20% more epithelium (Rotstein, 2017).
- Molars with periapical lesions on both roots had greater probing depths (Rotstein, 2017).
- Endodontic infection is a local modifying risk factor for periodontitis if untreated (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- **But**: the rarity of combined lesions suggests the effect is small, and causality is not established (Glickman and Iacono, 2021); Walton and Torabinejad consider the measured difference (0.5 to 1.0 mm) trivial and state that pulpal necrosis causes a sinus tract, not periodontal disease, and that bone loss from pulpal necrosis is reversible (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

### 4. Effect of endodontic treatment on the periodontium
- Medicaments (calcium hydroxide, corticosteroids, antibiotics) at high concentration can irritate the attachment (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Extruded sealers and filling materials, perforations, strip perforations and fractures from obturation or posts damage the attachment (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Coronal leakage reinfects the canal and sustains periodontal inflammation (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Successful root canal treatment lets periodontal tissues of pulpal origin heal; there is general agreement on this (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Root-filled teeth and periodontal healing: one study found 60% regeneration on untreated teeth against 33% on root-filled teeth, but a monkey study found regeneration independent of pulp status (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

### 5. Effect of periodontal disease on the pulp
The effect is controversial and studies conflict (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Rotstein, 2017).

| Evidence of an effect | Evidence of little effect |
|---|---|
| Inflammation and localised necrosis next to exposed lateral canals (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) | 70% of roots unchanged despite 30 to 40% attachment loss (Bergenholtz and Lindhe, animal) (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) |
| Fibrosis, calcification, collagen resorption, narrower canals in long-standing disease (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, 2017) | Little or no effect on human pulps; intact caries-free periodontally involved teeth histologically normal (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |
| Bacteria grown from 87% of pulps and radicular dentine of periodontally involved teeth (Rotstein, 2017) | Only 3 to 4% of periodontally treated teeth needed root canal treatment over long follow-up (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |

**Consensus**
- Intact cementum protects the pulp (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- The pulp survives while the main apical vessels are intact; necrosis follows when the pocket reaches the apex (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- The limited effect is due to tubule sclerosis and secondary dentine (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).

### 6. Effect of periodontal treatment on the pulp
- Scaling, root planing and curettage remove cementum and open tubules, causing sensitivity and sometimes pulpitis (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
- A curette can sever the vessels of a lateral canal, leading to pulpal inflammation and necrosis (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- The pulpal reaction depends on the type of periodontal treatment, not only the stage of disease (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Curettage of a lesion surrounding the apex severs the pulp supply, so root canal treatment should be done first (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Root canal treatment of a "doubtful" pulp before periodontal regenerative surgery must be weighed against the harm of extruded materials (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).

### 7. Common aetiological factors
- **Microbiology**: periodontal and endodontic pathogens are similar; PCR found red-complex and other periodontal pathogens in all endodontic samples tested; there is no specific microbial profile for the endo-periodontal lesion (Rotstein, 2017; Herrera et al, 2018).
- Other organisms: L-forms, fungi (subgingival fungi in about 20% of adult periodontitis patients), herpesviruses (cytomegalovirus in about 65% of pocket samples) (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Non-living: foreign bodies such as paper-point cellulose; cholesterol crystals (Seltzer and Bender Ch.16, 2012).
- Contributing: trauma (a major factor), perforation, resorption, poor endodontics and restorations, coronal leakage, developmental grooves (Gopikrishna, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- The longer the cause persists, the more likely combined treatment is needed (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 8. Clinical outcome of the interrelationship: endo-perio lesions
**Simon, Glick and Frank (1972)** (Gopikrishna, 2021)
1. Primary endodontic lesion
2. Primary endodontic with secondary periodontal involvement
3. Primary periodontal lesion
4. Primary periodontal with secondary endodontic involvement
5. True combined lesion
6. Concomitant lesion (Belk and Gutmann)

**2017 World Workshop** (Herrera et al, 2018): with root damage (fracture or cracking, perforation, external resorption) or without root damage (periodontitis or non-periodontitis patient), graded 1 to 3 by pocket width and number of surfaces.

| Lesion | Treatment | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| Primary endodontic | Root canal treatment only | Excellent; complete resolution expected (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) |
| Primary endodontic, secondary periodontal | RCT, then periodontal therapy | Endodontics alone heals only part (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) |
| Primary periodontal | Periodontal therapy only | Depends exclusively on periodontal therapy (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) |
| Primary periodontal, secondary endodontic | RCT, then continued periodontal therapy | Depends on periodontal treatment (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) |
| True combined | RCT, then periodontal therapy, regeneration or resection | Guarded; depends mainly on periodontal therapy (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) |

### 8A. Telling the two origins apart
| Feature | Endodontic origin | Periodontal origin |
|---|---|---|
| Pulp test | Non-vital | Vital |
| Pocket | Narrow, deep, isolated; traced to the apex with a gutta-percha cone | Broad, conical; generalised |
| Pain | Acute, spontaneous | Dull, chronic |
| Percussion | Vertical | Lateral, mild |
| Mobility | Rare, localised | Common |
| Plaque and calculus | Not causative | Present |
| Response | Heals after root canal treatment | Needs periodontal therapy |
*(Gopikrishna, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002)*
- A deep solitary pocket in a mouth without periodontal disease suggests endodontic origin (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- A sinus tract is narrow and admits only a gutta-percha cone; an infrabony pocket admits wider instruments (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Vertical root fractures and palatogingival grooves mimic both and must be excluded (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

### 9. Clinical implications for treatment sequence
- Endodontic treatment first, whatever the origin (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Reassess after 2 to 3 months before periodontal treatment (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Avoid aggressive removal of ligament and cementum in the interim (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Drainage through the sinus into the defect would interfere with periodontal repair (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Both disciplines aim at the same thing, decontaminating a surface (canal wall or root surface), by different procedures (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).

### 10. Conclusion
The pulp and periodontium communicate through the apical foramen, lateral and accessory canals and exposed tubules, with developmental and pathological routes adding to these. Pulpal disease affects the periodontium predictably but usually reversibly; periodontal disease affects the pulp less often, mainly when the apex or a large lateral canal is reached. Both diseases share a similar microbiota, and both treatments can injure the other tissue. Understanding these links explains why endodontic treatment comes first and why the long-term prognosis of a combined lesion rests on the periodontal component.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Main pathways: apical foramen, lateral/accessory canals, dentinal tubules.
- Lateral canals in 30 to 40% of teeth, but only 2% associated with pockets (Kirkham).
- Dentine exposed at the CEJ in about 18% of teeth.
- Pulp to periodontium: retrograde periodontitis, reverse pocket; local modifying risk factor.
- Periodontium to pulp: controversial; pulp survives while apical vessels are intact.
- 70% of roots unchanged despite 30 to 40% attachment loss (Bergenholtz and Lindhe).
- Periodontal treatment can sever lateral-canal vessels.
