## STUDY MODE
*(The pathways, the two-way effects and the classification controversy are explained in Q01's Study Mode. This Study Mode covers only the diagnostic and planning reasoning.)*

**Why diagnosis is hard.** Gulabivala and Ng locate the problem in one place: the pulp cannot be seen, so its status must be inferred. Total pulp necrosis is easy to diagnose because pulp tests are sensitive and specific in that state; partial necrosis and infection, particularly in multirooted teeth, are the real difficulty (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. They advise testing each root or cusp separately with cold, heat and electric tests, because enamel and dentine thickness vary and partial necrosis gives mixed responses (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Root-filled teeth add another trap: the technical quality of a root filling does not necessarily tell you whether residual infection is present (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad agree that a positive test in a multirooted tooth deserves less confidence than one in a single-canal tooth, and add a reasoning rule: irreversible pulpitis seldom produces a large periapical lesion, so a large lesion means at least one necrotic canal (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**The probe is the main diagnostic instrument.** Walton and Torabinejad's chapter (from Harrington) argues that, given the limits of pulp testing and radiographs, the discrimination "must be made primarily" with a fine periodontal probe, and distinguishes probing (to the epithelial attachment) from sounding (through the attachment to bone) (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Its reasoning is anatomical: periodontal bone loss always begins at the crest, so a periodontal lesion probes as a cone, and an intact sulcus excludes periodontal disease as the cause of any radiolucency (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. A sinus tract probes as a break of about 1 mm with normal depths on either side; a "blow-out" lesion drops precipitously across the width of a swelling (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng record the probing profile continuously around the tooth rather than at six points, because the shape of the defect (long and narrow versus broad and wide) is the information wanted (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. They add a caution: a lateral endodontic abscess can give a wide, deep "blow-out" profile that still heals with endodontics alone (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Width is a clue, not proof.

**Radiographs.** When bone loss runs from the crest to the apex, the film cannot show the cause (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng give a practical threshold: in a patient with generalised periodontitis, intrabony or horizontal loss affecting more than two-thirds of the root is more likely to have been influenced by pulp infection, especially with large restorations, abnormal pulp tests or poor root fillings (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Cohen's insists on more than one angulation, because a small change can reveal a fracture or furcation involvement (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC].

**Diagnosis by response.** Several sources treat the outcome of root canal treatment as the final diagnostic test. Harty's says that if healing follows root canal treatment, a valid retrospective diagnosis can be made, and the degree of healing sets the classification; Gulabivala and Ng call the response a retrospective diagnostic tool (Rotstein, 2017; Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. This is why the reassessment interval matters.

**Planning: the question changed in 2017.** The traditional plan follows Simon's class: treat whichever tissue is diseased, endodontics first. Herrera's review argues the first planning question should be whether to keep or extract, decided by the present state and the prognosis, not by guessing which disease came first; it puts root damage (fracture, perforation, resorption) as the first thing to look for, and gives three prognostic groups (hopeless, poor, favourable) (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Cohen's frames the same choice as "treatment alternatives" when routine endodontic and periodontal treatment are insufficient: resection or regeneration, and if extraction, implants or fixed partial dentures (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng add the time dimension: retaining a compromised tooth must be balanced against damage to a future implant site from continued bone loss (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

**Sequence and timing.**
- *Grossman's*: endodontics before periodontics regardless of cause, because endodontics is predictable, the periodontal lesion cannot heal while the endodontic lesion persists, and periodontitis is slow (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].
- *Ingle's and Harty's (Rotstein)*: evaluate 2 to 3 months after endodontics before periodontal treatment; avoid aggressive removal of ligament and cementum in the interim (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC].
- *Walton and Torabinejad*: if the periodontal prognosis is satisfactory, complete root canal treatment before definitive periodontal therapy, because drainage through the sinus into the defect interferes with repair; a pure periodontal lesion will not respond to root canal treatment even on a pulpless tooth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].
- *A caution from Gulabivala and Ng*: root canal treatment of a doubtful pulp before periodontal regenerative surgery should be weighed against the harm of extruded materials (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

**Weine's planning summaries** are useful for a clinical answer: a Class I tooth (pulpal disease simulating periodontal disease) needs endodontics only and heals well even in old patients; in Class II, once the pulp is damaged, periodontal treatment will not heal it; a Class IV tooth (periodontal disease simulating pulpal disease) improves briefly after endodontics, then relapses, and the prognosis is very poor unless periodontal treatment is added (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "The response to root canal treatment is a reliable way to diagnose the origin."**
Textbook: Harty's and Gulabivala and Ng both use it (Rotstein, 2017; Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Evidence: the supporting material is case-based (recall radiographs and probing). Walton and Torabinejad give the timescales that make it usable: blow-out lesions reattach within a week; furcal sinus-type defects may take 2 to 3 months after obturation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Interpretation: healing confirms an endodontic component; failure to heal does not by itself prove a periodontal origin, since inadequate root canal treatment, a fracture or a horizontal furcation component in an older patient also fail (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC] [Likely]. Limitation: no diagnostic accuracy study was retrieved. Clinical point: plan a defined review (2 to 3 months), and look for fracture before attributing a residual defect to periodontitis.

**Claim 2: "Plan by prognosis and root damage, not by which disease came first."**
Textbook: Simon's class-based planning remains standard (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Gulabivala and Ng say no classification especially helps diagnosis or treatment (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Evidence: Herrera's review finds root-damage lesions carry the worst prognosis and that history is usually unknown (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT], but rates the evidence as limited, mostly case reports (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Interpretation: textbook experience and the review agree on the practical point (look for root damage first, then periodontal extent) even though the 2017 grades have not been validated in the sources I read [Likely].

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: about 125 minutes)*

### 1. Introduction
The pulp and periodontium form a biological unit linked anatomically and through the vascular system (Gopikrishna, 2021; Glickman and Iacono, 2021). Disease of one can affect the other, and pulpal and periodontal problems together are said to account for more than half of tooth loss (Glickman and Iacono, 2021). Because either disease can mimic the other, accurate diagnosis is the basis of treatment planning.

**Definition (2017 World Workshop):** an endo-periodontal lesion is a pathological communication between the pulpal and periodontal tissues at a given tooth, acute or chronic (Herrera et al, 2018; Papapanou et al, 2018).

### 2. Endo-perio interrelationship
**A. Pathways**
| Type | Pathway | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Anatomical | Apical foramen | Main route (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) |
| | Lateral and accessory canals | 30 to 40% of teeth, mostly apical; only 2% related to pockets in 1000 periodontally diseased teeth (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Rotstein, 2017) |
| | Dentinal tubules | Open when cementum is denuded (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) |
| Developmental | Palatogingival groove, cervical enamel projection | Self-sustaining pocket (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) |
| Pathological | Perforation, vertical root fracture, external resorption | Worst prognosis (Herrera et al, 2018) |

**B. Pulp to periodontium**: necrotic pulp products cause apical, lateral or furcal bone loss and may drain through the sulcus; endodontic infection is a local modifying risk factor for periodontitis if untreated; infected canals give 20% more epithelial downgrowth in experimental defects (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, 2017).

**C. Periodontium to pulp**: controversial; 70% of roots unaffected despite 30 to 40% attachment loss in Bergenholtz and Lindhe's animal study; the pulp survives while the main apical vessels are intact and accessory canals are covered by cementum (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, 2017). Periodontal instrumentation may sever lateral canal vessels, and curetting around the apex severs the pulp supply (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**D. Aetiology that guides the diagnosis**
- Microorganisms: periodontal and endodontic pathogens are similar; spirochaetes (up to 10% in periapical and up to 60% in lateral periodontal abscesses in one report) can hint at origin but are not decisive (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
- Risk factors for an endo-periodontal lesion: advanced periodontitis, trauma and iatrogenic events; also grooves, furcation involvement, metal-ceramic crowns and active caries (Herrera et al, 2018).
- Iatrogenic and traumatic causes (perforation, fracture, resorption) must be searched for first, because they decide whether the tooth can be kept (Herrera et al, 2018).
- Coronal leakage and inadequate root canal treatment sustain the endodontic component (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

### 3. Classification (basis for diagnosis)
**Simon, Glick and Frank (1972)** (Gopikrishna, 2021): primary endodontic; primary endodontic with secondary periodontal; primary periodontal; primary periodontal with secondary endodontic; true combined; concomitant (Belk and Gutmann).

**2017 World Workshop** (Herrera et al, 2018):
- With root damage: root fracture or cracking; root canal or pulp chamber perforation; external root resorption.
- Without root damage: in periodontitis or non-periodontitis patients, each graded 1 (narrow deep pocket, one surface), 2 (wide deep pocket, one surface), 3 (deep pockets, more than one surface).

**Weine's clinical classification** (useful for planning) (Weine, 2004):
- Class I: simulates periodontal disease but is pulpal; endodontics only; heals rapidly with an excellent prognosis even in older patients.
- Class II: pulpal and periodontal disease together; once the pulp is damaged, periodontal treatment alone will not heal it.
- Class III: no pulpal disease, but endodontics plus root amputation is needed for periodontal healing.
- Class IV: simulates pulpal disease but is periodontal; improves briefly after endodontics, then relapses; very poor prognosis unless periodontal treatment is added.

### 3A. Diagnostic picture of each class
**Primary endodontic lesion**
- Non-vital pulp; caries, deep restoration or trauma history.
- Sinus tract through the ligament into the sulcus or furcation; narrow and deep, traced with a gutta-percha cone to the apex; may resemble a grade III furcation (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- A deep solitary pocket in a mouth without periodontal disease (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

**Primary endodontic lesion with secondary periodontal involvement**
- Necrotic pulp plus plaque and calculus in the long-standing sinus; angular defect at the site of drainage (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Look for perforations, misplaced posts or pins, and root fractures, which give the same picture (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

**Primary periodontal lesion**
- Vital pulp; generalised periodontitis; broad pockets becoming shallower as the probe moves laterally; plaque and calculus; mobility (Rotstein, 2017; Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

**Primary periodontal lesion with secondary endodontic involvement**
- History of long-standing periodontitis or periodontal treatment; deep pocket reaching the apex or a lateral canal; recent acute pulpal pain; pulp now non-vital (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Radiographically indistinguishable from the endodontic lesion with secondary periodontal involvement; the difference is the order of events (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

**True combined lesion**
- Two independent lesions that have joined; large attachment loss; non-vital pulp; plaque and calculus (Rotstein, 2017; Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Probes as a cone with a narrow drop at its base (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- A fracture into the pulp space can look identical but is untreatable; explore surgically if in doubt (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

**Concomitant lesion**
- A periapical lesion and a separate periodontal pocket that do not communicate on probing; often only one is diagnosed (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

### 4. Diagnosis
**Step 1: History**
- Pulpal pain history (thermal sensitivity, spontaneous pain), trauma, previous restorations, endodontic treatment or post placement (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014; Herrera et al, 2018).
- Periodontal history: bleeding gums, mobility, drifting, abscesses; risk factors such as smoking, stress and diabetes; motivation and compliance (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
- Acute pain is not a common feature, because the lesion is open to the mouth (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).

**Step 2: Clinical examination**
- Caries, large restorations, cracks, grooves, colour change, swelling, sinus tracts, mobility, recession, furcation involvement.
- Oral hygiene and calculus; generalised versus localised disease.

**Step 3: Pulp tests**
- Cold, heat and electric tests on the involved and adjacent teeth; test each root or cusp of a multirooted tooth separately (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
- Partial necrosis in multirooted teeth is the commonest diagnostic difficulty (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
- A large lesion implies at least one necrotic canal (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Step 4: Probing** (fine probe, continuous profile around the tooth; probing, not sounding) (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014)
| Pattern | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Normal sulcus all round | Excludes periodontal cause of any radiolucency |
| Narrow (about 1 mm), deep, isolated | Sinus tract: endodontic |
| Precipitous drop across a swelling | Blow-out endodontic abscess; reattaches within a week of endodontic treatment |
| Gradual cone | Periodontal |
| Cone with a narrow drop at its base | True combined |
| Narrow defects on buccal and lingual | Suggests vertical root fracture |
| Wide sinus-type defect on the palatal surface of a maxillary tooth | Will not resolve with root canal treatment |

**Special probing situations** (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002)
- A vertical sinus-type defect in a molar furcation closes after root canal treatment, but may take 2 to 3 months after obturation.
- A horizontal furcation component on a pulpless tooth resolves in young patients but rarely in older patients; judge it by periodontal criteria.
- A narrow lingual defect on a recently traumatised anterior tooth needs no treatment; it resolves over some months.
- A sinus can occasionally drain through the ligament of a tooth next to the pulpless one; pulp testing all neighbouring teeth reveals this.
- A sinus-type probing is a sign, not a disease: it also occurs with enamel spurs, developmental grooves, vertical root fractures and perforations.

**Step 5: Sinus tract tracing**: gutta-percha cone into the tract, radiograph to see where it points (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019). A sinus admits only a gutta-percha cone; an infrabony pocket admits wider instruments (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Step 6: Radiographs**
- Periapical views at more than one angle (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Bone loss from crest to apex does not reveal the cause (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- In generalised periodontitis, loss beyond two-thirds of the root suggests pulpal influence (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
- Look for lateral canals (notch, sealer extrusion), perforations, posts, resorption, grooves (teardrop radiolucency).

**Step 7: Exploratory flap** when a fracture is suspected or the cause remains unclear (Rotstein, 2017).

**Features suggesting origin** (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014)
| Primary endodontic | Primary periodontal |
|---|---|
| No generalised periodontal disease | Generalised periodontal disease |
| Adequate oral hygiene | Poor oral hygiene, calculus |
| Narrow, deep, isolated defect | Broad defects |
| Pulp non-vital | Pulp vital |

**Differential diagnosis (Grossman's Table 20.1)**
| Feature | Endodontic | Periodontal |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Acute, spontaneous | Dull, chronic |
| Swelling | Diffuse | Localised |
| Percussion | Vertical | Lateral, mild |
| Probing | Sulcus under 3 mm except at the sinus | Over 3 mm |
| Mobility | Rare, localised | Common, generalised |
| Junctional epithelium | Normal | Migrated apically |
| Gingiva | Normal | Inflamed, receded |
*(Gopikrishna, 2021)*

**Mimics to exclude**: vertical root fracture (hopeless when into the sulcus), palatogingival groove (teardrop radiolucency; scaling alone fails), cervical enamel projection, cemental tear, lateral periodontal cyst (under 1 cm, between apex and margin), and rarely systemic disease or malignancy (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019). A radicular groove may mimic a fracture radiographically (Herrera et al, 2018). Do not label every lesion "combined" (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

**2017 diagnostic sequence** (Herrera et al, 2018): first exclude root damage (fracture, perforation, resorption); then full-mouth periodontal assessment with vitality and percussion tests; a pocket reaching or near the apex with a non-vital pulp confirms an endo-periodontal lesion.

**Retrospective diagnosis**: the healing response to root canal treatment confirms the endodontic component (Rotstein, 2017).

### 5. Treatment planning
**A. Aims**: eliminate infection from the root canal and the root surface (identical goal, different procedures), restore attachment where possible, and keep a functional, maintainable tooth (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).

**B. Factors deciding the plan**
1. Pulp status: vital, partially necrotic, necrotic, previously treated (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
2. Type and extent of periodontal destruction: grade, number of surfaces, furcation (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Herrera et al, 2018).
3. Root damage: fracture, perforation, resorption (Herrera et al, 2018).
4. Restorability and strategic value of the tooth.
5. Root anatomy for resection: fused roots, long root trunk, short thin roots are unfavourable (Rotstein, 2017).
6. Patient: plaque control, motivation, smoking, diabetes (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014; Rotstein, 2017).
7. Effect on a future implant site (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).

**C. Keep or extract: prognostic groups** (Herrera et al, 2018)
| Group | Typical lesion | Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Hopeless | Root damage (fracture, extensive perforation) | Extract; implant or bridge |
| Poor | Advanced periodontitis, deep pockets on several surfaces | Retain only with resection or regeneration and strict maintenance |
| Favourable | No periodontitis, narrow pocket on one surface | Endodontic treatment, periodontal therapy if needed |

**D. Sequence of treatment**
1. Emergency: drain abscess, relieve pain.
2. Initial periodontal therapy: oral hygiene instruction, supragingival scaling.
3. Root canal treatment of the non-vital tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021).
4. Avoid aggressive root planing at this stage (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
5. Reassess at 2 to 3 months (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
6. Definitive periodontal therapy for the residual defect: subgingival debridement, then surgery, regeneration or resection.
7. Definitive restoration.
8. Maintenance and recall.

Reasons for endodontics first: endodontics is highly predictable; the periodontal component cannot heal while the endodontic lesion persists; periodontitis progresses slowly (Gopikrishna, 2021); drainage into the defect would interfere with periodontal repair (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**F. Adjunctive procedures in the plan**
- **Root resection, hemisection, bicuspidization**: for molars where one root is lost to periodontitis, fracture, perforation, resorption or an untreatable canal (Glickman and Iacono, 2021); endodontics first, as vital resected roots lose vitality (38% vital at 1 year, 13% at 5 years) (Glickman and Iacono, 2021); survival about 68% (Rotstein, 2017), 62% to 100% (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- **Resection or implant**: one textbook finds survival not substantially different (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); in periodontitis patients one study found 32% complications for hemisected molars against 11% for implants (Andreana, 2010).
- **Guided tissue regeneration and bone grafts**: GTR helps large through-and-through lesions (Kinaia et al, 2012); the combined lesion has the least favourable prognosis with GTR (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- **Perforation repair** with MTA; prognosis by size, location and time (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- **Orthodontic extrusion** for cervical defects; extrusions under 4 mm; retain twice as long as the extrusion took (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- **Palatogingival groove**: surgical elimination of the groove; extract if this fails; early diagnosis improves prognosis (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Rotstein, 2017).
- **Cracks into the root**: careful restoration may give up to 10 years' service, weighed against the implant site (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
- **Extraction** early when the prognosis is poor, to avoid compromising implant treatment (Rotstein, 2017).

**G. Reassessment and maintenance**
- At 2 to 3 months: probing depths, mobility, sinus closure, radiographic healing.
- Furcal sinus-type defects may need 2 to 3 months after obturation to close (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Long-term periodontal maintenance, since the prognosis of combined lesions depends on periodontal therapy (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

**H. Referral**: when the cause is unclear or treatment is complex, consult or refer (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

### 6. Conclusion
The pulp and periodontium communicate mainly through the apical foramen, lateral canals and exposed tubules. Diagnosis rests on pulp testing of each root, careful probing and radiographs, and is confirmed by the response to root canal treatment. Treatment planning first excludes root damage, then treats the endodontic component, reassesses, and treats the periodontal residue; the long-term prognosis depends mainly on the periodontal component.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Probe, then pulp test each root, then radiograph from two angles.
- Narrow probing is endodontic, conical probing is periodontal, cone plus narrow drop is combined.
- 2017 sequence: exclude root damage first.
- Endodontics first; reassess at 2 to 3 months; no aggressive root planing meanwhile.
- Healing after root canal treatment gives a retrospective diagnosis.
- Prognostic groups: hopeless (root damage), poor, favourable.
- Weine Class IV relapses after endodontics alone unless periodontal treatment is added.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Relying on a single pulp test of a multirooted tooth; test each root.
- Root planing before root canal treatment in a primary endodontic lesion.
- Doing root canal treatment on a vital tooth with a purely periodontal lesion.
- Not mentioning vertical root fracture and grooves in the differential diagnosis.
- Writing a plan without a reassessment interval.
