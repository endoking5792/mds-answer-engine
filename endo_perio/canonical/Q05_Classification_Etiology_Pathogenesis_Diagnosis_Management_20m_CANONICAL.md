## 1. Introduction

#### ✎ Exam core

An endo-periodontal lesion is a pathological communication between the pulpal and periodontal tissues of a tooth, acute or chronic (Herrera et al, 2018).

## 2. Classification

**Classification depends on pathogenesis.** Rotstein's classification is explicitly "based on how these lesions are formed" (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. Herrera's review criticises exactly this, because the history of formation is usually unknown at presentation (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Writing both, with this reason, is what distinguishes a good answer.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Simon, Glick and Frank (1972)**, based on origin and progression (Gopikrishna, 2021): primary endodontic; primary endodontic with secondary periodontal; primary periodontal; primary periodontal with secondary endodontic; true combined; concomitant (Belk and Gutmann).

**2017 World Workshop** (Herrera et al, 2018): with root damage (fracture or cracking, perforation, external resorption); without root damage, in periodontitis or non-periodontitis patients, grades 1 (narrow, one surface), 2 (wide, one surface), 3 (more than one surface).

## 3. Aetiology

**Aetiology: live and non-living.** Ingle's makes microorganisms the essential cause: Kakehashi's germ-free rats did not develop periapical lesions from exposed pulps, whereas conventional rats did (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. The organisms in canals and pockets overlap: PCR detected periodontal pathogens such as A. actinomycetemcomitans, T. forsythia, P. gingivalis, P. intermedia and T. denticola in all endodontic samples studied (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC], and Herrera's review found red and orange complex species in both sites with no microbial profile specific to the lesion (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Fungi, L-forms and herpesviruses are listed as further live agents (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. Seltzer and Bender's chapter adds the biofilm mode of growth (about 15% cells, 85% matrix) and the non-living agents: foreign bodies (dentine chips, filling materials, paper-point cellulose, food) and intrinsic agents (epithelium, cholesterol, Russell bodies, Charcot-Leyden crystals) that may explain some failures (Seltzer and Bender Ch.16, 2012) [SRC].

| Group | Factors |

- Main risk factors (2017 review): advanced periodontitis, trauma, iatrogenic events; grooves account for most single lesions in patients without periodontitis (Herrera et al, 2018).
- The longer the cause persists, the more likely combined therapy is needed (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 4. Pathogenesis

**Pathogenesis: why the direction matters.** In the pulp-to-periodontium direction, the infected canal is a closed reservoir; products reach the ligament through the foramen and lateral canals, and drainage through the ligament into the sulcus creates a narrow tract. Harty's stresses that this lesion is "only periodontal in that it passes through the periodontal ligament space" (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]. If left, plaque settles in the tract and a true marginal periodontitis is added (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]. In the other direction, plaque-induced loss of attachment exposes tubules and lateral canals; the pulp usually copes while its apical vessels are intact, and becomes necrotic when the pocket reaches the apex (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. A true combined lesion forms when an apically spreading pocket meets a coronally spreading endodontic lesion (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC].

**The pathways, and why their number overstates their importance.** Ingle's names three main pathways: dentinal tubules, lateral and accessory canals, and the apical foramen (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's adds neural (reflex) pathways, palatogingival grooves, the periodontal ligament, alveolar bone and vasculolymphatic drainage, and says the best-evidenced link is vascular (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]. Prevalence figures look high: Rubach and Mitchell found accessory canals in 45% of 74 serially sectioned teeth, Gutmann found furcation canals in 28% of 102 molars (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC], Ingle's estimates 30 to 40% of teeth have lateral or accessory canals, mostly apical (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC], and Gulabivala and Ng give a mean of about 45% from pooled studies (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Yet Kirkham found only 2% of lateral canals associated with periodontal pockets, in 1000 teeth with extensive periodontal disease (Rotstein, 2017; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. The lesson is that anatomy permits communication; it does not make it common [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pathways**: apical foramen, lateral and accessory canals (30 to 40% of teeth), dentinal tubules (exposed at the CEJ in about 18% of teeth), palatogingival grooves; the vascular link is the best evidenced (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

**A. Pulp to periodontium**
- Pulp necrosis, then infection, then products exit through the foramen and lateral canals.
- Inflammation and bone resorption apically, laterally or in the furcation; drainage through the ligament gives a narrow tract into the sulcus, a "reverse pocket" (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Untreated: plaque and calculus in the tract cause secondary marginal periodontitis.
- Infected canals promote epithelial downgrowth on denuded dentine (20% more epithelium experimentally) (Rotstein, 2017).

**B. Periodontium to pulp**
- Plaque, then loss of attachment, then exposure of tubules and lateral canals.
- Pulp shows inflammation, fibrosis or calcification but survives while the main apical vessels are intact (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- When the pocket reaches the apex, the pulp becomes necrotic (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Periodontal instruments can sever lateral-canal vessels (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

**C. True combined**: an endodontic lesion spreading coronally joins a pocket spreading apically; less common (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

**D. Developmental groove**: once the epithelial attachment is breached, a self-sustaining infrabony pocket forms along the groove (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

**E. Iatrogenic and traumatic**: perforation, fracture or resorption opens a new, usually large communication; these lesions carry the worst prognosis (Herrera et al, 2018).

## 5. Diagnosis

**Reading the probe.** Walton and Torabinejad's chapter is the most practical source. Periodontal disease starts at the crest, so a periodontal lesion probes as a cone; a sinus tract probes as a narrow (about 1 mm) defect with normal depths 1 mm either side; a "blow-out" lesion drops precipitously over the width of a swelling and reattaches within a week after endodontic treatment; a true combined lesion probes as a cone with a sinus-type drop at its base (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Probing must be distinguished from sounding, which goes through the attachment to bone (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng warn that a lateral endodontic abscess can produce a wide and deep "blow-out" profile yet heal with endodontics alone (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC], so width alone does not prove periodontal origin.

#### ✎ Exam core

- Pulp tests on each root (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
- Probing: narrow (about 1 mm) is endodontic; conical is periodontal; cone with a narrow drop is combined (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Gutta-percha tracing of the sinus; radiographs at two angles (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Exclude root fracture, perforation and grooves (teardrop radiolucency) (Herrera et al, 2018; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Healing after root canal treatment confirms the endodontic component (Rotstein, 2017).

## 6. Management protocol

**Why endodontics first.** Grossman's gives three reasons: endodontic treatment is predictable, the periodontal component cannot resolve while the endodontic lesion persists, and periodontitis is slow (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's adds timing and a biological mechanism: reassess after 2 to 3 months, and avoid aggressive removal of ligament and cementum in the interim because it harms periodontal healing (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad's reason is drainage: a sinus into the periodontal defect interferes with repair (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Grossman's notes that some clinicians start with whichever disease came first, or prepare and disinfect the canal, do the periodontal treatment, then obturate (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng add a caution in the other direction: periodontists who ask for root canal treatment on teeth of doubtful pulp status before regenerative surgery must remember that extruded root filling material can harm the outcome as much as the infection would (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

1. Root damage present: usually extraction; resect the affected root or repair a perforation where feasible (Herrera et al, 2018).
2. Vital pulp, periodontal lesion: periodontal therapy only (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
3. Non-vital pulp: root canal treatment first, whatever the origin (Gopikrishna, 2021).
4. No aggressive root planing in the interim (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
5. Reassess at 2 to 3 months; treat any residual periodontal defect (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
6. Poor response: root resection or hemisection in molars, GTR for large through-and-through defects, or extraction (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Kinaia et al, 2012).
- Prognosis: primary endodontic excellent; combined lesions depend mainly on periodontal therapy (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim: endodontic and periodontal lesions share a common microbiota.** Textbook (Rotstein): periodontal pathogens were found in all endodontic samples of a PCR study (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]. Newer evidence (Herrera's review of culture, PCR, checkerboard and sequencing studies): great similarity, no lesion-specific profile, though few studies exist (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Gulabivala and Ng add a refinement: species may match while strains differ, reflecting the "open" pocket versus the "closed" canal, and spirochaete proportions differ (up to 10% in periapical, up to 60% in lateral periodontal abscesses) (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Conclusion: similarity at species level is consistent; microbiology cannot yet be used to diagnose origin [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Germ-free animals: no periapical lesion without bacteria (Kakehashi).
- Canal and pocket share a similar microbiota; no lesion-specific profile.
- Pulp survives periodontitis while the apical vessels are intact.
- Combined: coronal endodontic spread meets apical periodontal spread.
- Simon (origin) versus 2017 (root damage, periodontitis, grade).

- Omitting the 2017 classification.
