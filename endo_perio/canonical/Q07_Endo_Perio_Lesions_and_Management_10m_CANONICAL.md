## Explanation

A 10m answer has room for one classification, one management table and the sequence of treatment. What earns the marks here: naming both Simon's and the 2017 classification, stating "endodontics first, reassess in 2 to 3 months" (Gopikrishna, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC], and giving a prognosis for each class. The commonest error is root planing a primary endodontic lesion, which fails if the necrotic pulp is missed (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC].

**Why the classification has changed.** Simon, Glick and Frank (1972) classified by origin and progression, with the concomitant lesion added by Belk and Gutmann (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Rotstein groups them as endodontic, periodontal and combined, saying the classification is based on how the lesions form (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. Oliet and Pollock classified by treatment need (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Weine by clinical presentation and therapy (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng are blunt that none of the classifications especially contributes to diagnosis or treatment (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Herrera's review made the same criticism formally: history of the disease is usually unavailable, and the primary source is not relevant to treatment because both tissues need treating; the 1999 single category "combined periodontal-endodontic lesions" under "periodontitis associated with endodontic lesions" was too generic and wrongly implied periodontitis (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Hence the 2017 table, based on root damage, periodontitis status and pocket extent (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT].

**Why endodontics first.** Grossman's gives three reasons: endodontic treatment is predictable, the periodontal component cannot resolve while the endodontic lesion persists, and periodontitis is slow (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's adds timing and a biological mechanism: reassess after 2 to 3 months, and avoid aggressive removal of ligament and cementum in the interim because it harms periodontal healing (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. Walton and Torabinejad's reason is drainage: a sinus into the periodontal defect interferes with repair (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Grossman's notes that some clinicians start with whichever disease came first, or prepare and disinfect the canal, do the periodontal treatment, then obturate (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng add a caution in the other direction: periodontists who ask for root canal treatment on teeth of doubtful pulp status before regenerative surgery must remember that extruded root filling material can harm the outcome as much as the infection would (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** An endo-periodontal lesion is a pathological communication between the pulpal and periodontal tissues of a tooth, through the apical foramen, lateral canals or dentinal tubules (Herrera et al, 2018; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

**1. Classification**
- **Simon, Glick and Frank (1972)** (Gopikrishna, 2021): primary endodontic; primary endodontic with secondary periodontal; primary periodontal; primary periodontal with secondary endodontic; true combined; concomitant (Belk and Gutmann).
- **2017 World Workshop** (Herrera et al, 2018): with root damage (fracture, perforation, external resorption); without root damage, in periodontitis or non-periodontitis patients, grades 1 to 3 by pocket width and number of surfaces.

**2. Diagnosis**
- Pulp tests: non-vital in endodontic and combined lesions, vital in primary periodontal lesions.
- Probing: narrow sinus-type defect traced with a gutta-percha cone to the apex in endodontic lesions; broad conical pockets in periodontal lesions (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Radiographs; exclude vertical root fracture, which is usually hopeless when it reaches the sulcus (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

**3. Management principles**
- Endodontic treatment first, regardless of cause (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Reassess after 2 to 3 months, then periodontal therapy (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Avoid aggressive root planing in the interim (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

**4. Management of each class**
| Class | Treatment | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| Primary endodontic | RCT only | Excellent |
| 1° endo, 2° perio | RCT, then periodontal therapy | Good if periodontal therapy succeeds; RCT alone heals only part |
| Primary periodontal | Periodontal therapy only | Depends on periodontal therapy |
| 1° perio, 2° endo | RCT, then periodontal therapy; root resection in molars | Poor in single-rooted teeth |
| True combined | RCT, then periodontal therapy, regeneration or resection | Guarded |
| Root damage | Usually extraction | Hopeless |
*(Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Rotstein, 2017; Herrera et al, 2018)*

**5. Adjuncts**: root resection, hemisection or bicuspidization for molars (Glickman and Iacono, 2021); GTR for large through-and-through defects (Kinaia et al, 2012); extraction and implant if hopeless.

**Conclusion.** The endodontic component heals predictably; the prognosis of combined lesions depends mainly on the periodontal component (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Simon (1972) plus 2017 classification.
- Endodontics first; reassess at 2 to 3 months.
- Primary endodontic: excellent prognosis with RCT alone.
