## Explanation

**Terms.** Harty's separates three procedures: root amputation removes one or more roots and leaves the crown largely intact; tooth resection (hemisection) removes a root with its part of the crown; bicuspidization separates a multirooted tooth through the furcation and keeps both halves (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's defines root resection as removal of a root with odontoplasty, preferably after endodontic treatment, and now restricts it to multirooted teeth where one or more roots cannot be saved (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]. Kinaia et al place root amputation among the early periodontal surgical treatments that healed by repair (long junctional epithelium) rather than regeneration (Kinaia et al, 2012) [SRC].

**Why an "opinion" question needs evidence on both sides.** The case for: it keeps a natural tooth when one root is lost to periodontitis, fracture, perforation, resorption or an untreatable canal (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]. The case against: it is technique-sensitive, and the remaining roots are at risk of fracture (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]. The outcome figures do not agree (see synthesis).

**Root resection versus implant.** The evidence disagrees, so it must be shown side by side.
- *Cohen's*: retrospective studies of 3 to 12 years report 62% to 100% success, with endodontic and restorative failure, not periodontal breakdown, the main cause of failure (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC].
- *Harty's*: anatomical redesigning has a survival of about 68%; one 10-year study of 100 patients lost 38 teeth, 47% of them by root fracture (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC].
- *Walton and Torabinejad*: survival of root-resected teeth is not substantially different from osseointegrated implants (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].
- *Andreana (DCNA)*: in periodontitis patients, 32% of hemisected mandibular molars had complications against 11% of implants; another study of 691 molars had a 30% failure rate over 10 years (Andreana, 2010) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definitions**
- **Root amputation (radisection)**: removal of one or more roots of a multirooted tooth, leaving most of the crown (Rotstein, 2017).
- **Hemisection (tooth resection)**: removal of a root with its portion of the crown (Rotstein, 2017; Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- **Bicuspidization**: vertical separation through the furcation, retaining both halves (Rotstein, 2017).

**Indications** (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, 2017)
- Severe bone loss or grade II/III furcation involvement affecting one root
- Root fracture, perforation, resorption or caries of one root
- A canal that cannot be treated (calcified, blocked) where root-end surgery is not feasible
- Close root proximity

**Contraindications** (Rotstein, 2017; Glickman and Iacono, 2021)
- Poor plaque control, unmotivated patient
- Inadequate bone support of the remaining roots
- Fused roots; short, thin roots; long root trunk
- Non-restorable tooth

**Procedure** (Rotstein, 2017; Glickman and Iacono, 2021)
1. Root canal treatment of the roots to be kept; seal the chamber into the orifice of the root to be removed.
2. Full-thickness flap; check the furcation.
3. Section the root with a long bur held at about 45 degrees at the level of the furcation; elevate it.
4. Reshape the stump so no ledge or food trap remains; narrow the occlusal table.
5. Re-evaluate the periodontium after about 3 months; definitive restoration.
- Commonest site: the distobuccal root of the maxillary first molar (Rotstein, 2017).
- If done on a vital tooth, root canal treatment should follow quickly: only 38% of vital resected roots stayed vital at 1 year and 13% at 5 years (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).

**Outcome**
- Success 62% to 100% over 3 to 12 years; failure mainly endodontic or restorative (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Survival about 68%; root fracture is a common cause of failure (Rotstein, 2017).
- Survival not substantially different from implants (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); in periodontitis patients, more complications than implants (32% against 11%) (Andreana, 2010).

**Opinion**
Root amputation is a worthwhile, tooth-preserving treatment for a molar with one irretrievable root, provided the remaining roots have good support, the tooth is restorable and the patient maintains good plaque control. It is technique-sensitive, and outcome depends more on case selection and on the quality of the endodontic and restorative work than on the surgery itself. Where the prognosis is poor, early extraction protects a future implant site (Rotstein, 2017).

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim: root amputation is a reliable alternative to extraction and implant.** Cohen's reports 62% to 100% success over 3 to 12 years, with endodontic and restorative failures as the main cause (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's gives about 68% survival and a 10-year study in which 47% of 38 failures were root fractures (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC]; Walton and Torabinejad find survival not substantially different from implants (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; Andreana reports more complications than implants in periodontitis patients (32% against 11%) and 30% failure of 691 molars over 10 years (Andreana, 2010) [SRC]. All are retrospective; the spread reflects case selection and patient periodontal status, so no source supports ranking one approach above the other in general [Likely]. My opinion follows from this: a valid option for a carefully selected molar in a patient with good plaque control, not a default.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Amputation keeps the crown; hemisection removes root and crown portion.
- Endodontics first; vital resected roots lose vitality.
- 62% to 100% success; root fracture common cause of failure.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Using "root amputation" and "hemisection" as synonyms.
- Omitting endodontic treatment before resection.
- Giving an opinion without evidence for and against.
