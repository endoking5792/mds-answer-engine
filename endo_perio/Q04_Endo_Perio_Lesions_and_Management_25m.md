## STUDY MODE
*(Pathways, the two-way effects and the classification debate are explained in Q01's Study Mode; diagnostic reasoning in Q02's. This section only adds what a 25m management answer must get right.)*

**The management logic in one line.** Treat each diseased tissue for its own cause; when the pulp is non-vital, treat the canal first and let the response show how much of the lesion was endodontic. Ingle's states the two extremes plainly: primary endodontic disease needs only endodontic therapy, primary periodontal disease only periodontal therapy (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. Everything else is a combination, and its prognosis depends mainly on the periodontal component (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC].

**Two errors examiners look for.** First, root planing a primary endodontic lesion: Harty's warns that periodontal treatment alone fails if the necrotic pulp is missed (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC], and Ingle's that aggressive removal of ligament and cementum during the interim harms periodontal healing (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019) [SRC]. Second, treating only one disease in a concomitant lesion; Cohen's says this situation often goes undiagnosed (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC].

**When to stop trying to save the tooth.** Herrera's prognostic grouping (hopeless, poor, favourable) puts root damage first: fracture, perforation and external resorption usually carry the worst prognosis (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Cohen's adds that a vertical root fracture reaching the sulcus is usually hopeless because bacteria keep entering the fracture (Glickman and Iacono, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's advises early extraction when the prognosis is poor, to avoid compromising an implant (Rotstein, 2017) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim: endodontic treatment first, periodontal treatment after 2 to 3 months.** All the textbooks agree on the order (Gopikrishna, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and the 2 to 3 month interval comes from Rotstein's chapters (Ingle's, Harty's), which share an author, so it is one view repeated, not two independent ones [Likely]. The supporting evidence is biological and from animal and case studies; the 2017 review rated the evidence on these lesions as limited (Herrera et al, 2018) [LIT]. Walton and Torabinejad add that some furcal sinus defects need 2 to 3 months after obturation to close (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], which explains the interval. Conclusion: a sound consensus sequence, not a trial-proven one.

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: about 42 minutes)*

### 1. Introduction
The pulp and periodontium are one biological unit, communicating mainly through the apical foramen, lateral and accessory canals and dentinal tubules (Gopikrishna, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019). An endo-periodontal lesion is a pathological communication between the two at a given tooth, acute or chronic (Herrera et al, 2018).

### 2. Classification
**Simon, Glick and Frank (1972)**, based on origin (Gopikrishna, 2021):
1. Primary endodontic lesion
2. Primary endodontic with secondary periodontal involvement
3. Primary periodontal lesion
4. Primary periodontal with secondary endodontic involvement
5. True combined lesion
6. Concomitant lesion (added by Belk and Gutmann)

**2017 World Workshop** (Herrera et al, 2018):
- **With root damage**: root fracture or cracking; root canal or pulp chamber perforation; external root resorption.
- **Without root damage**, in periodontitis or non-periodontitis patients: grade 1 narrow deep pocket on one surface; grade 2 wide deep pocket on one surface; grade 3 deep pockets on more than one surface.

### 3. Clinical features
| Lesion | Pulp | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Primary endodontic | Non-vital | Narrow sinus-type pocket traced to the apex with a gutta-percha cone; "periodontal" only in passing through the ligament |
| 1° endo, 2° perio | Non-vital | Plaque and calculus collected in the long-standing sinus |
| Primary periodontal | Vital | Broad conical pockets, calculus, generalised bone loss |
| 1° perio, 2° endo | Non-vital | Long-standing periodontitis reaching the apex or a lateral canal; recent acute pulpal pain |
| True combined | Non-vital | Two lesions merged; large attachment loss; cone with a narrow drop at its base |
| Concomitant | Non-vital | Separate, non-communicating lesions |
*(Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, 2017; Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002)*

### 4. Diagnosis
- **Pulp tests** on each root (Gulabivala, Darbar and Ng, 2014).
- **Probing pattern**: narrow, about 1 mm wide, means a sinus tract (endodontic); conical means periodontal; a cone with a narrow drop at its base means a true combined lesion (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- **Sinus tracing** with a gutta-percha cone (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- **Radiographs** from more than one angle (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- **Exclude** vertical root fracture, palatogingival groove (teardrop radiolucency) and perforation (Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- **Retrospective confirmation** by healing after root canal treatment (Rotstein, 2017).

### 5. Management
**A. Principles**
- Endodontic treatment first, regardless of cause: it is predictable, the periodontal component cannot heal while the endodontic lesion persists, and periodontitis is slow (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Reassess at 2 to 3 months, then plan periodontal therapy (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- No aggressive root planing in the interim (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- Sinus drainage into the defect would interfere with periodontal repair (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**B. Management of each lesion**
| Lesion | Treatment | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| Primary endodontic | Root canal treatment only; no curettage or flap | Excellent; complete resolution expected |
| 1° endo, 2° perio | RCT, reassess, then scaling and root planing or surgery | Depends on severity of the periodontal damage |
| Primary periodontal | Periodontal therapy only | Depends wholly on periodontal therapy |
| 1° perio, 2° endo | RCT, then long-term periodontal therapy; root resection in molars | Poor in single-rooted teeth |
| True combined | RCT, then periodontal therapy, regeneration, radisection or hemisection | Guarded |
| Concomitant | Treat both independently | Depends on each |
| Root damage | Usually extraction; resection of the affected root; perforation repair | Usually hopeless |
*(Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019; Glickman and Iacono, 2021; Rotstein, 2017; Herrera et al, 2018)*

### 6. Adjunctive procedures
- **Root amputation (radisection)**: removal of a root, leaving most of the crown; **hemisection**: removal of a root with its part of the crown; **bicuspidization**: separation through the furcation into two premolar-like units (Rotstein, 2017; Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Endodontics first whenever possible: only 38% of vital resected roots remained vital at 1 year and 13% at 5 years (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- Outcome: 62% to 100% success (Glickman and Iacono, 2021); survival about 68% (Rotstein, 2017).
- **GTR and bone grafts**: GTR helps large through-and-through lesions, not small confined ones (Kinaia et al, 2012); combined lesions have the least favourable prognosis with GTR (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- **Perforation repair** with MTA; prognosis depends on size, location and time to repair (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- **Orthodontic extrusion** for cervical defects, usually under 4 mm (Glickman and Iacono, 2021).
- **Palatogingival groove**: scaling and root planing alone will not work; surgical elimination of the groove, extraction if it fails (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).
- **Extraction and implant** when hopeless; extract early if the prognosis is poor (Rotstein, 2017).

### 7. Prognosis
The endodontic component heals predictably; the prognosis of combined lesions depends mainly on periodontal therapy, and true combined lesions have the most guarded prognosis (Rotstein, Zabalegui and Al-Hezaimi, 2019).

### 8. Conclusion
Successful management depends on correct diagnosis: endodontic treatment first, reassessment, then periodontal therapy for what remains, with resection, regeneration or extraction for teeth that do not respond.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Simon (1972) five classes plus concomitant (Belk and Gutmann).
- 2017: root damage, periodontitis status, grades 1 to 3.
- Endodontics first; reassess at 2 to 3 months.
- Primary endodontic: excellent prognosis with RCT alone.
- True combined: guarded; prognosis depends on periodontal therapy.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Root planing before root canal treatment in a primary endodontic lesion.
- Root canal treatment on a vital tooth with a purely periodontal lesion.
- Missing the concomitant lesion and the 2017 classification.
- Writing "combined lesion" for every deep pocket on a non-vital tooth.
- No reassessment interval in the treatment plan.
