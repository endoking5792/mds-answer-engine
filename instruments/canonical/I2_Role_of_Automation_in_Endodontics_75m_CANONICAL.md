## 1. Definition and scope of automation

### Explanation

Sikri defines automated devices as instruments which revolve fully or partially, or reciprocate, around an axis and work at higher speeds, and divides them into three variants: reciprocating (partial rotation or back-and-forth movement, for example Giromatic and Canal Finder), vibratory (ultrasonic and sonic) and rotary (continuous rotation at a fixed speed, for example ProFile, LightSpeed, RaCe) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The rotation or reciprocation is delivered by a handpiece that receives its energy from an engine-driven motor (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Jain describes the first endodontic handpiece as developed more than 100 years ago to reduce treatment time and simplify preparation (Jain, 2016) [SRC]; Baumann and Beer date the first engine-driven handpiece to 1899, by Rollins (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Automated devices are instruments that revolve fully or partially, or reciprocate around an axis, at higher speed than hand instruments (Sikri, 2019).

**Three types** (Sikri, 2019)
1. *Reciprocating*: partial rotation or back-and-forth movement (Giromatic, Canal Finder, Endo-Pulse).
2. *Vibratory*: ultrasonic (Cavi-Endo) and sonic (MM 1500) (see I8).
3. *Rotary*: continuous rotation at a fixed speed (ProFile, LightSpeed, RaCe).

**Origins.** The first endodontic handpiece was developed more than 100 years ago to reduce treatment time and simplify preparation (Jain, 2016); Rollins built the first engine-driven handpiece in 1899, but early handpieces allowed only limited speed and direction (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Automation is therefore not new; what changed it was the instrument alloy, and NiTi made continuous rotation workable (Jain, 2016).

**Why automate.** To shorten preparation time, centre the preparation, reduce operator fatigue and extrude less debris apically, at the price of reduced tactile sensation (Sikri, 2019).

**Components.** A motor (energy), a handpiece that transmits and may modify speed and torque by gears, and the instrument (Sikri, 2019; Jain, 2016). A handpiece holds the rotating instrument, transmits power, and positions it intraorally; gears modify its speed and torque (Jain, 2016).

## 2. Historical development of automation

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer record Rollins's first engine-driven handpiece in 1899; early handpieces allowed only limited motion in speed and direction. Later systems added clutch mechanisms that changed the motion as friction increased, intended to reduce fracture, but with stainless-steel instruments the consequences very often included ledging, perforation or fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The Racer, introduced in the 1950s, was the first engine-driven handpiece to achieve a significant market share, but it allowed only vertical motion and there were frequent reports of wedged and fractured instruments (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The Giromatic, from 1964, was widely accepted: its reciprocal 90-degree rotary motion made rapid, simple instrumentation possible (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The Endolift combined vertical motion with reciprocal rotation; the Excalibur combined aleatoric oscillations with variable-amplitude vertical motion; the Endoplaner combined filing and rotary motion; the Canal-Finder delivered vertical motion with free rotation as friction increased; the IMD 9X gave 360-degree rotation with reduced speed as friction rose; and the ENDOflash was the first to control a torque-dependent rotational speed from within the handpiece (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer conclude that engine-driven systems for stainless steel instruments were inadequate in safety and final canal shape, and that conventional motor systems with a rigid range of motion are no longer recommended, although more modern systems with a flexible range of motion and partially integrated irrigation clean markedly better (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

NiTi changed the picture. Jain notes that the discovery of NiTi allowed a steadily accelerating development of files first designed for hand use, and that the old idea of 360-degree rotation combined with the new alloy met with great success (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer date this new era to 1988 and describe its main advantage as excellent instrumentation of severely curved canals with considerable time saving (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Peters et al add that engine-driven stainless-steel instruments had been used for more than half a century, at first mainly in handpieces that allowed reciprocation (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC], and that simple electric motors of the first generation appeared in the early 1990s (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Timeline** (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016; Peters et al, 2021)
| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| 1899 or 1889 | Rollins: first engine-driven endodontic handpiece (1899 in Baumann and Beer, 2010; 1889 in Ingle, year not printed) |
| 1950s | Racer: purely vertical motion; wedging and fracture of instruments |
| 1964 | Giromatic: reciprocal 90-degree rotary motion, widely accepted |
| after the Giromatic (dates not given by Baumann and Beer) | Endolift (vertical plus reciprocal rotation); Excalibur (aleatoric oscillation with variable vertical motion); Endoplaner (filing plus rotation); Canal-Finder (vertical stroke with free rotation as friction rises); Canal-Leader; IMD 9X (360-degree rotation, slower with friction); Jain places the Canal Finder and Canal Leader in the 1980s (Jain, 2016) |
| ENDOflash | torque-dependent speed controlled in the handpiece |
| 1988 | NiTi instruments; 360-degree rotation with a slow, torque-controlled motor |
| Early 1990s | first-generation electric motors for rotary NiTi |

**Description of the early systems** (Baumann and Beer, 2010)
- *Early handpieces*: only limited speed and direction. Later models combined rotary and up-and-down motion and had a clutch that changed the motion once friction in the canal increased, to reduce fracture.
- *Racer (1950s)*: the first handpiece to gain a significant market advantage. Purely vertical motion, with frequent wedging and fracture of instruments.
- *Giromatic (1964)*: reciprocal 90-degree rotary motion, rapid and simple instrumentation, widely accepted; the motion was adopted by other systems.
- *Endolift (Kerr)*: vertical motion combined with reciprocal rotation.
- *Excalibur*: aleatoric oscillation with variable-amplitude vertical motion.
- *Endoplaner*: filing (vertical) with rotary motion.
- *Canal-Finder*: vertical motion with free rotation as intracanal friction increases; *Canal-Leader*: similar with more limited vertical and partial rotary movement.
- *IMD 9X*: 360-degree rotation with speed reduced as friction rises.
- *ENDOflash*: the first with torque-dependent rotational speed controlled in the handpiece.

**The change of 1988.** NiTi instruments introduced by Walia et al began a new era; fully rotating (360-degree) power-driven NiTi files gave excellent instrumentation of severely curved canals with considerable time saving, and also a high risk of fracture, hence torque-limited power (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Verdict on the early systems.** Stainless steel with rigid motion gave ledges, perforations and fracture; such "classic" systems have virtually no role today (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Systems with flexible motion and integrated irrigation clean markedly better (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 3. Handpieces, including the Giromatic

### Explanation

Sikri describes three contra-angle handpiece types: a full rotary one (latch or friction grip), a reciprocating or quarter-turn one, and a special handpiece that adds a vertical stroke to a quarter-turn (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The Giromatic rotates the instrument a quarter-turn in alternate directions at up to 3,000 cycles a minute, using Girofiles and Girobroaches; the manufacturers claimed fast working but studies observed little difference from hand files, and the loss of tactile sensation may lead to zipping, over-instrumentation and breakage (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The M4 Safety handpiece (300 reciprocating motions) and the Endo-grapper (10:1 gear ratio) are modified versions of the Giromatic (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The Canal Finder handpiece runs the file in a 0.4 mm up-and-down motion with an additional full turn when pressure is applied, with continuous irrigation and an A-file, and vertical reciprocation is said to reduce file separation and torque strain, and to avoid the need for NiTi files (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Endo-Eze is a hybrid system combining stainless steel and NiTi instruments in a reciprocating or oscillating motion for curved, ribbon-shaped canals (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Uses of the Giromatic handpiece** (Sikri, 2019)
- Rotates the instrument a quarter-turn in alternate directions, up to 3,000 cycles a minute.
- Used with Girofiles and Girobroaches.
- Claimed to be quicker, but studies found little difference from hand files.
- Limitations: loss of tactile sensation, with zipping, over-instrumentation and instrument breakage.
- Modified versions: M4 Safety handpiece (300 reciprocating motions) and the Endo-grapper (10:1 gear ratio).

**Three contra-angle handpieces** (Sikri, 2019)
1. Full rotary, latch or friction grip: straight-line drilling and side cutting; burs and diamonds for coronal access.
2. Reciprocating (quarter-turn): accepts only latch-type instruments; the M4 Safety handpiece has a chuck that locks ordinary hand files by their handle and is recommended for Safety Hedström files.
3. Vertical stroke (Canal Finder): a vertical stroke of 0.3–1.0 mm whose depth depends on the free movement of the file in the canal, plus a quarter-turn reciprocation.

**Other handpieces** (Sikri, 2019)
- Full rotary handpiece (latch or friction grip): drilling and side cutting, and coronal access.
- Canal Finder: 0.4 mm vertical stroke with a full turn under pressure; A-file; continuous irrigation; also used for obturation and removal of broken instruments; vertical reciprocation reduces file separation.
- Endo-Eze: hybrid system for curved or flattened canals.

## 4. Advantages and disadvantages of automation

### Explanation

Sikri lists these advantages: significantly quicker preparation, a smooth well-centred canal shape, better removal of smear layer, less debris extruded apically, and less operator fatigue; and these disadvantages: lack of tactile sensation, susceptibility to perforation and ledges, and susceptibility to file breakage (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer describe the same trade-off for NiTi: excellent instrumentation of severely curved canals with savings in time, but a high risk of fracture compared with hand stainless-steel files, which makes torque-limited power necessary (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Jain adds that a continuous rotating movement results in less straightening of the canal (Jain, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Advantages (Sikri, 2019) | Disadvantages (Sikri, 2019) |
|---|---|
| Significantly quicker | Loss of tactile sensation |
| Smooth, well-centred canal | Perforations and ledges possible |
| Better smear layer removal | File breakage |
| Less debris extruded apically | |
| Less operator fatigue | |

**Explanation for the answer book.** Automated devices cut faster than hand files, make a smoother and better-centred canal, remove more smear layer and extrude less debris apically, and tire the operator less (Sikri, 2019). Against this, the clinician loses tactile feedback, so the risk of perforation, ledge formation and instrument breakage rises (Sikri, 2019). In the early stainless-steel systems this risk produced ledges, perforations and fractures (Baumann and Beer, 2010), and Sikri's account of the Giromatic is that the claimed gain in speed was not found when tested against hand files (Sikri, 2019).

NiTi rotary: excellent shaping of severely curved canals and considerable saving of time, with the price of a high fracture risk (Baumann and Beer, 2010); less canal straightening with continuous rotation (Jain, 2016).

## 5. Speed and torque

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer state that NiTi must be run at a low constant speed, usually about 300 rpm, and that torque, as well as speed, affects the risk of fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. They define torque as force times lever arm, in N·cm, and report that the breakage torque of the instrument tip is measured 3 mm from the tip (D3), and that in many cases the working torque exceeds this limit (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Torque rises with instrument diameter and with the dentin contact area, and crown-down instrumentation reduces contact and so torque (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Jain agrees that the torque generated depends on factors, especially the contact area, and concludes that slow-speed, high-torque NiTi rotation leads to many iatrogenic errors, with the ideal being slow speed and low torque or, preferably, "right" torque, because each instrument has a specific ideal torque (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Peters et al report that torque-controlled motors are thought by some authors to increase operational safety but by others to benefit mainly inexperienced clinicians; they probably do not reduce cyclic fatigue fracture, and fracture at D2 remains possible even if torque is below the D3 limit (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer, in contrast, report that torque limitation leads to less loading and better longevity of NiTi files (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC], and Jain states that motors that stop and reverse at the set torque mean that instrument failure could be avoided (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. The accounts therefore differ: Baumann and Beer and Jain credit torque control with fewer fractures, whereas Peters et al doubt it. A possible reconciliation is that torque control limits torsional overload but not cyclic fatigue [Likely]; no book states this as a conclusion.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Speed.** A low constant speed, usually about 300 rpm, is advised for NiTi (Baumann and Beer, 2010); Peters et al give system-specific speeds of 300 to 600 rpm and note for Vortex that a higher rotational speed results in less torque generated (Peters et al, 2021).

**Torque.** Rotational force, force times lever arm (N·cm). Torque needed rises with the instrument diameter and the contact area between file and dentin (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016). Crown-down instrumentation reduces contact and so torque (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Ideal setting.** Slow speed with low or "right" torque, because every instrument has its own ideal torque; slow speed with high torque leads to many iatrogenic errors (Jain, 2016).

**Why speed and torque matter.** The torque needed to turn a file increases with its diameter and with the area of dentin it contacts, and that area depends on the taper and the instrumentation sequence (Jain, 2016). In straight canals, where resistance to dentin removal is low, the high torque a motor can provide may fracture a blocked instrument before the clinician can stop or retract it (Jain, 2016). If the tip lodges while the shank is driven, torsional overload follows (Baumann and Beer, 2010). To avoid the instrument becoming stuck and fracturing, instrumentation is done at a low constant speed, usually about 300 rpm (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Breakage torque** is measured 3 mm from the tip (D3) and often lower than the working torque (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Value of torque control: opinions differ**
- Torque limitation reduces loading and improves file life (Baumann and Beer, 2010); motors that stop and reverse at the set torque mean that instrument failure could be avoided (Jain, 2016).
- Some authors say torque-controlled motors increase operational safety; others that they mainly benefit inexperienced clinicians, and probably do not reduce cyclic fatigue fracture; fracture at D2 is still possible (Peters et al, 2021).

## 6. Motors and torque control

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer describe torque-limited handpieces, which interrupt rotation the instant that frictional resistance exceeds the fracture limit: the Anthogyr NiTi Control has a friction clutch with four-step regulation, and the SIRONiTi a magnetic clutch with five levels (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Because files differ in strength, they are grouped, and the weakest file in a group sets the torque (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Torque-control micromotors, some computer-controlled step-motors, control torque in the motor; the TCM Endo, TriAuto ZX and Quantec ETM have step-specific torque, while the EndoStepper and Endo IT Control program the torque for the specific file, with auto-reverse or twisting to release a blocked file (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Jain groups motors into four generations: first, no torque control; second, fully electronic with a sensitive torque limiter; third, simple torque-controlled; fourth, with built-in apex locator and torque control. Torque limits stop the motor and reverse rotation at the set torque. The latest development is gear systems in the handpiece that regulate torque by file size (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Peters et al add that electric motors with gear reduction ensure constant rpm and torque, can be programmed for alternative patterns such as reciprocation with chosen angles, and can deliver torques far above those that break tips (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer also describe integration of electronic apex localisation, so that working length is shown continuously during power-driven instrumentation, and systems in which the motor parameters adjust automatically to the file (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Generation (Jain, 2016) | Features |
|---|---|
| First | simple electric motor; no torque control |
| Second | fully electronic; sensitive torque limiter |
| Third | simple torque-controlled motor |
| Fourth | built-in apex locator and torque control |

**How torque-limited handpieces and motors work** (Baumann and Beer, 2010)
- The torque limitation interrupts rotation the instant that frictional resistance exceeds the fracture limit of the instrument.
- Multilevel handpieces: the Anthogyr NiTi Control has a friction clutch with four-step regulation; the SIRONiTi handpiece has a magnetic clutch with five levels. With the magnetic clutch the force falls and then reverse rotation releases the instrument.
- Files of different mechanical capacity are grouped by clinical safety, and the weakest instrument in a group provides the maximum torque; the other files in the group are then not stressed to their maximum, which can reduce their dentin cutting efficiency.
- Torque-control micromotors: some are computer-controlled step-motors, not continuous 360-degree motors. The TCM Endo (Nouvag), TriAuto ZX (Morita) and Quantec ETM (SybronEndo) have step-specific torque; the EndoStepper (Phytron) and Endo IT Control (VDW) program torque to the file used.
- Jain (2016): at the set torque the motor stops and reverses, and the latest handpieces regulate torque by file size through gears, so a torque motor is not needed.

**Features of modern motors** (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016; Peters et al, 2021)
- *Torque limitation*: the file stops, and in most motors reverses, at the preset torque.
- *Auto-reverse or twisting*: releases a blocked file (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- *File-specific programming*: torque (and speed) set for the file used; some motors identify the file and adjust automatically (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- *Gear reduction*: constant rpm and torque (Peters et al, 2021).
- *Programmable motion*: continuous rotation or reciprocation at chosen angles (Peters et al, 2021).
- *Apex locator*: working length checked continuously during instrumentation (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- *Handpiece clutch*: friction (Anthogyr, four steps) or magnetic (SIRONiTi, five levels) (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- *Warning*: motors can deliver torque far above the tip fracture limit (Peters et al, 2021).

## 7. Preparation techniques with automation

### Explanation

Jain states that most NiTi devices require preflaring of the upper canal because of their flexibility, which is unsuitable for initial negotiation or bypassing ledges; coronal widening provides straight-line access, and the final preparation is crown-down or step-back, with crown-down considered safer because it gives less tip contact, force and torque (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. NiTi files are generally used in a very-low-speed torque-control handpiece with full 360-degree rotation, which gives less canal straightening (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer note that power-driven instrumentation with NiTi is generally crown-down, without impinging on the apical constriction, and that a glide path made with hand files of ISO sizes 10 to 20 allows tapered files to be used with less danger of fracture in curved canals (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Jain also defines the hybrid concept of combining instruments and techniques from different systems (Jain, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principles of automated preparation** (Jain, 2016)
- NiTi files are generally used in a very-low-speed torque-control handpiece with full 360-degree rotation, which results in less straightening of the canal.
- Pre-flaring of the upper canal is needed because NiTi files are too flexible for initial negotiation or for bypassing ledges; coronal widening with Peeso and Gates Glidden reamers gives straight-line access and more control in the middle and apical thirds.
- Most rotary techniques are crown-down, to minimise torsional load and fracture risk. Crown-down is safer than step-back because it gives less tip contact, force and torque; step-back with stainless-steel files in curved canals often damaged the natural shape.
- Pre-enlarging the coronal region gives straighter access to the apical region, better tactile control and better irrigant penetration and debris suspension.
- Hybrid techniques combine files of different systems and different techniques to give the best cleaning and shaping with the fewest procedural errors.

**Technique** (Jain, 2016; Baumann and Beer, 2010)
1. Straight-line access and coronal pre-flaring (Peeso or Gates Glidden), because NiTi cannot negotiate the initial canal or bypass ledges.
2. Glide path with hand files (ISO 10 to 20).
3. Crown-down sequence at a low constant speed in a torque-controlled motor, without impinging on the apical constriction.
4. Working length monitored electronically during instrumentation.
5. Hybrid use of files and techniques where indicated (Jain, 2016).

## 8. Reciprocation, vibration and newer approaches

### Explanation

Reciprocation: Peters et al say that replacing continuous rotation by reciprocation mitigates taper lock, fatigue fracture and threading-in, and that two instruments, WaveOne and Reciproc, were designed for it, driven by special motors that alternate counterclockwise and clockwise rotations (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri notes that three reciprocating cycles complete one reverse rotation (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. See I5. Vibration: Baumann and Beer record that the primary use of ultrasonics today is passive ultrasonic irrigation, the opening of canals and retreatment, rather than canal preparation (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Sikri's SAF is vibrated in and out at 3,000 to 5,000 vibrations a minute (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. See I8 and I9.

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Reciprocation**: alternating arcs (about 150–170 degrees and 30–50 degrees) by dedicated motors; reduces taper lock and threading-in; WaveOne and Reciproc (Peters et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019). See I5.
- **Vibration**: ultrasonic and sonic units, used today for irrigation activation, access and retreatment rather than shaping (Baumann and Beer, 2010); the SAF is a shaping file that vibrates in and out (Sikri, 2019). See I8 and I9.
- **Integration**: motor with built-in apex locator and file-specific settings (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016).

**Comparison of the motion types** (Sikri, 2019; Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010)
| Motion | Examples | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical or quarter-turn reciprocation, stainless steel | Giromatic, Canal Finder, M4, Endo-grapper | cycles up to 3,000 a minute; little gain over hand files; loss of tactile sense; largely abandoned (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| Continuous rotation, NiTi | ProFile, LightSpeed, RaCe | 360-degree rotation at about 300 rpm with torque control; less straightening; fracture risk (Jain, 2016; Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| Reciprocation, NiTi | WaveOne, Reciproc | alternating arcs by a programmed motor; less taper lock (Peters et al, 2021) |
| Vibration | Ultrasonic and sonic units; SAF | ultrasonics: irrigation activation, retreatment, access (Baumann and Beer, 2010); the SAF is a shaping file driven by in-and-out vibration (Sikri, 2019) |

**Operating speeds quoted for individual systems** (Peters et al, 2021): about 500 rpm for Vortex, 600 rpm for EndoSequence, 300 rpm for TRUShape, and 500 rpm at 1.5 Ncm for TruNatomy. The speed is therefore set to the system, not fixed for all files.

## 9. Limitations and cautions of automation

### Explanation

Sikri's disadvantages (loss of tactile sensation, perforations, file breakage) and the books' discussion of fatigue and torque show where automation can fail (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Stainless steel with rigid motion produced ledges and perforations (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. No view in these books supports the idea that automation can replace operator skill, and Peters et al note that motors can deliver torque far above what breaks a tip (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- Loss of tactile sensation: zipping, ledges, perforation, breakage (Sikri, 2019).
- Fracture without warning (see I11).
- Rigid-motion handpieces with stainless steel: ledges and perforation (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Motors probably do not reduce fracture caused by cyclic fatigue (Peters et al, 2021).
- Needs a glide path, pre-flaring and operator judgement (Jain, 2016; Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 10. Automation beyond canal shaping

### Explanation

Automation is not confined to shaping. Baumann and Beer describe modified handpieces that allow electronic apex location during power-driven instrumentation (SIRONiTi APEX) and motors with an integrated apex locator, so that the estimated length is continuously displayed, and they require that power-driven devices be usable with new file systems through modified torque or electronic stepped controls (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri notes that the Canal Finder system is used also for obturation and the removal of broken instruments (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and that the SAF is operated with a special irrigation device that gives continuous flow at low pressure (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Other roles of powered devices**
- *Working length*: electronic apex locators integrated in the handpiece or motor give continuous length control during instrumentation (Baumann and Beer, 2010); this is a fourth-generation motor feature (Jain, 2016).
- *Irrigation*: continuous automated delivery with the SAF (VATEA device); sonic and ultrasonic activation (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010). See I8 and I9.
- *Obturation and retrieval*: the Canal Finder system has been used for root canal obturation and removal of broken instruments (Sikri, 2019).
- *Compatibility*: motors and handpieces must be adapted to new file systems by modifying torque or using electronic stepped controls (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 11. A safe protocol for automated instrumentation

### Explanation

The protocol below is a synthesis of statements in the books above, not a quotation. Its elements are: pre-flaring and glide path (Jain, 2016; Baumann and Beer, 2010), crown-down sequence (Jain, 2016), a low constant speed and torque control (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016), electronic length control (Baumann and Beer, 2010), and inspection and short use (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

1. Access to give straight-line entry (Jain, 2016).
2. Pre-flare the coronal third (Jain, 2016): this gives straighter access to the apex, better tactile control and better irrigant penetration (Jain, 2016).
3. Make a glide path with ISO 10–20 hand files (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
4. Choose a motor with torque control and the correct settings for the file; run NiTi at a low constant speed (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016).
5. Use a crown-down sequence; do not impinge on the apical constriction (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016).
6. Keep time in the canal short; use light pressure; clean and inspect the file (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
7. Monitor length electronically (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
8. Irrigate between files; confirm the apical size with a hand file [RECALL].

**Note.** The table of motors in Jain's chapter lists which systems suit which motors, and the "right torque" motors are the ones that apply a torque specific to each file (Jain, 2016).

## 12. Conclusion

### Explanation

The conclusion draws the books together: automation succeeded when the instrument (NiTi) and the motor (torque control, and later apex location) were matched, and failed when rigid motion was applied to stainless steel (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Automation of root canal preparation has passed through three stages. The first, from Rollins's handpiece of 1899 to the Giromatic of 1964 and its relatives, used stainless-steel files in rigid reciprocating or vertical motions; it produced ledges, perforations and fractures, was judged inadequate by Baumann and Beer; for the Giromatic Sikri found little difference from hand files; rigid-motion systems are no longer recommended (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Sikri, 2019). The second began in 1988 with NiTi, whose flexibility allowed continuous 360-degree rotation at low speed; it gave excellent shaping of severely curved canals in less time, but with a high fracture risk that made torque-limited motors necessary (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016). The third stage adds motors with torque control, auto-reverse, file-specific programming and integrated apex locators (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016), reciprocating motion designed for single NiTi files (Peters et al, 2021), and vibration for irrigation, access and retreatment (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

The value of torque control is disputed: Baumann and Beer and Jain credit it with fewer fractures, whereas Peters et al say it probably does not reduce fracture caused by cyclic fatigue and may mainly benefit inexperienced operators (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016; Peters et al, 2021). Automation therefore supports but does not replace a glide path, pre-flaring, a crown-down sequence, a low constant speed, short use and inspection of files, and the clinician's judgement (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: torque-controlled motors reduce instrument fracture.** *Baumann and Beer:* torque limitation reduces loading and improves longevity (Baumann and Beer, 2010). *Peters et al:* some believe it increases safety, others that it mainly benefits inexperienced operators; it probably does not reduce cyclic fatigue fracture (Peters et al, 2021). *Jain:* motors that stop and reverse at the set torque mean instrument failure could be avoided, and right-torque motors are ideal (Jain, 2016). *Assessment:* the books split, Baumann and Beer and Jain for torque control and Peters et al doubtful; a plausible reading is that torque control limits torsional overload but not cyclic fatigue [Likely]. The supporting studies were not read. *Conclusion:* use it as one safeguard, not a guarantee. [Likely]

**Claim 2: automation speeds preparation and improves shape.** Sikri lists faster, better-centred preparation with less apical debris (Sikri, 2019), and Baumann and Beer note time savings in curved canals (Baumann and Beer, 2010); Sikri found little difference between the Giromatic and hand files (Sikri, 2019), and Baumann and Beer judge the early engine-driven systems inadequate (Baumann and Beer, 2010). *Conclusion:* the gain belongs to NiTi rotary systems, not to automation in general. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Three types: reciprocating, vibratory, rotary.
- Giromatic 1964: quarter-turn reciprocation, up to 3,000 cycles a minute; limited advantage.
- NiTi 1988: 360-degree rotation at low constant speed (about 300 rpm) with torque control.
- Torque rises with diameter and contact area; crown-down lowers it.
- Four generations of motors; the fourth has an apex locator.
- Torque control: Baumann and Beer and Jain support it; Peters et al doubt it for cyclic fatigue.
