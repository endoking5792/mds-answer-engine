## 1. Definition, scope and place of rotary endodontics

### Explanation

Rotary endodontics is the preparation of root canals with engine-driven nickel-titanium (NiTi) files in a handpiece. Jain states that NiTi files are generally used in a very-low-speed, torque-controlled handpiece with full 360-degree rotation, which results in less straightening of the canal (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Peters et al state that the advent of NiTi rotaries in the early 1990s, with their much more flexible alloy, allowed continuous rotation and reduced both canal preparation errors and instrument fracture compared with earlier engine-driven techniques, and that more than 100 rotary systems are now marketed, differing greatly in design, alloy and recommended cutting movement (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Rotary endodontics is canal preparation with engine-driven NiTi files, rotating continuously or reciprocating, in a torque-controlled handpiece at low speed (Jain, 2016; Peters et al, 2021).

**Why it replaced the earlier methods.** The flexible alloy allowed continuous rotation and reduced both canal preparation errors and instrument fracture compared with earlier engine-driven techniques (Peters et al, 2021).


## 2. The nickel-titanium era

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer date the first NiTi endodontic instruments to the late 1980s (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Peters et al state that with NiTi rotaries in the early 1990s a much more flexible alloy allowed continuous rotation and reduced both canal preparation errors and instrument fracture compared with earlier engine-driven techniques (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC], and that more than 100 rotary systems are now marketed, varying greatly in design, alloy and cutting movement (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Jain and Sikri group them in generations (Jain, 2016; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; the two classifications are summarised below.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Generations of NiTi files** (Jain, 2016; Sikri, 2019)
| Generation | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| First (mid to late 1990s) | passive cutting, radial lands, fixed tapers, many instruments | ProFile, GT, LightSpeed |
| Second (2001) | active cutting edges, fewer instruments, some electropolished | Flexmaster, EndoSequence, BioRaCe, ProTaper, HERO |
| Third (2007) | thermomechanically treated; less cyclic fatigue | K3, Twisted File, GT Vortex, HyFlex |
| Fourth | reciprocation; single-file | M4, Endo-Eze; SAF, WaveOne, Reciproc |
| Fifth | offset centre of rotation; more flexible | ProTaper Next, One Shape, Revo-S |

**The first rotary NiTi systems** (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010)
- The first commercially successful rotary instruments were ProFile, LightSpeed and GT; the first commercial systems also included Mity Roto and Power-R, all with flattened cutting edges ("radial lands").
- LightSpeed (early 1990s): thin, non-cutting shaft and a short anterior cutting head.
- ProFile: U-shaped cross-section with radial lands, slightly negative rake, reaming rather than cutting.
- K3: slightly positive rake, wide radial lands and a third land to prevent threading-in.
- ProTaper: originally six instruments, three shaping and three finishing files; convex triangle, no radial lands; progressive tapers; faster than GT, ProFile and Quantec in plastic blocks, with somewhat more aberrations.
- HERO: positive rake angles for cutting efficiency; RaCe: alternating cutting edges; EndoSequence: alternating wall contact points to reduce torque.
- Twisted File (R-phase) and Vortex (M-wire; Vortex Blue from blue wire): heat-treated alloys.
- Mtwo: clinically an example of the single-length technique.

**Trend in design.** Radial-landed files were safe but reamed rather than cut; later active designs cut better but carried more risk of preparation errors (Peters et al, 2021).

Sikri's list places WaveOne among heat-treated third-generation files, and Jain places it in the fourth (reciprocating) generation; the examples differ between books.

## 3. Composition and history of the alloy

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer date the first NiTi endodontic instruments to the late 1980s (Walia et al, 1988, as cited by them), and say that this started a development that fundamentally changed root canal instrumentation (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The original 55-Nitinol, about 55% nickel and 45% titanium, still contained traces of iron and cobalt (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri adds that nitinol was introduced in dentistry by the Naval Ordnance Laboratory, that a 50:50 ratio gave way to the ratio found more effective for endodontic use (nickel 54–56% and titanium 44–46%), that traces of cobalt and boron were added to improve surface hardness, and that adding aluminium at the expense of nickel did not improve cutting efficiency and cost the super-elasticity (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Peters et al state that endodontic instruments are made of two types of alloy: stainless steel and NiTi, and that rotary instruments are typically NiTi because of its flexibility and corrosion resistance (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Composition.** About 55% nickel and 45% titanium (55-Nitinol), initially with traces of iron and cobalt (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Sikri gives 54–56% nickel and 44–46% titanium as the effective endodontic range, with traces of cobalt and boron added for surface hardness; adding 5% aluminium instead of nickel did not help and lost super-elasticity (Sikri, 2019).

**History.** NiTi hand and rotary endodontic instruments date from the late 1980s (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Stainless steel and NiTi are the two alloys used for endodontic instruments; rotary instruments are typically NiTi (Peters et al, 2021).

## 4. Properties: superelasticity, shape memory and phases

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer explain that the alloy is pseudoelastic: after loading and unloading it returns to its initial shape ("memory effect"), and in the phase change it exhibits hysteresis, unlike ordinary elasticity (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The phase change is diffusionless, like the martensitic change in hardening steel, and NiTi shows virtually no volume change on heating and cooling because twinned martensite and austenite are macroscopically almost identical (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Their caption states that NiTi instruments can withstand twisting up to 8% without permanent deformation, and the chapter on fracture says the reversible deformation is about 7% (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Peters et al also give up to 7% recoverable elastic response for austenitic instruments and add that martensitic instruments have less elastic range and are more likely to deform plastically (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Jain describes the two properties of interest in endodontics as superelasticity and high resistance to cyclic fatigue, produced by the stress-induced austenite-to-martensite transformation that is unique to NiTi (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Biologically, Baumann and Beer find the biocompatibility of Nitinol very good, and the high nickel content not problematic because it is bound to the titanium (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

The textbooks conflict on one point. Jain's early paragraph and Sikri's comparison table state that NiTi is not heat treatable (Jain, 2016; Sikri, 2019) [SRC], whereas the later parts of Jain's chapter and Peters et al describe NiTi whose properties are deliberately changed by heat treatment (Jain, 2016; Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. The statement belongs to the older conventional alloy; thermomechanical processing is the later development.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Properties**
- *Superelasticity (pseudoelasticity)*: the file returns to its shape after loading; stress-induced austenite-to-martensite change, reversible on unloading (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016).
- *Shape memory*: recovers shape on heating (martensitic files above their transformation temperature) (Jain, 2016).
- *Hysteresis* in the phase change, unlike ordinary elastic recovery (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- *Elastic range*: about 7% reversible strain (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Peters et al, 2021), up to 8% in one book's caption (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Martensitic files have a smaller elastic range (Peters et al, 2021).
- *Fatigue resistance*: high (Jain, 2016).
- *Biocompatibility*: very good; nickel is bound to titanium (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- *Volume*: no change in phase transformation (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Phase behaviour in answer-book form** (Jain, 2016; Peters et al, 2021)
- At rest the alloy is austenitic. Under stress, for example when a file is bent in a curved canal, it transforms to martensite, a more flexible crystal structure (Jain, 2016).
- On release of stress the metal returns to austenite and the file regains its shape (Jain, 2016).
- Temperature also drives the transformation: heat applied to the raw wire or to the finished file can be used to modify flexibility (Peters et al, 2021).
- Martensitic files can be deformed and then recover their shape on heating above the transformation temperatures (Jain, 2016).
- The R-phase is an intermediate rhombohedral phase between martensite and austenite (Jain, 2016).

| | NiTi | Stainless steel |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic potential | superelastic | much lower, so fatigue begins earlier (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| Flexibility | two to three times more | not flexible (Sikri, 2019) |
| Corrosion | good | fair (Sikri, 2019) |
| Heat treatment | traditionally "not heat treatable" (Sikri, 2019); now thermomechanically treated (Jain, 2016) | heat treatable (Sikri, 2019) |
| Warning before fracture | none | yes (Sikri, 2019) |

**Facts and numbers to remember** (Baumann and Beer, 2010)
- A NiTi file that is bent returns to its original shape and position; a steel file bent beyond its limit is permanently deformed (hysteresis curve comparison).
- Deformations up to 12% do not necessarily fracture a NiTi file but lead towards the point of potential fracture; reversible deformation is about 7–8%.
- NiTi is soft (HV 300–360) against stainless steel (HV 520–540), so cutting efficiency is low; its use with rotary motion outweighs this.
- Manufacture of the alloy is difficult and expensive, and earlier led to inconsistencies.

**Disadvantages of the alloy.** Low hardness and cutting efficiency compared with stainless steel, outweighed by use with rotary motion (Baumann and Beer, 2010); softer, lower tensile and yield strength than steel (Jain, 2016; Sikri, 2019). Conflict: some books call NiTi not heat treatable (Jain, 2016; Sikri, 2019); thermomechanical treatment is the later development.

## 5. Thermomechanical treatment: M-wire, R-phase, CM wire, gold and blue

### Explanation

Jain explains that heating the alloy during manufacture has created new forms of NiTi, and that the martensitic form has remarkable fatigue resistance; a proprietary thermomechanical process integrates hardening and heat treatment (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Sikri places this in the third generation of files, which used heating and cooling methods to reduce cyclic fatigue and improve safety (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

M-wire: Jain reports that M-wire technology improves the resistance to cyclic fatigue by almost 400% compared with commercial NiTi files and that GT Series X (GTX, 2008) was the first commercial system using it; ProFile Vortex, Vortex Blue, GTX and ProTaper Next are M-wire systems (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. R-phase: an intermediate rhombohedral phase; in 2008 instruments were made by transforming raw wire from austenite into R-phase by heat, which allowed twisting; the Twisted File and K3XF are R-phase files (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Controlled memory (CM) wire, introduced in 2010, makes files extremely flexible but without the shape memory of conventional NiTi; HyFlex and Typhoon use it (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Peters et al add the "gold" and "blue" alloys of Dentsply Sirona and the controlled memory alloy of HyFlex as heat-treated alloys, which are typically more flexible and have higher fatigue resistance (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC].

Two cautions. The 400% figure is the manufacturer's claim as reported by Jain and the primary studies were not read here. And the same literature notes that more martensitic instruments plastically deform more easily (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC], so flexibility and fatigue resistance gain comes with a different failure behaviour: the file may be visibly bent before it fails.

#### ✎ Exam core

| Alloy | Process | Property claimed | Examples (Jain, 2016; Peters et al, 2021) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional austenitic NiTi | not heat treated | superelastic; stiffer | most early systems [RECALL] |
| M-wire | thermomechanical processing | cyclic fatigue resistance increased by almost 400% (manufacturer's claim) | GTX (2008), ProFile Vortex, Vortex Blue, ProTaper Next |
| R-phase | wire heat-treated into R-phase before twisting (2008) | reduced stiffness; more fracture resistance | Twisted File, K3XF |
| CM wire | controlled-memory process (2010) | very flexible; no shape memory | HyFlex, Typhoon |
| Gold and blue alloys | heat treatment after manufacture | more flexible; higher fatigue resistance | Dentsply Sirona files (Peters et al, 2021) |

**Individual wires and systems** (Peters et al, 2021; Jain, 2016)
- *Twisted File*: the unique production process is believed to give superior physical properties; early studies suggested significantly better fatigue resistance than K3 of the same size (Peters et al, 2021).
- *Vortex*: two versions are on the market, one from M-wire and another from so-called blue wire (Peters et al, 2021); Vortex Blue showed greater cyclic fatigue and torque resistance (Peters et al, 2021).
- *ProTaper Next*: made of M-wire, with mechanical properties better than ProTaper Universal in current research (Peters et al, 2021).
- *Edge files*: made from heat-treated alloys; extended fatigue life has been reported, at least at room temperature, but research is limited (Peters et al, 2021).
- *XP-endo files*: made of a NiTi alloy that changes conformation between room and body temperature, which leads to some stiffening and specific preparation outcomes (Peters et al, 2021).
- *Third generation* (2007 onward): thermomechanically treated files with decreased incidence of fracture because cyclic fatigue is reduced (Jain, 2016).

**Timeline** (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016)
| Year | Development |
|---|---|
| 1988 | first NiTi endodontic instruments (Walia et al) |
| 1990s | LightSpeed, Mity Roto, ProFile, Power-R |
| 2007–2008 | M-wire; GTX the first commercial M-wire system; R-phase wire |
| 2010 | controlled-memory (CM) wire (HyFlex, Typhoon) |

**Why it matters.** Thermomechanically treated files are more flexible and more fatigue resistant, which suits curved canals, and M-wire files show better centring and less transportation (Jain, 2016). Martensitic instruments plastically deform more easily and so may show bending before they fail (Peters et al, 2021).

## 6. Design characteristics and their effects

### Explanation

Peters et al define the helical angle as the angle the leading edge forms with the long axis, and pitch as the distance between corresponding points on adjacent leading edges. A smaller pitch means more spirals and a greater helix angle, and many NiTi rotaries have a variable pitch (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. The rake angle may be positive (cutting), neutral (leading edge at 90 degrees to the surface) or negative (scraping), and the cutting angle is a better indicator of cutting ability (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. NiTi rotary files typically have rounded non-cutting tips (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer add that chip space determines how much debris can be removed before the file jams, and that taper has gone from none (LightSpeed) to 2, 4 and 6% and above, with reverse-taper designs thicker at the tip (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Design variables** (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010)
- *Tip*: rounded, non-cutting tip guides the file and prevents ledges.
- *Taper*: none (LightSpeed), 2, 4 and 6%, up to 12% or more; reverse taper (thicker at the tip) in some designs.
- *Cross-section*: U-shape, triangular, convex triangle, S-shape, with or without radial lands.
- *Helical angle and pitch*: smaller pitch gives more spirals; variable pitch is common.
- *Rake angle*: positive (cutting), neutral (90 degrees) or negative (scraping); positive rake cuts more efficiently, negative gives reaming.
- *Chip space*: determines how much debris is removed before the file binds.
- *Core*: the central mass that gives strength.

**Cross-section trends** (Baumann and Beer, 2010): the convex (expanded) triangle of FlexMaster and ProTaper; the square section, still common in steel, survives in NiTi only in RaCe; many instruments have a progressive distance between cutting edges (ProTaper, Mtwo), which increases chip space toward the shank.

**Recent strategies** (Peters et al, 2021): "conforming" shaping, with an adaptable, larger envelope of motion but a limited maximum flute diameter (TRUShape, XP-Shaper). TRUShape sizes 20, 25, 30 and 40 have a maximum fluted diameter of 0.8 mm, which limits coronal dentin removal.

**Why they matter** (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010): more than 100 rotary systems are marketed and they vary greatly in design, alloy and recommended cutting movement; most are made by multiaxial grinding or micromilling. A larger chip space reduces wedging; a smaller pitch gives more spirals and a larger helix angle; the success of a system depends on the cutting efficiency of the file and also on the ease of use and the logic of the file sequence (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 7. Passive and active designs, and relative efficacy

### Explanation

Peters et al divide rotary instruments into group I (designed for passive preparation), group II (active cutting) and group III (unique designs) (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Group I files (ProFile, LightSpeed, GT) have radial lands that guide the file; this makes them fairly safe against preparation errors, but they ream rather than cut and are less efficient (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Group II files lack radial lands, which gives higher cutting efficacy and a higher potential for preparation errors, particularly if taken through the foramen (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri uses the terms passive and active in the same way and says active instruments cut more aggressively and tend to straighten the canal (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; his lists agree with Cohen on ProFile and LightSpeed as passive, and name Flexmaster, RaCe, ProTaper, HERO and K3 as active (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen places K3, with its wide radial lands, in group I, while Sikri lists it with the active files; the two books differ here, and K3 is described by Cohen as having a slightly positive rake angle with radial lands (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri also reports that stainless steel cuts more efficiently (60% more) than NiTi, and that NiTi gives no indication before fracture (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| | Group I: passive (radial lands) | Group II: active (no radial lands) |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | ProFile, LightSpeed, GT; K3 per Cohen | ProTaper, HERO, RaCe, Flexmaster |
| Action | Reaming, not cutting | Cutting |
| Efficacy | Less efficient | More efficient; faster |
| Safety | Fairly safe against preparation errors | More potential for errors, e.g. ledges and straightening |
| Source | Peters et al, 2021 | Peters et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019 |

Sikri places K3 with active files; Cohen describes it with radial lands (disagreement between books). Group III: unique designs such as reciprocating and conforming files (Peters et al, 2021).

## 8. Individual systems

### Explanation

ProFile has a U-shaped cross-section with radial lands, a 20-degree helix angle, constant pitch, bullet-shaped non-cutting tips and a slightly negative rake angle that gives reaming (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. LightSpeed has a thin non-cutting shaft and a short cutting head, with three radial lands and three U-shaped grooves; it is the only rotary system whose cutting heads have three different geometric shapes (Peters et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. K3 has a slightly positive rake angle, wide radial lands, a peripheral blade relief, and a third land to help prevent threading-in (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. ProTaper has a convex triangle cross-section with sharp edges and no radial lands; the shaping files have tapers that increase coronally and the finishing files the reverse (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. RaCe has alternating cutting edges and EndoSequence alternating wall contact points to reduce torque (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Profile 29: Schilder proposed a constant 29% increase in diameter between sizes, which gives constant increase and fewer instruments than ISO, but works well only in small sizes (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Baumann and Beer note that a constant 29% remains under discussion (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. SimpliFill is a gutta-percha apical plug system made to be compatible with LightSpeed instrumentation (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **ProFile** (Peters et al, 2021): U-shape, radial lands, 20-degree helix, constant pitch, slightly negative rake, reaming.
- **LightSpeed** (Peters et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019): thin non-cutting shaft, short cutting head, three radial lands and U-grooves.
- **K3** (Peters et al, 2021): positive rake, wide radial lands, third land against threading-in.
- **ProTaper** (Peters et al, 2021): convex triangle, no radial lands; shaping files taper increasing coronally, finishing files reverse.
- **ProTaper detail** (Peters et al, 2021): shaping files 1 and 2 have tip diameters of 0.185 and 0.2 mm, 14 mm blades and partially active tips; finishing files F1–F5 have tip diameters of 0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5 mm. Use a laterally directed brushing stroke away from the furcation. It shaped canals faster than GT, ProFile and Quantec in plastic blocks but with somewhat more aberrations; wide canals may be insufficiently prepared. ProTaper Next, made of M-Wire, has better mechanical properties.
- **RaCe** (Peters et al, 2021): alternating cutting edges. **EndoSequence**: alternating wall contact points to reduce torque.
- **Profile 29** (Sikri, 2019): constant 29% size increase; gives constant diameter steps and fewer instruments, but works only in small sizes; under discussion (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- **LightSpeed SimpliFill** (Sikri, 2019): gutta-percha apical plug made to suit LightSpeed shaping.

## 9. Rotary NiTi files: merits and demerits

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer name the most significant advantage of fully rotating NiTi instruments as the excellent instrumentation of severely curved canals, with considerable saving of preparation time; the problem compared with hand stainless-steel files is the high risk of fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri adds that NiTi has low tensile and yield strength compared with stainless steel and so fractures more easily (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle states that most studies show good maintenance of canal curvature even in severely curved canals, but that the majority of NiTi systems cannot completely instrument and clean the canal walls (Ingle, year not printed) [SRC]. Cohen identifies the continuous-rotation problems that reciprocation was meant to mitigate: taper lock, fatigue fracture and threading-in (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Merits of rotary NiTi** (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Ingle, year not printed)
- Excellent shaping of severely curved canals; canal curvature is well maintained.
- Large saving in preparation time.
- Super-elasticity gives flexibility (Sikri, 2019).

**Demerits and limitations**
- High risk of fracture, mostly cyclic fatigue and torsional overload (Baumann and Beer, 2010); low tensile and yield strength of the alloy (Sikri, 2019).
- Taper lock and threading-in with continuous rotation (Peters et al, 2021).
- Most systems leave canal walls uninstrumented, including isthmi (Ingle, year not printed).
- Needs a glide path, torque-controlled motor and a low constant speed, about 300 rpm (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- No warning before fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010); cost and learning curve [RECALL].

**Operating parameters of rotary systems** (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010): speeds are system-specific, for example 500 rpm for Vortex, 600 rpm for EndoSequence, and 500 rpm with 1.5 Ncm for TruNatomy; a low constant speed of about 300 rpm is advised for NiTi generally, with torque-controlled motors (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Heat-treated alloys help: Vortex Blue showed greater resistance to cyclic fatigue and higher torque resistance (Peters et al, 2021).

**Historical note.** Engine-driven stainless-steel instruments have been used for more than half a century, at first mainly in handpieces that permitted reciprocation (alternating clockwise and counterclockwise motion) (Peters et al, 2021); the M4 safety handpiece and Endo-grapper are modified versions of the Giromatic handpiece (Sikri, 2019). Modern reciprocating files are a different concept: NiTi files designed for the motion (Peters et al, 2021).

## 10. Reciprocating files: concept and design

### Explanation

Peters et al explain that replacing continuous rotation by reciprocation mitigates taper lock, fatigue fracture and threading-in, and has generally been successful in preventing threading-in and fractures (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Two instruments were designed for reciprocation: WaveOne (Dentsply Sirona) and Reciproc (VDW) (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. WaveOne comes in tip sizes 21, 25 and 40 with tapers .06 and .08; its main cross-section is triangular, like ProTaper, whereas Reciproc has two flutes in a design similar to Mtwo (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri describes WaveOne as a single-file system made from M-Wire, which improves strength and resistance to cyclic fatigue, with three files (Small for fine canals, Primary for most canals, Large for large canals) selected from the first hand file that fits (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. A single-use file reduces instrument fatigue because one file does the work of three or more rotary files (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Concept.** Reciprocation replaces continuous rotation by alternating cutting and releasing movements, to reduce taper lock, fatigue fracture and threading-in (Peters et al, 2021).

**Systems** (Peters et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019)
| | WaveOne | Reciproc |
|---|---|---|
| Tip sizes | 21, 25 and 40 | 25, 40 and 50 |
| Tapers | .06 and .08 | .08, .06 and .05 (Peters et al, 2021) |
| Cross-section | triangular, like ProTaper | two flutes, like Mtwo |
| Alloy | M-Wire (Sikri, 2019) | |
| Concept | single-use, single file | single-use, single file |

**Technique with WaveOne** (Sikri, 2019): WaveOne Gold replaces the original series with improved flexibility; the rechargeable motor drives a 6:1 reduction handpiece; use a progressive up-and-down movement with little pressure, not more than three to four times, with continuous irrigation, then confirm the apical preparation with a hand file of the same size.

Selection of WaveOne by the first hand file: a size 10 K-file that feels resistant leads to the Small file; if it moves to length easily, use the Primary; if a 20 or larger touches working length, use the Large (Sikri, 2019).

## 11. Merits and demerits of reciprocating files

### Explanation

Merits supported by the books: single-file systems simplify preparation; the file works in one motion that reduces threading-in and fracture (Peters et al, 2021); life span is longer and cyclic fatigue resistance higher (Terauchi and Renton, 2021); single-use files avoid fatigue accumulating over cases (Sikri, 2019); and shaping ability appears similar to continuous-rotation systems in current in vitro data (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Terauchi and Renton recommend using reciprocation in severely curved canals when fatigue or torsion is expected, and discarding the instruments after use (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Demerits are largely [RECALL]: the cost of single-use files and dedicated motors, a larger apical file than a hand-prepared glide path would suggest, debris extrusion, and the fact that no NiTi system, including reciprocating single files, cleans all the canal walls and isthmi (Ingle, year not printed) [SRC].

Older reciprocating handpieces used stainless steel. Sikri lists the advantages of vertical reciprocation (less file separation, no NiTi needed, no torque strain) and the disadvantages (lack of tactile sensation, risk of perforation and ledges, risk of file breakage) (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| | Rotary NiTi | Reciprocating single-file |
|---|---|---|
| Motion | continuous rotation | alternating large and small arcs |
| Files per canal | several in sequence | one (single use) (Sikri, 2019) |
| Fatigue and fracture | more risk (Baumann and Beer, 2010) | longer life, higher resistance (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) |
| Taper lock and threading-in | possible | reduced (Peters et al, 2021) |
| Shaping | good in curved canals | similar in vitro (Peters et al, 2021) |
| Cleaning of walls and isthmi | incomplete (Ingle, year not printed) | incomplete (Ingle, year not printed) |
| Cost | lower per file [RECALL] | single-use files and special motor [RECALL] |

**Merits of reciprocation.** Fewer files; less fatigue; reduced taper lock and threading-in; similar shaping; suitable for severe curves (Peters et al, 2021; Terauchi and Renton, 2021; Sikri, 2019).

**Demerits.** Cost of single-use files and a dedicated motor [RECALL]; large apical size and possible debris extrusion [RECALL]; not shown to clean all walls (Ingle, year not printed); a lack of tactile feedback and ledging risk with the older vertical-reciprocation handpieces (Sikri, 2019).

**Choosing between them** (Terauchi and Renton, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010)
- Establish a glide path first with hand files, whichever system is used (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- In severely curved canals, where cyclic or torsional fatigue is expected, prefer reciprocation and discard the files after use (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- Smaller files with smaller tapers resist cyclic fatigue better but are weaker in torsion, so size is matched to the canal (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- Because no system cleans all walls and isthmi (Ingle, year not printed), supplement either with irrigation activation [RECALL].

## 12. Mechanisms of fracture

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer describe two mechanisms for NiTi fracture, cyclic fatigue and torsional overloading. With cyclic fatigue there is no plastic deformation first, and so there are no warning signs; with torsional loading deformation may be seen (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Stainless-steel instruments show clear plastic deformation before they fracture, whereas rotary NiTi can break without warning, and fracture of rotary NiTi is somewhat more common than that of stainless-steel K files (5.1% against 3.3%) (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Terauchi and Renton define cyclic fatigue as repetitive compressive and tensile stresses on the outer part of a file rotating in a curved canal, and torsional failure as occurring when the tip binds while the shank keeps rotating (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Cyclic fatigue is more prevalent in curved canals, whereas torsional failure can occur even in a straight canal (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC].

The two interact: fatigue reduces torsional resistance and vice versa (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC], and cyclic-fatigued instruments are reported to become less resistant to torsional failure (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer also describe "relaxation": when the tip lodges, the coronal end can often be rotated about two more full turns with reduced torque, after which torsional overload follows and fracture is inevitable (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Two mechanisms** (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Terauchi and Renton, 2021)
| | Cyclic fatigue | Torsional overload |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Repeated tension and compression of a file rotating in a curved canal | Tip binds while the shank continues to turn |
| Canal | Mainly curved | Even straight canals |
| Warning | None; no plastic deformation | Deformation may be seen |
| Favoured by | Larger size and taper | Smaller size and taper |

The two interact: fatigue lowers torsional resistance and the reverse (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Rotary NiTi breaks without warning and more often than steel K files (5.1% against 3.3%) (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Cyclic fatigue.** In a curved canal each turn of the file puts one side of the bend in tension and the opposite side in compression, so the metal is loaded once per rotation. Damage accumulates and the file fractures without warning (Terauchi and Renton, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010). In a severe curve rotary files break after a limited number of rotations, and manufacturing defects or microcracks help start the fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Torsional overload.** When the tip lodges, the shank keeps turning. The coronal end can often be turned about two more times with reduced torque ("relaxation"); then torsional overload follows and fracture is inevitable (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Alloy behaviour.** NiTi can deform reversibly by about 7% through its austenite–martensite change (Baumann and Beer, 2010), or up to 8% according to a figure caption in the same book (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Beyond this the damage is irreversible and not visible.

## 13. Prevention

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer recommend torque-controlled motors and careful handling, a glide path before rotary files, crown-down instrumentation that reduces the contact surface and so the torque, a short working time in the canal, and visual inspection of files before reuse (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Rotary NiTi should be driven at a low constant speed of about 300 rpm, and motors with torque limitation interrupt rotation when friction exceeds the fracture limit, with auto-reverse functions to release a blocked file (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Terauchi and Renton note that reciprocating motion extends instrument life and improves cyclic fatigue resistance, and recommend that instruments used in severely curved canals be discarded after use (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prevention**
- Glide path with hand files before rotary files (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Crown-down sequence: less contact, less torque (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Torque-controlled motor with auto-reverse; low constant speed, about 300 rpm (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Keep time in the canal short; do not apply apical pressure (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- Inspect before use and discard after use in severe curves (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- Consider reciprocating motion in severely curved canals (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
- Adequate access and straight-line entry (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).

**Torque-controlled motors.** The motor interrupts rotation the moment friction exceeds the instrument's fracture limit, and auto-reverse or twisting functions release a blocked file (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Files are grouped by clinical safety, so the weakest file in a group sets the torque (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Patient and record.** Inform the patient if a fragment is left; fracture is not in itself a professional error, but inadequate disinfection before fracture affects prognosis (Baumann and Beer, 2010). For retrieval see procedural errors Q07 and Q08.

## 14. Speed and torque

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer state that NiTi must be run at a low constant speed, usually about 300 rpm, and that torque, as well as speed, affects the risk of fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. They define torque as force times lever arm, in N·cm, and report that the breakage torque of the instrument tip is measured 3 mm from the tip (D3), and that in many cases the working torque exceeds this limit (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Torque rises with instrument diameter and with the dentin contact area, and crown-down instrumentation reduces contact and so torque (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Jain agrees that the torque generated depends on factors, especially the contact area, and concludes that slow-speed, high-torque NiTi rotation leads to many iatrogenic errors, with the ideal being slow speed and low torque or, preferably, "right" torque, because each instrument has a specific ideal torque (Jain, 2016) [SRC]. Peters et al report that torque-controlled motors are thought by some authors to increase operational safety but by others to benefit mainly inexperienced clinicians; they probably do not reduce cyclic fatigue fracture, and fracture at D2 remains possible even if torque is below the D3 limit (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer, in contrast, report that torque limitation leads to less loading and better longevity of NiTi files (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. These two accounts are not reconciled: the one concerns the torsional overload that torque control can limit, and the other the cyclic fatigue that it cannot.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Speed.** Low constant speed, usually about 300 rpm, for NiTi (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Torque.** Rotational force, force times lever arm (N·cm). Torque needed rises with the instrument diameter and the contact area between file and dentin (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016). Crown-down instrumentation reduces contact and so torque (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Ideal setting.** Slow speed with low or "right" torque, because every instrument has its own ideal torque; slow speed with high torque leads to many iatrogenic errors (Jain, 2016).

**Why speed and torque matter.** The torque needed to turn a file increases with its diameter and with the area of dentin it contacts, and that area depends on the taper and the instrumentation sequence (Jain, 2016). In straight canals, where resistance to dentin removal is low, the high torque a motor can provide may fracture a blocked instrument before the clinician can stop or retract it (Jain, 2016). If the tip lodges while the shank is driven, torsional overload follows (Baumann and Beer, 2010). To avoid the instrument becoming stuck and fracturing, instrumentation is done at a low constant speed, usually about 300 rpm (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Breakage torque** is measured 3 mm from the tip (D3) and often lower than the working torque (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Value of torque control: opinions differ**
- Torque limitation reduces loading and improves file life (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Some authors say torque-controlled motors increase operational safety; others that they mainly benefit inexperienced clinicians, and probably do not reduce cyclic fatigue fracture; fracture at D2 is still possible (Peters et al, 2021).

## 15. Conclusion

### Explanation

The sections above show a consistent picture across the books: the alloy made continuous rotation possible, design and heat treatment addressed safety and cutting, motors and technique limited fracture, and the remaining problems are fatigue fracture, incomplete cleaning of walls and isthmi, and the absence of outcome data (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Peters et al, 2021; Ingle, year not printed) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Rotary NiTi instrumentation has made the preparation of curved canals faster and more predictable than hand stainless-steel instrumentation, at the cost of a fatigue-related fracture risk that needs a glide path, torque control, a low constant speed and careful use (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016). Design and alloy advances have improved flexibility and fatigue resistance in the laboratory, and reciprocating single-file systems give a simpler technique (Peters et al, 2021; Jain, 2016), yet no system cleans all canal walls and isthmi, and clinical outcome differences between designs have yet to be shown (Ingle, year not printed; Peters et al, 2021).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: heat-treated NiTi resists fracture better.** *Textbooks:* M-wire by almost 400% in cyclic fatigue (Jain, 2016); heat-treated alloys more flexible with higher fatigue resistance (Peters et al, 2021). *Assessment:* the direction is consistent between books, but the 400% figure is a manufacturer's claim and the supporting studies are laboratory fatigue tests, so the clinical significance is unknown. Both books also note that more martensitic files deform plastically, so their behaviour at failure differs. [Likely]

**Claim:** reciprocation is safer than continuous rotation. Peters et al say the change has been generally successful in preventing threading-in and fracture (Peters et al, 2021), and Terauchi and Renton report longer life and higher cyclic fatigue resistance (Terauchi and Renton, 2021) [SRC]. These are laboratory fatigue findings; neither book shows that fewer files separate in patients, and in vitro shaping is described as similar to rotation. **Conclusion:** reciprocation is a reasonable choice for severe curves, but its clinical advantage is not established by these sources. [Likely]

**Claim 1: torque-controlled motors reduce instrument fracture.** *Baumann and Beer:* torque limitation reduces loading and improves longevity (Baumann and Beer, 2010). *Peters et al:* some believe it increases safety, others that it mainly benefits inexperienced operators; it probably does not reduce cyclic fatigue fracture (Peters et al, 2021). *Jain:* the ideal is right-torque motors (Jain, 2016). *Assessment:* torque control addresses torsional overload, not cyclic fatigue; the supporting studies were not read. *Conclusion:* use it as one safeguard, not a guarantee. [Likely]

**Claim:** active (non-landed) designs prepare canals better than passive ones. Peters et al state that radial-landed files are safe but less efficient and that active files cut better with more preparation errors (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri agrees that active files cut aggressively and straighten (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Efficacy depends on the measure used (speed, centring, debris removal). The books give no head-to-head clinical outcome data, and the in vitro comparisons were not read. **Conclusion:** passive designs trade speed for safety; neither group is shown to give better treatment outcomes. [Likely]

**Overall.** The consistent finding of these books is that rotary instrumentation improved shaping of curved canals and that its main weakness, fatigue fracture, has been addressed by alloy, motor and technique changes; the supporting evidence is largely laboratory-based, and none of the primary studies was read here.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- NiTi: superelastic, shape memory, about 7% reversible strain; low hardness, cuts less than steel.
- Generations: passive radial-land; active; heat-treated; reciprocating single-file; offset centre of rotation.
- Passive (radial land) = safe but reaming; active = efficient but more errors.
- M-wire (2008, ~400% claimed), R-phase (2008), CM wire (2010), gold and blue.
- Fracture: cyclic fatigue (curved canals, no warning) and torsional overload (tip locks).
- Prevent: glide path, crown-down, torque control, about 300 rpm, short use, inspect and discard.
- Reciprocation: large cutting arc (150–170 degrees), small release arc (30–50 degrees); WaveOne, Reciproc.
- No NiTi system cleans all walls or isthmi.
