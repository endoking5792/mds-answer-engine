## 1. Hand instruments: from the nineteenth century to the stainless-steel file

### Explanation

Ingle records that the Gates Glidden drill was introduced in 1885 and the K-file by the Kerr Company in 1904 (Ingle, year not printed) [SRC]; Sikri dates the Kerr patent of the basic hand instrument to 1915, with a 2% taper and 16 mm cutting surface (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The two dates for the K-file differ (1904 and 1915), and Baumann and Beer give the Kerr patent for all K-type instruments as 1915 as well (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC], so the later date is taken as the patent date and the earlier as the development date [Likely]. Baumann and Beer add that hand instruments were first made of carbon steel and have been of stainless steel since the 1960s (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Early instruments were made without pre-established criteria (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Trebitsch in 1929 and Ingle in 1958 suggested standardizing shape, and ISO specifications were published in 1974 (Ingle, year not printed) [SRC]. Hybrid instruments (K-Flex, Flexofile, Flexicut) combined the advantages of K-type with the cutting power of H-type files, and the Flex-R-File of Roane and colleagues (1985) introduced the non-cutting tip (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Date | Development | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1885 | Gates Glidden drill | Ingle, year not printed |
| 1904–1915 | K-file developed (1904) and patented (1915) by Kerr: 2% taper, 16 mm cutting surface | Ingle, year not printed; Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010 |
| 1929, 1958 | Trebitsch and Ingle propose standardization | Ingle, year not printed |
| 1960s | stainless steel replaces carbon steel | Baumann and Beer, 2010 |
| 1974 | ISO specifications published | Ingle, year not printed |
| 1985 | Flex-R-File: non-cutting tip (Roane et al) | Baumann and Beer, 2010 |

**The hand instruments** (Baumann and Beer, 2010). All hand instruments are based on three forms: K reamers, K files and Hedström files. Small K-type instruments have a square cross-section, which is stiffer and cuts less because of less chip space (36%); larger ones are triangular with wider chip space (60%), more flexible but more liable to fracture. Hedström files are milled from round steel stock. Standardization was needed because, before it, instruments of the same size differed significantly in configuration (Sikri, 2019).

**Hybrids.** K-Flex, Flexofile and Flexicut combined K-type flexibility with H-type cutting (Baumann and Beer, 2010). **Greater taper and half sizes** later departed from the 2% standard (Sikri, 2019). **Profile 29**: a constant 29% size increase (Sikri, 2019). Flutes were ground or milled instead of twisted to reduce stress (Sikri, 2019).

## 2. Engine-driven systems with stainless steel

### Explanation

Ingle notes that Oltramare described the first rotary system for root canal preparation in 1892 and that Rollins's handpiece rotated at about 100 rpm, like the motors used today with NiTi (Ingle, year not printed) [SRC]; Baumann and Beer date Rollins's handpiece to 1899 (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The 1928 Cursor filing contra-angle combined rotational and vertical motion; the Racer followed in 1958 and the Giromatic in 1964, both with reciprocal motion (Ingle, year not printed) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer give the Racer date as the 1950s and the Giromatic as 1964, and record frequent wedging and fracture with the Racer (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Stainless-steel engine-driven systems very often led to ledges, perforation or fracture and are of virtually no role today (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; see I2 for detail.

#### ✎ Exam core

- 1892: Oltramare described the first rotary system (Ingle, year not printed).
- 1899: Rollins's engine-driven handpiece, about 100 rpm, 360-degree rotation (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Ingle, year not printed).
- 1928: Cursor filing contra-angle, combining rotation and vertical motion (Ingle, year not printed).
- 1950s: Racer (vertical motion; 1958 in Ingle's account), with wedging and fracture of instruments (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- 1964: Giromatic, reciprocal 90-degree rotary motion (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Ingle, year not printed).
- ENDOflash: torque-dependent rotational speed within the handpiece (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Verdict: rigid motion with stainless steel gave ledges, perforation and fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Why this era ended.** The handpieces gave a limited range of motion and the stainless-steel instruments they drove could not follow curves, so ledges, perforation and fracture occurred (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Even the clutch, which changed the motion when friction increased, did not solve the problem. The era's legacy was the reciprocating quarter-turn motion and the idea of torque-dependent control, both of which reappear in modern NiTi reciprocation and motors [Likely] (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Peters et al, 2021).

## 3. Sonic and ultrasonic instruments

### Explanation

Ingle records that Richman introduced ultrasonics and sonics in 1957 and that Martin and Cunningham made ultrasonic devices popular in the 1970s (Ingle, year not printed) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer define sonic waves as 16 Hz–20 kHz and ultrasonic waves as above 20 kHz; research aimed at replacing manual instrumentation, but the primary uses today are intracanal obstructions, retreatment and retrograde work, and only passive ultrasonic irrigation has stood the test of time (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Apical microsurgery without ultrasonics and the microscope is no longer imaginable (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. See I8.

#### ✎ Exam core

- 1957: Richman introduces ultrasonics and sonics in endodontics (Ingle, year not printed).
- 1970s: Martin and Cunningham popularise ultrasonic devices (Ingle, year not printed).
- Today: passive ultrasonic irrigation; access, retreatment, removal of obstructions; microsurgery (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Canal shaping with ultrasonics no longer justified (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Tip types (Sikri, 2019): access refinement; vibratory tips for post and core; bulk removal tips; troughing tips for instrument retrieval; surgical retro-tips.

## 4. The nickel-titanium era

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer date the first NiTi endodontic instruments to the late 1980s (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Peters et al state that with NiTi rotaries in the early 1990s a much more flexible alloy allowed continuous rotation and reduced both canal preparation errors and instrument fracture compared with earlier engine-driven techniques (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC], and that more than 100 rotary systems are now marketed, varying greatly in design, alloy and cutting movement (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Jain and Sikri group them in generations (Jain, 2016; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; the two classifications are listed in I4 (section 7) and are summarised below.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Generations of NiTi files** (Jain, 2016; Sikri, 2019)
| Generation | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| First (mid to late 1990s) | passive cutting, radial lands, fixed tapers, many instruments | ProFile, GT, LightSpeed |
| Second (2001) | active cutting edges, fewer instruments, some electropolished | Flexmaster, EndoSequence, BioRaCe, ProTaper, HERO |
| Third (2007) | thermomechanically treated; less cyclic fatigue | K3, Twisted File, GT Vortex, HyFlex |
| Fourth | reciprocation; single-file | M4, Endo-Eze; SAF, WaveOne, Reciproc |
| Fifth | offset centre of rotation; more flexible | ProTaper Next, One Shape, Revo-S |

**The first rotary NiTi systems** (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010)
- The first commercially successful rotary instruments were ProFile, LightSpeed and GT; the first commercial systems also included Mity Roto and Power-R, all with flattened cutting edges ("radial lands").
- LightSpeed (early 1990s): thin, non-cutting shaft and a short anterior cutting head.
- ProFile: U-shaped cross-section with radial lands, slightly negative rake, reaming rather than cutting.
- K3: slightly positive rake, wide radial lands and a third land to prevent threading-in.
- ProTaper: originally six instruments, three shaping and three finishing files; convex triangle, no radial lands; progressive tapers; faster than GT, ProFile and Quantec in plastic blocks, with somewhat more aberrations.
- HERO: positive rake angles for cutting efficiency; RaCe: alternating cutting edges; EndoSequence: alternating wall contact points to reduce torque.
- Twisted File (R-phase) and Vortex (M-wire; Vortex Blue from blue wire): heat-treated alloys.
- Mtwo: clinically an example of the single-length technique.

**Trend in design.** Radial-landed files were safe but reamed rather than cut; later active designs cut better but carried more risk of preparation errors (Peters et al, 2021).

Sikri's list places WaveOne among heat-treated third-generation files, and Jain places it in the fourth (reciprocating) generation; the examples differ between books.

## 5. Alloy and surface advances

### Explanation

Heat treatment changed the alloy: R-phase wire in 2008, M-wire with GTX in 2008, and CM wire in 2010, each claimed to improve flexibility and cyclic fatigue resistance (Jain, 2016) [SRC]; ProTaper Next, made from M-wire, has better mechanical properties than ProTaper Universal according to current research (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Details are in I4.

#### ✎ Exam core

- 2008: GTX, the first commercial M-wire system; R-phase wire (Twisted File, K3XF) (Jain, 2016).
- 2010: controlled-memory wire (HyFlex, Typhoon) (Jain, 2016).
- Result: more flexible, more fatigue-resistant files; ProTaper Next (M-wire) better than ProTaper Universal (Peters et al, 2021).

## 6. Motors and torque control

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer and Jain describe the move from simple electric motors to torque-limited and then apex-locator motors (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Jain, 2016) [SRC]; Peters et al date the simple first-generation motors to the early 1990s (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. See I2.

#### ✎ Exam core

Motor generations (Jain, 2016): first, no torque control; second, fully electronic with sensitive torque limiter; third, simple torque-controlled; fourth, built-in apex locator and torque control. The first electric motors for rotary NiTi appeared in the early 1990s (Peters et al, 2021). Torque limitation and auto-reverse were introduced to reduce fracture (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

Later handpieces incorporate gear systems that regulate torque by file size, so a separate torque motor is not needed (Jain, 2016). Motors can also be programmed for reciprocation with chosen angles (Peters et al, 2021).

## 7. Reciprocation and the single-file concept

### Explanation

Peters et al explain that replacing continuous rotation by reciprocation mitigates taper lock, fatigue fracture and threading-in, and that WaveOne and Reciproc were designed for it (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Their shaping ability is similar to continuous-rotation systems in vitro (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. See I5.

#### ✎ Exam core

WaveOne (triangular cross-section) and Reciproc (two flutes), driven by motors alternating about 150–170 degrees counterclockwise with 30–50 degrees clockwise, reduce taper lock and threading-in; shaping equals continuous rotation in vitro (Peters et al, 2021). The books label the cutting direction differently (see I5).

WaveOne is available in tip sizes 21, 25 and 40 with tapers .06 and .08; Reciproc in sizes 25, 40 and 50 with tapers .08, .06 and .05 (Peters et al, 2021). Single-use single-file systems simplify preparation and reduce fatigue (Sikri, 2019).

## 8. Conforming and limited-diameter designs; the self-adjusting file

### Explanation

Ingle dates the SAF to 2010 (Ingle, year not printed) [SRC]; Sikri describes it as a hollow compressible lattice (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and Peters et al report more wall contact than rotary files in vitro (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen also describes "conforming" shaping with a limited maximum flute diameter in TRUShape and XP-Shaper (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC], and that XP files change conformation between room and body temperature (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- *SAF* (2010): hollow NiTi lattice that adapts to the canal cross-section (Ingle, year not printed; Sikri, 2019); more wall contact than rotary files in vitro (Peters et al, 2021). See I9.
- *Conforming designs*: an adaptable, larger envelope of motion with a limited maximum flute diameter, for example TRUShape (maximum fluted diameter 0.8 mm) and XP-Shaper (Peters et al, 2021).
- *XP files*: a NiTi alloy that changes conformation between room and body temperature (Peters et al, 2021).

## 9. Direction of development and the limits of the evidence

### Explanation

Peters et al conclude that differences exist in tip designs, cross-sections and manufacturing, that an attractive goal is instrumentation of more canal surface and preserving more dentin, and that meaningful differences in clinical outcomes in regard to specific design variations have yet to be ascertained (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer note that instrument fracture, while not itself a professional error, must be disclosed to the patient (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Trends.** Greater taper and active cutting (second generation); heat-treated alloys; reciprocation and single-file techniques; conforming shapes with limited flute diameter to preserve dentin; torque-controlled motors with apex locators (Jain, 2016; Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Caution.** Differences in clinical outcomes among design variations have yet to be ascertained (Peters et al, 2021). Fracture of rotary NiTi instruments remains a dreaded complication (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 9A. Vocabulary of instrument design

#### ✎ Exam core

Terms used when comparing instruments (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010):
- *Helical angle*: the angle the leading edge forms with the long axis of the file.
- *Pitch*: the distance between corresponding points on adjacent leading edges; many NiTi rotaries have a variable pitch.
- *Rake angle*: positive (cutting), neutral or negative (scraping).
- *Radial land*: a flat guiding surface behind the cutting edge in passive designs.
- *Chip space*: space between the flutes; it determines how much debris is carried before the file jams.
- *Tip*: NiTi rotary files typically have rounded, non-cutting tips.
- *Taper*: the increase in diameter per millimetre, from 2% in ISO files to 12% or more.

## 10. Supporting instruments

### Explanation

The journey also includes instruments that serve the main files. Cohen describes the Gates Glidden drill as having a long, thin shaft with parallel walls and a short, oval cutting head with safety tips, tip diameters 0.5 to 1.1 mm, made in stainless steel and NiTi, and calls them inexpensive, safe and clinically beneficial tools when used adequately (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Retreatment files were added to the armamentarium as dedicated gutta-percha removal instruments (GPX, ProTaper Universal retreatment files, Mtwo R) with cutting tips (Roda et al, 2021; Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. See I10.

#### ✎ Exam core

- *Gates Glidden drill* (1885): long thin shaft, short oval head with safety tip; tip diameters 0.5–1.1 mm; stainless steel and NiTi; inexpensive and safe when used adequately (Ingle, year not printed; Peters et al, 2021).
- *Retreatment files*: ProTaper Universal retreatment files, Mtwo R and GPX, with cutting tips for penetrating the root filling (Roda et al, 2021). See I10.
- *Sonic and ultrasonic tips*: for access, retreatment and irrigation activation (Baumann and Beer, 2010). See I8.

## 11. Landmarks in summary

#### ✎ Exam core

| Era | Instrument and alloy | Motion | Fate and lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| to 1960s | hand files, carbon then stainless steel | manual | standardization (1958 proposal; ISO 1974) (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Ingle, year not printed) |
| 1899–1980s | stainless-steel files in engine handpieces | vertical, quarter-turn reciprocation | ledges, perforation, fracture; abandoned (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| 1957 onward | ultrasonic and sonic tips | oscillation | irrigation activation, access, retreatment (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| from 1988 | NiTi rotary files | 360-degree rotation | curved canals shaped with fewer errors, but fatigue fracture (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| 1990s | first commercial rotary NiTi: LightSpeed, ProFile, GT, Mity Roto, Power-R | passive, radial-landed, fixed taper; several files per canal | safe but slow reaming; led to active designs (Peters et al, 2021; Jain, 2016) |
| 2000s | heat-treated NiTi (M-wire, R-phase, CM) | rotation | more flexible; claimed greater fatigue resistance (Jain, 2016) |
| 2010 onward | SAF, reciprocating single files, conforming shapes | vibration, reciprocation | simplification, more wall contact; outcome benefit not shown (Peters et al, 2021) |

**Fatigue and torsion remain the central problems of the NiTi era.** Rotary instruments fracture by cyclic fatigue or by torsion (Baumann and Beer, 2010), cyclic fatigue being more prevalent in curved canals (Terauchi and Renton, 2021).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim:** each generation of instruments has improved treatment. The books document clear technical gains: stainless-steel engine-driven systems gave ledges and fractures and were abandoned, whereas NiTi reduced preparation errors and fracture compared with them (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Beyond that comparison, Peters et al state that clinical outcome differences among designs have yet to be shown (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. **Conclusion:** the large step was from rigid stainless-steel automation to flexible NiTi; the later refinements are supported by laboratory data, not by outcome studies in these books. [Likely]

## What the journey means for practice

#### ✎ Exam core

1. Choose the instrument for the canal, not the fashion: radial-landed passive files are safe but slow; active files cut faster and carry more risk of preparation errors (Peters et al, 2021).
2. Control fracture: establish a glide path, use torque-controlled motors, short use and inspection, and discard files after use in severe curves (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Terauchi and Renton, 2021).
3. Keep expectations realistic: no NiTi system, reciprocating or rotary, cleans all walls and isthmi (Ingle, year not printed), so irrigation activation and ultrasonic tips complement the files (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
4. Treat claims of newer designs with caution: outcome differences have yet to be shown (Peters et al, 2021).
