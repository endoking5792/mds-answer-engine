## 1. Hand instruments: forms and modifications

### Explanation

Baumann and Beer state that all hand instruments are based on three forms: K drills (reamers), K files and Hedström files (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The square cross-section is stiffer, with less chip space (36%), while the triangular section is more flexible with wider chip space (60%) but more prone to fracture; Hedström files are milled from round steel stock (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Hybrids combined the flexibility of K-type files with the aggressive cutting of H files; the Flex-R-File had a non-cutting tip, after it was recognised that sharp edges at the tip can cause problems (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri adds that flutes are now ground or milled instead of twisted, to reduce internal stress (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Instrument | Cross-section | Chip space | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| K reamer / K file | square (small sizes) or triangular (larger) | 36% square; 60% triangular | square: stiffer, cuts less; triangular: more flexible, more fracture-prone (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| Hedström file | round, milled | | sharp cutting edges; milled from round stock (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| Hybrids (K-Flex, Flexofile, Flex-R) | rhomboid or triangular | | flexibility of K with cutting of H; Flex-R has a non-cutting tip (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |

Manufacture: flutes are ground or milled rather than twisted, to reduce internal stress (Sikri, 2019).

## 2. Design characteristics and their effects

### Explanation

Peters et al define the helical angle as the angle the leading edge forms with the long axis, and pitch as the distance between corresponding points on adjacent leading edges. A smaller pitch means more spirals and a greater helix angle, and many NiTi rotaries have a variable pitch (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. The rake angle may be positive (cutting), neutral (leading edge at 90 degrees to the surface) or negative (scraping), and the cutting angle is a better indicator of cutting ability (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. NiTi rotary files typically have rounded non-cutting tips (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer add that chip space determines how much debris can be removed before the file jams, and that taper has gone from none (LightSpeed) to 2, 4 and 6% and above, with reverse-taper designs thicker at the tip (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Design variables** (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010)
- *Tip*: rounded, non-cutting tips on NiTi rotary files help prevent preparation errors (Peters et al, 2021).
- *Taper*: none (LightSpeed), 2, 4 and 6% and up to 19% (Baumann and Beer, 2010); reverse taper (thicker at the tip) in some designs.
- *Cross-section*: U-shape, triangular, convex triangle, S-shape, with or without radial lands.
- *Helical angle and pitch*: smaller pitch gives more spirals; variable pitch is common.
- *Rake angle*: positive (cutting), neutral (90 degrees) or negative (scraping); positive rake cuts more efficiently, negative gives reaming.
- *Chip space*: determines how much debris is removed before the file binds.
- *Core*: the central part of the file seen in longitudinal section; the outer diameter increases from the tip toward the handle (Peters et al, 2021).

**Cross-section trends** (Baumann and Beer, 2010): the convex (expanded) triangle of FlexMaster and ProTaper; the square section, still common in steel, survives in NiTi only in RaCe; many instruments have a progressive distance between cutting edges (ProTaper, Mtwo).

**Recent strategies** (Peters et al, 2021): "conforming" shaping, with an adaptable, larger envelope of motion but a limited maximum flute diameter (TRUShape, XP-Shaper). TRUShape sizes 20, 25, 30 and 40 have a maximum fluted diameter of 0.8 mm, which limits coronal dentin removal.

**Why they matter** (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010): more than 100 rotary systems are marketed and they vary greatly in design, alloy and recommended cutting movement; most are made by multiaxial grinding or micromilling. A larger chip space reduces wedging; a smaller pitch gives more spirals and a larger helix angle; the success of a system depends on the cutting efficiency of the file and also on the ease of use and the logic of the file sequence (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 3. Passive and active designs, and relative efficacy

### Explanation

Peters et al divide rotary instruments into group I (designed for passive preparation), group II (active cutting) and group III (unique designs) (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Group I files (ProFile, LightSpeed, GT) have radial lands that guide the file; this makes them fairly safe against preparation errors, but they ream rather than cut and are less efficient (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Group II files lack radial lands, which gives higher cutting efficacy and a higher potential for preparation errors, particularly if taken through the foramen (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri uses the terms passive and active in the same way and says active instruments cut more aggressively and tend to straighten the canal (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; his lists agree with Cohen on ProFile and LightSpeed as passive, and name Flexmaster, RaCe, ProTaper, HERO and K3 as active (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen places K3, with its wide radial lands, in group I, while Sikri lists it with the active files; the two books differ here, and K3 is described by Cohen as having a slightly positive rake angle with radial lands (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri also reports that stainless steel cuts more efficiently (60% more) than NiTi, and that NiTi gives no indication before fracture (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| | Group I: passive (radial lands) | Group II: active (no radial lands) |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | ProFile, LightSpeed, GT; K3 per Cohen | ProTaper, HERO, RaCe; Flexmaster in Sikri's list |
| Action | Reaming, not cutting | Cutting |
| Efficacy | Less efficient | More efficient; faster |
| Safety | Fairly safe against preparation errors | More potential for errors, e.g. ledges and straightening |
| Source | Peters et al, 2021 | Peters et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019 |

Sikri places K3 with active files; Cohen describes it with radial lands (disagreement between books). Group III: unique designs such as reciprocating and conforming files (Peters et al, 2021).

## 4. Individual systems

### Explanation

ProFile has a U-shaped cross-section with radial lands, a 20-degree helix angle, constant pitch, bullet-shaped non-cutting tips and a slightly negative rake angle that gives reaming (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. LightSpeed has a thin non-cutting shaft and a short cutting head, with three radial lands and three U-shaped grooves; it is the only rotary system whose cutting heads have three different geometric shapes (Peters et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. K3 has a slightly positive rake angle, wide radial lands, a peripheral blade relief, and a third land to help prevent threading-in (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. ProTaper has a convex triangle cross-section with sharp edges and no radial lands; the shaping files have tapers that increase coronally and the finishing files the reverse (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. RaCe has alternating cutting edges and EndoSequence alternating wall contact points to reduce torque (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]. Profile 29: Sikri says that, in a scheme in which each size is increased by 29% rather than by 0.05 mm, Schilder named it the Profile 29 series, which gives constant increase and fewer instruments than ISO, but works well only in small sizes (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Baumann and Beer note that a constant 29% remains under discussion (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. SimpliFill is a gutta-percha apical plug system made to be compatible with LightSpeed instrumentation (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **ProFile** (Peters et al, 2021): U-shape, radial lands, 20-degree helix, constant pitch, slightly negative rake, reaming.
- **LightSpeed** (Peters et al, 2021; Sikri, 2019): thin non-cutting shaft, short cutting head, three radial lands and U-grooves.
- **K3** (Peters et al, 2021): positive rake, wide radial lands, third land against threading-in.
- **ProTaper** (Peters et al, 2021): convex triangle, no radial lands; shaping files taper increasing coronally, finishing files reverse.
- **ProTaper detail** (Peters et al, 2021): shaping files 1 and 2 have tip diameters of 0.185 and 0.2 mm, 14 mm blades and partially active tips; finishing files F1–F5 have tip diameters of 0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5 mm. Use a laterally directed brushing stroke away from the furcation. It shaped canals faster than GT, ProFile and Quantec in plastic blocks but with somewhat more aberrations; wide canals may be insufficiently prepared. Current research suggests that the mechanical properties of ProTaper Next, made of M-Wire, are better than those of ProTaper Universal.
- **RaCe** (Peters et al, 2021): alternating cutting edges. **EndoSequence**: alternating wall contact points to reduce torque.
- **Profile 29** (Sikri, 2019): constant 29% size increase; gives constant diameter steps and fewer instruments, but works only in small sizes; under discussion (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- **LightSpeed SimpliFill** (Sikri, 2019): gutta-percha apical plug made to suit LightSpeed shaping.

## 5. Relative efficacy: stainless steel and NiTi

### Explanation

Sikri compares the alloys: NiTi is softer, shows super-elasticity and shape memory, is two to three times more flexible, "resists fracture", and cuts less (stainless steel cuts about 60% more) and gives no indication of fracture, whereas stainless steel fractures easily but gives an indication (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri contradicts himself on fracture: his table lists NiTi as resisting fracture, yet elsewhere he says the low tensile and yield strength of NiTi makes it more susceptible to fracture (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The books on separation (see I11) show that fatigue fracture is a real problem.

#### ✎ Exam core

| Property | NiTi | Stainless steel |
|---|---|---|
| Hardness | softer | harder |
| Flexibility | two to three times more flexible; super-elastic with shape memory | not flexible |
| Cutting efficiency | less | more (about 60% more) |
| Warning before fracture | none | gives indication |

(Sikri, 2019.) **Benefit and cost of rotary NiTi.** The most important advantage is the excellent instrumentation of severely curved canals with savings in preparation time; the problem, compared with hand stainless-steel files, is the high risk of fracture, which makes torque-limited power necessary (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Sikri adds that NiTi has low tensile and yield strength compared with stainless steel, which makes it more susceptible to fracture (Sikri, 2019).

**Overall.** Rotary design represents a trade-off between flexibility, cutting efficiency, safety and fracture resistance; no single design is best in all canals (Peters et al, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim:** active (non-landed) designs prepare canals better than passive ones. Peters et al state that radial-landed files are safe but less efficient and that active files cut better with more preparation errors (Peters et al, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri agrees that active files cut aggressively and straighten (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Efficacy depends on the measure used (speed, centring, debris removal). The books give no head-to-head clinical outcome data, and the in vitro comparisons were not read. **Conclusion:** passive designs trade speed for safety; neither group is shown to give better treatment outcomes. ## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Hand files: K reamer, K file, H file; square 36% and triangular 60% chip space.
- Rake angle: positive cuts, neutral 90 degrees, negative scrapes.
- Radial land: passive and safe, less efficient; no radial land: active and efficient, more errors.
- ProTaper: convex triangle, no lands, progressive tapers.
- Profile 29: constant 29% step; fewer instruments; only for small sizes.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Confusing helix angle, pitch and rake angle.
- Describing ProFile as an aggressive cutter.
- Claiming radial lands give higher efficiency.
- Saying all NiTi files have cutting tips.
