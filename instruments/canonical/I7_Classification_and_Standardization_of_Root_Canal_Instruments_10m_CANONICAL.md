## 1. Classification

### Explanation

Sikri notes that several classifications have been proposed and none is universally accepted (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's classification groups instruments by use, with instruments for root canal preparation divided into hand instruments, power-assisted instruments and electronic measuring devices; Grossman's lists exploring, debriding, shaping and obturating instruments (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The simplest is by sequence of use: diagnostic, exploring, extirpating, shaping, obturating and miscellaneous (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. For shaping instruments, Baumann and Beer state that all hand instruments are based on three forms: K drills (reamers), K files and Hedström files (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification** (Sikri, 2019): none is universally accepted. By sequence of use:
1. Diagnostic
2. Exploring
3. Extirpating
4. Shaping
5. Obturating
6. Miscellaneous

Grossman: exploring, debriding, shaping, obturating. Harty: by phase of treatment, including hand, power-assisted and electronic measuring instruments for canal preparation (Sikri, 2019). Shaping hand instruments are K reamers, K files and H files (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 2. Standardization

### Explanation

Sikri records that early instruments were made without any pre-established criteria, with differences in configuration among instruments of the same size (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The design of the basic hand instrument was patented by Kerr in 1915 with 2% taper and 16 mm cutting surfaces (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Standardization followed Ingle's proposal, as Sikri records it: each number represents the diameter at the tip in hundredths of a millimetre; the flutes begin at the tip (D1) and extend 16 mm up the shaft (D2); D2 is 0.32 mm greater than D1, which gives a constant taper of 0.02 mm per mm; size increases by 0.05 mm at D1 between nos. 10 and 60; and handles are colour coded (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The shaft comes in 21, 25 and 31 mm lengths (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Separately, Sikri notes that ISO covers instruments for preparation (3630-1) and obturation (3630-3), and ANSI numbers K-type files and reamers 28 (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer add that the dimensions follow ISO standards with a colour code for rapid recognition of diameter (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Before standardization.** No fixed criteria for diameter, taper or blade length (Sikri, 2019). Kerr patent, 1915: 2% taper, 16 mm blade (Sikri, 2019).

**Standard** (Ingle's proposal, Sikri, 2019; ISO 3630-1 and ANSI no. 28, Sikri, 2019)
- Number = tip diameter in 1/100 mm (No. 10 = 0.10 mm).
- Blade starts at D1 (tip) and ends at D2, 16 mm up the shaft.
- D2 is 0.32 mm more than D1: constant 0.02 mm/mm taper.
- Size increases 0.05 mm at D1 from no. 10 to 60, then 0.1 mm to 100.
- Colour-coded handles; shaft lengths 21, 25, 31 mm (Sikri, 2019).
- A specified tip angle (75° ± 15° in Sikri's account). D0 and D16 denote length, while D1 and D2 denote diameter (Sikri, 2019).

## 3. Variations and their problems

### Explanation

Sikri describes the later variations: greater-taper instruments (4%, 6%, 8%, 10%) that allow more coronal flaring than the 0.02 standard, and half sizes such as 12.5 and 17.5 (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer point out that ISO numbers increase linearly while the file diameter increases erratically, that a constant increase of 29% (Profile 29) remains under discussion, and that the ISO colour code is still determined by tip diameter and so says nothing about the taper, which may be inverse or multiple. They record Ehrmann's early call for standardization and simplification of file coding (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Variations**
- Greater tapers: 4, 6, 8 and 10% instead of 2% (Sikri, 2019).
- Half sizes: 12.5, 17.5, 22.5, 27.5, 32.5, 37.5 (Sikri, 2019).
- Constant 29% increase in diameter (Profile 29): under discussion (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Limitations of the ISO system** (Baumann and Beer, 2010)
- Size numbers rise linearly but diameters rise unevenly.
- The colour code is still determined by the tip diameter, whereas the taper (0 to 19%, often inverse or multiple) is hard to comprehend (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Calls for simplified coding (Ehrmann, 2002).

## 4. Apical gauging and tuning

### Explanation

Sikri defines apical gauging as measuring the apical diameter of the canal, where the instrument fits snugly and resists further apical movement. It is done with a flexible NiTi instrument inserted straight in and out without rotation (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Apical tuning confirms that the master apical file represents the true apical diameter, by recapitulating with successively larger files; the file that reaches full length represents the true apical diameter (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Apical gauging.** Assess the apical diameter by the file that fits snugly and resists further apical movement; NiTi files are preferred because of their flexibility, inserted straight in and straight out, without rotation, with 17% EDTA used to remove the smear layer (Sikri, 2019).

**Apical tuning.** Confirm that the master apical file shows the true apical diameter; the file that goes full length represents the true apical diameter (Sikri, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Ingle standard: number = tip diameter in 1/100 mm; 16 mm blade; D2 = D1 + 0.32 mm; taper 0.02.
- ISO 3630-1 for preparation instruments, 3630-3 for obturation; ANSI no. 28 K-type.
- The ISO colour code follows tip diameter.
- Gauging and tuning = apical size concepts.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
- A K-file with D1 at the tip, D2 at 16 mm, and the 0.32 mm difference marked. Labels: D1, D2, taper 0.02.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing D1 and D2 as lengths (they are diameters; D0 and D16 are the lengths).
- Forgetting that taper is not part of the ISO colour code.
- Treating gauging and tuning as file-making standards.
