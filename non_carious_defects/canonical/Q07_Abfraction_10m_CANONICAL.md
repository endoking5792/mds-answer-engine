## 1. Definition and history

### Explanation

The definitions differ in how firmly they assert the cause:
- Singh: wedge-shaped defects in the cervical region caused by abnormal occlusal loading (Singh, 2020) [SRC].
- Sturdevant's: strong eccentric occlusal force resulting in microfractures at the cervical area of the tooth (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].
- Garg and Garg: microfractures appearing in enamel as the cervical area flexes under heavy loads (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].
- Pickard's Guide lists abfraction only as a "perhaps" contributor to tooth wear (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

Torres et al derive the word from the Latin *frangere*, to break (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Singh credits Grippo (1991) with proposing the term (Singh, 2020) [SRC]; Olley and Bartlett say it was first suggested by Lee and Eakle (1984) and named by Grippo in 1991 (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Goldstein et al place the tensile-stress concept in the early to mid-1980s (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Marzouk, writing without the term, already lists excessive tensile stress at the tooth cervix as one theory of cervical "erosion" (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], producing the characteristic wedge shape of some lesions (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Abfraction: loss of cervical enamel and dentine attributed to microfracture from tooth flexure under eccentric occlusal load (Ritter et al, 2019; Singh, 2020). Latin *frangere*, to break. Suggested by Lee and Eakle (1984); named by Grippo (1991) (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

## 2. Causes (proposed mechanism and predisposing factors)

### Explanation

**Mechanism.** Grippo's concept, as summarised by Goldstein et al, is that forces may be static (swallowing, clenching) or cyclic (chewing) (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], and that breakdown depends on the magnitude, duration, direction, frequency and location of the forces (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's Box 20.1 gives the chain: flexure, bending, tensile and compressive stresses generated in the cervical region (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], concentrated in the thinnest region of enamel at the CEJ (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Small molecules then enter the microfractures and prevent bonds re-forming (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Cervical enamel is more brittle than occlusal enamel because of DEJ scalloping differences (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Pickard's Guide adds that stresses may open pre-existing cracks (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

**Loads.** Intra-oral loads range from 10 N to 431 N, with 70 N considered a normal functional load (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Predisposing factors** (Goldstein et al, 2018; Singh, 2020) [SRC]:
- Bruxism, clenching and other eccentric loading.
- Heavy contacts on cuspal inclines; loss of canine guidance with group or premolar guidance; slight anterior open bite (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- Abfractions are not generally found in calm individuals with ideal Class I occlusion and canine rise (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], a clinical observation, not a trial result [Likely].
- Co-factors: acid erosion and brushing abrasion, which deepen a stress-initiated lesion.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Causes (proposed)**
- Eccentric or heavy load (bruxism, clenching, premature contacts, loss of canine guidance, open bite) → tooth flexes at the cervical fulcrum (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Tensile (and compressive) stress concentrates at the CEJ, where enamel is thinnest (Ritter et al, 2019) → enamel rods separate, microcracks form; small molecules entering them prevent rebonding.
- Loss depends on magnitude, frequency, direction and duration of load (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Co-factors: erosion and toothbrush abrasion enlarge the lesion.

## 3. The controversy: theory versus clinical evidence

### Explanation

This is where examiners award marks, and the books genuinely disagree. The table records the positions without ranking them.

| Position | Source |
|---|---|
| Sufficient experimental and clinical evidence now establishes tensile stress of occlusal origin as the primary etiology; still highly controversial | (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] |
| Abfraction is a hypothesis from finite element analysis; it has yet to be proven clinically; lesions cannot be differentiated from erosion/abrasion | (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC] |
| The concept is supported only by in vitro studies; clinically abfraction occurs facially, but in vitro location does not follow force direction; interpret with caution | (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] (same chapter, contradicting its own earlier statement) |
| FEA confirms cervical stress but does not confirm causation; proponents point to the correlation between wear facets and lesions | (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC] |
| Limited inconclusive data support the occlusion-abfraction-NCCL link; Sarode's review says the theory is not proven; the term is misleading | (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC] |

**Laboratory data (not clinical evidence).** Without acid, loading had no effect on brushed extracted teeth (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; with cyclic tensile loads in 1% lactic acid, enamel dissolution increased significantly (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt's chapter infers that liquid acid may be necessary to make occlusal loading a factor (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Clinical observations.** Lesions develop on teeth with no opposing teeth (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC], which argues against occlusion as a necessary cause [Likely]. The only longitudinal study Goldstein et al cite followed 55 people for 6 years and found dietary acid and brushing frequency associated with wear (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Goldstein et al conclude abfraction is unlikely to form lesions independently (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. No author claims occlusion is the solitary cause (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Live literature.** A systematic review of 69 studies (31 clinical, 38 laboratory) found 81% reported an association between occlusal stress and NCCLs, but no clinical study showed stress alone caused an NCCL, and 24 of the laboratory studies were FEA (Duangthip et al, 2017) [LIT; abstract only]. Association is not causation, and FEA shows where stress goes, not that it removes tooth [Certain]. A meta-analysis of ancient skulls found NCCLs in 17 of 805 skulls (only 2 of 6 studies reported any) and judged the cause complex, involving abrasion and erosion tied to diet (Dioguardi et al, 2024) [LIT; abstract only].

**Balanced reading.** Occlusal stress is plausible as a contributor that localises or accelerates acid and brushing damage, especially in deep lesions, but not established as an independent clinical cause [Likely]. Olley and Bartlett suggest occlusal stress matters more in extremely deep lesions (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], where it is a risk factor for de-coronation (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Controversy**
- For: FEA shows cervical stress concentration; wear facets often coexist (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Against: FEA and extracted-tooth loading are laboratory data; "yet to be proven clinically" (Bartlett, 2011); lesions form on teeth without antagonists (Mount et al, 2016); loading produced no lesion without acid (Overton et al, 2013).
- Systematic review: association reported, but no clinical study showed stress alone causes NCCLs (Duangthip et al, 2017).
- Consensus: abfraction is at most a co-factor with erosion and abrasion.

## 4. Incidence

### Explanation

Incidence of abfraction as a separate entity cannot be measured reliably, because its lesions cannot be clinically separated from erosion and abrasion (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. The figures in the books are for NCCLs or cervical wear in general:
- Prevalence ranges from rare to 89% across groups (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- In one Croatian sample, only 5% of subjects younger than 26 had cervical wear (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], against 48% of those older than 65 (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- A systematic review gives an average adult NCCL prevalence of 46.7% (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].
- The association of occlusal wear facets with NCCLs varies from 15% upwards across studies (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Summitt's chapter gives figures of 15%, 38% and 95% and attributes the spread to differing definitions of wear facets, populations and exclusion criteria (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- Historical: none in 3,927 teeth from Copper Age and medieval skulls (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; 17 of 805 ancient skulls in a meta-analysis (Dioguardi et al, 2024) [LIT; abstract only].
- Premolars are the teeth most commonly affected (Singh, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Incidence**
- No separate figure for abfraction: its lesions cannot be distinguished clinically (Bartlett, 2011). For NCCLs overall: rare to 89% across populations; 5% under 26 years versus 48% over 65 years in one survey (Overton et al, 2013); mean adult prevalence 46.7% (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).
- Premolars commonest (Singh, 2020).

## 5. Clinical features

### Explanation

- Sharp, angular, wedge-shaped defect, which may be completely subgingival (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- At or near the fulcrum (Singh, 2020) [SRC]; size depends on the magnitude and frequency of tensile force (Singh, 2020) [SRC].
- Torres et al: partly or completely under the gum (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; can involve a single tooth while abrasion involves many neighbours (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC].
- Without associated gingival recession in its commonest form (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; recession is not a hallmark (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- Wear facets or loss of anterior guidance highly probable (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; a heavy marking on a cusp incline is likely (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- Cervical Class V restorations without retention grooves may be lost (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Clinical features:** sharp wedge-shaped cervical notch with angular occlusal margin, often on a single premolar, may be subgingival, recession not typical; wear facets and heavy marking on a cusp incline; hypersensitivity; repeated loss of Class V restorations (Goldstein et al, 2018; Alani and Patel, 2025; Ritter et al, 2019).

## 6. Management

### Explanation

**Diagnose and control the load first.** A definitive plan needs an occlusal analysis with articulated diagnostic models (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and Goldstein et al insist on treating the cause before restoring (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Measures: selective reshaping so forces are directed along the long axis (Singh, 2020) [SRC]; occlusal adjustment and/or splints to reduce heavy contacts and bruxism (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; a nocturnal occlusal guard (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Control co-factors: diet acids and brushing technique (see Q4). Evidence caveat: there are no controlled clinical trials showing that occlusal adjustment improves Class 5 restoration longevity (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Restore when indicated** (depth, sensitivity, aesthetics, progression; see Q5). Goldstein et al report relatively high failure rates when the occlusal problem is not corrected (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Material choice is disputed:
- Low-modulus argument: microfilled composites, slightly more flexible than hybrids, are among restorative options (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Torres and Zanatta say elastic modulus is relevant in abfraction lesions (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC].
- Clinical data: no difference in retention between microfilled and hybrid composites (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; flexible flowables did not outperform stiff restorations (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; glass-ionomer materials show the highest retention rates (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- When a restoration is lost, Mount et al advise examining the occlusion because abfraction stresses may be involved (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Periodontal**: root-coverage graft where recession coexists (see Q5, Q3).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Management**
1. Occlusal analysis on articulated casts; history of bruxism, diet, brushing (Ritter et al, 2019).
2. Remove the cause: selective reshaping, occlusal adjustment, night guard/splint (Singh, 2020; Goldstein et al, 2018). No trials yet prove this prolongs restorations (Overton et al, 2013).
3. Control co-factors: acid diet, horizontal brushing.
4. Monitor small lesions; desensitise.
5. Restore deep, sensitive or progressing lesions: GIC/RMGI (best retention) or composite; microfill and flowable showed no retention advantage (Overton et al, 2013).
6. Graft if recession coexists.

**Conclusion:** control load and co-factors, then restore.

## High-yield points
- Suggested by Lee and Eakle (1984); named by Grippo (1991); Latin *frangere*.
- Mechanism: flexure → cervical tensile stress → enamel microfracture at the CEJ.
- Evidence is mostly FEA and in vitro; clinical causation unproven (Bartlett, 2011).
- Goldstein's chapter contradicts itself: "established" and "only in vitro" in successive paragraphs.
- No separate incidence for abfraction; NCCLs 46.7% average adult prevalence.
- Features: sharp wedge, single tooth, possibly subgingival, wear facets.
- Control occlusion before restoring; GIC/RMGI best retained; low-modulus composite claims not supported by trials.

## Common exam mistakes
- Presenting abfraction as proven; FEA and extracted-tooth studies are laboratory evidence only.
- Quoting an "incidence of abfraction" figure; the books give NCCL or cervical wear prevalence.
- Restoring without occlusal analysis or co-factor control.
- Recommending microfilled or flowable composite "because it flexes" without noting clinical trials found no retention advantage.
- Attributing the term to Grippo without mentioning Lee and Eakle's earlier tensile-stress hypothesis.
