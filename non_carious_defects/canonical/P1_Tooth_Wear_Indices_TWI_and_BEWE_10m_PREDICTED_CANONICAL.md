## 1. Why tooth wear indices are needed

### Explanation

Tooth wear is irreversible and often slow, so a clinician needs a numerical record that can be repeated and compared. Pickard's describes tooth-wear indices as clinical scoring systems that give objective numerical scores to be reassessed later; it recommends their use in research and epidemiological studies (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC] but warns that they are often complicated and time-consuming in a busy dental practice (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. This tension, research detail versus chairside simplicity, is exactly the difference between the two indices in this question [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Tooth wear indices give objective, repeatable scores of tooth surface loss to (1) record severity, (2) monitor progression, (3) guide management and (4) allow epidemiological comparison. Detailed indices suit research but are time-consuming in practice (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). The two best known are the Smith and Knight Tooth Wear Index (TWI, 1984) and the Basic Erosive Wear Examination (BEWE, 2008).

## 2. Smith and Knight Tooth Wear Index (1984)

### Explanation

Olley and Bartlett credit Smith and Knight with first distinguishing physiological from active or pathological tooth wear (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Goldstein et al call the TWI, published by Smith and Knight in 1984, one of the most frequently cited indices (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. It is aetiology-free: it measures loss, not cause, and scores buccal (B), lingual (L), occlusal (O), incisal (I) and cervical surfaces on a 0-4 scale. Goldstein's Table 22.1 gives the criteria:

| Score | B/L/O/I surfaces | Incisal | Cervical |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | No loss of enamel surface characteristics | as B/L/O | No change in contour |
| 1 | Loss of enamel surface characteristics (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] | as B/L/O | Minimal loss of contour |
| 2 | Loss of enamel exposing dentin for less than one-third of surface (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] | Loss of enamel just exposing dentin | Defect less than 1 mm deep (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] |
| 3 | Loss of enamel exposing dentin for more than one-third of surface | Loss of enamel and substantial loss of dentin, not exposing pulp | Defect 1-2 mm deep (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] |
| 4 | Complete loss of enamel, pulp exposure, or exposure of secondary dentin (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] | Pulp exposure or exposure of secondary dentin | Defect more than 2 mm deep, pulp exposure, or exposure of secondary dentin (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] |

Every surface of every tooth is scored and the scores are compared with age-related thresholds of acceptable wear to label wear as pathological [RECALL]. Pickard's lists the Smith and Knight Tooth Wear Index among its further-reading keywords (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC] but does not reproduce it.

**Limitations.** Donachie and Walls found the scoring criteria difficult to apply in the elderly without additional qualification, found the five-point scale inadequate for extreme wear, and challenged the TWI concept of "pathological" levels of wear (Donachie and Walls, 1996) [LIT]. Because every surface is scored it is slow chairside, which fits Pickard's general warning (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Singh and Gade print a simpler 0-4 index for attrition only (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC], which should not be confused with the TWI.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Smith and Knight TWI (1984)**
- First index to separate physiological from pathological wear (Olley and Bartlett, 2024); most frequently cited (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Measures wear irrespective of cause; all surfaces (buccal, lingual, occlusal, incisal, cervical) of all teeth scored 0-4:

| Score | B/L/O/I | Cervical |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | No loss of surface characteristics | No change in contour |
| 1 | Loss of surface characteristics | Minimal loss of contour |
| 2 | Dentine exposed, less than 1/3 of surface | Defect less than 1 mm |
| 3 | Dentine exposed, more than 1/3 | Defect 1-2 mm |
| 4 | Complete enamel loss, pulp or secondary dentine exposed | Over 2 mm, pulp or secondary dentine exposed |

- *Limitations:* slow; difficult in the elderly; 0-4 scale too coarse for extreme wear; its "pathological" thresholds are disputed (Donachie and Walls, 1996).

## 3. Basic Erosive Wear Examination (BEWE, 2008)

### Explanation

Bartlett, Ganss and Lussi designed the BEWE as a simple tool for general practice that can also be compared with more discriminative indices; the most severely affected surface in each sextant is recorded on a four-level score and the cumulative score is matched to risk levels that guide management (Bartlett et al, 2008) [LIT]. Singh and Gade date it to 2008, spelling the author "Bartleet" (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]; Pickard's reproduces the tables from Bartlett's 2010 BDJ paper, "A proposed system for screening tooth wear" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Pickard's compares it to the BPE for periodontal disease (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

**Clinical sequence** (Pickard's Table 2.7): diagnose wear and eliminate teeth with trauma and developmental defects from the score (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; examine all surfaces; identify the most severely affected tooth; calculate the score. Note an internal inconsistency: Table 2.7 says identify the worst tooth "in each quadrant" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], whereas Table 2.9 adds the scores of all sextants (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], and the original abstract specifies sextants (Bartlett et al, 2008) [LIT]. Write sextants.

**Scores per sextant:** 0 no erosive wear; 1 initial loss of surface texture; 2 distinct defect, hard tissue loss under 50% of the surface; 3 hard tissue loss of 50% or more. The two books differ at the boundary: Pickard's gives "≥ 50%" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], Singh and Gade ">50%" (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC].

**Risk levels and management** (Pickard's Table 2.9; Singh and Gade Box 21.1):

| Level | Cumulative score | Management |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (no risk) | 2 or less | Routine maintenance and observation; repeat at 3-year intervals (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC] |
| 1 (low) | 3-8 | Oral hygiene and dietary assessment and advice; repeat at 2-year intervals (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC] |
| 2 (medium) | 9-13 | Identify main aetiological factors; consider fluoridation measures; ideally avoid restorations and monitor with casts, photographs or silicone impressions (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; repeat every 6-12 months |
| 3 (high) | 14 or more | As level 2, plus fluoridation or other strategies to increase surface resistance (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; in severe progression consider special care that may involve restorations (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC] |

The high-risk cut-off differs: Pickard's "≥ 14", Singh and Gade "more than 14" (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC].

**Use.** In a large European study described by Olley and Bartlett, 29% of subjects had tooth wear recorded with the BEWE (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], and the BEWE is now recommended in UK practice following government guidance (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**BEWE (Bartlett, Ganss and Lussi, 2008)**
- Simple screening index for general practice, analogous to the BPE (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- *Method:* exclude teeth with trauma or developmental defects; examine all surfaces; score the most severely affected surface in each sextant; add the six scores.
- *Scores:* 0 no erosive wear; 1 initial loss of surface texture; 2 distinct defect, loss less than 50% of surface; 3 loss of 50% or more ("more than 50%" in Singh and Gade, 2020).

| Cumulative | Risk | Management (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) |
|---|---|---|
| 0-2 | None | Routine maintenance; repeat 3-yearly |
| 3-8 | Low | Oral hygiene and diet advice; repeat 2-yearly |
| 9-13 | Medium | Find cause, fluoride, avoid restorations, monitor with casts/photographs; 6-12 monthly |
| 14 or more | High | As above; restore in severe progression; 6-12 monthly |

## 4. Comparison and use in practice

### Explanation

The TWI is detailed, cause-blind and suited to epidemiology; the BEWE is quick, tied to management and suited to practice, but it records only the worst surface per sextant, so it is a screening tool rather than a precise measure [Likely]. Neither replaces dated records: Pickard's recommends serial dated study models (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC] and notes that laser scanning of serial models is a research tool (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Comparison:** TWI is detailed and suits research; BEWE is quick, sextant-based and linked to management. Both measure severity, not cause, so pair them with dated casts or photographs at recall.

## High-yield points
- Smith and Knight 1984: first to separate physiological from pathological wear; every surface scored 0-4; cervical depths under 1 mm, 1-2 mm, over 2 mm.
- TWI criticised in the elderly and for extreme wear (Donachie and Walls, 1996).
- BEWE 2008 (Bartlett, Ganss, Lussi): worst surface per sextant, 0-3, summed.
- Risk: 0-2 none, 3-8 low, 9-13 medium, 14 or more high; recall 3-yearly, 2-yearly, 6-12 monthly.
- Books differ on boundaries: score 3 "≥50%" (Pickard's) vs ">50%" (Singh); high risk "≥14" vs "more than 14".
- BEWE excludes trauma and developmental defects.

## Common exam mistakes
- Scoring BEWE per quadrant, or adding all teeth instead of the worst surface per sextant.
- Treating the TWI as a cause-based classification; it records loss only.
- Forgetting to exclude trauma and developmental defects from the BEWE.
- Quoting one BEWE cut-off as universal without noting the "≥" versus ">" difference.
