## 1. Definition, histology and why the lesion looks white

### Explanation

A white spot lesion (WSL) is, in the strict sense, the first visible sign of enamel caries. Kidd and Fejerskov describe the early active enamel lesion as one that often appears chalky white, "also called 'white spot' lesion" (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]; Sturdevant's adds that non-cavitated enamel lesions are also referred to as white spot lesions (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. In everyday clinical usage the term is stretched to any white enamel opacity, including fluorosis and other developmental defects, which is why the question asks about "etiology" in the plural and why differentiation (section 3) carries marks [Likely].

**Histology.** Sections through an active WSL show that the mineral loss is subsurface, under a surface zone whose thickness varies considerably (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Modern Operative Dentistry gives the classical four zones with their pore volumes: a relatively intact surface zone of about 1% porosity and a subsurface lesion body of 5-25% (Torres, 2020) [SRC], with a dark zone of 2-4% (Torres, 2020) [SRC] and an innermost translucent zone at the advancing front. Kidd's figure caption puts the white spot pore volume at more than 5% on a smooth surface (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Why is the surface zone preserved? In the pH range 5.5-4.5 saliva is undersaturated for hydroxyapatite but supersaturated for fluoridated apatite, so mineral leaves the interior while fluoridated apatite redeposits at the surface (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]; paradoxically, the surface over a WSL contains more fluoride than the surrounding sound enamel (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Under SEM the surface of an active lesion is "moth-eaten" (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC], which is why it feels rough to a gently drawn probe.

**Optics: why white.** Intact enamel is optically homogeneous; the lesion is a sponge of pores filled with water. Modern Operative Dentistry's cariology chapter gives enamel a refractive index of 1.62 (Torres, 2020) [SRC] against water at 1.33 (Torres, 2020) [SRC]; at each mineral-water interface light is scattered, trapped in an "optical maze" that is over-luminous and therefore perceived as white (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. Drying replaces water with air, whose index is 1 (Torres, 2020) [SRC], so scattering increases and a small lesion appears or enlarges. The same principle underlies resin infiltration: replacing the water with a resin whose index is close to enamel removes the scattering (section 6). The books do not agree on the enamel figure: 1.62 in the cariology chapter of Modern Operative Dentistry and in Summitt's (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC], but 1.65 in the infiltration chapter of the same Modern Operative Dentistry (Torres, 2020) [SRC].

Clinically useful corollaries follow from the optics. A WSL visible only after air drying is most likely limited to the outer half of enamel (Torres, 2020) [SRC]; one visible on a wet surface indicates demineralisation over halfway through enamel, possibly into dentine (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt's adds the prognostic point: the dry-only lesion has a better chance of complete reversal, while the wet-visible lesion may only be arrested (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. The surface zone is the patient's best friend: if it is breached, for example by probing with a dental instrument, bacteria invade the lesion body (Torres, 2020) [SRC], which is why sharp explorers should not be pushed into WSLs.

#### ✎ Exam core
**Introduction.** A white spot lesion is the earliest clinically visible, non-cavitated enamel caries lesion: an opaque, chalky area due to subsurface demineralisation (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016; Ritter et al, 2019). Clinically the term also covers developmental white opacities (fluorosis, MIH, hypoplasia), which must be differentiated.

**Histology** (Torres, 2020): four zones from surface inwards:
1. Surface zone: relatively intact, about 1% pore volume, fluoride-rich, maintained by redeposition from saliva.
2. Lesion body: 5-25% pores, maximum mineral loss.
3. Dark zone: 2-4%.
4. Translucent zone: advancing front.
Shape: cone with base at the surface on smooth surfaces.

**Why white:** enamel refractive index about 1.62, water 1.33, air 1.0. Pores scatter light at many interfaces, so the lesion looks opaque white; on drying, air increases scattering, so lesions look larger (Torres, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013). Visible only when dry: outer half of enamel, may fully reverse; visible wet: deeper, may only arrest.

## 2. Etiology of carious white spot lesions, including post-orthodontic lesions

### Explanation

The carious WSL is a biofilm disease of stagnation sites. Lesions form where plaque is left undisturbed: cervical margins, below contact points, pits and fissures, and around appliances. Sturdevant's gives the time scale: poor oral hygiene and frequent sucrose or acidic food can produce white spot lesions in 3 weeks (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and progression to cavitation on smooth surfaces is estimated at 18 months (±6 months) (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Pickard's caries-risk checklist also lists socio-economic status among the social factors (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Progression is not inevitable: in Backer's community study redrawn in Sturdevant's, only 9 of 72 non-cavitated lesions cavitated (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and more than half (37 of 72) regressed to become indistinguishable from normal enamel (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Post-orthodontic WSLs.** Brackets and bands are plaque-retentive. Summitt's states that WSLs appear within a few weeks of appliance placement, especially with ill-fitting bands (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC], and that mutans streptococci and lactobacilli rise after appliances are placed (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Modern Operative Dentistry describes smooth-surface WSLs as typically arising from inadequate hygiene during bracket treatment (Torres, 2020) [SRC]; Sturdevant's likewise (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Preventively, fluoride-releasing resin composites and RMGI cements inhibit demineralisation next to bands and brackets (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC], and Pickard's lists orthodontic appliances as an indication for fluoride mouthwash (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. After debonding the plaque trap is gone, and Kidd notes that considerable surface remineralisation has been observed particularly in lesions that developed during orthodontic treatment (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Sturdevant's adds that white spots with an intact, hard surface are often evidence of remineralisation and are not indications for invasive treatment unless for aesthetics (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Active versus arrested.** Once plaque is removed, the lesion arrests; porosities at the surface are abraded smooth by brushing (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC] and stains from the diet can enter the pores, producing an arrested brown spot lesion with a hard, shiny surface (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Torres, 2020) [SRC]. This is aesthetically important: an arrested lesion may be biologically harmless but remain visible.

#### ✎ Exam core
**Etiology of carious WSL**
- Plaque biofilm + frequent fermentable sugar → acid → pH falls below the critical pH → subsurface demineralisation at stagnation sites: cervical third, below contacts, fissures (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016).
- Can form within about 3 weeks of poor hygiene and high sugar; cavitation on smooth surfaces takes about 18 months; many lesions regress or arrest (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Modifying factors: saliva flow, fluoride exposure, diet, hygiene, socio-economic status (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**Post-orthodontic WSL:** brackets and bands retain plaque, raise mutans streptococci and lactobacilli; lesions appear within weeks (Hilton et al, 2013). After debonding many partly remineralise, but larger ones stay visible (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016; Torres, 2020).

**Arrested WSL:** plaque-free, shiny, hard; may take up stain (brown spot lesion) (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## 3. Developmental white opacities and how to tell them from caries

### Explanation

**Dental fluorosis.** Kidd and Fejerskov define it as an enamel hypomineralisation along the entire enamel surface (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC], caused because enamel maturation, from the end of secretion to eruption, is very sensitive to fluoride (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. The mildest form is a pearly appearance with enhanced striae of Retzius visible on drying (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]; white cusp tips are "snowcapping" (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Severity is scored on the Thylstrup-Fejerskov index from 1 to 9 (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Goldstein's notes that histologically fluorosis is a porous subsurface below a well-mineralised surface layer (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], the same architecture as a carious WSL, which is the reason resin infiltration also works on it (section 6). Fluoride opacities are poorly demarcated (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], whereas non-fluoride opacities are usually round or oval and well demarcated (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Pickard's describes fluorosis as chalky-white flecks, confluent blotches, brown discoloration and pitting (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

**Molar-incisor hypomineralisation (MIH).** Mount et al describe first molars and incisors that are chalky or show demarcated creamy-yellow or light-brown opacities (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]; severely affected molars break down rapidly after eruption (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC] with high caries susceptibility (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC], are sensitive, and may be difficult to anaesthetise (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]. The insult probably occurs between birth and about 3 years (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]; reported prevalence varies widely, about 4-38% in Europe (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]. Pickard's adds that the opacities are easily chipped (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

**Enamel hypoplasia and traumatic/infective (Turner) defects.** Goldstein's separates hypoplasia, a reduction in the thickness or amount of enamel (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], from hypocalcification, which can occur locally as a white spot (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Systemic causes give bilateral, symmetrical chronological bands [RECALL]; when isolated teeth are affected, irradiation, trauma or infection is usually the cause (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], which is the typical history of a single permanent incisor opacity after trauma or infection of its primary predecessor.

**Differentiation.** Summitt's Table 5-4 is the best single source: carious white spots are chalky, matte and rough when dried, whereas hypomineralised spots are smooth, glossy and less opaque (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]; caries sits in plaque stagnation areas, developmental opacities mainly in self-cleansing areas (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]; and opacities of similar shape on corresponding teeth in all four quadrants are likely developmental (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's adds a hydration test: carious white spots partly or totally disappear when wet, whereas hypocalcified enamel is affected less by drying and wetting (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; it also notes that hypocalcified enamel is a problem only of appearance (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Developmental white opacities**
- **Fluorosis:** excess fluoride during enamel maturation; hypomineralisation along the whole surface; bilateral, symmetrical, diffuse, poorly demarcated; fine white striae, snowcapped cusps, later brown stain and pitting; graded by Thylstrup-Fejerskov index 1-9 (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016; Goldstein et al, 2018).
- **MIH:** systemic insult birth to about 3 years; demarcated white-yellow-brown opacities on first molars and incisors; post-eruptive breakdown, hypersensitivity, poor anaesthesia (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Enamel hypoplasia/hypocalcification:** chronological bands if systemic; single tooth (Turner's) after trauma or infection of the primary predecessor (Goldstein et al, 2018).

**Differentiation** (Hilton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019)

| Feature | Carious WSL | Developmental opacity |
|---|---|---|
| Site | Plaque stagnation: cervical, below brackets | Self-cleansing: incisal, cusp tips |
| Distribution | Follows plaque | Symmetrical, corresponding teeth |
| Surface (dried) | Chalky, matte, rough (if active) | Smooth, glossy |
| Wet vs dry | Fades when wet | Little change |
| Border | Follows gingival margin | Fluorosis diffuse; MIH sharply demarcated |
| Natural course | Progress, arrest or regress | Stable (MIH may break down) |

## 4. Diagnosis: ICDAS and activity assessment

### Explanation

**Examination conditions.** Teeth must be clean and dried; WSLs are more obvious when dry (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Kidd's advice for uncertainty is practical: if in doubt whether a non-cavitated lesion is active, judge it as active and start non-operative care (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC].

**ICDAS (severity/depth).** A visual ranked scoring system of lesion depth including the non-cavitated stages (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Code 1: opacity hardly visible wet but distinctly visible after 5 seconds' air drying (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]; code 2: distinctly visible without drying (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]; code 3: localised enamel breakdown (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]; codes 4-6 dentine shadow and cavities. Pickard's mICDAS links code 1 to demineralisation limited to the outer 50% of enamel (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Each surface receives two digits, restoration status first (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. So a WSL is ICDAS 1 or 2.

**Activity (Nyvad).** Depth is not enough; activity decides treatment. Kidd and Fejerskov say they prefer the Nyvad criteria (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Machiulskiene describes active enamel lesions as matt/chalky, whitish or yellowish (Machiulskiene, 2019) [SRC], often covered with sticky plaque (Machiulskiene, 2019) [SRC], whereas inactive lesions are glossy and smooth on probing (Machiulskiene, 2019) [SRC]. Colour is secondary, because an early arrested lesion can be as white as an active one (Machiulskiene, 2019) [SRC]. Location also helps: arrested smooth-surface lesions sit away from the gingival margin (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. Active non-cavitated lesions carry a significantly greater risk of cavitation than inactive ones (Machiulskiene, 2019) [SRC].

**Where the books differ on probing and drying.** Machiulskiene recommends drying for 10-15 s (Machiulskiene, 2019) [SRC] and finds a sharp explorer, moved gently, more useful than a ball-ended probe (Machiulskiene, 2019) [SRC]; ICDAS, as given by Kidd, specifies 5 seconds of drying (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]; Sturdevant's counts a lesion as cavitated only if a blunt probe penetrates (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and Modern Operative Dentistry warns that probing can breach the surface zone (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. These are reported side by side, not reconciled.

**Adjunct devices.** Pickard's lists fibreoptic transillumination, QLF and the DiagnoDent laser-fluorescence system among in-vivo detection technologies (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; Sturdevant's cautions that the diagnostic accuracy of such devices has not been firmly established (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The mICDAS and ICDAS-ICCMS details are covered in the caries and minimal_intervention topics.

#### ✎ Exam core
**Diagnosis**
- Clean, isolate, air-dry; good light and magnification; no forceful sharp probing (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016; Torres, 2020).
- **ICDAS (depth):** code 1 = opacity seen only after 5 s drying (outer half of enamel); code 2 = opacity visible wet; code 3 = localised enamel breakdown (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Activity (Nyvad criteria):** active = chalky, matte, rough, plaque-covered, near the gingival margin; inactive = glossy, smooth, hard, away from gingiva, may be stained (Machiulskiene, 2019). When in doubt, treat as active.
- Adjuncts: bitewing radiographs for proximal lesions, transillumination, fluorescence devices (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); caries risk assessment (diet, saliva, hygiene).
- Developmental opacities excluded using the table in section 3.

## 5. Management: principles and non-invasive remineralisation

### Explanation

**Principles.** Management follows activity, cavitation and the patient's concern (health versus aesthetics). An active lesion needs the disease controlled; an arrested but visible lesion needs only aesthetic treatment if the patient wants it; a cavitated lesion that cannot be cleaned needs a restoration (section 7). The minimal intervention framework (identify, prevent, control, minimally invasive restoration, recall) is answered in minimal_intervention; only its application to WSLs is given here.

**Plaque control and diet.** Lesion arrest requires removal of the biofilm. For children over 3 years, family toothpaste of 1350-1500 ppm F (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC], "spit, don't rinse" (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC].

**Fluoride.** At the lesion, fluoride favours fluorapatite deposition, and concentrated applications form a calcium fluoride-like reservoir on the surface (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. Pickard's lists: high-concentration prescription paste of 2800 or 5000 ppm for high-risk patients (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; professionally applied varnish at 22 600 ppm F (2.2% F) (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], which in systematic reviews reduced caries increment by a third in the primary dentition (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC] and by nearly half in the permanent (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], but must be repeated 3- to 6-monthly (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Fluoride mouthwash, 0.05% NaF daily or 0.2% NaF weekly (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], is indicated for patients with orthodontic appliances. Sturdevant's specifies 3-monthly varnish for high-risk patients (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's recommends 5% NaF varnish where WSLs tend to develop (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**CPP-ACP.** Casein phosphopeptide stabilises amorphous calcium phosphate and keeps a supersaturated state at the tooth surface (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; ACP tends to convert to hydroxyapatite in a wet environment (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. A combined product, MI Paste Plus, carries 900 ppm F (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Two cautions recur: most CPP-ACP studies have not separated its effect from fluoride (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and long-term trials are still needed (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. It is contraindicated in milk-protein allergy (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Pickard's illustrates fluorotic lesions becoming less evident one month after professional application of topical fluoride/CPP-ACP paste (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; this is a single illustrated case, not trial evidence [Certain].

**What remineralisation can and cannot do.** The books differ in emphasis. Modern Operative Dentistry says small lesions may disappear completely, but once the surface pores close, mineral is not deposited homogeneously in the body, so larger lesions stay visible; clinical studies show only 33-49% of WSLs disappear on arrest (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. Kidd and Fejerskov are more sceptical: remineralisation of the lesion body is not obtained to any significant degree in vivo (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC], and what is seen is mainly surface abrasion and deposition. Sturdevant's reproduces Backer's figure of more than half of lesions becoming indistinguishable from sound enamel (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. A clinically useful synthesis: remineralisation reliably arrests; it masks only small, shallow lesions [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Principles of management:** control the disease first, then treat appearance; non-invasive → micro-invasive → minimally invasive → restorative (see minimal_intervention).

**A. Non-invasive (active, non-cavitated lesions)**
1. Plaque control: twice-daily brushing, site-specific instruction, interdental cleaning, disclosing; professional cleaning.
2. Diet: reduce frequency of free sugars.
3. Fluoride (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016):
   - toothpaste 1350-1500 ppm, "spit, don't rinse"; 2800/5000 ppm for high risk;
   - varnish 22 600 ppm F every 3-6 months; mouthrinse 0.05% NaF daily (orthodontic patients).
   - Mechanism: fluorapatite deposition, CaF2 reservoir, antibacterial action.
4. CPP-ACP (with or without fluoride, eg 900 ppm F paste): stabilises calcium and phosphate in supersaturated form for remineralisation; avoid in milk allergy (Ritter et al, 2019).
5. Sealing/protection of fissure WSLs; recall at 3-6 months, photographs.

Limitation: remineralisation arrests lesions but deeper ones remain visible, because the surface zone remineralises first (Torres, 2020).

## 6. Micro-invasive treatment: resin infiltration (ICON)

### Explanation

**Concept.** Infiltration is called micro-invasive because no preparation is made (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. Unlike a sealant, which covers the surface, it fills the pores within the lesion body with resin (Torres, 2020) [SRC]; Summitt's describes this as creating a diffusion barrier inside the lesion (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. It therefore arrests progression and, because the infiltrant's refractive index is 1.475, closer to enamel (Torres, 2020) [SRC], it also masks the whiteness.

**Technique (Icon).** The surface zone blocks penetration, so it is removed with 15% hydrochloric acid for 2 minutes, the only acid found adequate (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. After rinsing and drying, absolute ethanol is applied and dried to remove residual water (Torres, 2020) [SRC], so the low-viscosity infiltrant penetrates by capillary forces (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. The infiltrant is left 3 minutes (Torres, 2020) [SRC], light-cured, and the step repeated for 1 minute with a further 40 s cure (Torres, 2020) [SRC]; then polished. Alani and Patel add that the ethanol step lets the operator preview the colour change (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], and that etch-dry cycles are repeated as required (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Rubber dam or a light-cured gingival barrier is needed because of the acid.

**Indications and limits.** Only non-cavitated lesions (Torres, 2020) [SRC]: smooth-surface WSLs, post-orthodontic WSLs, and proximal lesions radiographically up to the outer third of dentine (D1) (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. Fluorosis responds because of its similar histology (Torres, 2020) [SRC]; MIH responds less well because its histology differs (Torres, 2020) [SRC], and may need the surface opened with a bur first ("deep infiltration") (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. Inactive, thickly remineralised or stained lesions mask poorly (Torres, 2020) [SRC], which is a reason to infiltrate post-orthodontic lesions before they have remineralised for too long [Likely]. The infiltrant is radiolucent, so it cannot be seen on radiographs (Torres, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. A practice-based RCT reported in Sturdevant's found infiltration better at preventing progression than hygiene, flossing and fluoride instructions (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Modern Operative Dentistry grades the systematic-review evidence for proximal lesions as moderate to low (Torres, 2020) [SRC] and stresses that infiltration does not replace preventive care (Torres, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**B. Micro-invasive: resin infiltration (Icon, DMG)** (Torres, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013)
- Principle: low-viscosity resin fills lesion pores; arrests progression (diffusion barrier) and masks (resin RI about 1.475, near enamel).
- Steps: isolation (rubber dam/liquid dam) → 15% HCl gel 2 min → rinse 30 s, dry → ethanol 30 s (preview colour) → repeat etch if needed → infiltrant 3 min, remove excess, cure 40 s → second application 1 min, cure 40 s → polish.
- Indications: non-cavitated smooth-surface and post-orthodontic WSLs, proximal lesions up to D1, fluorosis.
- Limitations: not for cavitated lesions; less effective on old arrested or stained lesions and MIH; radiolucent; needs preventive care alongside.

## 7. Microabrasion, bleaching and restoration

### Explanation

**Microabrasion.** A combination of acid and abrasive (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]; the acid weakens the outer 22-27 µm so the abrasive removes it (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Fluorosis is often amenable to it (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Kidd's principle explains why it works on arrested white spots at all: an inactive lesion's appearance cannot change unless the surface is polished, ground or stripped with strong acid (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. The protocols differ:

| | Summitt's 4e (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC] | Sikri (2020) [SRC] |
|---|---|---|
| Acid | low concentration HCl in water-soluble gel | preferably 18% HCl with pumice |
| Application | rubber cup or stiff brush, 20-30 s | 60 s, rinse, repeat until result |

Sikri lists small decalcified lesions from orthodontic bands as an indication (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] but decalcification "leading to caries" as a contraindication (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], and warns that teeth may look more yellow because enamel is removed (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Goldstein's lists contouring, microabrasion, bonding or veneers for hypocalcification (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Macroabrasion, using a fine diamond or carbide finishing bur on the enamel, is an alternative (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Bleaching (brief).** Bleaching does not remove white spots (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; it lightens the surrounding enamel so they are less noticeable (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC], with a temporary "splotchy look" in the first weeks (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Here Goldstein's two chapters differ: Ch.21 keeps 10% carbamide peroxide as the first treatment for white spots, partly to avoid removing fluoride-rich enamel (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], while Ch.12 says fluorotic white spots have an unpredictable prognosis (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] and in many cases stand out even more (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt's advises bleaching first unless the enamel is chalky (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Bonding should be delayed 1-2 weeks after bleaching (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Restoration.** Smooth-surface lesions should not be treated invasively while non-cavitated (Torres, 2020) [SRC]; once the surface zone is broken and the biofilm cannot be cleaned, a restoration is indicated (Torres, 2020) [SRC]. Kidd's rule is to give a doubtful cavity the benefit of the doubt (Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. For aesthetic residual spots, composite with an opaque layer is used (Hilton et al, 2013) [SRC], and veneers or crowns for severe fluorosis or MIH. Glass ionomer or RMGI suits cervical cavities in high-risk patients, and severely hypoplastic MIH molars may be restored with stainless steel crowns until adulthood (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]; these are treated in Q1, Q3 and the composite topic.

#### ✎ Exam core
**C. Minimally invasive aesthetic treatment**
- **Microabrasion:** HCl (low concentration to 18%) with pumice, rubber cup, short applications repeated; removes about 22-27 µm of surface enamel. Indicated: superficial fluorosis, arrested post-orthodontic WSLs, hypocalcification; not for deep defects. Follow with fluoride and polishing (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020).
- **Bleaching (10% carbamide peroxide):** does not remove white spots but lightens surroundings; may be combined with microabrasion or infiltration (Goldstein et al, 2018).

**D. Restorative (cavitated or deep lesions)**
- Cavitated, uncleansable lesion: minimal preparation and composite, GIC/RMGI in high-risk cervical sites (Torres, 2020).
- Residual aesthetic defects: composite with opaque layer, veneers; severe MIH molars: stainless steel crowns or onlays.
- Delay bonding 1-2 weeks after bleaching.

**Conclusion:** diagnose cause and activity first; arrest active lesions with plaque control and fluoride/CPP-ACP; mask visible lesions by infiltration or microabrasion; restore only when cavitated.

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim: resin infiltration masks white spot lesions better than remineralisation.** The textbooks give the mechanism and a cautious grading: infiltration masks because the resin's refractive index approaches enamel and works best on active, porous lesions (Torres, 2020) [SRC], while remineralisation leaves larger lesions visible (Torres, 2020; Kidd and Fejerskov, 2016) [SRC]. A meta-analysis of 11 clinical studies (413 patients) found significantly greater optical improvement with infiltration than with no treatment or fluoride varnish, graded moderate but resting on very few well-conducted RCTs (Bourouni et al, 2021) [LIT; abstract only]. For post-orthodontic WSLs specifically, an earlier meta-analysis of 20 RCTs found monthly fluoride varnish the best-supported adjunct for lesion area and fluorescence, with no considerable improvement in clinical (visual) evaluation (Höchli et al, 2017) [LIT; abstract only], and an umbrella review judged the evidence for infiltration and microabrasion unreliable because of few studies, while CPP-ACP was effective (Bakdach and Hadad, 2020) [LIT; abstract only]. What follows: for aesthetic masking of a visible WSL infiltration is probably superior [Likely], but outcomes, follow-up and quality vary, so remineralisation remains the first step and infiltration an add-on [Likely].

## High-yield points

- WSL = subsurface demineralisation under an intact, fluoride-rich surface zone; four zones (surface 1%, body 5-25%, dark 2-4%, translucent).
- White because pores scatter light (enamel RI about 1.62, water 1.33, air 1.0); visible only when dry = outer half of enamel.
- Active: chalky, matte, rough, plaque-covered; inactive: glossy, smooth, may stain brown (Nyvad).
- Caries in stagnation sites; developmental opacities symmetrical, self-cleansing sites, glossy.
- Fluoride varnish 22 600 ppm, 3-6 monthly; toothpaste 1350-1500 ppm, 5000 ppm for high risk; CPP-ACP adjunct.
- Icon: 15% HCl 2 min → ethanol → infiltrant 3 min + 1 min; non-cavitated only, up to D1; radiolucent.
- Microabrasion: HCl + pumice, removes about 22-27 µm; best for superficial fluorosis.
- Bleaching does not remove white spots; restore only cavitated lesions.

## Common exam mistakes

- Calling every white opacity "early caries"; fluorosis, MIH and hypoplasia must be differentiated by site, symmetry, surface and wet/dry behaviour.
- Pressing a sharp explorer into a WSL to "check for softness"; forceful probing can breach the surface zone (gentle explorer use is part of the Nyvad examination).
- Claiming fluoride makes all WSLs disappear; it arrests them, but larger lesions stay visible.
- Writing that bleaching removes white spots; it only lightens the surroundings.
- Using resin infiltration for cavitated lesions or forgetting that it is radiolucent.
