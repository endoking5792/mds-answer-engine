# Attrition and its management

**Marks:** 10 · Short essay

**Answer blueprint:** Definition → aetiology → clinical features → diagnosis → management (preventive, restorative) → conclusion.

## Introduction and definition

**Attrition** is loss of tooth structure by tooth-to-tooth contact during function or parafunction, affecting incisal, occlusal and proximal surfaces (Ritter et al., 2019; Goldstein et al., 2018; Torres et al., 2020).

- **Physiological:** slow, continuous and age-dependent (Singh, 2020).
- **Pathological:** faster than expected for age, so the dentition will not last for life (Kaidonis et al., 2016b; Bartlett, 2011). **It is judged by rate against age, not by absolute amount.**

## 1. Aetiology

- **Bruxism** (commonest): clenching or grinding, awake or asleep; driven by stress, airway problems and sleep apnoea (Goldstein et al., 2018; Ritter et al., 2019).
- **Malocclusion** (crossbite), **chronic dust and dirt** (strictly a third-body abrasive co-factor) and **amelogenesis or dentinogenesis imperfecta** (Goldstein et al., 2018).
- **Contributors:** acid-softened enamel (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); medication-induced dry mouth (Johansson et al., 2008).

**Occlusion versus stress:** occlusal interferences are disputed as a cause; stress is acknowledged by most clinicians (Bartlett, 2011; Kaidonis et al., 2016b).

## 2. Clinical features

- **Flat, shiny, hard facets with sharp margins**, matching on opposing teeth and clearly demarcated from unworn areas (Goldstein et al., 2018; Bartlett, 2011; Alani and Patel, 2025).
- **Sites:** occlusal posteriors, palatal of upper and labial of lower incisors, guiding canines and premolars.
- **Dentine** is worn flat with the enamel. **Cupped dentine suggests an added abrasive or erosive component** (Kaidonis et al., 2016a), although Sturdevant describes cupped cusps in older patients (Ritter et al., 2019).
- Fractured cusps and restorations; craze lines; flattened contacts with mesial drift.
- **Sequelae:** sensitivity, cheek biting, food impaction, muscle and TMJ pain (Singh, 2020).
- **Vertical dimension** is usually maintained by dentoalveolar compensation when wear is slow (Marzouk et al., n.d.).

## 3. Diagnosis

- **History:** partner-reported grinding, morning jaw stiffness, stress, diet, reflux.
- **Examination:** matching facets, masseter hypertrophy, muscle and TMJ tenderness.
- **Activity:** polished facets and incisal enamel flaking indicate active grinding (Kaidonis et al., 2016b).
- **Record:** Smith and Knight index, dated casts and photographs (Goldstein et al., 2018). Exclude an erosive component.

## 4. Preventive management

1. Treat pulpally involved teeth first (Singh, 2020).
2. **Occlusal splint:** full-coverage hard acrylic maxillary (Michigan) splint with canine guidance, worn at night (Bartlett, 2011). **It protects the teeth but does not stop bruxism** (Kaidonis et al., 2016b).
3. Stress management and treatment of temporomandibular disorder. Botulinum toxin is at most an adjunct for refractory sleep bruxism; the evidence is weak (De la Torre Canales et al., 2017).
4. Occlusal equilibration only for specific interferences; the texts disagree on its value.
5. Protect exposed dentine with fluoride or bonding agents; monitor with dated casts.

## 5. Restorative management

**Indications:** appearance, intractable sensitivity (Bartlett, 2011), fractures or loss of function.

| Situation | Approach |
|---|---|
| Localised, mild | Direct composite. |
| Localised anterior | Composite build-ups at increased vertical dimension (Dahl concept); posterior contacts return in about 3–9 months (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011). |
| Heavy loading | Gold or metal onlays, indirect composite or polished monolithic zirconia; avoid feldspathic porcelain against natural teeth (Torres et al., 2020; Alani and Patel, 2025; Lohbauer and Reich, 2017). |
| Generalised | Full-mouth rehabilitation planned by the **Turner and Missirlian (1984) categories**: 1, loss of vertical dimension; 2, no loss, space available; 3, no loss, limited space (Goldstein et al., 2018). |

**Finish every rehabilitation in a bruxist with an occlusal guard** (Goldstein et al., 2018).

## Conclusion

Attrition is pathological when its rate outpaces the patient's age, usually because of bruxism. Management controls the cause and protects the teeth with a hard splint, then restores only for sensitivity, appearance or function, with materials kind to the opposing teeth.
