## 1. Definition: physiological versus pathological attrition

### Explanation

The books define attrition in slightly different words, all built on tooth-to-tooth contact:
- "the wear of tooth against tooth" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC];
- mechanical wear of the incisal or occlusal surface from functional or parafunctional movements of the mandible (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC];
- loss of tooth structure by mechanical forces from opposing teeth (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC];
- wear by tooth-to-tooth contact without the impact of any other substance (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC].

**Physiological or pathological?** Harpreet Singh also defines attrition as the physiological wearing of the tooth in mastication (Singh, 2020) [SRC] and calls it more physiological than pathological because it is continuous and age-dependent (Singh, 2020) [SRC]; bruxism is the pathological cause (Singh, 2020) [SRC]. Marzouk similarly calls it a continuous, age-dependent and usually physiologic process (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Torres et al describe physiological wear as a slow degradation of tooth shape (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Pickard's places the dividing line at rate: physiological wear is slow and lifelong, pathological wear is much faster (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

The line is drawn against age, not against an absolute amount. Kaidonis et al consider wear pathologic in a young person if the dentition will clearly not last into old age (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]; Bartlett makes the same point, contrasting a 70-year-old with a 20-year-old in whom "alarm bells might sound" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. So the diagnosis "pathological attrition" is a judgement of rate relative to expected lifespan [Certain].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Attrition is loss of tooth structure by tooth-to-tooth contact, during function or parafunction, affecting incisal, occlusal and proximal surfaces (Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein et al, 2018).

- **Physiological:** slow, continuous, age-dependent; matched to lifespan.
- **Pathological:** faster than expected for age, usually from bruxism; the dentition will not last for life (Singh, 2020; Kaidonis et al, 2016).

## 2. Aetiology and bruxism

### Explanation

Goldstein et al state that the most common cause of attrition is bruxism (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; chewing and swallowing rarely bring teeth into contact (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Other contributors are malocclusion, environment and genetics (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]: teeth in crossbite wear more (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], chronic dust and dirt exposure increases wear (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], and amelogenesis or dentinogenesis imperfecta make teeth wear faster (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

**Bruxism** is grinding, gnashing or clenching of the teeth (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; sleep bruxism is classed as a sleep-related movement disorder (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sturdevant's lists stress, airway problems and sleep apnoea as the usual drivers (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

The books disagree on what drives bruxism, and the views are recorded side by side:
- **Stress, not occlusion.** Bartlett says stress is acknowledged by most clinicians and that the occlusal-interference theory remains unproven because interferences are common in people without symptoms (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al go further: grinding is genetically coded, central in origin and quite normal (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC], its main risk factor is stress (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC], and there is no correlation between grinding and occlusion (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC], although they add that occlusion cannot be entirely discounted (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC].
- **Occlusion contributes.** Goldstein et al list malocclusion among the causes (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], and Marzouk and Harpreet Singh treat attrition with occlusal adjustment or equilibration (section 5).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Aetiology** (Goldstein et al, 2018)
- Bruxism (commonest): clenching or grinding, awake or sleep; drivers stress, airway problems, sleep apnoea (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Malocclusion: crossbite, deep bite.
- Environmental: chronic dust and dirt.
- Genetic: amelogenesis or dentinogenesis imperfecta.
- Contributor: acid erosion softens the surface (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

Role of occlusal interferences is disputed; stress is acknowledged by most clinicians (Bartlett, 2011).

## 3. Clinical features

### Explanation

**Facets.** Facets appear on teeth used in guidance, such as canines and premolars (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Typical sites are occlusal surfaces of posterior teeth, palatal surfaces of upper anteriors and labial surfaces of lower anteriors (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. The surfaces are hard, smooth and shiny (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], and may turn yellowish-brown once dentine is exposed (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Opposing arches show paired facets (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; in severe cases the teeth look as if they have been filed (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Alani and Patel describe a definite demarcation between worn and spared areas (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], with upper and lower teeth mirroring each other (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], which is what separates attrition from the rounded, glassy surfaces of erosion [Likely]. Sturdevant's describes cupped-out cusp tips because the exposed dentine wears faster than enamel (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Severe cases show fractured restorations from heavy loading (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; enamel craze lines from clenching need no treatment (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Proximal attrition** flattens contacts and causes mesial drift (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], reducing arch length (Singh, 2020) [SRC].

**Sequelae.** Cheek biting (Singh, 2020) [SRC], sensitivity, food impaction and TMJ or muscle symptoms (Singh, 2020; Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

**Vertical dimension.** Marzouk explains that if wear is slow (ten years and more) the alveolar bone grows occlusally and the face height is kept (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; if it is rapid, there is no chance for the bone to compensate (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Goldstein et al say bruxism rarely causes loss of occlusal vertical dimension because of continued eruption (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Clinical features**
- Flat, shiny, hard facets with sharp margins; facets on opposing teeth match (Goldstein et al, 2018; Bartlett, 2011).
- Sites: occlusal posteriors, palatal of upper and labial of lower incisors; guidance teeth (canines, premolars) (Bartlett, 2011).
- Cupped-out cusp tips once dentine exposed (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Fractured cusps and restorations; craze lines.
- Proximal facets: mesial drift, shorter arch.
- Sequelae: sensitivity, cheek biting, food impaction, TMJ/muscle pain.
- OVD usually maintained by dentoalveolar compensation (Marzouk et al, undated).

## 4. Diagnosis and assessment

### Explanation

Wear facets are the most objective evidence of grinding (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Activity is judged from the facet: well-defined polished facets mean active grinding (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC], and enamel flaking at incisal edges does too (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. A night guard polished only to a matte finish shows new shiny facets if the patient grinds (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Wear may be historical rather than active (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], so it is monitored with casts and digital photographs repeated over time (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Severity can be scored with the Smith and Knight tooth wear index (1984) (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] or Harpreet Singh's 0-4 attrition index, in which score 2 is noticeable flattening parallel to the occlusal plane (Singh, 2020) [SRC]; indices are covered in P1.

Pure attrition is unusual: wear is usually a combination (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], and acid-softened surfaces are more easily worn by opposing teeth (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], so a diet history for erosion is part of diagnosis. Muscle and TMJ symptoms should be examined because bruxists may have myofascial pain dysfunction (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Diagnosis**
- History: grinding (partner report), morning jaw stiffness, stress, diet, reflux.
- Examination: matching facets, masseter hypertrophy, TMJ/muscle tenderness, fremitus.
- Activity: polished facets, enamel flaking (Kaidonis et al, 2016).
- Record: Smith and Knight index, dated study casts, photographs (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Exclude erosion component.

## 5. Preventive management and occlusal splints

### Explanation

**Splints.** Full-coverage splints are effective for patients with mainly a bruxist habit (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Bartlett's preferred design is a full-coverage upper hard acrylic splint with canine rises in lateral excursions (the Michigan splint) (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], adjusted for even contacts and canine guidance; vacuum-formed and heat-cured splints have not been shown to differ in effect, but acrylic lasts longer (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al are explicit that a night guard does not stop grinding but saves tooth at the expense of acrylic (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]; Torres et al agree that eliminating parafunction is hard, so acrylic splints are used (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Marzouk prescribes a "discluding-protecting" occlusal splint (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

**Occlusal adjustment: books disagree.**
- Harpreet Singh controls bruxism with occlusal adjustment and night guards (Singh, 2020) [SRC]; Marzouk performs occlusal equilibration after symptoms resolve (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].
- Kaidonis et al: occlusal equilibration is not normally advocated (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Other measures.** Kaidonis et al report that night-time lubricants, best tooth mousse, reduced wear (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Sensitive dentine is protected with fluoride or dentine bonding agents (Singh, 2020) [SRC]; stress management and referral for sleep disorders are recommended [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Management: control cause and protect** (Singh, 2020; Marzouk et al, undated)
1. Treat pulpally involved teeth (endodontics or extraction).
2. Occlusal splint: full-coverage hard acrylic maxillary (Michigan) splint with canine guidance, worn at night; protects teeth, does not stop bruxism (Bartlett, 2011; Kaidonis et al, 2016).
3. Stress management; TMD treatment.
4. Occlusal equilibration only for specific interferences (disputed).
5. Desensitise dentine: fluoride, bonding agents.
6. Monitor with dated casts.

## 6. Restorative options

### Explanation

**When to restore.** Sturdevant's restores areas of significant attrition with exposed dentine that are sensitive or annoying (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Bartlett gives appearance and intractable sensitivity as the indications that eventually demand restoration (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al caution that a worn dentition with scooped dentine is functional (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Material: the books disagree.**
- Marzouk: lost tooth structure is at high stress concentration, so only metallic restorations should be used (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].
- Alani and Patel: an attritive aetiology needs a harder occlusal surface, such as indirect composite, CAD-CAM resin-nano ceramic or metal alloy (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].
- Torres et al: gold is softer than enamel and kind to the opposing tooth, while feldspathic ceramic causes extensive antagonist wear, modern ceramics being significantly less abrasive (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC].

**Space and vertical dimension.** Goldstein et al use Turner's 1984 classification (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]: category 1, excessive wear with loss of OVD (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; category 2, without loss of OVD but with space (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; category 3, without loss of OVD and limited space (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. They warn that routinely opening the bite may be ill advised (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. After rehabilitation an occlusal guard should be made (Goldstein et al, 2018; Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Details of space creation are in Q10 and P2.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Restorative options**
- Indications: appearance, intractable sensitivity (Bartlett, 2011); also function, fractures, pulp risk.
- Localised mild: direct composite (hybrid).
- Localised anterior: composite or palatal veneers at increased OVD (Dahl).
- Heavy loading: gold or metal onlays, indirect composite/ceramic (Marzouk et al, undated; Alani and Patel, 2025).
- Generalised: full-mouth rehabilitation planned by Turner category (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Finish with an occlusal guard (Goldstein et al, 2018).

## High-yield points
- Attrition = tooth-to-tooth wear; physiological versus pathological is judged by rate against age.
- Bruxism is the commonest cause of pathological attrition; stress is widely acknowledged; the occlusal-interference theory is unproven.
- Matching flat shiny facets with sharp margins distinguish attrition from erosion.
- Slow wear: OVD kept by dentoalveolar compensation; rapid wear: OVD can be lost.
- Michigan splint protects teeth but does not stop bruxism.
- Turner categories 1-3 decide whether OVD is increased.
- Night guard after every rehabilitation for a bruxist.

## Common exam mistakes
- Calling all attrition pathological; it is physiological when matched to age.
- Writing that the splint cures bruxism; it only protects the teeth.
- Assuming worn teeth have lost OVD and opening the bite without assessing speaking space.
- Recommending routine occlusal equilibration as fact; the books disagree.
- Ignoring the erosive component that accelerates attrition.
