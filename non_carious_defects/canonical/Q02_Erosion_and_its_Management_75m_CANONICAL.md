## 1. Introduction and definition

### Explanation

Every book defines erosion as acid loss of tooth tissue without bacteria, but they put the boundary in different places, and the differences matter for an essay that will later discuss abrasion and "erosive tooth wear". Side by side:

| Source | Definition (as printed) |
|---|---|
| Banerjee and Watson, 2015 (Pickard's Guide 10th ed) | "the irreversible loss of dental hard tissues by a chemical process (acid attack) not involving bacteria" [SRC] |
| Bartlett, 2011 (Advanced Operative Dentistry) | "The loss of tooth structure caused by acids and not involving bacterial fermentation is called erosion" [SRC] |
| Torres et al, 2020 (Modern Operative Dentistry Ch.1) | a "pathologic, chronic localized, and usually painless loss of hard tissues that is chemically attacked by acids without the involvement of bacteria" [SRC] |
| Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020 (same book, Ch.18) | erosive tooth wear is "dental hard tissue loss due to a chemical-mechanical process not involving bacteria" [SRC] |
| Goldstein et al, 2018 (Esthetics in Dentistry 3rd ed) | "the noncarious loss of tooth structure due to chemical dissolution not related to acids produced by the dental plaque" [SRC] |
| Sikri, 2020 | "dissolution of tooth structure subsequent to chemical attack of either endogenous or exogenous origin, or combined chemico-mechanical attack" [SRC] |
| Ritter et al, 2019 (Sturdevant's 2nd SA ed) | loss of tooth surface "by chemical action in the continued presence of demineralizing agents with low pH" [SRC] |

Two terminology points follow. First, Bresciani and Sikri fold mechanical action into the definition (chemical-mechanical), whereas Pickard's and Bartlett keep erosion purely chemical; the textbooks do not agree whether "erosion" names the chemical event or the clinical wear that results. Second, some authors prefer "corrosion" (Rose, Haveman and Davis, 2013) [SRC]; Mount's chapter uses "corrosion (erosion)" throughout (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC], and Sikri uses "biocorrosion" to take in "the chemical, biochemical, and electrochemical degradation of tooth substance" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Bartlett adds the process detail that matters for prevention: acids raise surface porosity, and "Optimum concentrations of fluoride and calcium may allow some remineralization", but a further acid or mechanical attack then removes the weakened layer (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

Importance. Pickard's calls erosion "the common denominator in the multifactorial aetiology of tooth wear" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; Olley and Bartlett: it "continues to be considered the most common and important aetiological factor for tooth wear globally" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Bartlett estimates that severe wear "probably" affects "around 10% of the population" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Kaidonis et al give a prevalence of corrosion "of up to 40–60% in some populations" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Schlueter and Luka's review found global prevalence ranging from 0 to 100%, with a rough mean of 30-50% in primary and 20-45% in permanent teeth, and a clear effect only for GORD and vomiting-related eating disorders (Schlueter and Luka, 2018) [LIT]; their 2025 update gives a rough mean of 30-50% in adults (Schlueter, Mulic and Luka, 2025) [LIT]. Index differences explain most of this spread, so a single prevalence figure should not be quoted as fact. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Dental erosion is the irreversible loss of dental hard tissue by a chemical process (acid attack) not involving bacteria (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Torres et al (2020) describe it as a chronic, localized, usually painless loss of hard tissue attacked by acids without bacteria. When the acid-softened surface is then worn by brushing, food or opposing teeth, the result is called erosive tooth wear, a chemical-mechanical process (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020). Sikri (2020) prefers "biocorrosion" (chemical, biochemical and electrochemical degradation), and Mount's text uses "corrosion" (Kaidonis et al, 2016).

**Significance.** Erosion is the common denominator in multifactorial tooth wear (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) and the most important cause of tooth wear worldwide (Olley and Bartlett, 2024). Severe wear affects about 10% of people (Bartlett, 2011); reported erosion prevalence varies from 0 to 100% because indices differ, with rough means of 20-45% in permanent teeth (Schlueter and Luka, 2018). Unlike caries, eroded tissue cannot be restored biologically, so management is preventive first and restorative only when needed.

**Aims of management:** identify and remove the acid source; reduce the frequency and contact time of acid; raise the acid resistance of the tooth surface; relieve sensitivity; monitor activity; and restore form, function and appearance with the least destruction of tooth tissue, only when the cause is controlled.

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

By source of acid, books use extrinsic, intrinsic and idiopathic: Torres et al state that lesions "can be classified as extrinsic, intrinsic, or idiopathic" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC], and Sikri explains idiopathic erosion as possibly due to "genetic disturbance or metabolic imbalance of the body fluids" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. By activity, Sikri divides lesions into "Manifest or active" and latent or inactive (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; histologically, manifest erosion shows a honeycomb enamel surface, latent erosion less obvious prisms.

Two clinical indices appear in Sikri: "Eccles (1979) classified erosion lesions" into Class I (early, enamel), Class II (dentine involved, less than one-third of the surface) and Class III (more extensive dentine loss, subdivided a-d by surface) (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; and the "Vialati and Belser (2010)" anterior classification, which "establishes six levels of wear according to the level of dentin exposure in the palatal areas" plus incisal edge length, facial enamel and pulp vitality, and links each class to a therapy, from prevention only (Class I) through composite, length enhancement and the "sandwich" approach, which "refers to the application of a resin-based material to treat the palatal surface wear, followed by the application of a labial/facial ceramic veneer" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The BEWE is a screening and severity score rather than a classification; it is covered in section 7 and in full in P1.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**
1. **By source of acid** (Torres et al, 2020; Sikri, 2020)
   - Extrinsic (exogenous): diet, medication, occupation, lifestyle.
   - Intrinsic (endogenous): gastric acid from vomiting, regurgitation, GORD.
   - Idiopathic: no identifiable source.
2. **By activity** (Sikri, 2020): manifest (active) and latent (inactive).
3. **By severity, Eccles (1979)** (Sikri, 2020): Class I enamel only, smooth glazed surface; Class II dentine exposed over less than one-third of the surface; Class III more extensive dentine loss (IIIa facial, IIIb palatal, IIIc incisal/occlusal, IIId all surfaces).
4. **Anterior classification for treatment planning, Vialati and Belser (2010)** (as printed in Sikri, 2020): six classes by palatal dentine exposure, incisal edge length, facial enamel and pulp vitality, from Class I (prevention only) through Class II (composite) to Classes IV-VI (palatal composite plus labial ceramic veneer, the "sandwich" approach).
5. **Screening score:** Basic Erosive Wear Examination (BEWE) (section 7).

## 3. Etiology

### Explanation

**Extrinsic acids.** Bartlett: "the most common cause of erosion is the frequent consumption of acids", and "probably the most important component is the frequency of consumption" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. His Table 4.1 grades erosive potential: high for citrus fruits, apples, cranberries and grapes; "Medium erosive potential" for cola, vinegar and wine; low for beer and carbonated water (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Sources outside the diet:
- medicines: "effervescent tablets of vitamin C" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; chewing ascorbic acid and aspirin tablets, iron tablets and salivary stimulants (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; cocaine rubbed onto the gingiva (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC];
- occupation: "jobs involving wine tasting or working near acidic industrial vapours" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; "Wine has been shown to lead to erosion in wine makers" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC];
- sport and lifestyle: "improperly chlorinated swimming pools" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; sports drinks with dehydration (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Pickard's notes that dietary erosion is "Often associated with a healthy lifestyle" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Bresciani et al regard occupational and sports acids as "secondary factors" (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020) [SRC].

**Intrinsic acids.** GORD "occurs primarily due to transient relaxation or incompetence of the lower oesophageal sphincter" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Bartlett warns that "in around 25% there are no symptoms" of reflux (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Sturdevant's notes that regurgitation "occurs without any nausea or abdominal contractions" and that many patients "are often not aware of their gastric symptoms" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Eating disorders: the books give different age bands for the same group; Bartlett, females "between the ages of 13 and 20 years old" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Sturdevant's, "young women in the age of 20–30 years" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Pickard's, bulimia "affects 1–2% of the adolescent population; female:male ratio is 10:1" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Other causes of vomiting or regurgitation are rumination ("voluntary regurgitation followed by re-digestion of stomach contents"; Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], pregnancy, alcoholism, hiatus hernia and peptic ulcer (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC], and drugs causing "gastric irritation, alcohol, aspirin, and other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Gastric acid has "a pH of 1–1.5" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC], and the outcome of gastric erosion is "generally more severe than that found with dietary erosion" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Etiology**

**A. Extrinsic (exogenous) erosion**
1. **Diet** (commonest): citrus fruits and juices, apples, grapes, carbonated and cola drinks, wine, vinegar, pickles, sports and energy drinks, herbal teas. Frequency of intake matters more than amount (Bartlett, 2011); sipping, swishing and holding drinks prolong contact.
2. **Medications:** effervescent and chewable vitamin C, aspirin tablets, iron preparations, salivary stimulants, acidic asthma inhalers (Torres et al, 2020; Olley and Bartlett, 2024; Kaidonis et al, 2016).
3. **Occupational:** wine tasters and makers, workers exposed to acid fumes, e.g. battery factories (Olley and Bartlett, 2024; Goldstein et al, 2018; Sikri, 2020).
4. **Sport and lifestyle:** swimmers in poorly chlorinated pools, athletes using sports drinks with dehydration, "health" diets rich in fruit (Sikri, 2020; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

**B. Intrinsic (endogenous) erosion** (gastric HCl, pH about 1-1.5; Torres et al, 2020)
1. **GORD:** incompetent lower oesophageal sphincter (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); about 25% of reflux is silent (Bartlett, 2011).
2. **Eating disorders:** bulimia nervosa (binge and self-induced vomiting) and anorexia with purging; mainly young women, female:male 10:1 (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
3. **Other vomiting/regurgitation:** pregnancy, chronic alcoholism, peptic ulcer, hiatus hernia, rumination, drug-induced nausea (Torres et al, 2020).
Gastric erosion is usually more severe than dietary erosion (Bartlett, 2011).

**C. Idiopathic:** no source found (Sikri, 2020).

## 4. Pathogenesis and chemistry

### Explanation

**Critical pH.** The books give a single number for enamel but do not agree on what it means for erosion:
- Goldstein et al: "a drop in oral pH below 5.5 initiates demineralization" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- Ngo, Wolff and Hume: "Normally the critical pH of hydroxyl apatite is 5.5" (Ngo, Wolff and Hume, 2016) [SRC].
- Olley and Bartlett give two values: "pH 5.5 for enamel and pH 6.5 for dentine" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].
- Kaidonis et al frame it against fluorapatite: "many popular drinks now have a pH well below the critical pH of fluoroapatite (FA)" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC].
- Bresciani et al avoid a single number: erosion is initiated by acids "undersaturated with respect to tooth mineral" (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020) [SRC].
The saturation statement explains why a fixed critical pH is only a guide: a drink at low pH that carries calcium and phosphate dissolves less, which is the point of Sikri's statement that near pH 4.0 added calcium phosphate can counteract enamel dissolution, while solubility "At a pH 3.0, solubility is 75 g/L; rises sharply to 400 g/L at a pH 2.5" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. [Likely]

**Open versus closed system.** Kaidonis et al contrast caries, where a biofilm keeps dissolved ions near the tooth, with erosion, where strong acid displaces saliva and biofilm and the products are swallowed: "That is an 'open system' where all the essential ions necessary for remineralization are permanently lost" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Hence erosion is surface loss, not the subsurface white spot of caries.

**pH versus titratable acidity.** Bartlett defines titratability as "the volume of alkali needed to neutralize an acid" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Olley and Bartlett: "titratability was likely to be more important than pH in determining erosive potential" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; Kaidonis et al: "acids from grapefruit and pineapple juices are more damaging than cola drinks even though they have a lower pH" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]; Olley and Bartlett similarly rank orange and pineapple juice above cola and lemonade (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Sikri, by contrast, states that phosphoric acid in soft drinks "is three times more erosive than any organic acid" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The books contradict each other on whether cola or fruit juice is the more erosive; this answer records both and does not rank them.

**Chelation and other modifiers.** Citric, malic and tartaric acids "contain more than one carboxyl group" and bind calcium (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; "The citrate ions are believed to bind with calcium in enamel and dentin forming soluble calcium citrate" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Goldstein et al add that "extreme alkaline conditions promote chelation of calcium out of teeth" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Severity also rises with "the duration of the acid challenge, temperature, and ion concentration" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Tissue changes.** In enamel, "First, the prism sheath area and then the prism core are dissolved, leaving a honeycomb appearance" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; dentine "is more susceptible to acid dissolution because its hydroxyapatite crystals are smaller than that of enamel" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], though its organic matrix partly slows acid diffusion.

**Softening and the mechanical interaction.** Torres et al: acids cause "softening of the surface layer, which is then worn by the friction of the food bolus or brushing" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; Pickard's: "Acid-softened tooth surfaces are more susceptible to long-term 'wear' forces from opposing teeth (attrition) or other external influences (abrasion)" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; Sikri: once hypomineralized, "wear resistance of dental hard tissues is lessened" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Erosion-abrasion and erosion-attrition are therefore the usual clinical forms, not exceptions. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pathogenesis**
1. **Critical pH.** Hydroxyapatite dissolves below about pH 5.5 (Goldstein et al, 2018; Ngo, Wolff and Hume, 2016); Olley and Bartlett (2024) give 5.5 for enamel and 6.5 for dentine. Many soft drinks lie below the critical pH even of fluorapatite (Kaidonis et al, 2016). Strictly, erosion occurs when the acid is undersaturated with respect to tooth mineral (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020); drinks containing calcium and phosphate are less erosive.
2. **Open system.** Strong acids displace saliva and biofilm; dissolved ions are swallowed, so loss is at the surface, not subsurface as in caries (Kaidonis et al, 2016).
3. **Erosive potential of an acid** depends on pH, titratable acidity (alkali needed to neutralise it; Bartlett, 2011), type of acid, calcium chelation (citrate binds Ca2+), temperature, duration and frequency (Olley and Bartlett, 2024). Books differ: fruit juices more erosive than cola because of higher titratable acidity (Kaidonis et al, 2016) versus phosphoric acid three times more erosive than organic acids (Sikri, 2020).
4. **Histology.** Enamel: prism sheath then core dissolve, giving a honeycomb surface; enamel solubility rises sharply from pH 3.0 to 2.5. Dentine: smaller crystals dissolve faster; the exposed collagen matrix remains (Sikri, 2020).
5. **Softening and interaction.** The acid-softened layer is easily removed by brushing (abrasion), food, tongue and opposing teeth (attrition), so most clinical wear is erosion-abrasion or erosion-attrition (Torres et al, 2020; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
6. **Dentine matrix.** After the mineral is lost, the demineralised collagen layer of dentine can be degraded by host matrix metalloproteinases, which speeds progression in advanced lesions (Buzalaf et al, 2025).

## 5. Patient and saliva factors

### Explanation

Sikri groups the influences into "Biological factors b. Behavioral factors c. Chemical factors", modified by knowledge, systemic health and socioeconomic status (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Saliva dilutes, clears and buffers acid: it acts "by deluting, removing, and buffering acidic substances" and "forms a protective salivary pellicle on the tooth surface"; "patients presenting hyposalivation are more prone to erosion" (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020) [SRC]. Sikri: "Hyposalivation may induce dental erosion without excessive exposure to erosive agents" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Bartlett disagrees on the strength of this: "The role of saliva in erosion is unclear" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC] (see Critical evidence synthesis and P3). Behaviour: sipping or holding drinks (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; continual sipping is "more damaging than consuming the beverage over a shorter period of time" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Soft tissues: "the posterior teeth are protected by the neutralizing saliva from the parotid gland", and lower lingual surfaces "are covered by the tongue and thereby protected" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Modifying factors** (Sikri, 2020)
- **Biological:** salivary flow and buffering, acquired pellicle, tooth composition (fluoride content), soft-tissue movement. Saliva dilutes, clears and buffers acid and forms a protective pellicle; hyposalivation (Sjogren's syndrome, salivary gland irradiation, xerogenic drugs, dehydration in sport; Sikri, 2020) increases risk (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020). The tongue protects lower lingual surfaces and parotid saliva the posterior teeth (Torres et al, 2020).
- **Behavioural:** frequency and timing of acid intake, sipping, swishing or holding drinks, sucking lemons, bedtime acid drinks, vigorous brushing straight after acid, alcohol and drug use.
- **Chemical:** pH, titratable acidity, calcium/phosphate/fluoride content, chelating properties of the product.
- **Modifying:** patient knowledge, systemic health, socioeconomic status.
**Acquired pellicle:** a protein-based, semipermeable layer rapidly formed on the tooth; it acts as a diffusion barrier to acid and a reservoir of remineralising ions, and it is removed by brushing and by strong acids (Sikri, 2020; Olley and Bartlett, 2024). Its protective role is the reason for advising against brushing straight after acid. Saliva and the pellicle are discussed in detail in P3.

## 6. Clinical features

### Explanation

Surface. Early erosion gives "a glassy appearance, particularly on the facial/buccal surfaces of the upper anterior teeth" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; "the teeth first exhibit a diminished luster" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; enamel "becomes smooth and featureless" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Bresciani: wear "starts with a silky-shining surface progressing into concavities (smooth surfaces) or grooves (cusps)" (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020) [SRC]. A cervical enamel rim persists: "an intact rim of enamel along the gingival margin" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; Alani and Patel record that this band "has been theorised to signify neutralisation of the acid by the gingival crevicular fluid" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

Palatal surfaces with exposed dentine and a peripheral enamel rim give "the characteristic appearance of perimolysis"; "The presence of incisal translucency is indicative of a late stage" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; thinned edges end "in the fracturing of the remaining incisal portion" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Occlusally, early "cavities, unlike caries, are hard and have a halo of whitened enamel surrounding the deeper yellow of dentine" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; once dentine is reached, "the softer dentine will dissolve at a greater rate, resulting in the characteristic 'cupping' defect" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; "restorations appear to be proud" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], "often termed 'amalgam islands'" (Rose, Haveman and Davis, 2013) [SRC].

Distribution suggests the source. Bresciani: erosive wear "affects initially the palatal and occlusal surfaces (intrinsic erosion) or the labial surfaces (extrinsic erosion)" (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al add finer patterns: reflux "affects the palatal of the upper third molars more than the second molars, with the first molars affected least", whereas acidic "Liquids affect occlusal surfaces of the lower teeth more than upper occlusal surfaces due to gravity", and they warn that the patterns overlap (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Fizzy drinks give "a characteristic halo shape centred on the mesial portion of the central incisors" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; lemon sucking gives "crescent-shaped or dished defects (rounded as opposed to angular)" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Cervical erosion lesions are "broad, shallow, saucer-shaped depressions" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

Symptoms and activity. Dentine sensitivity "is a sign of acid erosion and normally occurs in patients with meticulous oral hygiene" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; "sensitivity to cold is a good diagnostic feature for active corrosion" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. "A stain-free surface can suggest that any acid erosion is active whilst a stained surface suggests the process is inactive" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Kaidonis et al describe "a pristine mouth with no evidence of staining or plaque build-up" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. The pulp responds with reactionary dentine (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Olley and Bartlett call dentine hypersensitivity "a clinical sign of an active tooth wear process" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Clinical features**
1. **Early:** loss of lustre; smooth, glassy, silky-shining, featureless enamel with loss of perikymata and mamelons (Bartlett, 2011; Goldstein et al, 2018; Alani and Patel, 2025).
2. **Smooth surfaces:** broad, shallow, saucer-shaped concavities, usually wider than deep, no sharp line angles, coronal to the CEJ, with an intact enamel rim at the gingival margin (Torres et al, 2020; Sikri, 2020).
3. **Palatal (intrinsic):** exposed dentine with peripheral enamel rim (perimolysis); incisal translucency and chipping in late stages (Bartlett, 2011).
4. **Occlusal:** hard circular "cupped" cusp tips with a whitened enamel halo, grooves, flattening; restorations standing proud ("amalgam islands") (Bartlett, 2011; Rose, Haveman and Davis, 2013).
5. **Distribution:** extrinsic dietary acid mainly on labial surfaces of upper anteriors and occlusal surfaces of lower posteriors; intrinsic acid on palatal surfaces of upper teeth and occlusal/buccal surfaces of lower molars (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020; Kaidonis et al, 2016); lemon sucking gives crescent-shaped labial defects (Ritter et al, 2019).
6. **Activity:** active lesions are clean, unstained and cold-sensitive; inactive lesions become stained (Bartlett, 2011; Kaidonis et al, 2016).
7. **Sequelae:** dentine hypersensitivity, loss of crown height and occlusal vertical dimension (dentoalveolar compensation), anterior open bite in gastric erosion (Olley and Bartlett, 2024), poor aesthetics, pulp exposure rarely (reactionary dentine usually protects).

## 7. Diagnosis

### Explanation

History. Pickard's lists targeted questions for erosion: diet (with an analysis sheet), regurgitation or pregnancy sickness, "past/present slimming habits, eating disorders, periods of weight loss", alcohol, medicines and occupation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Bartlett: "a diet diary should be carried out like that for dental caries management" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Generalised palatal erosion of the upper anteriors should raise suspicion: "Bulimia or GERD will be the likely cause", and "a dentist may be the first medical professional to recognize signs of bulimia" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt advises that "The dentist must be tactfully candid in discussing this possible etiology" (Rose, Haveman and Davis, 2013) [SRC]. Bilateral parotid enlargement: "This hypertrophy of the gland is commonly seen in bulimics" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

BEWE. Pickard's: it "can be used to stage tooth wear in a similar way to that in which the Basic Periodontal Examination" stages periodontal disease (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. "The most severely affected surface in a sextant is recorded with a 4-level score" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; "The maximum score per subject is 18" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Olley and Bartlett report that the BEWE "is recommended in practice" as a screening tool following UK guidance (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. The books disagree on details:

| Item | Pickard's Guide (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) | Sikri (2020) | Singh and Gade (2020) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Score 3 | "Hard tissue loss ≥ 50% of the surface area" [SRC] | 50% (symbol lost in the extract) | ">50%" [SRC] |
| Cumulative ≤2 | routine; "Repeat at 3-year intervals" [SRC] | "Periodic evaluation: 2–3 years" [SRC] | "no risk" |
| 3-8 | advice; repeat 2-yearly | 1-2 years | low risk |
| 9-13 | advice, fluoride; "Ideally, avoid the placement of restorations and monitor erosive wear with study casts" [SRC]; 6-12 months | 6-12 months | medium risk |
| ≥14 | as above; restorations only "in cases of severe progression" [SRC]; 6-12 months | "Periodic evaluation: 3–6 months" [SRC] | "more than 14 signifies high risk" [SRC], leaving 14 itself unassigned |

The review interval for the highest band differs (6-12 months in Pickard's, 3-6 months in Sikri). Both are recorded; neither is ranked.

Differential diagnosis. Erosive lesions lie coronal to the CEJ with rounded margins; "In contrast to erosive lesions, wedge-shaped lesions are located apical from the CEJ and have sharp margins" (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020) [SRC]. Attrition gives matching flat facets; caries is soft and plaque-related. Age must be considered: "the severity of the tooth wear must always be balanced against the patient's age" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Diagnosis**
1. **Targeted history** (Banerjee and Watson, 2015): diet and drinking habits; reflux symptoms (heartburn, acid taste, regurgitation); vomiting, slimming, eating disorders, weight loss; pregnancy; alcohol; medicines (vitamin C, iron, aspirin, inhalers); occupation and sport; oral hygiene (timing and force of brushing); salivary symptoms.
2. **Diet diary**, as for caries, recording type, frequency, timing and manner of acidic intake (Bartlett, 2011).
3. **Examination:** pattern and site of lesions, activity (staining, sensitivity), occlusion and vertical dimension, salivary flow and buffering, general signs (bilateral parotid enlargement in bulimia; Goldstein et al, 2018). Generalised palatal erosion of upper anteriors suggests bulimia or GORD (Goldstein et al, 2018); the dentist may be first to detect them and must ask tactfully.
4. **BEWE** (introduced by Bartlett et al in 2008, as reported by Sikri, 2020): buccal, occlusal and palatal surfaces of all teeth except third molars are scored 0 (no wear), 1 (initial loss of surface texture), 2 (distinct defect, hard tissue loss less than 50% of the surface), 3 (loss of 50% or more) (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). The highest score per sextant is summed (maximum 18) (Sikri, 2020).
   - 0-2: routine maintenance, review every 2-3 years.
   - 3-8: oral hygiene and dietary advice, review 1-2 years.
   - 9-13: as above plus fluoride and monitoring; avoid restorations; review 6-12 months.
   - 14 or more: as above; restore only if progression is severe; review 3-6 months (Sikri, 2020) or 6-12 months (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
5. **Records:** dated study casts, photographs, silicone indices.
6. **Medical referral** to confirm GORD or an eating disorder.
7. **Differential diagnosis:** abrasion (wedge-shaped, sharp margins, apical to CEJ, often with recession; Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020), attrition (matching flat shiny facets on opposing teeth, sharp edges), abfraction (disputed), caries (soft, plaque-related, chalky), hypoplasia and amelogenesis imperfecta (developmental, present at eruption). Erosive lesions are smooth, rounded and hard, with no facet that fits the opposing tooth, and often coexist with these other forms of wear.

## 8. Prevention

### Explanation

Diet and behaviour. Pickard's recommends gradual change, for example that initially "one in three drinks could be tap water", then alternate drinks (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Bartlett advises "the use of a straw placed toward the back of the mouth", avoiding acid drinks at night, and "not to brush their teeth immediately following an acid drink, but rather rinse with water to clear and dilute the acid" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Sikri: "do not sip or swish acid beverages" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

Timing of brushing is contested. Goldstein et al advise waiting at least 30 minutes because "the time needed for softened enamel and dentin to reharden after an erosive challenge ranges from 2 to 30 min" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Torres et al: some studies suggested patients "should not brush their teeth immediately after acid exposure, although others did not confirm this recommendation" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Olley and Bartlett: delay may not matter for wear, but "it is recommended to avoid brushing after vomiting" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; Goldstein et al: brushing straight after vomiting "will accelerate the loss of enamel" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Hong et al's meta-analysis of in situ and in vitro studies found no significant difference in human enamel wear between delayed and immediate brushing, and concluded that delayed brushing alone cannot prevent erosive enamel wear (Hong et al, 2020) [LIT].

Fluoride. Bartlett: laboratory and in situ evidence is good but "there is virtually no clinical patient-based evidence"; nevertheless "fluoride is protective for erosive tooth wear" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Products: "A neutral 2% sodium fluoride gel or 5% fluoride varnish can be applied on dental visits" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; "Use neutral pH fluoride mouth rinses daily" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; high-fluoride paste and mouthrinse daily (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al distinguish the very active case, where "remineralization is relatively ineffective", and recommend that "metal ion fluorides (e.g. SnF) can have the desired effect through the production of an insoluble precipitate" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Live literature supports the stannous preference at the experimental level (section 11).

Other agents. CPP-ACP creams as "pellicle 'mimicking' agents" have shown "some positive results" without long-term research (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]; sugar-free gum stimulates saliva (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; saliva substitutes in hyposalivation (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020) [SRC]. Sealing: "dentine bonding agents can reduce the amount of wear by half over a period of 6 months", with "repeated applications at regular review" because they wear away (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; dentine sealants "seem to be rather resistant against erosive conditions" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC].

Referral and intrinsic erosion. Medical management of GORD includes antacids, H2 antagonists and "proton pump inhibitors (e.g. omeprazole (Losec))"; "appropriate referral to medical colleagues may be required" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; "In case of intrinsic erosion, referral to an appropriate medical specialist becomes necessary" (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020) [SRC]. After vomiting, "sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) rinses can neutralize the acidity", and the patient should be approached "in a factual, nonconfrontational, concerned manner" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Acidic inhaler use or vomiting "may be mitigated through diluting the acids by swishing with water" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Special groups: wine tasters may leave the biofilm undisturbed "for 1–2 days before a wine-tasting session" and apply fluoride or CPP-ACP to it; a nocturnal refluxer is at risk because acid combines "with the reduced salivary flow" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Splints help bruxists, but their use where erosion is the major factor is "probably ineffective" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Preventive management** (the main treatment; Pickard's BEWE guidance avoids restorations until wear progresses)
1. **Dietary counselling** from the diet diary, with positive, gradual change (e.g. replace one in three acidic drinks with water; Banerjee and Watson, 2015): reduce frequency; take acids in moderation and at mealtimes (Goldstein et al, 2018); include milk, cheese and yoghurt (Sikri, 2020); avoid acidic drinks at bedtime; drink quickly through a straw placed towards the back of the mouth; do not sip, swish or hold drinks; rinse with water afterwards (Bartlett, 2011; Sikri, 2020).
2. **Oral hygiene:** soft brush, low-abrasive fluoride toothpaste, light force; do not brush immediately after acid or vomiting. Goldstein et al (2018) advise waiting 30 minutes; meta-analysis shows delay alone does not prevent enamel wear (Hong et al, 2020).
3. **Fluoride:** fluoride toothpaste (high-fluoride where needed), daily neutral fluoride rinse, professional 2% NaF gel or 5% NaF varnish (Torres et al, 2020). In active erosion stannous (metal-ion) fluorides are preferred because they form an acid-resistant precipitate (Kaidonis et al, 2016).
4. **Saliva:** sugar-free gum, water intake, saliva substitutes in hyposalivation (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020); CPP-ACP as a supplementary agent.
5. **Surface protection:** dentine bonding agent or sealant over exposed dentine, renewed at recall; it can halve wear over 6 months (Bartlett, 2011).
6. **Referral** for intrinsic erosion: physician or gastroenterologist for GORD (PPIs, H2 antagonists, antacids, lifestyle; Banerjee and Watson, 2015); psychiatrist or psychologist for eating disorders, approached in a non-confrontational way.
7. **Bulimia and vomiting:** rinse with water or sodium bicarbonate solution after vomiting, do not brush, apply fluoride (Goldstein et al, 2018).
8. **Occupational:** protective measures against acid fumes; wine tasters can leave plaque undisturbed before tasting and apply fluoride (Kaidonis et al, 2016).

## 9. Monitoring

### Explanation

Bartlett: "The most important aspect to prevention is monitoring the rate of the tooth wear", traditionally with dated study casts kept in durable stone, although "perhaps the simplest method is to use a periodontal probe" to measure crown height and width (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Scratch test: "A number 12 scalpel blade is used to superficially scratch the tooth surface" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; "The disappearance or loss of definition of the scratch over a period of 2–4 weeks suggests that the problem is active" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Monitoring matters because wear "varies in activity with periods of progression and quiescence" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC] and because "'relapse' is still a possibility" after successful prevention (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Monitoring.** Monitoring the rate of wear is the most important part of prevention (Bartlett, 2011). Methods: dated study casts in die stone compared at recall; standardised photographs; silicone indices; periodontal probe measurement of crown height; repeat BEWE scores; the scratch test, where loss of a faint scalpel scratch within 2-4 weeks indicates activity (Goldstein et al, 2018; Kaidonis et al, 2016); and staining and sensitivity as clinical signs of activity. Review intervals follow the BEWE band. Wear is episodic, and relapse is possible after successful prevention, so monitoring continues long term (Kaidonis et al, 2016). Casts can be given to the patient to bring to each review. Activity at recall decides the next step: stable wear continues on prevention and monitoring alone; progressive wear, or wear threatening pulp or function, moves the patient to the restorative phase.

## 10. Restoration of eroded lesions

### Explanation

When to restore. "Once structural tooth surface loss has occurred, it cannot be reversed" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], but "The presence of such defects does not automatically warrant intervention" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Bartlett: "The main indications which eventually demand restorative treatment of worn teeth are appearance and intractable sensitivity" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. All books insist on control first: "Treatment of these types of lesions should be done only when the causative problem is under control", though "provisional restorations during the corrective phase" are acceptable (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; in eating disorders treatment is limited "to emergency, preventive, and/or temporary measures until the disorder is brought under control" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; "if there is active corrosion that remains uncontrolled, any form of treatment will fail" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al admit that "Opinions vary as to the management approach to corrosive cases and clinical studies are lacking" (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Marzouk et al agree: "There should not be any rush to attempt restorative modalities" (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

Principles. Alani and Patel: "The additive provision of adhesive restorations in supra-occlusion is a minimally invasive technique", avoiding the "restorative failure spiral" of conventional full-coverage rehabilitation (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Composite can restore eroded lesions "with little or no preparation with a bur" (Rose, Haveman and Davis, 2013) [SRC]. For cervical lesions Sikri states that "No instrumentation is desired" with composite except, optionally, to "create a bevel on the enamel" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The palatal enamel rim "provides an avenue for a bonded restoration to seal the periphery of the margin with an enamel bond" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

Substrate. Exposed eroded dentine is often sclerotic, which "is much more resistant to acid etching than 'normal' dentin" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; a meta-analysis cited by Sturdevant's found "Roughened dentin resulted in a statistically significantly higher retention rate than unprepared dentin", and GIC-based materials' clinical retention "is more effective and durable than any other type of adhesive material" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein et al: "Bonded composite resin or glass ionomer materials may reduce sensitivity and prevent the erosion from progressing" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sandwich: "Glass-ionomer serves as a dentin replacement and provides chemical adhesion" with composite over it (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Singh and Gade warn that adhesive restorations "may require periodic replacement" (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC].

The older view. Marzouk et al recommend metal where possible: "Metallic restorations have proven to be more resistant to the" erosion process than non-metallic ones (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. This conflicts with the modern additive adhesive approach in Alani and Patel and Pickard's; it is recorded, not reconciled.

Localized anterior wear and the Dahl concept. Dentoalveolar compensation keeps teeth in contact: "The OVD and the freeway space are unaltered as the occlusal contacts are maintained by alveolar bone remodelling" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], so there is no space to restore palatal surfaces. "The concept was first investigated by Dahl and colleagues in 1975": restorations placed in supra-occlusion on the anterior teeth produce posterior eruption and anterior intrusion; "The split between the processes is 60:40, respectively" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Sikri gives the same split, "combined intrusion (40%) and extrusion (60%)", and timing: "Most of the cases re-establish occlusal contacts within 4–6 months; however, it may take 18–24 months" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Alani and Patel use "a 2+ mm volume of restorative material" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], and "The diagnostic wax-up prescription is key to a successful definitive restoration as it forms the blueprint" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Details are in P2.

Materials for palatal restorations: direct composite; indirect composite or resin-nano-ceramic palatal veneers; metal (Ni-Cr, Co-Cr or type III gold) palatal veneers, which "will be more robust and harder wearing than resin-based materials", gold being "kinder to opposing teeth" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; harder surfaces are preferred where "an attritive aetiology requires a more durable, harder occlusal surface" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

Generalised wear. Space may be gained "by surgical crown lengthening, orthodontic intrusion, or shortening the opposing tooth", or by increasing the vertical dimension (Rose, Haveman and Davis, 2013) [SRC]. Crowns are a late option: "Full coverage crown with or without root canal treatment is the final verdict" (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]. The European consensus on severe wear recommends delaying restorative intervention as long as possible and then using a conservative, minimally invasive, adhesive approach (Loomans et al, 2017) [LIT]. In the Radboud RCT on generalised severe wear (41 patients, 3 years), palatal veneers showed no survival difference between direct and indirect composite, while indirect composite "tabletop" restorations on first molars failed more often than direct ones (Crins et al, 2021) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Restoration of eroded lesions**

**Indications** (only after the cause is controlled and activity confirmed by monitoring): unacceptable appearance; sensitivity not relieved by desensitisers; structural weakness or risk of fracture or pulp exposure; progressive loss despite prevention; loss of function or occlusal vertical dimension; protection of exposed dentine (Bartlett, 2011; Ritter et al, 2019). The presence of a lesion alone does not justify restoration (Ritter et al, 2019). While erosion is active only provisional or emergency treatment should be given, because uncontrolled erosion makes any treatment fail (Goldstein et al, 2018; Kaidonis et al, 2016).

**Principles:** minimal or no preparation; additive adhesive restorations; bond to the preserved enamel rim; diagnostic wax-up and mock-up; avoid the "restorative failure spiral" of crowns (Alani and Patel, 2025).

**Options by severity**
1. **Exposed dentine, small lesions:** dentine bonding agent or sealant, renewed periodically (Bartlett, 2011).
2. **Cervical and smooth-surface lesions:** composite with no cavity preparation except an enamel bevel (Sikri, 2020); roughen sclerotic dentine to improve retention; GIC or RMGI where enamel is absent or moisture control is poor, since GIC retention is the most durable clinically (Ritter et al, 2019); sandwich of RMGI and composite (Sikri, 2020).
3. **Occlusal cupping:** direct composite into cupped cusps with little or no preparation (Rose, Haveman and Davis, 2013).
4. **Localized anterior palatal erosion (Dahl concept):** composite or palatal veneers placed about 2 mm in supra-occlusion on a wax-up; posterior teeth re-contact by eruption and anterior intrusion in a 60:40 ratio, usually within 4-6 months, up to 18-24 months (Alani and Patel, 2025; Sikri, 2020). Palatal veneers can be direct composite, indirect composite or resin-ceramic, or cast metal (gold kinder to opposing teeth) where attrition coexists. Labial defects: composite veneers, or palatal composite plus labial ceramic veneer (sandwich approach).
5. **Generalised severe wear:** reorganise occlusion at an increased vertical dimension with direct composite (first choice), indirect composite or ceramic onlays and veneers; crown lengthening or orthodontics to gain space (Rose, Haveman and Davis, 2013). In an RCT, direct composite performed better than indirect composite on molars (Crins et al, 2021).
6. **Last resort:** full-coverage crowns, with endodontics if needed (Singh and Gade, 2020).

Marzouk et al (undated) recommended metallic restorations as most erosion-resistant; current texts prefer adhesive, additive techniques. All restorations need maintenance and periodic replacement (Singh and Gade, 2020), and an occlusal splint protects them when bruxism coexists.

## 11. Recent advances

### Explanation

Recent evidence opened this session (abstracts only):
- **Stannous fluoride.** A network meta-analysis of in situ studies found polyvalent fluorides, especially tin-containing ones, prevented enamel erosion and erosion-abrasion better than non-fluoride and monovalent fluoride groups, and monovalent NaF at low or high concentration did not (da Silva et al, 2022) [LIT]. A meta-analysis of dentifrices found SnF2 better than NaF only in in vitro studies, with insufficient evidence overall (Trentin et al, 2021) [LIT]. An umbrella review of eight systematic reviews rated certainty moderate for stannous systems and low or very low for calcium-based and biomimetic products, and cautioned that the evidence is in vitro/in situ with short follow-up and should not be read as proof of reduced clinical progression (Gomez et al, 2026) [LIT].
- **Beyond fluoride.** Buzalaf et al review adhesives, sealants and flowables as effective but needing re-sealing every few months; film-forming polymers and pellicle engineering (chitosan, salivary proteins) as promising; calcium/phosphate delivery products as unsuccessful; and MMP inhibition (EGCG, chlorhexidine, fluorides) to protect eroded dentine matrix (Buzalaf et al, 2025) [LIT]. Hill and Gillam report that "synthetic statherin peptides" promote a protective pellicle-like layer but "are not economically feasible" (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Sikri lists self-assembling peptide P11-4 for erosion (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; no clinical erosion data for P11-4 were opened here, so treat it as unproven for erosion. [Guessing]
- **Management guidance.** European consensus (Loomans et al, 2017) [LIT] and the Radboud RCT (Crins et al, 2021) [LIT] support delayed, minimally invasive, direct adhesive restoration.
- **Diagnosis and monitoring.** BEWE as a recommended screening tool (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Intraoral scanning for monitoring is widely discussed, but no source opened this session reports its accuracy, so it is listed without evidence. [Guessing]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Recent advances**
1. **Stannous (polyvalent) fluorides:** network meta-analysis of in situ studies showed tin-containing fluorides prevent erosion and erosion-abrasion better than NaF (da Silva et al, 2022); an umbrella review rates this evidence moderate certainty but mostly in vitro/in situ, not yet proven clinically (Gomez et al, 2026).
2. **Delayed brushing** after acid does not by itself prevent enamel wear (Hong et al, 2020); fluoride toothpaste matters more.
3. **Beyond fluoride** (Buzalaf et al, 2025): resin sealants (re-applied every few months), film-forming polymers (chitosan), pellicle engineering with salivary proteins or statherin-like peptides, and MMP inhibitors (EGCG, chlorhexidine) to protect eroded dentine collagen; calcium-phosphate products have not shown a significant anti-erosive effect.
4. **Consensus on severe wear:** delay restoration, then use minimally invasive adhesive techniques (Loomans et al, 2017); direct composite outperformed indirect composite on molars in an RCT (Crins et al, 2021).
5. **BEWE** recommended for screening and monitoring in general practice (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

## 12. Conclusion

### Explanation

The books agree on the sequence (identify, prevent, monitor, restore late and conservatively) and disagree on details: critical pH, the relative erosivity of cola and juice, BEWE review intervals, brushing timing and the role of saliva. The clinical evidence for most preventive measures is thin; restorative evidence is stronger for minimally invasive composite than for any preventive agent. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** Erosion is acid dissolution of tooth tissue without bacteria, from dietary, medicinal, occupational or gastric sources, made worse by abrasion and attrition of the softened surface. Management is a sequence: diagnose and find the cause (history, diet diary, BEWE, referral for GORD or eating disorders), control it by counselling, fluoride (preferably stannous) and protection, monitor activity, and restore only when needed, using additive, minimally invasive adhesive techniques such as the Dahl approach. Restoration without control of the cause will fail. Because the evidence for most preventive agents is still mainly laboratory and in situ, and textbooks disagree on details such as critical pH and BEWE review intervals, the clinician should combine prevention, monitoring and minimally invasive restoration and review the patient for life.

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim 1: "Patients should wait 30-60 minutes before brushing after an acid challenge."**
*Textbook says:* Goldstein et al advise at least 30 minutes because softened tissue rehardens in 2-30 minutes (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Sikri and Bartlett advise not brushing immediately (Sikri, 2020; Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
*What other sources show:* Torres et al note that other studies "did not confirm this recommendation" (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; Olley and Bartlett say delay may not matter for wear but advise against brushing after vomiting (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Hong et al's meta-analysis found no difference in human enamel wear between delayed and immediate brushing, and a benefit from fluoride toothpaste (Hong et al, 2020) [LIT].
*Agreement or challenge:* challenge. The waiting rule rests largely on bovine enamel and laboratory rehardening data. [Likely]
*Strength:* in situ and in vitro only; no clinical trial. [Certain about the study type]
*Conclusion and clinical implication:* advise rinsing with water and fluoride toothpaste; do not present the 30-minute rule as proven; keep the no-brushing advice after vomiting.

**Claim 2: "Fluoride prevents erosion."**
*Textbook says:* "fluoride is protective for erosive tooth wear" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; NaF gels and varnishes recommended (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC].
*What other sources show:* Bartlett himself notes "virtually no clinical patient-based evidence" (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Kaidonis et al: in very active erosion "remineralization is relatively ineffective" and metal-ion fluorides are preferred (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Network meta-analysis: monovalent NaF did not prevent in situ erosion, tin compounds did (da Silva et al, 2022) [LIT]; umbrella review: moderate certainty for stannous, mostly experimental (Gomez et al, 2026) [LIT].
*Agreement or challenge:* partial agreement; the claim holds for stannous formulations in experimental models, not for NaF in general. [Likely]
*Strength:* in situ and in vitro; clinical outcome trials lacking. [Certain]
*Conclusion:* prefer stannous fluoride toothpaste in at-risk patients, combine with diet control, and say that clinical proof is lacking.

**Claim 3: "Hyposalivation increases erosion."**
*Textbook says:* "patients presenting hyposalivation are more prone to erosion" (Bresciani, Torres and Wiegand, 2020) [SRC]; hyposalivation "may induce dental erosion without excessive exposure to erosive agents" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].
*Challenge:* "The role of saliva in erosion is unclear"; clinical data are equivocal, "with some studies showing a positive correlation and others not", possibly because xerostomic patients avoid acidic drinks (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
*Strength:* mechanistic reasoning and mixed observational data; no source opened gives effect sizes. [Likely]
*Conclusion:* treat hyposalivation as a plausible risk factor and manage it, but do not write that it is proven to cause erosion.

## High-yield points
- Definition: chemical loss without bacteria (Pickard's); erosive tooth wear is chemical-mechanical (Bresciani); "corrosion"/"biocorrosion" are alternative terms.
- Extrinsic (diet, drugs, occupation, sport), intrinsic (GORD, bulimia, vomiting; gastric pH 1-1.5), idiopathic.
- Critical pH 5.5 enamel, 6.5 dentine (Olley and Bartlett); undersaturation is the true condition; titratable acidity and chelation matter.
- Signs: glassy enamel, cervical enamel rim, perimolysis, cupping, proud restorations; unstained and sensitive means active.
- BEWE 0-3 per sextant, maximum 18; 0-2, 3-8, 9-13, 14 or more; books differ on review intervals.
- Prevention: diet diary and counselling, straw, no swishing, stannous fluoride, sealants, referral; delayed brushing alone not proven.
- Restore late, additively: composite with no preparation, GIC/RMGI cervically, Dahl (60:40, 4-6 months), palatal veneers, crowns last.
- Recent: stannous fluoride, pellicle engineering, MMP inhibitors, European consensus, Radboud RCT.

## Common exam mistakes
- Defining erosion as "loss by acids" without saying "not involving bacteria", which fails to separate it from caries.
- Giving one critical pH as absolute and omitting titratable acidity; cola versus juice erosivity is disputed between books.
- Writing that restorations should be placed as soon as erosion is detected; every source says control and monitor first.
- Stating "wait 30 minutes before brushing" as proven fact; the meta-analysis does not support delay alone.
- Omitting medical referral for GORD and eating disorders, the commonest cause of severe erosion.
