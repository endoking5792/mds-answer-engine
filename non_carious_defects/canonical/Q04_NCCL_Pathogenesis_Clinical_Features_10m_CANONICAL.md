## 1. Definition and types

### Explanation

Summitt's chapter defines the entity by exclusion: a Class 5 lesion resulting from factors other than dental caries is a non-carious cervical lesion (NCCL) (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. The term describes a site and a non-bacterial cause; it does not name a single mechanism. Three mechanisms are usually listed: erosion (chemical), abrasion (mechanical, foreign object) and abfraction (occlusal stress). Sturdevant's places abfraction alongside "other" NCCLs, contrasting its sharp notch with the saucer-shaped defects of the others (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

The books do not agree on which mechanisms belong under the NCCL heading, and this is recorded side by side:

| Book | NCCL includes |
|---|---|
| Singh, 2nd ed | attrition, abrasion, erosion and abfraction collectively known as non-carious cervical lesions (Singh, 2020) [SRC] |
| Sturdevant's 2nd SA ed | abrasion and abfraction under "Non-carious cervical lesions"; attrition treated separately as occlusal wear (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] |
| Summitt's 4th ed | erosion, abrasion and abfraction (stress corrosion) implicated in the formation of some NCCLs (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC] |

Attrition is by definition tooth-to-tooth wear on occlusal, incisal and proximal surfaces, so including it as a cervical lesion is hard to defend anatomically [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** NCCL: loss of tooth structure at the cemento-enamel junction (gingival third of facial/lingual surfaces) from causes other than caries (Overton et al, 2013).

**Types (by mechanism):** erosion (biocorrosion, acid), abrasion (friction from foreign objects, mainly toothbrush/dentifrice) and abfraction (occlusal flexure stress). Singh (2020) also lists attrition; most books treat attrition as occlusal wear.

## 2. Pathogenesis

### Explanation

**Multifactorial, not single-cause.** Summitt's chapter states that there is overwhelming evidence that the cause is multifactorial (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], with nearly equal evidence to support toothpaste/toothbrush abrasion, occlusal forces and low pH as the primary cause (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's says well-designed clinical research studies are required to gain better understanding of cervical abrasion and abfraction (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Abrasion, accelerated by acid.** Mount et al consider vigorous toothbrushing (especially with a horizontal movement) of acid-softened enamel and dentine still the primary cause of NCCLs (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC], and Kaidonis et al add that acid plays a role in the development of all these lesions (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. The mechanism is softening first, then removal: citric acid softens enamel within minutes, but it takes in the order of 12 hours for enamel to harden (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]. Brushing alone, without acid, does little: the effects of normal toothbrushing on enamel wear are negligible (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], and laboratory studies show normal brushing forces are unlikely to cause significant wear of enamel or dentine (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

**Abfraction (flexure).** Sturdevant's defines abfraction as strong eccentric occlusal force resulting in microfractures at the cervical area of the tooth (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The sequence in its Box 20.1 is tooth flexure, bending, then tensile and compressive stresses generated in the cervical region (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], with the maximum stress in the thinnest region of enamel at the cementoenamel junction (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein et al add a chemical step: small molecules enter the microfractures and prevent the reformation of the chemical bonds (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

**Interaction of stress and acid (laboratory evidence).** These findings are laboratory data, not clinical proof:
- Finite element analysis predicts that occlusal loads could cause stress fractures or stress corrosion in the cervical areas of teeth (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- Without any acids present, occlusal loading had no effect in creating NCCLs in brushed extracted teeth (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- Enamel dissolution increased significantly when teeth were subjected to cyclic tensile loads while immersed in 1% lactic acid (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
Summitt's chapter concludes that liquid acid, frequently found in modern diets, may be necessary to make occlusal loading a factor (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**How strong is the occlusal component?** The books disagree:
- Goldstein et al: sufficient experimental and clinical evidence has now been gathered to establish the primary etiology as tensile stress of occlusal origin (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- Bartlett: the phenomenon has yet to be proven clinically (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
- Olley and Bartlett: the role of occlusal loading is not fundamental; erosion and abrasion are now more likely the important aetiologies (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Modern disease.** No NCCLs were found in 3,927 teeth from 259 individuals of the Copper Age and Middle Ages (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], which fits a role for modern acidic diets and toothbrushing [Likely]. Prevalence ranges from rare to 89% (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], increasing with age (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; a systematic review cited by Olley and Bartlett gives an average adult prevalence of 46.7% (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pathogenesis: multifactorial** (Overton et al, 2013), with near-equal evidence for brushing abrasion, occlusal forces and low pH.

1. **Erosion/biocorrosion:** dietary or gastric acid softens enamel and dentine; enamel takes about 12 hours to re-harden (Mount et al, 2016).
2. **Abrasion:** horizontal brushing with dentifrice removes the softened surface; brushing alone on sound enamel causes negligible wear (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).
3. **Abfraction:** eccentric load → tooth flexure → tensile/compressive stress at the CEJ, where enamel is thinnest → microfractures of enamel rods → loss (Ritter et al, 2019).
4. **Synergy:** in the laboratory, load caused no lesions without acid but increased enamel dissolution in acid (Overton et al, 2013).

Prevalence rises with age; mean adult prevalence 46.7% (Olley and Bartlett, 2024). Lesions are absent in pre-modern skulls.

## 3. Clinical features

### Explanation

**Site.** Premolars are most commonly involved in cervical abrasion (Singh, 2020) [SRC], and toothbrush abrasion is most often seen on canines and premolars (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

**Shape by mechanism.** The books describe characteristic shapes, but they disagree on abrasion:
- Abrasion: a rounded notch (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; dish- or V-shaped (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; a v-shaped lesion with horizontal striations (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Mount et al report horizontal abrasive scratch marks in the vast majority of lesions (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC].
- Erosion (corrosion) alone: typically dish- or saucer-shaped (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC].
- Abfraction: a sharp, angular defect that may lie completely beneath the gingival margin (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; V-shaped lesions with no history of abrasion (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

**Distinguishing features proposed.** Goldstein et al distinguish abrasion from abfraction by the smooth, rounded nature of abrasion (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], and note that gingival recession may accompany abfraction but is not its hallmark (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Torres et al add that abrasion involves many neighbouring teeth while abfraction can occur on a single tooth (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC], and that abfractions may be partly or completely subgingival (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Wear facets or loss of anterior guidance are highly probable when abfraction is present (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

These shape rules are not reliable, which is the key exam point: differentiating aetiology from lesion shape has yet to be adequately supported with evidence (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], and Bartlett says the lesions cannot be differentiated from cervical erosion and abrasion (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

**Symptoms and dentine.** Hypersensitivity is the usual presenting complaint (Singh, 2020) [SRC]. The exposed dentine becomes sclerotic, much more resistant to acid etching than normal dentine (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], which matters for bonding (Q5). In leaking restorations, flexure may change fluid flow and cause sensitivity and pulpal inflammation (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Most NCCLs are not carious (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Activity.** A No. 12 scalpel scratch can be placed and checked later; loss of the scratch indicates active loss (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

**Consequences.** Untreated NCCLs can cause progressive tooth loss, sensitivity, need for endodontic therapy, tooth loss and further lesions (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Clinical features**
- Site: facial cervical third, commonest on premolars and canines; rises with age.
- Symptom: dentine hypersensitivity is the usual complaint; deep lesions risk pulp exposure and cervical fracture.
- Dentine: sclerotic, glassy, resists acid etching (Ritter et al, 2019); caries usually absent.

| Feature | Abrasion | Erosion | Abfraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | rounded or V notch, horizontal scratches | broad dish/saucer | sharp wedge, angular margins |
| Teeth | several adjacent | many surfaces | often single tooth |
| Gingiva | recession common | normal | may be subgingival |
| Clue | brushing habit | acid history | wear facets, occlusal interference |

(Goldstein et al, 2018; Mount et al, 2016; Torres et al, 2020)

Shape alone does not reliably identify the cause; most lesions are mixed (Overton et al, 2013). Activity can be checked with a scalpel scratch test.

## High-yield points
- NCCL = cervical tooth loss not caused by caries; mechanism mix of erosion, abrasion, abfraction.
- Acid softens, brushing removes; brushing alone barely wears sound enamel.
- Abfraction: flexure stress at the CEJ; supported by FEA and laboratory data, not proven clinically.
- In vitro, load produced no lesion without acid.
- Prevalence rises with age (mean adult 46.7% in one review); lesions absent in pre-modern skulls.
- Sclerotic dentine resists etching, a key restorative problem.
- Lesion shape does not reliably indicate cause.

## Common exam mistakes
- Writing abfraction as the established cause of all wedge-shaped lesions; the books are divided and clinical proof is lacking.
- Presenting FEA or extracted-tooth loading data as clinical evidence.
- Claiming that the shape of a lesion reliably identifies its cause.
- Forgetting sclerotic dentine and hypersensitivity in the clinical features.
- Including attrition as a cervical lesion without noting that most books treat it as occlusal wear.
