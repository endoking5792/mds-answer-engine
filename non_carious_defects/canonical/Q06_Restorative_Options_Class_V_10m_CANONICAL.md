## 1. Definition and etiology

### Explanation

"Class 5 lesions are carious and noncarious defects found in the gingival third of facial and lingual tooth surfaces" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Carious lesions arise from plaque at the gingival margin, including root caries after recession. For NCCLs "there is overwhelming evidence that the cause is multifactorial": abrasion, erosion and occlusal stress (abfraction) (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Q4 and Q7 cover pathogenesis.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Class V lesions are carious or non-carious defects in the gingival third of facial and lingual surfaces (Overton et al, 2013).

**Etiology**
- Carious: plaque acids at the gingival margin; root caries after recession, hyposalivation, poor hygiene.
- Non-carious (multifactorial): abrasion (brushing), erosion (dietary or gastric acid), abfraction (occlusal stress), usually combined (Overton et al, 2013).

## 2. When to restore

### Explanation

Active caries is treated to control disease (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. For NCCLs, the Academy of Operative Dentistry criteria quoted in Summitt start with "Inability to eliminate or greatly reduce the rate of lesion progression through elimination of etiologic factors", then aesthetics, sensitivity and threat to tooth strength (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. "The first goal of any treatment is to remove the causes of the NCCL(s)" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Before restoring:** remove the cause (diet, brushing, occlusion, plaque). Restore when caries is active, progression continues, aesthetics or sensitivity demand it, or tooth strength is threatened (Overton et al, 2013).

## 3. Restorative options

### Explanation

Summitt's Box 15-1 divides Class 5 materials into non-aesthetic (amalgam; "Gold foil (direct, not widely used)"; gold inlay) and aesthetic (composite with bonding agent, sandwich, flowable, glass ionomer, RMGI, compomer, "Porcelain inlay (not widely used)") (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

- **GIC/RMGI.** Franco et al "reported that 96.4% of RMGI restorations were retained at 5 years, while only 51.5% of bonded resin composite restorations were retained", and a review found glass ionomers "showed the highest retention rates" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: RMGIs "are the materials of choice for the restoration of NCCLs where esthetics is not of primary importance", and root caries in active-caries patients is "the primary indication for the use of a glass ionomer" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Conventional GIC suffers "surface degradation rather rapidly, especially in the presence of acidic foods" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo prefer aesthetic glass ionomer for Site 3 because adhesion and fluoride release "are superior" with similar aesthetics (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].
- **Composite.** Predominates "in areas of esthetic concern" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Enamel margins are bevelled; "Beveling the gingival margin that ends on cementum is not recommended" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt: "no differences in retention rates between microfilled and hybrid resin composites", and for flowables "Clinical trials have proven the theoretical to be incorrect" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Q03 records the books that disagree.
- **Compomer.** "the marginal integrity of compomers has been worse than that of resin composites in long-term clinical trials" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- **Sandwich.** "Glass ionomer is used to replace the missing dentin, reduce leakage", with a composite veneer for aesthetics; "a 96% survival rate of sandwich restorations at 5 years" in one trial (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- **Amalgam.** Indicated where "esthetics is not a concern", access is poor and moisture control difficult (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; it needs "grooves ... in the dentin of both the occlusal and gingival walls" when not bonded, and no bevel (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- **Gold.** Historical; the direct gold preparation "had a trapezoidal outline form" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Restorative options** (Overton et al, 2013, Box 15-1)

| Option | Indication | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| GIC | Root caries, high caries risk, xerostomia | Chemical adhesion, fluoride; degrades in acid; no bevel |
| RMGI | NCCLs where aesthetics is secondary (Ritter et al, 2019) | Best retention: 96.4% vs 51.5% for composite at 5 years in one trial |
| Composite + adhesive | Aesthetic zone, good isolation | Bevel enamel, never cementum; incremental; microfill vs hybrid retention equal (Summitt) |
| Flowable | Small lesions | No proven retention benefit |
| Compomer | Low-stress, moderate risk | Needs bonding agent; poorer margins than composite |
| Sandwich (GIC + composite) | Large or subgingival NCCLs in aesthetic areas | GIC replaces dentine; composite veneer; 96% survival at 5 years |
| Amalgam | Posterior, poor access or isolation | Retention grooves, no bevel (Torres et al, 2020) |
| Gold foil/inlay, porcelain inlay | Rarely used | Trapezoidal outline removed sound tooth |

## 4. Technique essentials

### Explanation

"While a rubber dam is the ideal method of field isolation", cord and cotton rolls often suffice (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's offers "rubber dam and No. 212 retainer or with a cotton roll and retraction cord" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. GIC is conditioned with "a mild acid, such as 10% polyacrylic acid" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and "careful finishing with a no. 12 or 12B scalpel blade is recommended" at the gingival margin (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. "For NCCLs, little or no preparation is required" for adhesive materials (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Technique:** rubber dam with No. 212 clamp, or cord and cotton rolls (Ritter et al, 2019). NCCLs need little or no preparation for adhesive materials (Overton et al, 2013). Condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for GIC (Ritter et al, 2019); etch and bond actively for composite; finish gingival margins with a No. 12 blade (Overton et al, 2013).

**Conclusion.** No single material suits every Class V lesion; choose by caries risk, aesthetics, isolation and lesion depth.

## High-yield points
- Class V = gingival third, carious or non-carious; NCCL cause multifactorial.
- Remove the cause first; restore by AOD criteria.
- RMGI best retention; composite best aesthetics; sandwich combines both.
- No bevel on cementum, GIC or amalgam.

## Common exam mistakes
- Listing materials without indications.
- Bevelling cementum margins or GIC/amalgam margins.
- Omitting cause control before restoration.
- Claiming flowables or microfills are proven superior without mention of contrary trials.
