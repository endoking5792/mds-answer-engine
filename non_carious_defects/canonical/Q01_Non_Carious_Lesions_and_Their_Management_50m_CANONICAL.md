## 1. Introduction and definitions

### Explanation

Caries is not the only cause of tooth tissue loss. Singh and Gade (2020) and Marzouk et al (undated) both repeat the same estimate, that 25% of tooth destruction does not originate from a caries process (Singh and Gade, 2020; Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Singh and Gade credit John Hunter with first describing attrition, abrasion and erosion in 1778 (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC].

The modern umbrella term is **tooth wear (tooth surface loss)**. Pickard's defines it as the irreversible surface loss of dental hard tissues caused by factors other than caries or trauma (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC]; Torres et al put the same idea as loss of tooth structure without the participation of bacterial biofilm (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Two features follow from these definitions and drive the whole management: the loss is irreversible, so prevention and monitoring come first, and the cause is not bacterial, so caries-style restorative thinking does not fit.

A cariology glossary in the Dental Clinics of North America gives compact definitions that are useful for the exam (Fontana et al, 2010, drawing mainly on Imfeld, 1996). It notes that "Erosion, abrasion, and attrition are increasingly being termed" tooth wear (Fontana et al, 2010) [SRC]. Erosion is loss "chemically etched away from the tooth surface by acid and/or chelation without bacterial involvement"; "Abrasion is the pathologic wearing away of dental hard tissue through abnormal mechanical processes involving foreign objects or substances repeatedly introduced in the mouth"; "Demastication describes the wearing away of tooth substance during the mastication of food" with the bolus between the teeth; "Attrition is the physiologic wearing away of dental hard tissue as a result of" tooth-to-tooth contact; and "Abfraction is a wedge-shaped defect at the CEJ of a tooth caused by eccentrically applied occlusal forces leading to tooth flexure" (Fontana et al, 2010) [SRC]. Two points differ from the textbooks: erosion includes chelation as well as acid, and attrition is called physiologic, whereas Goldstein et al name bruxism as its most common cause (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; both are recorded. The glossary gives the abfraction definition without the textbooks' caveat, though it also says that for cervical lesions "The etiology of these lesions appears to be multifactorial" (Fontana et al, 2010) [SRC].

How common is it? Bartlett writes that early signs are almost universal in adults, while the more severe forms probably affect around 10% of the population (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. For cervical lesions, Overton et al report that none were found in 3,927 teeth from 259 Copper Age and Middle Ages individuals (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], whereas prevalence today ranges from rare to 89% depending on the group (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. The contrast suggests these are largely diseases of modern diet and hygiene habits [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Non-carious lesions are losses or defects of tooth structure not caused by bacterial caries. About 25% of tooth destruction is estimated to be non-carious (Singh and Gade, 2020; Marzouk et al, undated). John Hunter first described attrition, abrasion and erosion in 1778.

**Definition (tooth wear).** "Irreversible surface loss of dental hard tissues caused by factors other than caries or trauma" (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). It may be physiological (slow, age-related) or pathological (rapid, symptomatic, out of proportion to age).

**Significance.** Severe wear affects about 10% of adults (Bartlett, 2011). Cervical lesions were absent in 3,927 medieval teeth but today range from rare to 89% of groups studied (Overton et al, 2013), so they are diseases of modern diet and hygiene. Because the loss is irreversible and non-bacterial, management is based on diagnosis of the cause, prevention and monitoring; restoration is the last step, not the first. Adhesive materials (GIC, composite) now allow minimally invasive repair.

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

The books classify differently, and the lists are best shown side by side:

| Source | Categories listed |
|---|---|
| Singh and Gade, 2020 | Attrition, abrasion, erosion, abfraction, amelogenesis imperfecta, dentinogenesis imperfecta, non-hereditary enamel hypoplasia (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC] |
| Marzouk et al, undated | Twelve "non-caries destructive processes": attrition, abrasion, erosion, localized non-hereditary enamel hypoplasia and hypocalcification, localized non-hereditary dentin hypoplasia and hypocalcification, discolorations, malformation, amelogenesis imperfecta, dentinogenesis imperfecta, trauma (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] |
| Garg and Garg, 2011 | Attrition, abrasion, erosion, abfraction, resorption, localized non-hereditary enamel hypoplasia and hypocalcification; resorption is "a condition associated with either a physiologic or a pathologic process resulting in the loss of dentin, cementum or bone" (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] |
| Pickard's | Erosion (intrinsic, extrinsic dietary, extrinsic environmental), attrition, abrasion, and abfraction marked "contentious" (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC] |

Marzouk's list explains the old exam wording "destruction and disfigurement": it covers both tissue loss and developmental or colour defects. The four wear processes rarely act alone; Bartlett stresses that a combination is the rule (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. A practical scheme for the exam is therefore (a) acquired wear (attrition, abrasion including demastication, erosion, abfraction, and their combinations), (b) developmental defects, (c) others (resorption, discolouration, trauma) [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**

A. **Tooth wear (acquired, post-eruptive)**
1. Attrition: tooth-to-tooth
2. Abrasion: tooth-to-foreign object (including demastication)
3. Erosion (corrosion): chemical, non-bacterial acid; intrinsic or extrinsic
4. Abfraction: stress-induced cervical loss (contentious, Banerjee and Watson, 2011)
5. Combined lesions (e.g. erosion-abrasion, attrition-abfraction)

B. **Developmental defects:** amelogenesis imperfecta, dentinogenesis imperfecta, non-hereditary enamel hypoplasia (fluorosis, Turner's tooth) (Singh and Gade, 2020); hypocalcification; MIH

C. **Others** (Marzouk et al, undated; Garg and Garg, 2011): resorption (internal, external), discolorations, malformations, trauma

Marzouk et al list twelve "non-caries destructive processes", which explains the older wording "destruction and disfigurement". In practice the wear processes coexist in one mouth (Bartlett, 2011), so the diagnosis names the dominant process and the co-factors.

## 3. Attrition

### Explanation

Goldstein et al define attrition as loss of tooth structure by mechanical forces from opposing teeth (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Bartlett, more briefly, as the wear of tooth against tooth (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Goldstein et al state that the most common cause is bruxism (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. The diagnostic sign is matching facets: Bartlett describes paired wear facets on opposing teeth (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], and Pickard's adds a blue-grey appearance of thinned incisal edges (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Marzouk et al describe flattening of occluding surfaces with facet formation (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Heavy clenching can also produce enamel craze lines, which Sturdevant's says are not sensitive and do not require treatment (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

The vertical dimension question matters for later restoration. Marzouk et al explain that slow wear lets the alveolar bone grow occlusally, bringing the teeth to their original occlusal termination (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Goldstein et al agree that with bruxism there is rarely loss of the vertical dimension of occlusion (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Singh and Gade give a simple 0-4 attrition index (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]. Attrition is covered in depth in Q9.

#### ✎ Exam core

**3. Attrition**
- *Definition:* loss of tooth structure by tooth-to-tooth contact (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- *Etiology:* physiological (age); pathological mainly bruxism, also malocclusion, edge-to-edge bite, missing posterior teeth.
- *Features:* flat, shiny, sharp-margined facets that match the opposing tooth (Bartlett, 2011); cupped dentine on cusp tips; thin, blue-grey incisal edges; craze lines; proximal facets causing drifting and loss of arch length.
- *Sequelae:* sensitivity, cheek biting, fractured restorations, TMJ strain; vertical dimension usually maintained by dentoalveolar compensation (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- *Assessment:* 0-4 attrition index (no wear to dentine exposure/loss of contour) (Singh and Gade, 2020).
- *Treatment sequence:* control bruxism (splint), resolve TMJ symptoms, protect sensitive dentine (fluoride, bonding agent), occlusal equilibration, then restoration only if needed; endodontics for pulpally involved teeth.

## 4. Abrasion and demastication

### Explanation

Bartlett defines abrasion as mechanical wear of teeth from forces other than teeth (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Toothbrushing is the classic cause: Marzouk et al call toothbrush abrasion the most predominant abrasion (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Singh and Gade list horizontal brushing, force, bristle hardness and dentifrice, noting toothpowder is more abrasive than other dentifrices (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]; Marzouk et al add that natural bristles are more abrasive (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Singh and Gade state that lesions are more commonly seen on the left side of the mouth in right-handed people (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC], but Overton et al say such anecdotal claims have not been verified with clinical studies (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

Other causes are occupational and habit-related (pins, nails, pipe stems), toothpicks and ill-fitting clasps of partial dentures (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]. Food can also abrade: Goldstein et al describe wear from friction of the food bolus (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], termed masticatory abrasion or demastication (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

The books disagree on what a toothbrush lesion looks like:
- Singh and Gade classify abrasion morphology as notch/V-shaped, C-shaped, undercut concave and divergent box (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC], with depth grades starting at shallow (S): 0.1-0.5 mm (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC].
- Goldstein et al: a v-shaped lesion with horizontal striations (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- Sturdevant's: a rounded notch in the gingival portion of the facial surface (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Garg and Garg: saucer-shaped or wedge-shaped (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

Kaidonis et al add a useful clinical point: dentine exposed by abrasion alone is generally not sensitive (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC], because brushing burnishes the tubules; sensitivity on an abraded surface therefore suggests superimposed acid. Bartlett goes further and doubts abrasion as the main cause of cervical lesions: cervical wear is often mistakenly referred to as abrasion (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], and, citing mostly laboratory studies, that normal brushing forces are unlikely to cause significant wear (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. See the critical synthesis.

#### ✎ Exam core

**4. Abrasion**
- *Definition:* mechanical wear by objects other than teeth (Bartlett, 2011).
- *Etiology:* horizontal, forceful brushing with hard bristles and abrasive dentifrice (powder more abrasive than paste, Singh and Gade, 2020); occupational habits (pins, nails, pipe); toothpicks; denture clasps. Wear from coarse food is called demastication (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- *Features:* buccal cervical lesions on canines and premolars; smooth, hard, polished surface; sharp margins; plaque-free. Morphology: notch/V-shaped, C-shaped, undercut concave, divergent box; depth shallow (0.1-0.5 mm), deep (over 0.5 mm, no exposure) or exposure (Singh and Gade, 2020). Abraded dentine alone is often insensitive (Kaidonis et al, 2016); sensitivity suggests an acid component. Books differ on shape: V-shaped with striations (Goldstein et al, 2018) versus rounded notch (Ritter et al, 2019).
- *Treatment:* correct brushing (vertical, modified Stillman), super-soft brush, paste not powder, remove the habit or faulty clasp; desensitizers; GIC, RMGI or composite to seal deeper defects (Singh and Gade, 2020).

## 5. Erosion

### Explanation

Bartlett defines erosion as loss of tooth structure caused by acids and not involving bacterial fermentation (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Sturdevant's as loss by chemical action in the continued presence of demineralizing agents with low pH (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al prefer "corrosion", defined as superficial loss of dental hard tissue due to chemical demineralization by acids of non-bacterial origin (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC], arguing it is a more accurate term for what occurs (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]; Goldstein et al make the same point but keep "erosion" by convention (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

**Extrinsic acids.** Diet (citrus, carbonated and sports drinks, wine, vinegar), medicines, occupation and swimming pools. Bartlett regards frequency of consumption as probably the most important component (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Goldstein et al note that oral pH below 5.5 initiates demineralization (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

**Intrinsic acids.** Gastric acid, which Torres et al give a pH of 1-1.5 (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC], reaches the mouth through GORD or vomiting. Bartlett notes that in around 25% of reflux patients there are no symptoms (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Pickard's gives bulimia nervosa as affecting 1-2% of the adolescent population (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC]; Sturdevant's places eating disorders mainly in young women aged 20-30 years (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Saliva modifies risk: Pickard's says dry-mouth patients may be more at risk (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], while Bartlett finds the role of saliva unclear (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; P3 discusses this.

**Clinical features.** Glassy, smooth surfaces (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], a "frosted glass" appearance in active lesions (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]; palatal perimolysis from gastric acid (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; occlusal "cupping" that, unlike caries, is hard with a halo of whitened enamel (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; restorations standing proud (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; incisal translucency as a late sign (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Marzouk et al add that there is no demarcation between lesion and adjacent surface (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and cite an estimate that 18% of adults exhibit erosive lesions (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Wang and Lussi set erosion against caries: caries is a subsurface lesion under an intact surface layer, whereas "tooth erosion is characterized by initial surface softening and subsequent bulk material loss" (Wang and Lussi, 2010) [SRC]; Fontana et al put it the same way, "in dental erosion stronger nonbacterial acids affect the surface layer directly" (Fontana et al, 2010) [SRC]. They explain the intact cervical enamel rim by "plaque residues, which can act as a diffusion barrier to acids, and on the other hand by sulcular fluid" (Wang and Lussi, 2010) [SRC], place intrinsic erosion on "the occlusal and palatal tooth surfaces of the maxillary teeth" (Wang and Lussi, 2010) [SRC], and report rising incidence in a UK adolescent cohort: "Almost 5% of the subjects at baseline and more than 13% 2 years later had deep enamel or dentin lesions" (Wang and Lussi, 2010) [SRC]. Q2 covers erosion at 75m.

#### ✎ Exam core

**5. Erosion (corrosion)**
- *Definition:* loss of tooth structure by acids not involving bacteria (Bartlett, 2011). Kaidonis et al (2016) prefer "corrosion".
- *Extrinsic:* citrus fruit and juices, carbonated and sports drinks, wine, vinegar, vitamin C tablets, industrial fumes, chlorinated pools. Frequency matters most (Bartlett, 2011); pH below 5.5 demineralizes (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- *Intrinsic:* gastric acid (pH 1-1.5, Torres et al, 2020) from GORD, bulimia, anorexia, pregnancy, alcoholism; reflux is silent in about 25% (Bartlett, 2011).
- *Nature:* unlike caries (a subsurface lesion under an intact surface), erosion softens the surface, which is then lost layer by layer (Wang and Lussi, 2010).
- *Features:* smooth, glazed, "frosted glass" featureless enamel; shallow, broad lesions with no demarcation from adjacent surface (Marzouk et al, undated); palatal perimolysis with an enamel rim (intrinsic); labial lesions (extrinsic); hard occlusal cupping with a halo of white enamel, unlike caries; proud restorations; incisal translucency (late sign) (Bartlett, 2011); sensitivity; gingiva usually healthy.
- *Risk modifiers:* reduced salivary flow and buffering, sipping or swishing drinks, brushing immediately after acid.
- *Treatment outline:* identify and remove the acid source (diet change, medical treatment of reflux or bulimia), fluoride and remineralising agents, seal exposed dentine, and restore adhesively only when the process is controlled (sections 9-10). Generalized palatal erosion should raise suspicion of bulimia or GERD (Goldstein et al, 2018).

## 6. Abfraction and the non-carious cervical lesion

### Explanation

**Concept.** Singh and Gade credit Grippo (1991) with the term (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC], as do Goldstein et al (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Torres et al derive it from the Latin frangere, to break (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Sturdevant's defines it as strong eccentric occlusal force resulting in microfractures at the cervical area of the tooth, causing wedge-shaped defects (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Singh and Gade as wedge-shaped cervical defects caused by abnormal occlusal loading (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]. The proposed mechanism: lateral loading flexes the tooth at its cervical fulcrum; tensile stress disrupts thin cervical enamel and dentine, which then erodes and abrades more easily. Sturdevant's quotes a functional load of 70 N as clinically normal (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Features claimed.** Sharp, wedge-shaped lesions instead of saucer-shaped ones (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; premolars most affected (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]; lesions partly or completely subgingival (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; often on a single tooth, unlike abrasion (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Wang and Lussi, without assigning a cause, describe the wedge-shaped defect at or apical to the CEJ: "The coronal part of wedge-shaped defects ideally has a sharp margin and cuts at a right angle into the enamel surface", the apical part runs out onto the root, and "Thereby the depth of the defect exceeds its width" (Wang and Lussi, 2010) [SRC].

**The controversy** (see critical synthesis):
- For: Goldstein et al state that sufficient experimental and clinical evidence now establishes tensile stress of occlusal origin as the primary etiology (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], while admitting the topic remains highly controversial (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], that the concept is supported only by in vitro studies (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], and that abfraction is unlikely to cause lesions independently (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- Against: Bartlett says the phenomenon has yet to be proven clinically (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC] and the lesions cannot be differentiated from cervical erosion and abrasion (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Pickard's labels abfraction contentious (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC]; Kaidonis et al regard acid-softened tissue plus vigorous brushing as still the primary cause of NCCLs (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC].
- Middle: Overton et al find overwhelming evidence that the cause is multifactorial (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

Combined lesions are therefore the realistic diagnosis: Singh and Gade describe attrition-abfraction and abrasion-abfraction (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC], and Goldstein et al list five combinations including erosion-abfraction and abrasion-erosion (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Q4 and Q7 cover NCCL pathogenesis and abfraction.

#### ✎ Exam core

**6. Abfraction and NCCLs**
- *Definition:* wedge-shaped cervical loss from eccentric occlusal loading causing cervical microfractures (Ritter et al, 2019). Term: Grippo, 1991 (Singh and Gade, 2020); Latin *frangere*, to break.
- *Mechanism (theory):* lateral load → tooth flexure at cervical fulcrum → tensile stress → enamel rod and dentine microfracture → loss, accelerated by acid and brushing.
- *Features:* deep, sharp-angled wedge, often subgingival, single teeth (premolars), with occlusal wear facets or interferences on the same tooth (Torres et al, 2020); coronal margin cut at a right angle into enamel, depth greater than width (Wang and Lussi, 2010).
- *Status:* contentious. Goldstein et al (2018) accept tensile stress as primary; Bartlett (2011) says it is unproven clinically and indistinguishable from erosion-abrasion. Most cervical lesions are multifactorial (Overton et al, 2013): erosion-abrasion, abrasion-abfraction, attrition-abfraction, erosion-abfraction, attrition-erosion (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- *Clinical implication:* whatever the theory, check the occlusion of a tooth with a cervical lesion before restoring it, because unresolved lateral loading is blamed for loss of Class V restorations (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 7. Developmental and other non-carious defects (disfigurement)

### Explanation

For the "destruction and disfigurement" wordings, the developmental defects in Marzouk et al and Singh and Gade must be named. Singh and Gade define non-hereditary enamel hypoplasia as incomplete or defective formation of the organic enamel matrix (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC] and list nutritional deficiency, exanthematous fevers, congenital syphilis (Hutchinson's incisors, mulberry molars), local infection of the predecessor (Turner's tooth) and fluoride. Amelogenesis imperfecta (Witkop: hypoplastic, hypocalcified, hypomaturation) and dentinogenesis imperfecta (types I-III) are managed cosmetically: Singh and Gade give full coverage crowns wherever possible for amelogenesis imperfecta (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC] and porcelain crowns anteriorly with cast metal crowns posteriorly for dentinogenesis imperfecta (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]. Marzouk et al also list discolorations (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], managed by microabrasion, bleaching, resin infiltration or veneers. Resorption is listed by Garg and Garg (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC] and is managed endodontically. White spot lesions, fluorosis and MIH are answered in Q11.

#### ✎ Exam core

**7. Developmental and other defects**
- Non-hereditary enamel hypoplasia/hypocalcification: fluorosis, Turner's tooth, exanthematous fevers, vitamin A, C, D deficiency, congenital syphilis (Hutchinson's incisors, mulberry molars) (Singh and Gade, 2020).
- Hereditary: amelogenesis imperfecta (hypoplastic, hypocalcified, hypomaturation); dentinogenesis imperfecta (types I-III).
- Discolorations, malformations (peg laterals), trauma, resorption (Marzouk et al, undated; Garg and Garg, 2011).
- *Management:* microabrasion, bleaching, resin infiltration, composite or ceramic veneers; full coverage crowns wherever possible for amelogenesis imperfecta; porcelain anterior and cast metal posterior crowns for dentinogenesis imperfecta (Singh and Gade, 2020); endodontic or surgical care for resorption. Details of white spot lesions and fluorosis are in a separate answer.

## 8. Diagnosis

### Explanation

Pickard's insists that unless the aetiological factor is found, tooth wear will never be cured (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Diagnosis therefore has three parts.

**Targeted history.** Diet (acidic food and drink, frequency, sipping, timing), gastric symptoms, eating disorders, medicines, occupation; clenching/grinding and stress, with masseteric hypertrophy on examination (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC]; brushing technique and habits. Bartlett recommends a diet diary as for caries (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

**Examination and differential diagnosis.** Site and shape help, though Overton et al caution that differentiating etiology by lesion shape has yet to be adequately supported with evidence (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein et al distinguish abrasion from abfraction by the smooth, rounded nature of abrasion (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Activity is judged by staining: a stain-free surface suggests active erosion, a stained one inactivity (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Goldstein et al also describe a scratch test with a scalpel blade (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sensitivity may indicate activity, though Pickard's notes it is surprisingly uncommon because of tertiary dentine (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Vitality tests, radiographs (pulp recession, proximity), articulated study casts and photographs complete the record.

**Indices.** The BEWE stages wear in the way the BPE stages periodontal disease (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; Singh and Gade attribute it to "Bartleet [sic] et al", 2008 (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC] and give risk levels ending with 9-13 medium and more than 14 high (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]; Pickard's Table 2.9 scores the cumulative total of all sextants (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], with level 0 at 2 or less managed by routine maintenance and observation, repeated at 3-year intervals (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], and level 3 at 14 or more, where restorations may be considered especially in severe progression (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Levels 2 and 3 are reviewed at 6- to 12-month intervals, ideally avoiding restorations (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Wang and Lussi, reproducing the table of the original 2008 BEWE paper, give a different review schedule: "Repeat at 1- to 2-year intervals" for low risk and "Repeat at 6-month intervals" for high risk (Wang and Lussi, 2010) [SRC]; both versions are recorded (details in P1). Goldstein et al call the Smith and Knight tooth wear index (1984) one of the most frequently cited indices (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Full details are in P1.

#### ✎ Exam core

**8. Diagnosis**

*History:* diet diary (acid type, frequency, sipping, timing; Bartlett, 2011), reflux and vomiting, eating disorders, medicines, occupation, bruxism and stress, brushing technique and habits.

*Principle:* unless the cause is found, wear will never be cured (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

*Examination:* site, shape and surface of lesions; facets matching opposing teeth; occlusal interferences; masseter hypertrophy; staining (stain-free surface = active erosion, Bartlett, 2011); scratch test; sensitivity and vitality; radiographs; dated study casts and photographs. Lesion shape alone is not proof of cause (Overton et al, 2013).

*Special investigations:* salivary flow rate and buffering capacity; referral to a physician for suspected reflux (oesophageal pH studies) or an eating disorder; articulated casts for occlusal analysis.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cause | Tooth-tooth | Foreign object | Acid | Occlusal stress |
| Site | Incisal/occlusal | Buccal cervical | Palatal, labial, occlusal | Cervical, often subgingival |
| Shape | Flat facets, match | V/notch or saucer | Broad, shallow, rounded | Deep, sharp wedge |
| Surface | Shiny, sharp margin | Smooth, polished | Glazed, no margin | Sharp angles |
| Teeth | Opposing pairs | Many adjacent | Generalized pattern | Single teeth |

*Indices:* BEWE (Bartlett et al, 2008): worst surface per sextant scored 0-3; cumulative score 0-2 no risk, 3-8 low, 9-13 medium, 14 or more high (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; "more than 14" in Singh and Gade, 2020). Smith and Knight TWI (1984) for research (Goldstein et al, 2018).

## 9. Prevention and monitoring

### Explanation

Bartlett insists that wear must be judged against age: wear acceptable at 70 would be alarming at 20 (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. For many patients monitoring is an effective and acceptable procedure (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], because wear varies in activity with periods of progression and quiescence (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Monitoring tools: dated study casts, a periodontal probe to measure crown dimensions (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], standardised photographs and indices, and serial dated study models (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; laser profilometry is a research tool (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC].

Prevention targets each cause; Sturdevant's puts the primary goal as halting or modifying the etiology (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].
- **Motivation.** Pickard's suggests gradual change, e.g. initially one in three drinks could be tap water (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC].
- **Erosion.** Reduce frequency, drink through a straw, avoid acid drinks at night; do not brush straight after acid. The waiting time differs between books: Bartlett advises rinsing with water instead of brushing immediately (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Goldstein et al say wait at least 30 min (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Overton et al at least 1 hour (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Kaidonis et al note enamel takes in the order of 12 hours to harden (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]; Torres et al report that some studies did not confirm the advice (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Wang and Lussi, citing an in vitro study that "suggested that a 6-hour remineralization time should be reached to completely reharden the softened enamel", advise instead: "Avoid tooth brushing immediately after an erosive challenge, instead brush before", since "This procedure does not completely remove the pellicle"; they also name tin-containing fluorides: "fluoride preparations with polyvalent metal cations, such as tin ion, are potential erosion inhibitors" (Wang and Lussi, 2010) [SRC]. These data are in vitro and in situ. Bartlett judges fluoride protective (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], although the support is mainly in situ and there is virtually no clinical patient-based evidence (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Torres et al suggest neutral 2% NaF gel or 5% fluoride varnish (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Dentine bonding agents can halve wear over 6 months (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], and dentine sealants resist erosive conditions (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Medical referral for GORD or eating disorders; Goldstein et al note that bulimia or GERD is the likely cause of generalized palatal erosion (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- **Attrition.** Splints work for bruxists (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], typically a full-coverage hard acrylic (Michigan) splint with canine rises (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Torres et al agree (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC].
- **Abrasion.** Correct technique, soft brush, low-abrasive paste, break habits.
- **Abfraction.** Singh and Gade recommend selective reshaping so forces are directed down the long axis, occlusal adjustment and night guards (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC].
- **Sensitivity.** Desensitizing agents (see dentin_hypersensitivity topic).

#### ✎ Exam core

**9. Prevention and monitoring**

*Principle:* stop the cause first; restorations placed while the cause persists fail.

- **Erosion:** reduce frequency of acids, confine to meals, use a straw, no acid drinks at bedtime; rinse with water and delay brushing (30 min, Goldstein et al, 2018; 1 hour, Overton et al, 2013) or brush before the acid (Wang and Lussi, 2010); fluoride (2% NaF gel, 5% varnish, Torres et al, 2020); sugar-free gum; CPP-ACP; dentine bonding agents (halve wear over 6 months, Bartlett, 2011) or sealants on exposed dentine; medical referral for GORD or bulimia.
- **Attrition:** stress management; hard acrylic full-coverage (Michigan) splint with canine guidance (Bartlett, 2011).
- **Abrasion:** modified Stillman technique, soft brush, low-abrasive paste, habit correction.
- **Abfraction:** occlusal adjustment, selective reshaping, night guard (Singh and Gade, 2020).
- **Sensitivity:** desensitizing dentifrices, fluoride varnish, bonding agents.

*Motivation:* show the patient casts and photographs; change behaviour gradually, e.g. replace one in three acidic drinks with tap water, then alternate drinks (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). Without the patient's understanding no treatment succeeds.

*Monitoring:* judge wear against age (Bartlett, 2011); dated study casts, probe measurements, standardised photographs, BEWE, intraoral scans; review every 6-12 months when BEWE risk is medium or high (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; 6-monthly for high risk in Wang and Lussi, 2010).

## 10. Restorative management

### Explanation

**When to restore.** Pickard's says early asymptomatic lesions may be left untreated (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) [SRC]. The books' indications overlap:
- Bartlett: appearance and intractable sensitivity (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
- Sturdevant's Box 20.2 (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]: depth compromising structural integrity (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], intolerable sensitivity, caries, periodontal problems, RPD design, proximity to pulp, active progression (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], aesthetics.
- Academy of Operative Dentistry, via Overton et al (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]: inability to control progression through elimination of etiologic factors (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], aesthetic unacceptability, significant sensitivity, and threat to the strength of the tooth because of the lesion's depth (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- Torres et al: restore when the lesion is active and non-operative treatment cannot arrest it (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC].
- Depth thresholds differ: Sturdevant's says lesions over about 2 mm axial depth typically need restoration (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and that grafting alone suits limited tooth loss (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Marzouk et al restore a wedge lesion that exceeds 0.5 mm into dentin (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

Several books counsel restraint: Marzouk et al say there should be no rush to restore erosion (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Goldstein et al treat only when the cause is under control (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Bartlett suggests monitoring with intensive prevention when the patient cannot afford complex care (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Sturdevant's states that the presence of a defect does not automatically warrant intervention (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Overton et al warn that early restoration starts the rerestoration cycle (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Cervical lesions.** Overton et al treat shallow sensitive lesions first with bonding resins or desensitizing agents (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's calls GIC-based materials still the standard for NCCL retention (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], with a 2% annual failure rate, the lowest of the adhesive classes in the review it cites (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Sclerotic dentine is much more resistant to acid etching than normal dentine (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], so roughening it helps. Goldstein et al list microfilled composites, slightly more flexible than hybrids (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], and warn of high failure if occlusal causes are not corrected (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Gingival grafting is an option where recession accompanies the lesion. Sturdevant's adds that occlusal forces can also cause the loss of bonded Class V restorations in preparations without retention grooves (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Detailed material choice is in Q3, Q5 and Q6.

**Worn occlusal and incisal surfaces.** An occlusal analysis on articulated casts is required (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Older texts favoured metal: Marzouk et al say only metallic restorations should replace attrition loss in stress areas (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and that metal resists erosion better (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Singh and Gade call full crowns, with or without RCT, the final verdict for erosion (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC]. Wang and Lussi disagree on materials: laboratory studies show that in acid "all dental restorative materials show degradation over time", but "ceramic and composite materials seem to exhibit substantial durability", and the guiding rule is "selection of the least invasive intervention" (Wang and Lussi, 2010) [SRC]. Marzouk (metal) and Wang and Lussi (ceramic and composite, laboratory data) are recorded side by side. Modern practice is additive and adhesive: direct composite, palatal veneers, onlays.

**Space.** Worn anterior teeth often remain in contact through dento-alveolar compensation (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. The Dahl concept uses intrusion and eruptive potential to create space (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC], usually within 3-4 months (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; it applies only to localized wear (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC], while generalized wear needs full-mouth rehabilitation (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Composites on worn anterior teeth survive 3-5 years in the studies cited (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Crown lengthening is needed when height is insufficient (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Where vertical dimension has truly been lost, Marzouk et al calculate the increase allowing for a free-way space of 2-3 mm (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. P2 covers Dahl.

#### ✎ Exam core

**10. Restorative management**

*Indications to restore* (Ritter et al, 2019; Academy of Operative Dentistry in Overton et al, 2013): progression not controlled by prevention; structural weakening or depth near the pulp; intractable sensitivity; aesthetics; caries; periodontal or prosthetic (clasp) needs. Lesions over about 2 mm deep usually need restoration, while shallower lesions with recession may be grafted alone (Ritter et al, 2019); Marzouk et al restore a wedge lesion exceeding 0.5 mm into dentine and leave shallow, wide lesions in enamel or cementum, smoothing their edges and applying fluoride. Early, inactive, asymptomatic lesions are monitored; premature restoration starts the rerestoration cycle.

*Cervical lesions (Class V):*
- Shallow, sensitive: bonding agent or desensitizer.
- GIC/RMGI: standard for retention (2% annual failure, Ritter et al, 2019); good for erosion, older patients, poor moisture control.
- Composite (microfill or flowable, flexible) with roughened sclerotic dentine; sandwich technique.
- Correct occlusion first, otherwise failure rates are high (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Gingival graft with or without restoration for recession.

*Occlusal/incisal wear:*
- Articulated casts, diagnostic wax-up, occlusal analysis.
- Localized anterior wear: Dahl concept with composite build-ups at increased height; space in 3-4 months (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011).
- Direct composite, palatal veneers (gold or ceramic), onlays, crowns; crown lengthening or elective RCT for very short teeth.
- Generalized wear with lost vertical dimension: full-mouth rehabilitation, trial in provisional restorations or splint first.

*Erosion cases:* restore only after the acid source is controlled (Goldstein et al, 2018); provisional composite during the corrective phase; older texts preferred metal as more acid-resistant (Marzouk et al, undated), whereas laboratory data show ceramic and composite to be the most durable in acid (Wang and Lussi, 2010).

*Maintenance:* splint after restoration in bruxists; recall, refurbish and repair rather than replace.

## 11. Conclusion

### Explanation

Every book that discusses management ends on the same message: diagnose the cause, control it, monitor, and restore only when there is a clear indication, accepting that most lesions are multifactorial [Certain].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Conclusion.** Non-carious lesions are multifactorial, irreversible and increasingly common, and abfraction remains contentious. Success depends on early recognition, a targeted history, identifying and controlling the cause, monitoring with indices and dated records, and minimally invasive adhesive restoration only when indicated, protected afterwards by splints and recall.

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim 1: "Abfraction is a distinct entity caused by occlusal stress."**
*Textbook says:* Goldstein et al: sufficient experimental and clinical evidence establishes tensile stress of occlusal origin as primary etiology (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Sturdevant's and Singh and Gade define it as an independent lesion (Ritter et al, 2019; Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC].
*Challenge:* the same Goldstein chapter says the concept is supported only by in vitro studies (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] and that abfraction is unlikely to cause lesions independently (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Bartlett: not proven clinically, lesions indistinguishable from erosion-abrasion (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al: teeth with no opposing teeth still develop wedge lesions (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC], and finite element analysis does not confirm causation (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. Overton et al: liquid acid may be necessary for loading to matter (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Bartlett and Shah's critical review concludes there is little evidence, apart from laboratory studies, that abfraction exists other than as a hypothetical component of cervical wear (Bartlett and Shah, 2006) [LIT].
*Strength:* the supporting evidence is finite element analysis and in vitro loading, which are laboratory data, not clinical evidence. [Certain]
*Conclusion:* write abfraction as a proposed, contributory mechanism within multifactorial NCCLs, quote both positions, and still check occlusion before restoring a cervical lesion. [Likely]

**Claim 2: "Toothbrush abrasion causes cervical lesions."**
*Textbook says:* Marzouk et al: toothbrush abrasion is the most predominant abrasion (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Singh and Gade list it as the main cervical cause (Singh and Gade, 2020) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Bartlett: normal brushing forces are unlikely to cause significant wear (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], and cervical wear is often mistakenly called abrasion (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al take a middle view: brushing of acid-softened tissue is the primary cause (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC]. The handedness sign is unverified (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
*Strength:* largely laboratory brushing studies and expert opinion; no clinical trial was read. [Certain about the source type]
*Conclusion:* brushing is probably a co-factor acting on acid-softened tissue rather than a sole cause; advise both diet change and gentle brushing. [Likely]

**Claim 3: "Patients should wait before brushing after an acid challenge."**
*Textbook says:* 30 min (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; at least 1 hour (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; rinse with water instead of brushing (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Torres et al report that some studies did not confirm this recommendation (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]; Kaidonis et al put enamel rehardening in the order of 12 hours (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC], longer than any advised wait; Wang and Lussi cite in vitro data suggesting 6 hours and recommend brushing before rather than after an acid challenge (Wang and Lussi, 2010) [SRC].
*Strength:* the rehardening data are in situ/laboratory; no clinical outcome trial was read. [Likely]
*Conclusion:* give the advice (rinse, delay brushing, use fluoride paste) but do not quote one waiting time as established; name the book. [Likely]

## High-yield points
- Tooth wear: irreversible, non-bacterial, non-traumatic loss; 25% of tooth destruction is non-carious (Singh; Marzouk).
- Attrition = tooth-tooth (matching facets); abrasion = foreign object; erosion = acid (Mount: "corrosion"); abfraction = occlusal stress (contentious).
- Demastication = abrasion by food bolus (Goldstein).
- Erosion: frequency matters most; reflux silent in 25%; stain-free surface = active.
- Abfraction: Grippo 1991; supported mainly by FEA and in vitro data; Bartlett: unproven clinically.
- BEWE (Bartlett, Ganss, Lussi 2008): 0-3 per sextant; cumulative 0-2, 3-8, 9-13, 14 or more (Pickard's; Singh says "more than 14").
- Restore for progression, weakening, sensitivity, aesthetics; over about 2 mm depth (Sturdevant's); GIC best retention.
- Dahl concept for localized anterior wear: space in 3-4 months.

## Common exam mistakes
- Calling every cervical wedge lesion "abrasion" or "abfraction" without stating that most are multifactorial.
- Presenting abfraction finite element and in vitro data as clinical proof.
- Restoring before identifying and controlling the cause, especially in bulimia or GORD.
- Omitting developmental defects and discolouration when the question says "disfigurement".
- Writing a single "wait time" before brushing, or the BEWE cut-offs, as if all books agree.
