## 1. Non-carious disfigurement of teeth

### Explanation

The question's phrase comes from Marzouk's chapter title, "Non-carious destruction and disfigurement of teeth". Marzouk opens by noting that an estimated 25% of tooth destruction does not originate from caries (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; Singh repeats the same estimate (Singh, 2020) [SRC]. Neither book defines "disfigurement" separately; Marzouk lists twelve conditions together, and the disfiguring ones are the developmental and colour defects:
- Localized non-hereditary enamel hypoplasia (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and hypocalcification; dentine hypoplasia and hypocalcification.
- Discolorations (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], including tetracycline discoloration, which Marzouk calls a sort of permanent stain (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].
- Malformation (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].
- Amelogenesis and dentinogenesis imperfecta (Singh also lists these as non-carious lesions; Singh, 2020) [SRC].
- The destructive group: attrition, abrasion, erosion (and, in later books, abfraction), and trauma.

So "disfigurement" is best read as non-carious change in colour, form or surface that spoils appearance, as distinct from "destruction", which is loss of tooth substance [Likely]. For discoloured or hypoplastic teeth Marzouk lists vital bleaching, laminated veneering, composite veneering and full crowns as the treatments to be used (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** About 25% of tooth destruction is not caused by caries (Marzouk et al, undated; Singh, 2020). Marzouk groups these conditions as "non-carious destruction and disfigurement".

**Non-carious disfigurement:** non-carious change in the colour, form or surface of teeth that spoils appearance, with or without loss of tooth substance.

**Classification** (Marzouk et al, undated)
1. Destruction (wear): attrition, abrasion, erosion, abfraction.
2. Developmental: localized non-hereditary enamel hypoplasia and hypocalcification; dentine hypoplasia and hypocalcification; amelogenesis and dentinogenesis imperfecta; malformations (e.g. Hutchinson's incisors).
3. Discolorations: extrinsic stains; intrinsic (fluorosis, tetracycline, pulpal necrosis).
4. Trauma.

**Management of disfigurement:** vital bleaching, composite or ceramic veneers, crowns (Marzouk et al, undated).

## 2. Cervical abrasion: features and the decision to restore

### Explanation

**Definition and form.** Abrasion is tooth loss from friction between teeth and external objects (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Premolars are most often involved (Singh, 2020) [SRC]; Goldstein et al see toothbrush abrasion most often on canines and premolars (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Marzouk describes walls meeting in a V (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC] and an extremely smooth, polished surface (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Singh classifies abrasion morphology as notch/V-shaped (Singh, 2020) [SRC], C-shaped (Singh, 2020) [SRC], undercut concave (Singh, 2020) [SRC] and divergent box (Singh, 2020) [SRC], and depth as shallow (0.1-0.5 mm) (Singh, 2020) [SRC], deep, or with pulp exposure. These morphologies decide preparation: a divergent or C-shaped lesion has no mechanical retention, while an undercut concave lesion already has some [Likely].

**When to restore.** The presence of a defect does not automatically warrant intervention (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The Academy of Operative Dentistry criteria quoted in Summitt's chapter are: inability to control progression by removing the cause (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], aesthetic concern, significant sensitivity, and threat to the strength of the tooth and the integrity of the coronal-radicular unit (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's adds a deep defect compromising structural integrity (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and intolerable sensitivity unresponsive to desensitizing (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Marzouk gives a depth rule: multiple shallow lesions not exceeding 0.5 mm in dentine need no restoration (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], while a wedge lesion deeper than 0.5 mm should be restored (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Shallow sensitive lesions should first receive bonding resins or desensitizing agents (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. The habit must be corrected first, e.g. modified Stillman's technique (Singh, 2020) [SRC], because the restoration will otherwise wear too. Summitt's chapter ends on a sober note: restorations are not permanent (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Cervical abrasion** is facial cervical tooth loss from toothbrush/dentifrice friction, commonest on premolars and canines (Ritter et al, 2019; Singh, 2020; Goldstein et al, 2018). Morphology: notch/V-shaped, C-shaped, undercut concave, divergent box; depth shallow (0.1-0.5 mm), deep, or exposing pulp (Singh, 2020).

**First** stop the cause: brushing technique, soft brush, low-abrasive paste; desensitise shallow lesions.

**Indications to restore** (Academy of Operative Dentistry, in Overton et al, 2013): progression not controlled; aesthetic concern; sensitivity not relieved; depth threatening tooth strength or pulp; caries; plaque retention or planned clasp. Marzouk restores wedge lesions deeper than 0.5 mm in dentine.

## 3. Why cervical abrasion is difficult to restore

### Explanation

- **Sclerotic dentine.** NCCL dentine is much more resistant to acid etching than normal dentine (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; bonding to it gives low bond strengths whether etch-and-rinse or self-etch is used (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's chapter adds that current self-etching systems appear to bond less well to sclerotic dentine than etch-and-rinse systems (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- **Margins.** The gingival margin is in dentine or cementum, so cervical bevels are not recommended for lack of enamel (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Composite needs sound enamel to etch (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC].
- **Polymerisation shrinkage** can pull composite away from the cavity walls (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- **Flexure** (abfraction component, Q7) may dislodge restorations. Mount et al note composite or GIC may be lost from erosion lesions without trace, generally from surface contamination at placement, alternatively from abfraction stresses (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC].
- **Access, moisture and gingival fluid**, and an aesthetic site.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Problems specific to cervical abrasion**
1. Sclerotic, hypermineralised dentine resists etching, giving low bond strengths (Ritter et al, 2019).
2. Little or no enamel at the gingival margin; margin on cementum/dentine.
3. Polymerisation shrinkage stress pulls composite off the gingival wall.
4. Tooth flexure may dislodge restorations.
5. Moisture, crevicular fluid, access and aesthetics.
6. The cause (brushing, acid) continues to act on the margins unless corrected.

These problems explain why retention, not strength, is the main outcome measured in cervical restoration trials.

## 4. Materials: critical evaluation

### Explanation

Summitt's Box 15-1 lists non-aesthetic options (amalgam; gold foil, not widely used (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; gold inlay) and aesthetic ones (composite with bonding agent, composite with GIC liner, flowable composite, GIC, RMGI, compomer and porcelain inlay, not widely used (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]).

**Amalgam and gold.** For NCCLs amalgam needs removal of sound tooth to create a retentive box (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], so adhesive materials are preferred. Marzouk accepts adhesive tooth-coloured materials with no preparation in most cases (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. Metallic options now have only a historical or posterior non-aesthetic role [Likely].

**Conventional GIC.** Strengths: chemical adhesion, fluoride release, no preparation. One study reported 80% retention at 10 years without mechanical retention (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Weaknesses: rapid surface degradation, especially with acidic foods (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], brittle fracture (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC], early water sensitivity needing an immediate resin coat (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC], and poorer aesthetics. Mount et al regard restorative aesthetic GIC as the material of choice for these lesions (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]; Torres and Zanatta reserve GIC/RMGI especially for elderly, high caries-risk patients (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC].

**RMGI.** Clinical retention figures in Summitt's chapter: 96.4% of RMGI versus 51.5% of bonded composite restorations retained at 5 years (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; in another study 94% of RMGIs retained, composite 81-85%, conventional GIC 67% (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; a review found glass-ionomer materials had the highest retention (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Mount et al note its dentine-like elastic modulus suits cervical lesions (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]. It is better looking than conventional GIC but less polishable than composite [Likely].

**Composite.** Sturdevant's recommends composite and RMGI for Class V (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein et al call composite bonding the most conservative Class V restoration (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Best aesthetics and polish, but retention depends on the adhesive and on sclerotic dentine.

**Microfill and flowable: a direct disagreement.**
- For low modulus: Torres and Zanatta call microfills an excellent cervical option (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC] and credit flowables with the same low-modulus advantage (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC]; Goldstein et al list microfills as slightly more flexible options (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Sturdevant's explains the stress-relief theory (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].
- Against: Sturdevant's then cites a 2-year trial showing no retention difference by composite stiffness (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's chapter reports no retention difference between microfilled and hybrid composites (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], says clinical trials proved the flexible-flowable theory incorrect (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], and that a flowable liner has not improved performance (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Compomer.** Good handling, but marginal integrity worse than composite in long-term trials (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Sandwich (lamination).** GIC replaces dentine and reduces leakage (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], composite provides the surface. Reported: 100% retention at 3 years (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC] and 96% survival at 5 years (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Mount et al laminate an unsatisfactory GIC by cutting it back and adding composite (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Overall.** Sturdevant's states that GIC-based materials, though weaker bonders in vitro, have the most effective and durable clinical retention (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and are still the standard for NCCL retention (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Where caries risk, moisture or home care are poor, non-adhesive-dependent materials should be considered (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Materials** (Overton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016)

The ideal cervical material would bond chemically to sclerotic dentine, have a dentine-like modulus, release fluoride, match tooth colour, polish well and resist abrasion and acid. No single material does all of this, so each is judged on retention, marginal integrity and aesthetics.

| Material | For | Against | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amalgam, gold foil/inlay | durable, wear-resistant | needs retentive box (sound tooth removed), unaesthetic, no seal | obsolete for NCCL |
| Conventional GIC | chemical bond, F release, no preparation; 80% retained at 10 years | brittle, water-sensitive, erodes in acid, opaque | high-caries, elderly, root surfaces |
| RMGI | best clinical retention (96.4% at 5 years; 94% in another trial); dentine-like modulus; better aesthetics | less polishable than composite | standard for retention |
| Composite (hybrid/nanofill) | best aesthetics, polish, wear | depends on bonding to sclerotic dentine; shrinkage; 51-85% retention in comparative trials | aesthetic zone with good isolation |
| Microfill/flowable | low modulus "flexes with tooth" | trials show no retention gain over hybrids | theory not supported |
| Compomer | handling | poorer margins than composite | limited |
| Sandwich (GIC/RMGI + composite) | GIC retention plus composite surface; 96% at 5 years | two-stage, technique sensitive | good compromise |
| Porcelain inlay/veneer | aesthetics, wear | cost, tooth removal | rarely used |

## 5. Techniques: preparation, bonding, isolation, placement

### Explanation

**Preparation.** Sturdevant's: an NCCL usually needs only surface debridement of exposed dentine and roughening or bevelling of enamel margins with a diamond (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount et al: no instrumentation for a corrosion lesion (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]; clean with pumice and water, not prophylaxis paste (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Roughening sclerotic dentine: books disagree.**
- A meta-analysis summarised in Sturdevant's found roughened dentine gave significantly higher retention than unprepared dentine (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].
- Summitt's chapter cites one trial with no gain in retention after roughening sclerotic lesions with a bur (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Bevel or butt joint: depends on material.**
- GIC and amalgam: bevels contraindicated because of low edge strength (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; a 90-degree butt joint about 1 mm deep is the minimum (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Torres and Zanatta also omit the bevel for GIC (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC].
- Composite: an enamel bevel is optional (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC], about 45 degrees (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; never bevel a cementum margin (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Clinical data say enamel bevelling does not seem a significant factor for retention (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; its value is aesthetic blending [Likely].
- Retention grooves are usually unnecessary (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Adhesive strategy.** Peumans et al (2014), summarised in Sturdevant's: GIC-based materials had the lowest annual failure rate, 2% (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; mild two-step self-etch 2.5% and three-step etch-and-rinse 3.1% (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; one-step strong self-etch 5.4%, two-step etch-and-rinse 5.8% and two-step strong self-etch 7.9% (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Earlier, one-step self-etch performed worst (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Selective enamel etching did not significantly change self-etch retention (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], but a universal adhesive in full self-etch mode showed marginal degradation (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Chemical bonding to dentine calcium may explain the success of GIC and mild self-etch systems (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. For GIC: 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]; Torres and Zanatta give 10-11.5% for 20-30 s (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC].

**Isolation.** Rubber dam with No. 212 retainer or cotton roll and retraction cord (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; the 212SA clamp is effective (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Rubber dam may decrease failure rates, although data conflict (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. For a subgingival non-carious margin, finishing short is better than an overhang (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Placement and finishing.** Avoid increments bridging occlusal and gingival walls (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; increments no thicker than 2 mm (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; cervical matrices help GIC/RMGI (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC]; seal conventional GIC immediately with low-viscosity resin (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]; finish the gingival margin with a No. 12 or 12B blade (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Occlusion.** Occlusal analysis is part of the plan (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], but there are no controlled trials proving occlusal adjustment prolongs Class 5 restorations (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Techniques**
1. **Preparation:** minimal; pumice clean, debride surface. Roughen sclerotic dentine with a bur (meta-analysis: higher retention; one trial: no gain). Grooves rarely needed, even in large lesions.
2. **Margins:** GIC: 90° butt joint, no bevel (low edge strength). Composite: optional 45° enamel bevel for aesthetics; no bevel on cementum (Overton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
3. **Conditioning/bonding:** GIC: 10% polyacrylic acid, 10-30 s. Composite: three-step etch-and-rinse or mild two-step self-etch (annual failure 3.1% and 2.5% versus GIC 2%); avoid one-step strong self-etch (5.4%) (Ritter et al, 2019). Universal adhesive: signs of marginal degradation in full self-etch mode.
4. **Isolation:** rubber dam with 212 clamp, or retraction cord and cotton rolls.
5. **Placement:** incremental composite, not bridging occlusal and gingival walls; cervical matrix for GIC/RMGI; resin-coat conventional GIC.
6. **Finishing:** No. 12 blade at the gingival margin, fine diamonds, discs; avoid overhangs.
7. **Occlusal analysis** if facets suggest flexure.

**Step-by-step, RMGI restoration:** isolate (cord or 212 clamp) → pumice and water → condition with polyacrylic acid, rinse, dry lightly → apply primer if supplied → syringe RMGI in one increment → adapt a clear cervical matrix → light-cure → remove matrix, trim excess with a No. 12 blade → finish with fine diamonds under water spray → apply surface glaze.

**Step-by-step, composite restoration:** isolate → roughen dentine and bevel occlusal enamel → etch enamel (selective etch) or etch-and-rinse → apply adhesive by scrubbing, air-thin, cure → increments of 2 mm or less, the first from the mid-gingival floor to the occlusal margin → shape with a brush before curing → finish with flame-shaped finishing bur, discs and rubber points.

## 6. Periodontal and combined approaches

### Explanation

Sturdevant's restores lesions deeper than about 2 mm axially (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and suggests grafting alone for limited tooth loss with recession (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's chapter uses the maximum root coverage line: if it is apical to the CEJ, restore first, then graft (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Periodontal option:** shallow lesions (<2 mm) with recession can be covered by connective tissue graft or coronally advanced flap; deeper lesions are restored first (margin at the predicted root-coverage line), then grafted (Ritter et al, 2019; Overton et al, 2013).

## 7. Selection and conclusion

### Explanation

Shallow sensitive lesions are desensitised rather than restored (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Selection follows from sections 4 and 5: retention favours glass-ionomer materials, aesthetics favours composite, and the sandwich combines them [Likely]. Mount et al and Goldstein et al start from opposite defaults (GIC versus composite), which is a genuine disagreement about priorities, not about data [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Selection**
- Retention priority, high caries risk, poor isolation, root margins: RMGI or GIC.
- Aesthetic zone, good isolation, enamel margin: composite with etch-and-rinse or mild self-etch adhesive.
- Large lesions: sandwich (RMGI dentine, composite enamel).

- Shallow sensitive lesion (<0.5 mm): no restoration; desensitiser or bonding resin, review.
- Lesion with recession and thin gingiva: graft alone or restore then graft.

**Conclusion.** Correct the cause, use the least invasive preparation, choose the material by retention and aesthetic need, and review; restorations of cervical abrasion are not permanent (Overton et al, 2013).

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim: in NCCLs, glass-ionomer-based materials are retained better than composite, and bevelling or etch mode matters less than the material.** Summitt's chapter reports RMGI retention of 96.4% versus 51.5% for composite at 5 years, and 94% for RMGI versus 81-85% for composite and 67% for conventional GIC in another study (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Peumans et al, as summarised in Sturdevant's, found the lowest annual failure for GIC-based materials (2%), close to mild two-step self-etch (2.5%) and three-step etch-and-rinse (3.1%) (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], so the gap between GIC and a good adhesive is small while the gap to one-step strong self-etch is larger [Likely]. On bevelling, a meta-analysis found no retention difference (risk difference 0.0, 95% CI -0.04 to 0.04), but it rested on only two low-risk trials at 1 year (Schroeder et al, 2015) [LIT; abstract only], consistent with Sturdevant's (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Dentine preparation may matter more: in a 7.7-year RCT of composites placed after enamel bevelling and selective enamel etching, restorations with cleaning only showed the highest loss rate (27.8%) (Luhrs et al, 2020) [LIT; abstract only], whereas Summitt's chapter cites a trial with no benefit from roughening (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. On etch mode, a universal adhesive retained 100% in total-etch and 94.9% in self-etch mode at 24 months (Lawson et al, 2015) [LIT; abstract only], and a 2026 network meta-analysis of 74 RCTs found selective-etch or etch-and-rinse strategies with universal adhesives and nanocomposites gave the lowest retention loss, with RMGI worse for marginal adaptation (Chaple Gil et al, 2026) [LIT; abstract only], which suggests that with modern universal adhesives the composite-GIC gap may be narrowing [Guessing]. What follows: RMGI remains the retention-safe default, a well-bonded composite with enamel etching is an acceptable aesthetic alternative, and the bevel is chosen for appearance, not retention [Likely].

## High-yield points
- "Disfigurement" (Marzouk): non-carious colour/form defects; "destruction": wear lesions.
- Restore only if progressive, sensitive, aesthetic, deep or weakening; stop the habit first.
- Sclerotic dentine resists etching; the main reason composites fail in NCCLs.
- GIC-based materials: lowest annual failure (2%); RMGI 96.4% at 5 years and 94% in another comparative trial.
- GIC: butt joint, no bevel; composite: optional enamel bevel, never on cementum.
- Microfill/flowable "flexibility" is not supported clinically.
- Avoid one-step strong self-etch; mild two-step self-etch and three-step etch-and-rinse perform well.
- Sandwich technique combines GIC retention with composite aesthetics.

## Common exam mistakes
- Answering only the restorative half and ignoring "non-carious disfigurement".
- Bevelling a GIC restoration or a cementum margin.
- Recommending microfill or flowable composite for retention without noting trials found no benefit.
- Listing materials without retention data or without saying which is preferred and why.
- Restoring before correcting the brushing habit.
