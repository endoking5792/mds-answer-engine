## 1. Wasting disease: the term and its components

### Explanation

"Wasting diseases of the teeth" is the older collective term for non-carious, non-traumatic loss of tooth substance, i.e. what current books call tooth wear or tooth surface loss [RECALL]. None of the repository textbooks defines the phrase; Goldstein et al mention "chemical wasting of the teeth" only as one of the historical theories of tooth wear, alongside toothbrushing and lateral forces (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Pickard's gives the modern definition of tooth wear: "the irreversible surface loss of dental hard tissues caused by factors other than caries or trauma" (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]; it may be physiological, slow and lifelong, or pathological (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC]. Alani and Patel call it a multifactorial process without a bacterial component (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

Components, as defined by Bartlett:
- **Erosion:** loss caused by acids not involving bacterial fermentation (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
- **Abrasion:** mechanical wear from forces other than teeth (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
- **Attrition:** wear of tooth against tooth (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
- **Abfraction:** a hypothesis from finite-element laboratory studies that is yet to be proven clinically (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Laboratory modelling is not clinical evidence; see Q7.

The components rarely act alone (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Early signs are almost universal in adults, while severe forms probably affect around 10% of the population (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Marzouk estimates that 25% of tooth destruction does not originate from caries (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** "Wasting diseases" is the older term for tooth wear: irreversible loss of dental hard tissue by causes other than caries or trauma (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Components (Bartlett, 2011):
- Erosion: non-bacterial acid dissolution.
- Attrition: tooth against tooth.
- Abrasion: wear by objects other than teeth.
- Abfraction: proposed flexure-induced cervical loss; clinically unproven.

They usually coexist, so management targets the whole process, not one lesion. Early wear is almost universal; severe wear probably affects around 10% of the population (Bartlett, 2011). Wear is physiological when slow and matched to age, pathological when the dentition will not last a lifetime.

**Aims of management:** find and control the cause, prevent progression, relieve symptoms, monitor, and restore only when indicated, preserving tooth tissue.

## 2. Assessment and diagnosis

### Explanation

Assessment answers four questions: how much wear, what cause, is it active, and does it matter to this patient?

**Severity against age.** The amount of wear has to be weighed against the patient's age (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Kaidonis et al call wear pathologic when the dentition will clearly not last into old age (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC].

**Activity.** Bartlett notes that there are no reliable signs of activity apart from staining: a stain-free surface suggests active erosion, a stained one inactivity (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Wear may reflect past rather than current habits (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], and wear seems to alternate between progression and quiescence (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

**Recording.** Indices such as Smith and Knight's tooth wear index (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] or BEWE (P1) record severity. Sturdevant's requires an occlusal analysis on articulated diagnostic models before definitive treatment (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Assessment**
- Complaint: appearance, sensitivity, function, fear of further wear.
- History: diet (diary), reflux, vomiting, eating disorder, medication, dry mouth, grinding, brushing habits, occupation.
- Examination: site and pattern, facets, cupping, proud restorations, dentine exposure, OVD, freeway space, TMJ, saliva.
- Grade with an index (Smith and Knight, BEWE); photographs, dated articulated casts (Goldstein et al, 2018; Ritter et al, 2019).
- Activity: unstained surfaces, sensitivity suggest active erosion (Bartlett, 2011).
- Judge severity against age: the same wear is acceptable at 70 but alarming at 20.
- Special tests: salivary flow and buffering; medical opinion for suspected reflux.

## 3. Identifying the cause

### Explanation

Pattern points to cause (Q1 and Q2 have the details):
- **Extrinsic erosion:** labial/buccal surfaces of upper anteriors, occlusal cupping; dietary acids and frequency of intake.
- **Intrinsic erosion:** palatal surfaces of upper anteriors with an enamel rim, from vomiting or reflux; Alani and Patel point to medical referral, e.g. proton pump inhibitors (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].
- **Attrition:** matching flat facets in both arches (Q9).
- **Abrasion:** cervical notches, habit grooves.

Acid-softened surfaces wear faster under attrition and abrasion (Banerjee and Watson, 2015) [SRC], so erosion is often the common factor [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Identifying the cause (from pattern)**
- Labial upper anteriors, occlusal cupping, proud restorations: dietary erosion.
- Palatal upper anteriors with enamel rim: intrinsic acid (GORD, bulimia); refer to physician.
- Matching flat facets: attrition/bruxism.
- Cervical notches, habit grooves: abrasion.
- Mixed patterns are usual; acid softening accelerates mechanical wear (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- Proud restorations and "islands" of amalgam indicate tooth tissue lost around them by acid.
- The cause must be controlled before restoration, or restorations will fail by the same process.

## 4. Prevention

### Explanation

Bartlett regards early detection and identification of risk factors as the most important aspect of management (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], and some evidence suggests that preventive advice reduces the rate of wear (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].

- **Diet:** a diet diary as for caries (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; reduce frequency, use a straw, avoid acid drinks at night, and do not brush straight after an acid drink (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
- **Fluoride:** laboratory and in situ evidence support it, but there is virtually no clinical patient-based evidence (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Alani and Patel advise daily high-fluoride toothpaste and mouthrinse (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].
- **Dentine bonding agents:** reduced wear by half over 6 months, though they wear away and need reapplication (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC].
- **Splints:** effective for bruxists (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC] but probably ineffective where erosion is a major factor (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]; Alani and Patel use them for interim protection once acid is controlled (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].
- **Medical:** GORD and eating disorders referred (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prevention**
- Diet: diary; reduce frequency of acids, no acid drinks at bedtime, straw, rinse with water, delay brushing after acid (Bartlett, 2011).
- Medical referral: GORD (PPIs), eating disorders (Alani and Patel, 2025).
- Fluoride: high-fluoride toothpaste, varnish; clinical evidence limited.
- Seal exposed dentine with bonding agents; reapply.
- Brushing: soft brush, low-abrasive paste.
- Occlusal splint (full-coverage hard acrylic, Michigan type) for bruxism; of little value where erosion dominates (Bartlett, 2011).
- Sensitivity: desensitising pastes, fluoride varnish, bonding agents.
- Reinforce at every recall; patient understanding is the key to compliance.

## 5. Monitoring and the decision to restore

### Explanation

There is no justification for believing that wear inevitably destroys teeth (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Monitoring uses sequential study casts (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC], or simply a periodontal probe to measure tooth heights (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. The main indications that eventually demand restoration are appearance and intractable sensitivity (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Cost matters: if a patient cannot afford complex care and aftercare, monitoring with intensive prevention may be the right outcome (Bartlett, 2011) [SRC]. Kaidonis et al add that a worn dentition with scooped dentine is functional (Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Monitoring:** dated casts, photographs, index scores and probe measurements at recall (Bartlett, 2011). If wear is slow, continue monitoring.

**Indications to restore:** poor appearance, intractable sensitivity, functional loss, risk to pulp or tooth survival, progression despite prevention. Restoration is best delayed as long as possible (Loomans et al, 2017); it is costly and needs aftercare (Bartlett, 2011). Informed consent covers cost, number of visits, maintenance and the option of monitoring alone; this is documented.

## 6. The space problem and vertical dimension

### Explanation

**Dentoalveolar compensation.** Worn anterior teeth usually stay in contact because of dento-alveolar compensation (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; OVD and freeway space are maintained by alveolar remodelling (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. If wear outpaces eruption, an open bite develops (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Ibbetson stresses that slowly worn patients generally do not lose OVD (Ibbetson, 2001) [SRC]; Goldstein et al agree for bruxism (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] and call routine bite opening possibly ill advised (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

**Deciding on OVD: books differ in method.**
- Turner's categories (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]: (1) loss of OVD (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; (2) no loss, space available (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; (3) no loss, limited space (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Space is checked by closest speaking space (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] or a splint at the new OVD worn for about three months (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- Marzouk estimates the lost height as the indicated face height (found as for complete dentures) minus the occlusal face height, less a 2-3 mm freeway space (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]; above 2 mm increase a temporary restoration or splint is tried first (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC], and after more than 15 years of wear not all lost dimension can be tolerated (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC].

**Ways to gain space.**
1. **Conformative approach** at existing OVD (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; but reducing worn teeth further for crowns is inappropriate (Ibbetson, 2001) [SRC].
2. **Surgical crown lengthening** where height is insufficient (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; it can produce an adverse crown:root ratio (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC] and reduces periodontal support (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].
3. **Orthodontics:** prolongs treatment and adds cost (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].
4. **Occlusal adjustment** (RCP-ICP slide): only with an intact posterior dentition (Ibbetson, 2001) [SRC], and few patients qualify (Ibbetson, 2001) [SRC].
5. **Increasing OVD:** "somewhat contentious" (Ibbetson, 2001) [SRC]; it increases overjet (Ibbetson, 2001) [SRC].
6. **Relative axial movement (Dahl)** for localised wear (P2).
7. **Overdentures** when roots are too short (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Space and OVD.** Slow wear is compensated by dentoalveolar eruption, so OVD is usually maintained and there is no room to restore (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011; Ibbetson, 2001). Classify by Turner (Goldstein et al, 2018):
1. Loss of OVD: restore at increased OVD.
2. No loss, space available.
3. No loss, no space: create space.

Assess freeway space and speaking space; trial new OVD with splint or provisionals.

**Space creation:** crown lengthening (crown:root ratio worsens), orthodontic intrusion, occlusal adjustment of RCP-ICP slide, increased OVD, Dahl movement, elective endodontics, overdentures.

Increasing OVD needs restorations on all teeth of at least one arch, increases overjet and should be modest; a large increase (over about 2 mm) is first tested with a splint or provisionals (Marzouk et al, undated; Ibbetson, 2001).

## 7. Localised wear

### Explanation

Management depends on whether wear is localised or generalised (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. For localised anterior wear the Dahl concept uses intrusion and eruption to create space (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC], applicable only when wear is localised (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Composite build-ups survive 3-5 years (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; residual composite cannot be relied on to retain later crowns (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Where attrition continues, Alani and Patel prefer harder indirect palatal surfaces (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Full mouth rehabilitation for localised wear risks the "restorative failure spiral" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Localised (usually upper anterior) wear**
- Diagnostic wax-up; mock-up.
- Additive direct composite (or palatal veneers in composite, ceramic or gold) placed at increased OVD without preparation: Dahl concept; posterior contacts re-establish by intrusion and eruption (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011).
- Crowns later if composites repeatedly fail.
- Contraindicated if wear is generalised, in active TMD or periodontitis.
- Lower incisors can be built up at the same time; even contacts on all restored teeth are essential to avoid fracture.
- Space usually re-established within months; review occlusion and adjust.

## 8. Generalised wear: full-mouth rehabilitation

### Explanation

In generalised wear the Dahl approach is ineffective and full-mouth rehabilitation is indicated (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC], aiming to leave the less worn teeth unrestored (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Steps (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]: casts on a semi-adjustable articulator with a retruded contact position record; wax-up to determine the increased OVD; provisionals to test appearance, occlusion and function; definitive crowns copied from provisionals. A reorganised occlusion is built on the terminal hinge axis, the most reproducible jaw relationship (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. A quadrant or sextant approach avoids one exhausting session (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC], with an occlusal splint used to hold space for the remaining teeth (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Goldstein et al use Dawson's sequence starting from the maxillary incisal edge (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; when the incisal edge is already correct, extra length must come gingivally (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Generalised wear: full-mouth rehabilitation** (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011)
1. Facebow, RCP record, casts on semi-adjustable articulator.
2. Diagnostic wax-up from maxillary incisal edge position (Dawson); decide OVD.
3. Trial OVD: splint or composite/provisional restorations for some months.
4. Crown lengthening where height is short.
5. Restore in terminal hinge axis, by quadrant/sextant; splint holds space.
6. Definitive restorations copy provisionals; even contacts, smooth guidance.
7. Restore only teeth that need it; leave less worn teeth unrestored (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011).
8. Elective endodontics or overdentures where crowns are too short.

## 9. Direct versus indirect restorations

### Explanation

Alani and Patel list the advantages of direct work as time efficiency, lower cost and serviceability (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC], guided by a wax-up that is "the blueprint" (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]; indirect restorations are more resilient with superior physical properties (Alani and Patel, 2025) [SRC]. Material choice for wear surfaces differs between books: gold is kinder to opposing teeth (Alani and Patel, 2025; Torres et al, 2020) [SRC], feldspathic ceramic wears antagonists (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC], and Marzouk restricts attrition areas to metallic restorations (Marzouk et al, undated) [SRC]. After rehabilitation, bruxists need an occlusal guard (Goldstein et al, 2018; Kaidonis et al, 2016) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Direct versus indirect**

| | Direct composite | Indirect (composite, ceramic, gold) |
|---|---|---|
| Tooth preparation | none (additive) | minimal to moderate |
| Cost, time | lower | higher |
| Repair | easy | difficult |
| Durability | lower; maintenance | higher |
| Use | first line, trial OVD | heavy bruxism, failed composites |

Gold is kinder to antagonists; feldspathic porcelain wears opposing teeth (Alani and Patel, 2025; Torres et al, 2020). Direct composite is now the usual first choice because it is additive and reversible; indirect work follows failure or heavy loading.

**Maintenance:** night guard for bruxists, continued diet and fluoride measures, recall with casts, repair or refurbish composites.

**Conclusion:** management of wasting disease is preventive first and restorative second, with minimum intervention throughout.

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim: direct composite at increased OVD is an adequate first-line restoration for worn teeth.** Bartlett and Ricketts cite reasonable evidence that such composites survive 3-5 years with maintenance (Bartlett and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. A single-centre prospective study of 283 composite Dahl restorations in 26 patients found median survival of 5.8 years (4.75 years for replacements), failures mainly by wear, fracture and marginal discolouration, rare biological complications and high patient satisfaction (Gulamali et al, 2011) [LIT; abstract only]. A 2025 systematic review (43 studies, 6 RCTs) found higher failure rates for direct than indirect ceramic restorations, the direct annual failure rate in RCTs being 8.38% for major failures against 0.13% for indirect ceramic, but noted indirect work is more invasive (Alani et al, 2025) [LIT; abstract only]. The European consensus recommends delaying restoration as long as possible and, when needed, a minimally invasive adhesive approach (Loomans et al, 2017) [LIT; abstract only]. What follows: direct composite is a defensible, reversible starting point that buys time, but patients should be told it needs maintenance and may later be replaced by indirect work [Likely].

## High-yield points
- "Wasting disease" is the old term for tooth wear; no current textbook in the repository defines it.
- Identify cause first; prevention and monitoring before any restoration.
- Slowly worn teeth rarely lose OVD; dentoalveolar compensation removes restorative space.
- Turner categories decide whether OVD is increased.
- Localised: Dahl/additive composite; generalised: full-mouth rehabilitation at a tested OVD.
- Indications to restore: appearance, intractable sensitivity, function, pulp risk.
- Direct = minimal, cheap, repairable; indirect = durable, invasive.
- Night guard and recall after rehabilitation.

## Common exam mistakes
- Jumping to crowns without cause identification, prevention and monitoring.
- Assuming every worn dentition has lost OVD.
- Reducing already short teeth further for crowns.
- Applying the Dahl concept to generalised wear.
- Presenting abfraction finite-element data as clinical proof.
