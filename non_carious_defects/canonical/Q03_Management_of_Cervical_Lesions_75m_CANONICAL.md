## 1. Introduction: cervical lesions and cervical restorations

### Explanation

Summitt's Class 5 chapter opens with the definition that frames the essay: "Class 5 lesions are carious and noncarious defects found in the gingival third of facial and lingual tooth surfaces" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. A cervical restoration is therefore any restoration of this gingival-third or exposed-root defect, whatever caused it. The same authors point out that the restorative steps "are similar for caries lesions and noncarious lesions, except that carious dentin is removed" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; the difference between the two groups lies in diagnosis, cause control and material choice, not in the restoration itself.

Mount and Ngo use the Site/Size system. "The Site 3 lesion occurs in the gingival one-third of the crown or on the exposed root surface of any tooth" (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC], and it is graded from Size 0 to Size 4:
- 3.0: early active caries or non-carious loss at the gingival margin; "There is no equivalent in the Black classification" (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC]. It is managed by controlling the disease, not by restoring.
- 3.1: more extensive early lesion needing restoration.
- 3.2: advancing caries, a failed restoration or extensive non-carious loss on the facial or lingual surface.
- 3.3: approximal root-surface lesion below the contact area.
- 3.4: more than one surface involved, needing a complex matrix.

The value of this system over Black's Class V is that it includes the root surface all round the tooth and a "Size 0" stage that is treated without a restoration. That suits the ageing patient with recession [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Cervical lesions are carious and non-carious defects in the gingival third of the facial and lingual surfaces (Overton et al, 2013). A cervical restoration is the restoration of such a defect, on the crown, the root or both. Restorative steps are the same for carious and non-carious lesions, except that carious dentine is removed (Overton et al, 2013). Management is more than a filling: diagnose the cause, control it, decide whether to restore, choose a material, isolate, bond and maintain.

**Classification**
- G.V. Black: Class V, gingival third of facial or lingual surfaces.
- Mount and Ngo (2016), Site 3 (cervical third or exposed root), Sizes 0-4: 3.0 earliest lesion, managed without restoration; 3.1 small, needs restoration; 3.2 extensive facial/lingual; 3.3 approximal root lesion below the contact; 3.4 multi-surface, needs a complex matrix.
- By cause: carious (coronal cervical caries, root caries) and non-carious cervical lesions (NCCL: erosion, abrasion, abfraction, usually combined).

**Why cervical lesions are difficult to manage**
- Access is awkward and margins often lie at or below the gingiva.
- Moisture control: crevicular fluid, saliva and bleeding contaminate bonding.
- Substrate: margins on cementum and dentine; sclerotic dentine bonds poorly, so retention is a unique challenge (Overton et al, 2013).
- Pulp is close, so the lesion is biologically deep.
- Aesthetics, plaque retention and gingival health must all be respected.
- Recurrent problems: loss of retention, secondary caries, discoloration and sensitivity (Goldstein et al, 2018).

## 2. Carious versus non-carious cervical lesions

### Explanation

**Root caries.** The definition quoted in Summitt is "a soft, irregularly shaped lesion either (1) totally confined to the root surface" or undermining enamel at the CEJ while clearly starting on the root (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. "These lesions generally begin at or slightly occlusal to the free gingival margin" where plaque stagnates (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. Colour misleads: "Tooth color is not a good predictor of root caries damage", and "The best correlation to date for clinical detection of caries lesions on root surfaces is the softness of the surface" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. A dark, hard, sclerotic surface may need no restoration at all.

**NCCLs.** For the non-carious lesion, "there is overwhelming evidence that the cause is multifactorial" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]: toothbrush/dentifrice abrasion, low pH and occlusal stress all have support, and "no authors claim occlusion to be the solitary etiology for NCCLs" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's group concludes from the clinical data that "abfraction is unlikely to lead to cervical lesion formation independently" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Shape (wedge versus saucer) is a weak guide: "the ability to differentiate etiologies based on lesion shape has yet to be adequately supported with evidence" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Q4 and Q7 cover the pathogenesis and the abfraction argument.

**Epidemiology.** NCCLs appear to be a modern problem: in Copper Age and medieval skulls "no NCCLs were found in 3,927 teeth from 259 individuals" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Today "Prevalence in various patient and population groups ranges from rare to 89%" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], and in one Croatian sample "Cervical wear was identified in 48% of subjects older than 65 years of age" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's reminds the reader that "(Most NCCLs are not carious.)" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. "Caries lesions and noncarious lesions are more common on facial surfaces of teeth than on lingual surfaces", partly because of brushing force and partly, the authors suggest, because of differences in saliva (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Feature | Carious cervical lesion (incl. root caries) | Non-carious cervical lesion |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Plaque acids; risk factors: recession, poor hygiene, diet, low saliva | Multifactorial: abrasion, erosion (biocorrosion), occlusal stress (abfraction) (Overton et al, 2013) |
| Site | At or just coronal to the gingival margin, plaque-stagnation area; may spread round the root | Facial cervical area; both groups commoner facially than lingually (Overton et al, 2013) |
| Shape | Irregular, poorly defined | Wedge, notch or saucer; shape is a weak guide to cause |
| Surface | Soft, "leathery" when active; hard and dark when arrested | Hard, smooth, polished, often sclerotic |
| Colour | Not reliable; softness is the best test (Overton et al, 2013) | Yellow to brown sclerotic dentine |
| Plaque | Present | Usually clean |
| Main problem | Disease activity, recurrence | Retention of the restoration on sclerotic dentine, progression if the cause persists |

**Consequences if left unmanaged:** progressive tooth loss, sensitivity, pulp involvement needing endodontics, tooth loss and new lesions (Overton et al, 2013).

NCCLs are a modern disease (none found in 3,927 ancient teeth); prevalence ranges from rare to 89%, and cervical wear reached 48% of people over 65 in one survey (Overton et al, 2013). Abfraction is unlikely to act alone (Goldstein et al, 2018).

## 3. Diagnosis and assessment

### Explanation

Summitt asks that "the dentist should obtain a careful health history and provide a thorough dental examination" that includes an occlusal analysis, a discussion of hygiene habits and, where indicated, a dietary analysis, and that treatment start only after the probable causes are known (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. For root caries Ch.13 cites Lynch, who found "texture to be the best predictor of microbiologic activity in root caries lesions"; an active lesion is tacky or leathery to light probing, "while offering some resistance to removal of the explorer tip" (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. Because plaque hides root surfaces, "a thorough dental prophylaxis should precede any clinical examination of patients at risk for root caries", and for lesions that may be treated preventively "it is important to conduct the tactile exploration gently" so the surface can remineralise (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. "Vertical bitewing radiographs permit better evaluation of the proximal root surfaces" where attachment is lost, and ICDAS "includes codes for root caries" (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. Torres and Zanatta add a biological warning: "the Class V carious lesions are shallow on the mechanic point of view, but deep on the biological point of view" (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC], because the pulp is close.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Diagnosis**
1. **History:** chief complaint (sensitivity, appearance, food trapping), medical history (reflux, bulimia, xerostomia-inducing drugs, radiotherapy), diet (acidic drinks, frequency), brushing technique, dentifrice and timing, habits, bruxism (Overton et al, 2013).
2. **Examination:**
   - Lesion: site, size, depth, shape, margin position (supra-, equi- or subgingival), surface texture.
   - Caries activity: an active root lesion is soft, tacky or leathery and resists removal of the explorer; arrested lesions are hard and dark. Texture predicts activity better than colour (Matis et al, 2013). Probe gently.
   - Periodontium: recession, attached gingiva width, plaque, inflammation.
   - Occlusion: wear facets, interferences, parafunction.
   - Pulp: vitality and sensitivity tests; Class V lesions are mechanically shallow but biologically deep (Torres and Zanatta, 2020).
3. **Radiographs:** vertical bitewings for proximal root surfaces (Matis et al, 2013).
4. **Indices:** ICDAS root caries codes; tooth-wear indices for NCCLs.
5. **Risk assessment:** caries risk, salivary flow.

## 4. The decision to restore or to monitor

### Explanation

Summitt lists the consequences of neglecting NCCLs: "progressive loss of tooth structure, (2) tooth sensitivity, (3) the need for endodontic therapy, (4) tooth loss", and new lesions (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Active caries is straightforward: "if active caries is present, treatment should be initiated to control the active disease" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. For NCCLs and arrested caries the decision is harder, and Summitt reproduces the Academy of Operative Dentistry criteria for restoring an NCCL: "Inability to eliminate or greatly reduce the rate of lesion progression through elimination of etiologic factors", unacceptable aesthetics, significant sensitivity, and "Threat to the strength of the tooth and integrity of the coronal-radicular unit" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

Sturdevant's says "the operator first must decide, with input from the patient, whether the area needs to be restored", and adds two indications not in the AOD list: restore "If the defect is determined to be contributing to the development of gingival inflammation", and restore a deep defect "to avoid further defect development that may cause a pulpal exposure" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Torres and Zanatta allow small lesions to remain unrestored "if they are small and the etiological factors were identified and controlled" (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC].

The case for monitoring is the restorative cycle: a minimal lesion should be stopped, not filled, to avoid "repetitious replacement of lost or defective restorations with increasingly larger ones" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt's chapter ends: "The decision to restore should be tempered by the certainty that restorations are not permanent" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Decision to restore**

*Carious lesion:* active caries is treated to control disease (Overton et al, 2013). Arrested, hard, cleansable lesions are monitored.

*NCCL: restore when* (Academy of Operative Dentistry criteria, in Overton et al, 2013):
1. progression cannot be stopped or greatly slowed by removing the causes;
2. aesthetics is unacceptable to the patient;
3. significant sensitivity of exposed dentine to cold liquids, food and air;
4. depth threatens tooth strength and the coronal-radicular unit.

Sturdevant's adds: the defect causes plaque retention or gingival inflammation; the lesion is deep enough to risk pulp exposure (Ritter et al, 2019).

*Monitor when:* the lesion is small, inactive, cleansable, symptom-free and the cause is controlled (Torres and Zanatta, 2020). Record baseline photographs or study models and review.

**Patient factors that steer the material away from composite** (Ritter et al, 2019): decreased salivary function, poor motivation or ability for home care, difficult isolation, and medical or physical problems that make the procedure difficult. In these patients materials other than composite should be considered.

Monitoring avoids starting the "re-restoration cycle" (Overton et al, 2013). The patient decides with the dentist, and should know that restorations are not permanent.

## 5. Treatment plan

### Explanation

Summitt states the order: "The first goal of any treatment is to remove the causes of the NCCL(s)" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. The chapter changes the old hygiene advice: abrasion loss "can be minimized by delaying brushing by at least 1 hour" after an acid challenge, with little dentifrice and light force (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sensitivity comes next: "Shallow lesions that lead to thermal sensitivity or sensitivity to touch should first be treated with bonding resins or desensitizing agents" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo add that "it is desirable to determine the cause and eliminate it at the time of treatment otherwise the tooth loss may continue around the restoration" (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].

On occlusion the books differ in confidence. Goldstein et al: "Occlusal adjustment and/or occlusal splints can be used to reduce heavy contacts and bruxism", and they report "relatively high failure rates if the occlusal problems that initiated the lesions are not corrected" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Summitt: "there are no controlled clinical trials to verify improved longevity of Class 5 restorations after altering occlusal contacts" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Both positions are written; they are not reconciled here.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Treatment plan (phased)**

**Phase 1: Systemic and urgent.** Medical referral for reflux, eating disorders or hyposalivation; relieve acute pain or sensitivity.

**Phase 2: Remove the cause and control disease** (the first goal; Overton et al, 2013)
- Erosion: diet counselling, reduce frequency of acidic drinks, use a straw, no brushing for at least 1 hour after acid (Overton et al, 2013).
- Abrasion: soft brush, low-abrasive paste, light force, correct technique.
- Occlusal stress: occlusal adjustment or a night guard (Goldstein et al, 2018); Summitt notes no controlled trial proves this improves restoration survival (Overton et al, 2013).
- Caries: plaque control, diet, fluoride, chlorhexidine varnish, saliva management.

**Phase 3: Non-restorative treatment.** Desensitising agents or bonding resins for shallow sensitive lesions (Overton et al, 2013); remineralisation and monitoring of small lesions.

**Phase 4: Restorative.** Restore when the criteria are met: material selection, isolation and gingival management, bonding, finishing.

**Phase 5: Periodontal.** Root coverage graft alone or combined with restoration.

**Phase 6: Maintenance.** Recall based on risk, fluoride, review of habits, repair or refurbish restorations.

The sequence matters: if the cause is not removed, tooth loss may continue around the restoration (Mount and Ngo, 2016).

## 6. Isolation and gingival management

### Explanation

"While a rubber dam is the ideal method of field isolation and moisture control for all direct-placement restorations", many Class 5 lesions can be managed with retraction cord, cotton rolls and suction (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. For lesions at or below the margin a retraction clamp helps: "A no. 212SA clamp is effective for this purpose", it may need bending to fit, and "Modeling compound is the traditional stabilizing material used" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's gives the alternative pair: "rubber dam and No. 212 retainer or with a cotton roll and retraction cord" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Torres and Zanatta use "the No. 212 or No. 212M retraction clamps" for one tooth, 212L and 212R for neighbours, and with cotton rolls "an extra fine (size 000) non-impregnated gingival retraction cord is applied into the gingival sulcus" (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo control seepage with trichloracetic acid (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].

When the margin is beyond the reach of cord or clamp, surgery is used:
- **Miniflap:** short vertical incisions in keratinised gingiva; "The incision(s) should not be extended past the mucogingival junction" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Torres and Zanatta describe a comparable "trans-surgical restoration", made through a small envelope flap from an intrasulcular incision (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC].
- **Gingivoplasty:** "An inadequate zone of attached gingiva is an absolute contraindication for gingivoplasty"; a graft or apically positioned flap is needed "If gingivoplasty would result in less than 3 mm of attached gingiva adjacent to the restoration" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- **Mucoperiosteal flap and crown lengthening** when the biologic width is invaded. "A combined surgical-restorative procedure provides better access to the restorative site than a two-step procedure"; in a two-step plan for posterior teeth "the restorations should be placed 4 to 8 weeks after surgery", longer anteriorly because "the gingival crest height was not fully stable at 6 months" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

Two points on margins. For a non-carious lesion extending under the gingiva, "Finishing short is almost certainly superior to placing an overhanging restoration" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Where a flap is raised, Summitt suggests "placement of an all-RMGI restoration or an open sandwich restoration with the apical aspect of the restoration restored with RMGI" to favour tissue reattachment (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Isolation and gingival management.** Isolation is the key to long-term success of root-surface restorations (Matis et al, 2013).
- **Rubber dam** is ideal; a No. 212/212SA retraction clamp (212L/212R for adjacent teeth) retracts dam and gingiva; stabilise the clamp with modelling compound (Overton et al, 2013; Torres and Zanatta, 2020).
- **Cotton rolls, suction and retraction cord** (size 000, non-impregnated or with haemostatic agent) for supragingival or equigingival margins (Torres and Zanatta, 2020).
- **Haemostasis:** trichloracetic acid (Mount and Ngo, 2016).
- **Surgical access** when cord or clamp cannot expose the margin:
  - miniflap, incisions kept within the mucogingival junction (Overton et al, 2013), or a small envelope flap from an intrasulcular incision (trans-surgical restoration; Torres and Zanatta, 2020);
  - gingivoplasty only if at least 3 mm of attached gingiva will remain; contraindicated if attached gingiva is inadequate;
  - mucoperiosteal flap with crown lengthening when the biologic width is invaded; combined surgical-restorative placement gives the best access; in two-step plans restore 4-8 weeks after posterior surgery, later anteriorly (Overton et al, 2013).
- For a subgingival NCCL, finishing short is better than an overhang. Under a flap, RMGI at the apical margin (all-RMGI or open sandwich) favours tissue reattachment (Overton et al, 2013).

## 7. Material choices

### Explanation

Summitt's Box 15-1 lists the Class 5 materials (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]:
- non-aesthetic: amalgam; "Gold foil (direct, not widely used)"; gold inlay (not widely used);
- aesthetic: resin composite with a bonding system; resin composite over glass-ionomer (sandwich); flowable composite; glass ionomer; RMGI; compomer; "Porcelain inlay (not widely used)".

The challenge is retention: "Because of the sclerotic nature of the tooth structure in a cervical lesion and the physical properties of restorative materials", long-term retention is difficult (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Glass ionomer and RMGI.** "One clinical study reported an 80% retention rate of restorations placed without mechanical retention at 10 years", but "Traditional glass-ionomer materials suffer surface degradation rather rapidly, especially in the presence of acidic foods" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Franco et al "reported that 96.4% of RMGI restorations were retained at 5 years, while only 51.5% of bonded resin composite restorations were retained", and in a later study "94% of Class 5 RMGIs were retained, which exceeded the success of composites (81% to 85%)"; a review concluded that "glass-ionomer materials (both conventional and resin-modified) showed the highest retention rates" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's agrees: "RMGI restorative materials are the materials of choice for the restoration of NCCLs where esthetics is not of primary importance" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; root caries in patients with active caries is "the primary indication for the use of a glass ionomer" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo go further and make an aesthetic glass ionomer the material of choice for small Site 3 (3.1) and corrosion lesions because "the aesthetic result is very similar to that which can be achieved with composite resin and the adhesion and fluoride release are superior" (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].

**Resin composite.** Sturdevant's notes that "the use of composite as a restorative material for cervical caries lesions predominates in areas of esthetic concern", but advises other materials where there is "decreased salivary function", "decreased patient motivation or ability for home care", or difficult isolation (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Microfilled versus hybrid, flowable.** This is the clearest textbook disagreement in the topic, recorded side by side:
- Summitt: an earlier recommendation of microfills "appears to have been refuted"; "there are no differences in retention rates between microfilled and hybrid resin composites" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. On flowables, "Clinical trials have proven the theoretical to be incorrect", and "The use of a flowable resin composite as a liner has not been shown to improve clinical performance" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- Sturdevant's: "Traditionally, materials with a low elastic modulus such as micro-filled composite resins and resin-modified glass ionomers have been more successful in restoring NCCLs than stiff materials", although modern filled adhesives "can act as stress breakers during tooth flexure, permitting successful restoration with stiffer composite resins" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].
- Torres and Zanatta: "The microfilled composites are an excellent option for the cervical restorations", and "The flowable composites also have the same advantages related to the low elastic modulus" (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC].

**Compomer.** Recommended for cervical lesions, but "They require the use of a dentin bonding system", and "the marginal integrity of compomers has been worse than that of resin composites in long-term clinical trials" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Sandwich (laminate).** "Glass ionomer is used to replace the missing dentin, reduce leakage", aid tissue attachment and retention; "A veneer of resin composite is placed to enhance esthetics, increase color stability", improve margins and wear (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. One trial reported "a 96% survival rate of sandwich restorations at 5 years" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo laminate a glass ionomer later if its colour disappoints: "it can be trimmed back with a fine diamond bur under air/water spray to allow for lamination with composite resin" (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].

**Amalgam and gold.** Amalgam is indicated "on areas where esthetics is not a concern", with poor access and difficult moisture control (Torres et al, 2020) [SRC]. Matis et al: "While not recommended for use in xerostomic patients, it may still be the material of choice when isolation is a problem" (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. Direct gold is historical; "The traditional preparation for a Class 5 direct gold restoration had a trapezoidal outline form" that removed sound tooth (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Materials for cervical restorations** (Box 15-1, Overton et al, 2013)

| Material | Advantages | Limitations | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional GIC | Chemical adhesion, fluoride release and recharge; 80% retention at 10 years without mechanical retention (Overton et al, 2013) | Surface degradation in acid, opaque, moisture sensitive early | Root caries, high caries risk, xerostomia, non-aesthetic areas |
| RMGI | Adhesion, fluoride, better handling and aesthetics than GIC; 96.4% retained at 5 years vs 51.5% for composite in one trial (Overton et al, 2013) | Aesthetics deteriorates; not for stress-bearing areas | Material of choice for NCCLs where aesthetics is not critical (Ritter et al, 2019) |
| Resin composite + adhesive | Best aesthetics and polish, wear resistance | Shrinkage, technique-sensitive bonding to sclerotic dentine, little fluoride | Aesthetic zone, good isolation, low caries risk |
| Flowable composite | Easy adaptation, low modulus | Higher shrinkage; trials show no retention benefit (Overton et al, 2013) | Small lesions; liner role unproven |
| Compomer | Good handling, some fluoride | Needs bonding agent; marginal integrity worse than composite (Overton et al, 2013) | Low-stress cervical lesions in moderate-risk patients |
| Sandwich (GIC/RMGI + composite) | GIC replaces dentine, seals the gingival margin, fluoride; composite gives aesthetics; 96% survival at 5 years (Overton et al, 2013) | Two materials, more time | Large NCCLs and subgingival margins in the aesthetic zone |
| Amalgam | Durable, less technique-sensitive | Needs mechanical retention, unaesthetic, no fluoride | Posterior, poor access or isolation; not for xerostomia (Matis et al, 2013) |
| Direct gold / gold or porcelain inlay | Durable | Removes sound tooth, costly, not widely used | Historical |

**Books disagree on microfill versus hybrid:** Summitt finds no retention difference and says trials disproved the flexible-restoration theory (Overton et al, 2013); Sturdevant's says low-modulus microfills and RMGI have traditionally done better, though filled adhesives now allow stiffer composites (Ritter et al, 2019); Torres and Zanatta (2020) still favour microfills and flowables for NCCLs.

## 8. Adhesive protocol on sclerotic dentine

### Explanation

Sclerotic cervical dentine is a poor bonding substrate. Breschi et al: "bonding to sclerotic dentin may be reduced as compared with normal dentin" (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC]. Nakabayashi and Pashley explain why: the dentine is hypermineralised inside and between tubules "with acid-resistant forms of calcium phosphate such as whitlockite", resin tags are "short, blunt, funnel-shaped tags containing a core of mineral crystals", and bond strengths were lower "in sclerotic cervical root dentin than in artificial" wedge-shaped lesions cut in normal dentine (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998) [SRC]. Torres and Zanatta describe arrested cervical dentine as "more resistant to the acid etching, impairing a proper bonding to the restorative material" (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC].

The suggested remedies are not all supported by trials:
- **Longer etching.** Possible with etch-and-rinse adhesives, but "this risks overetching dentin" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], because "sclerotic dentin is often surrounded by normal dentin that may be overetched by such treatments" (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998) [SRC].
- **Roughening with a bur.** Torres and Zanatta recommend "a gentle removal of the superficial sclerotic layer with round burs, exposing an underlying less sclerotic substrate" (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC]; Breschi et al say burs or air abrasion "may be beneficial" (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC]. Against this, Summitt cites "one clinical trial found no increase in retention when sclerotic lesions were roughened with a bur" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
- **Adhesive strategy.** "The current self-etching adhesive systems appear to have inferior bonds to sclerotic dentin" compared with etch-and-rinse systems (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Breschi et al report clinical outcomes showing "enhanced performance of the adhesives containing stronger acids when dealing with sclerotic dentin", but also one study with "equivalent results at 8 years for Class 5 restorations bonded to sclerotic and nonsclerotic dentin" (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC].
- **Application.** Summitt asks for multiple primer coats where needed and "the application of primer or adhesive with an active scrubbing motion when indicated" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's cites a trial in which "92.5% of the restorations in the vigorous rubbing action group were retained" at 24 months, against 82.5% without or with slight rubbing (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

History shows why bonding matters: the first-generation adhesive "Cervident had poor clinical results when used to restore NCCLs without mechanical retention" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. NCCLs remain the standard clinical test of adhesives because they offer no mechanical retention [Likely].

**Recent evidence.** A 2023 network meta-analysis of double-blind RCTs found that, without selective enamel etching, self-etch universal adhesive use gave more retention loss and fractures in NCCLs over time than etch-and-rinse; selective enamel etching performed like etch-and-rinse, and the authors recommend it for self-etch systems (Ma, Wang and Blatz, 2023) [LIT]. A 2026 network meta-analysis of 74 RCTs found that selective-etch or etch-and-rinse protocols with universal adhesives and nanocomposites had the lowest retention-loss rates (Chaple Gil et al, 2026) [LIT]. Both were read as abstracts only.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Why sclerotic dentine bonds poorly.** Tubules are blocked with acid-resistant mineral (e.g. whitlockite) and intertubular dentine is hypermineralised, so acids demineralise it poorly, monomer infiltration is limited and resin tags are often short and blunt (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998). In laboratory studies bond strength is lower than to normal dentine (Breschi et al, 2013).

**Protocol for an NCCL**
1. Clean the lesion with pumice and water; avoid prophylaxis pastes (Mount and Ngo, 2016).
2. Bevel or roughen the enamel margin with a diamond.
3. Optionally roughen the sclerotic surface with a round bur (Torres and Zanatta, 2020); one trial found no retention gain from this (Overton et al, 2013).
4. Etch enamel with phosphoric acid (selective enamel etch or full etch-and-rinse). Prolonged dentine etching risks over-etching the surrounding normal dentine (Overton et al, 2013).
5. Apply the adhesive actively: scrub, multiple primer coats if needed, evaporate solvent (Overton et al, 2013). Vigorous rubbing gave 92.5% retention at 24 months against 82.5% (Ritter et al, 2019).
6. Prefer etch-and-rinse or selective enamel etching to self-etch alone, which bonds less well to sclerotic dentine (Overton et al, 2013).
7. For GIC/RMGI: condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds, rinse, leave moist (Mount and Ngo, 2016).

**Recent evidence:** network meta-analyses of RCTs favour etch-and-rinse or selective enamel etching over self-etch alone for NCCL retention (Ma, Wang and Blatz, 2023; Chaple Gil et al, 2026).

## 9. Preparation design: bevel and mechanical retention

### Explanation

**Adhesive restorations in NCCLs.** "For NCCLs, little or no preparation is required" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's: the NCCL "requires only surface debridement of any exposed dentin and roughening/bevelling all enamel margins with a diamond" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo: "No instrumentation is required for the restoration of a corrosion lesion" when a glass ionomer is used (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].

**Bevels.** "Beveling of enamel margins is recommended when it would expose the ends of the enamel rods", but "Beveling the gingival margin that ends on cementum is not recommended" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Torres and Zanatta agree that "no bevel is done on the cementum margin" (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC], and Sturdevant's explains: at the cervical margin "bevels are usually not recommended because of the absence of enamel in this area" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

**Large carious Class V for composite (Sturdevant's).** "The features of the preparation include a 90-degree cavosurface margin"; the enamel margin is then bevelled "resulting in an angle approximately 45 degrees to the external tooth surface", "prepared to a width of at least 0.5 mm"; "Groove retention form usually is not necessary but may be used if retention of the restoration is a concern" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Mount and Ngo add that for composite "the outline will need to be extended to reach sound, fully mineralized enamel", whereas glass ionomer can be left on demineralised enamel (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].

**Amalgam and glass ionomer.** For an NCCL, amalgam "requires the removal of sound tooth structure to create a box form for amalgam bulk and retention", so adhesive materials are preferred (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. "There is no need to create sharp internal line angles or to remove sound dentin for axial depth greater than 1 mm"; "Cavosurface bevels are contraindicated in preparations for amalgam or glass ionomer because of the low edge strength of these materials"; for non-bonded amalgam "grooves should be placed in the dentin of both the occlusal and gingival walls to help retain the amalgam" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. For glass ionomer, "A 90-degree butt joint approximately 1 mm deep is a reasonable minimum thickness" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Placement.** "incremental resin composite placement is recommended to decrease the effects of polymerization shrinkage" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's adds "The operator should avoid placing increments that connect to both occlusal/incisal walls and gingival walls" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. "Resin composite should be placed in increments no thicker than 2 mm", and "careful finishing with a no. 12 or 12B scalpel blade is recommended" at the gingival margin (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. A conventional glass ionomer gets "a thin coat of light-activated, resin-based coating" at once (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Preparation design**

| | Composite (bonded) | GIC / RMGI | Amalgam |
|---|---|---|---|
| NCCL | Debride dentine, roughen/bevel enamel only; little or no preparation (Overton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019) | No instrumentation for a corrosion lesion (Mount and Ngo, 2016) | Box form needed; removes sound tooth, so avoided |
| Carious | Remove caries; extend to sound enamel; 90° cavosurface initially | Remove infected dentine only; demineralised enamel may stay | Remove caries and friable enamel |
| Axial depth | As caries dictates | About 1 mm butt joint minimum | No need to remove sound dentine for axial depth beyond 1 mm (Overton et al, 2013) |
| Bevel | Enamel (occlusal/incisal) margin 45°, at least 0.5 mm wide; never on cementum (Ritter et al, 2019) | No bevel (low edge strength) | No bevel |
| Retention | Bond; grooves only if retention is doubtful | Chemical adhesion | Occlusal and gingival retention grooves; pins in large cavities |
| Walls | Outward divergent, follow tooth contour | Same | Diverge with tooth curvature; custom matrix if needed |

**Placement:** composite in increments no thicker than 2 mm, not joining occlusal and gingival walls in one increment (Overton et al, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019); clear cervical matrix optional; finish the gingival margin with a No. 12 blade; protect conventional GIC with a resin coat.

## 10. Periodontal and root-coverage options

### Explanation

Summitt concedes that surgery is "more expensive and more invasive than direct restorations" but calls it valid because "NCCLs involve exclusively or predominantly the anatomical root of the tooth" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. It describes the Zucchelli method for predicting the maximum root coverage (MRC) from the ideal papilla height. "If the MRC is coincident or nearly so with the CEJ, then only root coverage surgery is performed", and "If the MRC is apical to the CEJ, then a Class 5 restoration should be accomplished first", with its apical margin at the MRC, so that the flap is placed over a smooth, convex surface (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's mentions gingival grafts used alone or with restorations (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein et al: "a soft tissue graft or coronally positioned flap are possible treatment options for improved esthetics" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Where surgery is refused, Torres and Zanatta describe how "a gingiva-colored composite can be used" over the cervical part of the restoration (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC].

**Recent evidence (abstracts only).** A 2022 meta-analysis found periodontal plastic surgery less effective for complete root coverage when an NCCL is present; a connective tissue graft without restoration gave better coverage outcomes, while restoration plus graft was best for hypersensitivity (Gennai et al, 2022) [LIT]. A 2022 review found mean root coverage of 69% to 97% and a more stable gingival margin with a connective tissue graft whether or not the NCCL was restored (de Oliveira et al, 2022) [LIT]. A 2026 meta-analysis of four RCTs found no significant difference between combined and periodontal-only treatment in most coverage outcomes, except less dentine sensitivity and a slight increase in probing depth with the combined approach; certainty was low to very low (Arbildo-Vega et al, 2026) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Periodontal management.** NCCLs lie mainly on root surface, so root coverage is a valid option (Overton et al, 2013).
- **Procedures:** coronally advanced flap, connective tissue graft, combinations (Goldstein et al, 2018; Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Zucchelli MRC method:** predict maximum root coverage from ideal papilla height. If MRC is at the CEJ, root coverage alone; if MRC is apical to the CEJ, first place a Class V restoration with its apical margin at the MRC, then graft (Overton et al, 2013).
- **Combined periodontal-restorative approach:** restoration (composite or RMGI) restores a convex profile; graft covers the root.
- **If surgery is refused:** gingiva-coloured composite on the cervical part (Torres and Zanatta, 2020).
- **Before surgery:** plaque and inflammation controlled, cause of the lesion removed, adequate interdental papilla height (it sets the MRC); surgery is more expensive and more invasive than a direct restoration (Overton et al, 2013).

**Recent evidence:** an NCCL reduces complete root coverage; a connective tissue graft gives stable margins with or without restoration; adding the restoration mainly reduces sensitivity (Gennai et al, 2022; de Oliveira et al, 2022; Arbildo-Vega et al, 2026, low-certainty evidence).

## 11. The ageing patient: recession, root caries and xerostomia

### Explanation

Torres and Zanatta link the rise in Class V work to "the population aging associated with the success of the caries disease preventive measures" (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's Ch.13 quantifies it: "About 75% of the US population aged 60 years or older are dentate", and recession of 1 mm or more affected "86.5% of people aged 65 years or older" (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Saliva.** "salivary flow rate is considered the most important of the nonmicrobial salivary parameters", and "an unstimulated flow rate of less than 0.1 mL/min is considered to be below normal" (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. Causes are radiotherapy, autoimmune disease and many drugs. Pilocarpine and cevimeline "can be extremely effective salivary gland stimulants" but need physician consultation, and among saliva substitutes "some commercial products have been found to have the potential to demineralize dentin and should be avoided" (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. Other risks: "The use of removable partial dentures has also been noted as a risk factor in this disease", and people with "active coronal caries lesions are 2 to 3.5 times more likely to develop root caries lesions" (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Prevention first.** "root caries lesions can be arrested, obviating restorative therapy" (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. Effective measures are fluoride in many forms, including "an increase in concentration of fluoride in a dentifrice (ie, prescription level or 5,000 ppm)"; chlorhexidine varnish "regularly is effective in reducing root caries incidence", whereas "regular rinsing with 0.12% chlorhexidine did not have a substantial effect on reducing root caries" in low-income elderly people (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. Xylitol is expected to help on roots; "consuming 5 to 10 g of xylitol daily" in chewing gum reduced dental caries in many studies, but its specific root-caries effect is not well known (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. "Accessible, shallow lesions can be made caries-free and easy to clean through debridement with hand instruments", finishing burs or discs (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC].

For an approximal Site 3.3 lesion at an old restoration, Mount and Ngo note that "Replacement of the entire original restoration may not be necessary" (Mount and Ngo, 2016) [SRC].

**Restoring root caries.** The challenges are "impaired visibility, difficult access, moisture control, pulpal proximity, and the nature of the dentinal substrate itself"; "when possible, adhesive fluoride-releasing restorative materials are preferred" (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC]. "Glass-ionomer cement is the material of choice for most root caries lesions", with 10-year longevity and "reasonable success in xerostomic patients"; composites "are primarily indicated in root caries situations in which esthetics is of major importance"; amalgam is not for xerostomic patients (Matis et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Recent evidence (abstracts only).** The 2024 Cochrane review found that SDF likely prevents new root caries compared with placebo or no treatment (moderate-certainty evidence; 3 studies, 439 participants), while evidence for root caries arrest was very uncertain (Worthington et al, 2024) [LIT]. A 2021 systematic review of trials in middle-aged and older adults identified 38% SDF, arginine toothpaste and high-fluoride toothpaste as the agents with valuable results, and suggested annual 38% SDF as possibly the most effective (Castelo et al, 2021) [LIT]. The Cochrane authors found the impact of SDF staining poorly reported (Worthington et al, 2024) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Cervical lesions in the ageing population.** More people keep their teeth into old age (about 75% of US adults aged 60+ are dentate), and recession of 1 mm or more affects 86.5% of those aged 65+ (Matis et al, 2013). Exposed roots develop NCCLs, hypersensitivity and root caries.

**Special risks:** hyposalivation (unstimulated flow below 0.1 mL/min is abnormal) from drugs, radiotherapy and systemic disease; reduced dexterity and cognition; removable partial dentures; previous coronal caries (2-3.5 times higher risk); diet changes (Matis et al, 2013).

**Management**
1. **Risk control:** saliva assessment; physician review of xerostomic drugs; pilocarpine or cevimeline with medical consultation; saliva substitutes that do not demineralise dentine (Matis et al, 2013).
2. **Arrest before restoring:** 5000 ppm fluoride toothpaste, fluoride varnish, chlorhexidine varnish (0.12% chlorhexidine rinse was ineffective in one large trial), xylitol, calcium-phosphate products; recontour and polish shallow cleansable lesions (Matis et al, 2013). SDF probably prevents new root caries (Worthington et al, 2024); annual 38% SDF, arginine and high-fluoride pastes performed best in older adults (Castelo et al, 2021).
3. **Restoration:** adhesive fluoride-releasing materials preferred. GIC/RMGI is the material of choice for most root caries, including in xerostomia; composite only where aesthetics dominates and moisture control is good; amalgam not for xerostomic patients (Matis et al, 2013). Atraumatic, short appointments; isolation is often the hardest step.
4. **Maintenance:** short recall interval, professional fluoride, denture hygiene, carer instruction.
5. **Practical points:** thorough prophylaxis before examination, because plaque hides root lesions; gentle probing to avoid damaging lesions that could remineralise; plan for limited mouth opening and medical problems (Matis et al, 2013); an approximal root lesion at an old restoration can often be treated without replacing the whole restoration (Mount and Ngo, 2016).

## 12. Recent advances and conclusion

### Explanation

Recent advances in this field are mostly in evidence, not new materials. The adhesive network meta-analyses support selective enamel etching with universal adhesives (Ma, Wang and Blatz, 2023; Chaple Gil et al, 2026) [LIT]. For the elderly, SDF has moderate-certainty evidence for preventing root caries (Worthington et al, 2024) [LIT]. Periodontal-restorative reviews define when to graft, restore or combine (Gennai et al, 2022; Arbildo-Vega et al, 2026) [LIT]. Textbook-level options include resin infiltration of non-cavitated cervical enamel lesions ("The caries infiltration technique may also be applied, completely stopping the lesion progression") and gingiva-coloured composites (Torres and Zanatta, 2020) [SRC]. Goldstein et al list the recurring problems: "difficulty in obtaining moisture control, gaining access to subgingival margins, loss of retention, secondary caries, discoloration, and sensitivity" (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Recent advances**
- Universal adhesives with selective enamel etching; network meta-analyses favour etch-and-rinse or selective etch over self-etch alone for NCCLs (Ma, Wang and Blatz, 2023; Chaple Gil et al, 2026).
- Nanofilled and nanohybrid composites and gingiva-coloured composites (Torres and Zanatta, 2020).
- RMGI restoratives with better handling and aesthetics than early glass ionomers (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Resin infiltration for non-cavitated cervical enamel lesions (Torres and Zanatta, 2020).
- Silver diamine fluoride and 5000 ppm fluoride pastes for root caries in the elderly (Worthington et al, 2024; Matis et al, 2013).
- Combined periodontal-restorative treatment guided by the MRC method (Overton et al, 2013).

**Conclusion.** Management of cervical lesions starts with diagnosis of the cause and caries activity, then cause control, prevention and monitoring. Restore only when progression, aesthetics, sensitivity or tooth strength require it. GIC/RMGI give the best retention and fluoride release, particularly for root caries and xerostomia; composite gives the best aesthetics but depends on careful bonding to sclerotic dentine; the sandwich combines both. Restorations are not permanent (Overton et al, 2013), so maintenance is part of the treatment.

## Critical evidence synthesis

**Claim 1: "Glass ionomer and RMGI are retained better than composite in cervical lesions."**
*Textbook says:* Summitt reports 96.4% RMGI versus 51.5% composite retention at 5 years in one trial, 94% versus 81-85% in another, and a review in which glass ionomers "showed the highest retention rates" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's calls RMGI the material of choice where aesthetics is not primary (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].
*What other sources show:* the same chapter notes conventional glass ionomer degrades in acid and is unaesthetic (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; Sturdevant's adds that with RMGI "the long-term esthetic result is compromised" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The 2026 network meta-analysis found selective-etch universal adhesive with nanocomposite reduced marginal adaptation failure compared with RMGI (Chaple Gil et al, 2026) [LIT].
*Agreement or challenge:* partial challenge. Retention (the restoration staying in place) favours glass ionomers in the older trials; marginal quality and aesthetics favour modern bonded composites. [Likely]
*Strength:* the textbook figures come from individual RCTs cited secondhand; the network meta-analysis was read as an abstract only. [Certain about the source type]
*Clinical implication:* choose RMGI where caries risk, moisture control or saliva are poor; choose composite with a careful adhesive protocol where aesthetics matters; the sandwich covers both.

**Claim 2: "Low-modulus materials (microfilled, flowable composite) last longer in NCCLs because they flex with the tooth."**
*Textbook says:* Sturdevant's records that low-modulus materials have traditionally been more successful (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and Torres and Zanatta (2020) [SRC] support them.
*Challenge:* Summitt: no retention difference between microfilled and hybrid composites, and for flowables "Clinical trials have proven the theoretical to be incorrect" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's itself concedes that modern adhesives act as stress breakers and allow stiffer composites (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].
*Strength:* the theory rests on abfraction and finite element analysis, which is laboratory modelling, not clinical evidence; Summitt cites clinical trials against it. [Likely]
*Conclusion:* write both positions; the flexibility argument should not decide material choice on its own.

**Claim 3: "Roughening sclerotic dentine or prolonging etching improves retention."**
*Textbook says:* Torres and Zanatta (2020) and Breschi et al (2013) [SRC] suggest bur roughening; Nakabayashi and Pashley (1998) [SRC] suggest modified etching.
*Challenge:* one clinical trial found no retention gain from roughening (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; extended etching risks over-etching adjacent normal dentine (Overton et al, 2013; Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998) [SRC]. One clinical study found equal 8-year results on sclerotic and non-sclerotic dentine (Breschi et al, 2013) [SRC].
*Strength:* mechanism from laboratory bond-strength and microscopy; clinical data limited and mixed. [Likely]
*Conclusion:* the adhesive strategy (phosphoric acid etching of enamel) is better supported by clinical evidence (Ma, Wang and Blatz, 2023) [LIT], and active application by one trial (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], than roughening or long dentine etching.

**Claim 4: "Restoring the NCCL before grafting improves root coverage."**
*Textbook says:* Summitt restores first when the MRC is apical to the CEJ, to give a convex surface for the flap (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].
*What other sources show:* meta-analyses find that restoration adds little to coverage outcomes; a graft alone does as well or better for coverage, while the combined approach reduces sensitivity (Gennai et al, 2022; Arbildo-Vega et al, 2026) [LIT].
*Strength:* few RCTs, low to very low certainty (Arbildo-Vega et al, 2026) [LIT]; abstracts only.
*Conclusion:* restore when the lesion needs restoring for its own reasons (depth, sensitivity, part of the defect beyond the MRC), not as a routine step for coverage. [Likely]

## High-yield points
- Class V/Site 3: gingival third or exposed root; Mount's Size 0 lesion is managed without restoration.
- Root caries is diagnosed by softness/texture, not colour; NCCL cause is multifactorial; abfraction does not act alone.
- AOD criteria to restore an NCCL: progression not controlled, aesthetics, sensitivity, threat to tooth strength.
- First goal: remove the cause; delay brushing at least 1 hour after acid.
- Isolation: rubber dam + 212 clamp, or cord + cotton rolls; miniflap, gingivoplasty (3 mm attached gingiva rule), flap or crown lengthening.
- RMGI best retention (96.4% vs 51.5% at 5 years in one trial); compomer marginal integrity worse than composite; sandwich 96% at 5 years.
- No bevel on cementum or for GIC/amalgam; enamel bevel 45°, at least 0.5 mm, for composite.
- Elderly: GIC for root caries and xerostomia; 5000 ppm fluoride, chlorhexidine varnish, SDF.

## Common exam mistakes
- Writing only restorative steps with no diagnosis, cause control or decision-to-restore criteria.
- Bevelling a cementum margin, or bevelling for GIC or amalgam.
- Stating that microfills or flowables are proven better for NCCLs without saying Summitt reports trials against this.
- Recommending amalgam or composite for root caries in a xerostomic patient instead of GIC/RMGI.
- Ignoring the ageing-patient part (saliva, recession, root caries prevention) when the question names the aging population.
