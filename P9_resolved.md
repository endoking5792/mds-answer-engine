## STUDY MODE

### S1. Why GIC suits children [treatment relevance]
GIC bonds without much preparation, releases fluoride, tolerates moisture better than composite, and is easy to place in uncooperative children (Mount et al, 2016). Mount says many deciduous teeth can be restored safely with glass ionomer alone (Mount et al, 2016). Sikri lists Class I to VI cavities, rampant caries and nursing-bottle caries as uses in deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020). Summitt cautions that even in primary teeth fluoride-releasing materials should be used selectively, weighing the tooth's expected life against strength and wear (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S2. Sealants [clinical decision-making]
GIC sealants bond chemically to enamel and dentine, release fluoride for a long time, are more technique-tolerant, and are the material of choice for partially erupted teeth under an operculum (Mount et al, 2016). Recommendations include caries-active children, difficult moisture control, uncooperative patients and no light-curing equipment (Mount et al, 2016). The protocol is pumice cleaning, 10-20% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds, wash, light drying (Mount et al, 2016). A systematic review of mostly laboratory studies found GI and resin-modified GI sealants consistently showed higher fluoride release and recharge than resin sealants (Dobrzynski et al, 2025). Retention was poorer in earlier studies, related to first-generation GIC sealants (Mount et al, 2016). GIC sealants are more soluble than resin sealants but leach fluoride that may be cariostatic (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S3. Cermets [classification]
Cermet (ceramic-metal) powder is made by sintering silver or gold onto glass particles, and up to 5% titanium dioxide may be added to restore colour (Mount et al, 2016). Silver cermet has about 40% by weight micro-fine silver (Mount, 1994). McCabe says cermets are grey, unsuitable for aesthetic restorations, and have greater compressive strength and fatigue limit, but flexural strength and abrasion remain limited (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Sikri says glass cermet is preferred for small Class I cavities in deciduous teeth and for core preparation (Sikri, 2020). Fluoride release of cermet is said to be similar to other GICs by Mount (Mount, 1994), but McCabe and Bonsor say silver cermets release much less (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### S4. Evidence in children [prognosis]
ART with high-viscosity GIC performed no worse than conventional restorations in primary molars (single and multi-surface) in trials up to 5 years, though only 2 of 14 trials had low risk of bias (Frencken et al, 2021). An umbrella review found GIC a good choice in both dentitions with more evidence in the primary dentition, especially ART (Panetta et al, 2024). In molar-incisor hypomineralisation molars, 5-year survival was lower for glass hybrid (59.2%) than for fibre-reinforced composite (75.5%) (Dokumacigil et al, 2026).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement is widely used in children because it bonds, releases fluoride, tolerates moisture and needs little cooperation (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Restorations in primary teeth
- Class I to VI cavities, rampant and nursing-bottle caries (Sikri, 2020).
- Many deciduous teeth can be restored with GIC alone; GIC is often enough in anterior teeth (Mount et al, 2016).
- High-viscosity GIC for posterior deciduous teeth; strength is little different from conventional GIC, but wear resistance is better (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- Glass cermet for small Class I cavities in deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020).
- Resin-modified GIC or composite for aesthetic anterior lesions (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3. Stabilisation and transitional use
Rampant caries: transitional restorations of GIC or RMGIC (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); hypoplastic first permanent molars temporarily restored with GIC then stainless steel crowns (Mount et al, 2016).

### 4. Hall technique and crown cementation
Preformed metal crowns cemented with GIC over carious primary molars without excavation (Sikri, 2020); GIC is the preferred cement for stainless steel crowns after pulp therapy in primary molars (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Fissure sealants
- Chemical bond to enamel and dentine; prolonged fluoride release; technique tolerant (Mount et al, 2016).
- Material of choice for partially erupted teeth under an operculum (Mount et al, 2016).
- Protocol: pumice, 10-20% polyacrylic acid 10 seconds, wash, light dry, place (Mount et al, 2016).
- Recommended in caries-active children and where moisture control is poor (Mount et al, 2016).
- More soluble than resin but leaches fluoride (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); higher fluoride release and recharge than resin sealants in laboratory studies (Dobrzynski et al, 2025).

### 6. Cermets
| Feature | Silver cermet and glass cermet |
|---|---|
| Composition | glass sintered with silver or gold; 5% titanium dioxide whitener (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Properties | greater compressive strength and fatigue limit; limited flexural strength (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Disadvantage | grey colour, metal-carboxylate interface failure (Sikri, 2020) |
| Uses | small Class I in primary teeth, cores, root surfaces, tunnels (Sikri, 2020; Mount, 1994) |

### 7. Evidence
ART with high-viscosity GIC in primary molars: no significant difference from conventional restorations to 5 years (Frencken et al, 2021); evidence best for the primary dentition (Panetta et al, 2024); in MIH molars glass hybrid survival 59.2% against 75.5% for fibre-reinforced composite at 5 years (Dokumacigil et al, 2026).

### 8. Limitations
Wear and fracture in multi-surface or load-bearing cavities, opacity, early moisture sensitivity, need for varnish or resin coat (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020).

### 9. Conclusion
GIC is a first-line material in paediatric restorations, sealing and stabilisation; cermet is a niche variant; the evidence is best for single-surface cavities and primary teeth.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Uses: Class I-VI in primary teeth, rampant caries, transitional, Hall technique cement, sealant (Sikri, 2020).
- GIC sealant: material of choice for partially erupted teeth (Mount et al, 2016).
- Cermet: glass plus sintered silver; grey; small Class I in deciduous teeth (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
- ART in primary molars: no significant difference from conventional to 5 years (Frencken et al, 2021).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Using a low-powder, weak GIC as a sealant.
2. Calling cermet aesthetic.
3. Treating the evidence from primary teeth as proof for permanent molars.
