## STUDY MODE

### S1. Why GIC suits children [treatment relevance]
GIC bonds chemically to tooth and releases fluoride (Sikri, 2020). For sealants, Mount lists moisture tolerance and ease in uncooperative children as advantages (Mount et al, 2016). Mount says many deciduous teeth can be restored safely with glass ionomer alone (Mount et al, 2016). Sikri lists Class I to VI cavities, rampant caries and nursing-bottle caries as uses in deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020). Mount adds that small cavities and enamel defects in young children may be restored with composite resins or GIC, and that stainless steel crowns are the restoration of choice for carious primary molars in young children (Mount et al, 2016). Summitt cautions that even in primary teeth fluoride-releasing materials should be used selectively, weighing the tooth's expected life against strength and wear (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S2. Sealants [clinical decision-making]
Mount says GIC sealants bond chemically, release fluoride for a long time, are more technique-tolerant, and are the material of choice for partially erupted teeth under an operculum, but that most publications recommend resin as the first-choice fissure sealant material; a systematic review gave routine GIC sealants a guarded recommendation, as an interim agent when moisture control is a concern (Mount et al, 2016). Banerjee and Watson report reviews finding no clinically significant advantage of one sealant type, with resin showing longer life and better wear resistance and GIC still recommended where moisture control is difficult (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sakaguchi et al report significantly lower retention of GIC than resin sealants but more fluoride in enamel, hence latent protection after loss (Sakaguchi et al, 2019), and Ritter et al call the efficacy of GI for pit-and-fissure sealants questionable (Ritter et al, 2019; Sturdevant 2nd ed South Asia). Recommendations include caries-active children, difficult moisture control, uncooperative patients and no light-curing equipment (Mount et al, 2016). The protocol is pumice cleaning, 10-20% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds, wash, light drying, placement, then a thin coat of unfilled resin to prevent surface drying and crazing (Mount et al, 2016). A systematic review of mostly laboratory studies found GI and resin-modified GI sealants consistently showed higher fluoride release and recharge than resin sealants (Dobrzynski et al, 2025). Retention was poorer in earlier studies, related to first-generation GIC sealants (Mount et al, 2016). GIC sealants are more soluble than resin sealants but leach fluoride that may be cariostatic (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S3. Cermets [classification]
Cermet (ceramic-metal) powder is made by sintering a metal such as silver onto glass particles, and up to 5% titanium dioxide may be added to restore colour (Mount et al, 2016); gold variants never became commercially available (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- **Silver content.** About 40% by weight micro-fine silver (Mount, 1994); glass and silver of equal volumes, 17.5/82.5 wt% (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Strength and wear.** Mount (1994): abrasion resistance matches amalgam and composite in the products described, compressive strength and fracture resistance improved only to a limited extent. McCabe and Walls (2008): greater compressive strength and fatigue limit, but flexural strength and abrasive wear no better than conventional GIC. Sakaguchi et al (2019): wear resistance better, flexural strength and abrasion resistance not significantly better. Anusavice et al (2013): metallic fillers do not improve mechanical properties, and clinical performance and life in deciduous Class II are no better than conventional GIC, though the cement hardens fast and can be finished early.
- **Colour.** McCabe and Walls (2008), Sakaguchi et al (2019) and Bonsor and Pearson (2013): grey or silver. Sikri (2020) separates silver cermet (Miracle Mix; grey/black, silver acts as a stress absorber, metal-carboxylate interface failure) from glass cermet (sintered; better wear and flexural strength; maintains aesthetics; preferred for small Class I cavities in deciduous teeth and cores). Bonsor lists Miracle Mix among admixes (amalgam alloy added), not cermets.
- **Fluoride.** Similar to other GICs (Mount, 1994); lower (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Anusavice et al, 2013). A secondary source says cermets release an appreciable amount initially and the magnitude increases over time (Exam Manual, 2016).
- **Current status.** van Noort and Barbour (2013): wear adequate for small Class I only, silver cermets have not lived up to expectations especially as an occlusal restorative in the deciduous dentition, continued use highly questionable, off the market. Bonsor and Pearson (2013): never used with great frequency. Mount et al (2016): metal technologies no longer as popular. Sikri (2020) still lists glass cermet for small deciduous Class I.

Silver diamine fluoride can arrest cavitated lesions but stains them black: about 30% solution needing an aesthetic cover (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); 38% applied twice yearly (Sikri, 2020).

### S4. Evidence in children [prognosis]
ART with high-viscosity GIC showed no significant difference from conventional restorations in primary molars (single and multi-surface) at years 1 to 5, though only 2 of 14 trials had low risk of bias and the difference was significant, favouring ART, at years 4.3 and 6.3 (Frencken et al, 2021; abstract only). An umbrella review found GIC a good choice in both dentitions with more evidence in the primary dentition, especially ART, but rated the evidence conflicting and medium- to long-term performance still questionable (Panetta et al, 2024; abstract only). A systematic review found the conventional technique better than ART for single-surface occlusal and multi-surface GIC restorations (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018; abstract only). In molar-incisor hypomineralisation molars, 5-year survival was lower for glass hybrid (59.2%) than for fibre-reinforced composite (75.5%) (Dokumacigil et al, 2026).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement is widely used in children because it bonds chemically and releases fluoride (Sikri, 2020); as a sealant it tolerates moisture and needs less cooperation (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Restorations in primary teeth
- Class I to VI cavities, rampant and nursing-bottle caries (Sikri, 2020).
- High-viscosity GIC for posterior deciduous teeth; strength is little different from conventional GIC, but wear resistance is better (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- Glass cermet for small Class I (Sikri, 2020); silver cermet use highly questionable (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- Stainless steel crowns: restoration of choice for carious primary molars in young children (Mount et al, 2016).
- Composite or GIC for small cavities (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3. Stabilisation and transitional use
Rampant caries: transitional restorations of GIC or RMGIC (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); hypoplastic first permanent molars temporarily restored with GIC then stainless steel crowns (Mount et al, 2016).

### 4. Hall technique and crown cementation
Preformed metal crowns cemented with GIC over carious primary molars without excavation (Sikri, 2020); GIC is the preferred cement for stainless steel crowns after pulp therapy in primary molars (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Fissure sealants
- Resin first choice in most publications; GIC interim where moisture control is poor (Mount et al, 2016); no clinically significant advantage of either (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- GIC: chemical bond, prolonged fluoride release, technique tolerant; material of choice for partially erupted teeth under an operculum; caries-active children (Mount et al, 2016).
- Protocol: pumice, 10-20% polyacrylic acid 10 seconds, wash, light dry, place, thin unfilled resin coat (Mount et al, 2016).
- More soluble than resin but leaches fluoride (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); higher fluoride release and recharge than resin sealants in laboratory studies (Dobrzynski et al, 2025).

### 6. Cermets
| Feature | Silver cermet | Glass cermet |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | glass sintered with silver (Mount et al, 2016) | glass sintered with metal; about 5% titanium dioxide whitener (Sikri, 2020) |
| Properties | compressive strength greater, flexural strength no better than GIC (McCabe and Walls, 2008) | better wear and flexural strength (Sikri, 2020) |
| Disadvantage | grey, metal-carboxylate interface failure (Sikri, 2020) | none listed (Sikri, 2020) |
| Uses | cores, root surfaces, tunnels (Mount, 1994) | small Class I in primary teeth, cores (Sikri, 2020) |

### 7. Evidence
ART with high-viscosity GIC in primary molars: no significant difference from conventional restorations to 5 years, but 2 of 14 trials at low risk of bias and a difference favouring ART at 4.3 and 6.3 years (Frencken et al, 2021); evidence conflicting (Panetta et al, 2024); conventional technique better for multi-surface restorations (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018); in MIH molars glass hybrid survival 59.2% against 75.5% for fibre-reinforced composite at 5 years (Dokumacigil et al, 2026).

### 8. Limitations
Wear and fracture in multi-surface or load-bearing cavities, opacity, early moisture sensitivity, need for coat (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020).

### 9. Conclusion
GIC is a first-line material in paediatric restorations, sealing and stabilisation; cermet is largely obsolete (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); the evidence is best for single-surface cavities and primary teeth.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Uses: Class I-VI in primary teeth, rampant caries, transitional, Hall technique cement, sealant (Sikri, 2020).
- GIC sealant: material of choice for partially erupted teeth, resin first choice otherwise (Mount et al, 2016).
- Cermet: glass plus sintered silver; silver cermet grey (Sikri, 2020), glass cermet keeps aesthetics (Sikri, 2020); largely off the market (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- ART in primary molars: no significant difference to 5 years; 2 of 14 trials low risk of bias (Frencken et al, 2021).
