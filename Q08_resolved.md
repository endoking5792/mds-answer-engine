## STUDY MODE

### S1. Why biocompatibility is a question for GIC [basic science]
The fresh cement is acidic, resin-modified types contain HEMA, and the material is placed close to dentine and sometimes pulp. The answer must separate three issues: the acid, the monomers, and the bacteria that leak around a poorly sealed restoration.

### S2. Pulp response to conventional GIC [treatment relevance]
Fresh GIC has a very low pH (about 1-2) that rises quickly: Bhat gives 2.33 at 2 minutes, 4.18 at 20 minutes and 5.67-6.0 at one day (Bhat et al, 2019); Sikri says near 2 rising to 6-7 (Sikri, 2020). The curve is product- and mix-specific: for a luting cement at the standard ratio Mount gives about 1.8 rising to 4.5 within 30 minutes, and a reduced powder content keeps the pH low for longer (Mount, 1994); Phillips likewise says luting GIC is a greater pulpal hazard when mixed at a low powder:liquid ratio because the pH stays acidic longer (Anusavice et al, 2013). The pulp response is mild; Mount says the inflammation resolves within 10-20 days, McCabe within about 30 days, so the figure depends on the book (Mount et al, 2016; McCabe and Walls, 2008). Reasons: the polyacrylic acid is weak and its large molecules do not pass down dentinal tubules, and dentine buffers the acid (Bhat et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020). The ion-enriched layer also seals the tubules (Mount et al, 2016). The response is moderated by the thickness of residual dentine, and McCabe considers a lining necessary only in very deep cavities (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Remaining-dentine rules differ: Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed uses no liner or base at 2 mm or more, a light-cured RMGI base at 0.5-2 mm, and calcium hydroxide 0.5-1 mm then an RMGI base under 0.5 mm, with liners no thicker than 0.5 mm (Ritter et al, 2019); Singh 2nd ed gives a zinc phosphate or GIC base of 0.5-0.75 mm at 0.5-2 mm (Singh, 2020); Garg and Garg say GIC needs nothing special except calcium hydroxide where less than 0.5 mm remains (Garg and Garg, 2011); Craig 14th gives 1 mm of dentine (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Craig 11th found no difference from zinc oxide-eugenol in monkey pulp (Craig, 2002). For luting use, Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says sensitivity is anecdotal and linked to technique such as overdrying or saliva contamination (Ritter et al, 2019); Goldstein 3rd ed reports little histological pulpal response, particularly if remaining dentine exceeds 1 mm, and no support from clinical trials (Goldstein et al, 2018); Mount reports sensitivity similar to zinc phosphate (Mount et al, 2016), Phillips and Craig 11th record a greater hazard or prolonged hypersensitivity (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig, 2002), and Craig 14th says thin mixes raise the risk while suggesting hydrostatic pressure as another cause (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### S3. Direct pulp contact [clinical decision-making]
The books give different advice on GIC over a pulp exposure. Mount et al (2016) say that without intervening dentine it is quite toxic to the exposed pulp, and the response is better if the pulp is first covered with calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate material, then sealed with GIC (Mount et al, 2016). Mount also records that the pulp may heal in direct contact with GIC, that GIC can cover a mechanical or traumatic exposure of an otherwise healthy pulp with dentine-bridge formation, and that a healthy, uninfected mechanically exposed pulp needs only to be sealed from bacteria to heal; if infection is in any doubt he still prefers a small amount of calcium hydroxide over the exposure (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994). In contrast, van Noort and Barbour (2013) report localised pulp necrosis and inhibited calcific repair in direct contact (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); Berman et al (2021) say GIs are toxic in direct contact with pulp cells and are inferior to calcium hydroxide for direct pulp capping (Berman et al, 2021). In a study of 248 pulp-capped teeth, outcomes were less favourable in teeth restored with GIC (Berman et al, 2021). Singh 2nd ed says Biodentine sets faster so it can be covered with glass ionomer in the same visit (Singh, 2020).

### S4. Resin-modified GIC and HEMA
HEMA is hydrophilic; its cytotoxicity is minimal after polymerisation, but if conversion is low unconverted monomer can leach out (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Polymerised HEMA placed directly on vital pulp may still kill pulp tissue, so RMGIC in direct contact with pulp is contraindicated, and at least 0.2-0.5 mm of dentine is needed under an RMGIC lining (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Summitt says RMGI formulations tend to show greater cytotoxicity than conventional GIC in direct cell contact, though both make excellent liners and bases when not in direct pulp contact (Hilton et al, 2013). Human teeth with mechanical exposures capped with an RMGIC liner showed moderate to intense inflammation, large necrotic zones and no dentine bridge (Hargreaves et al, 2012). Within 0.5 mm of the pulp an RMGIC reduced odontoblast numbers by more than half in one study, yet in human deep dentin there was no difference in inflammation between a calcium hydroxide liner and an RMGIC liner under composite (Hargreaves et al, 2012). Laboratory work links cytotoxicity to HEMA release (Agha et al, 2021), and experimental RMGICs (including a 5 wt% HEMA version) gave 89-98% cell viability against 4% for a commercial RMGIC liner in cell culture, although the authors found that adding HEMA did not significantly change cytotoxicity within the experimental series, so HEMA content alone is not shown to explain the difference (Potiprapanpong et al, 2023) [abstract only].

### S5. Why GIC is nonetheless a good biological material
It reduces bacterial penetration at the interface by chemical bonding, fluoride release, or initial low pH (the mechanism is uncertain) (Hilton et al, 2013); bacterial colonies fail to thrive on its surface (Mount et al, 2016); Braga (2021) states glass ionomers show no evidence of intrinsic antimicrobial activity, that freshly mixed cement is antibacterial through high fluoride and low pH but this is lost with ageing, and that clinical evidence for antibacterial and remineralising effects is scant (Braga, in Ionescu and Hahnel, 2021); Goldstein 3rd ed says the anticariogenic effect promoted for fluoride-releasing luting cement has not been documented clinically (Goldstein et al, 2018); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed gives the fluoride burst as complete in 24 h and protection as dependent on reloading about three times a day (Ritter et al, 2019); Brambilla and Ionescu (2021) place most release in the first 2 days with slow release for up to 3 years; soft tissue shows high tolerance, and gingival and bone-related uses exist (Mount, 1994). Mount (1994) holds that pulpal irritation is the direct result of bacterial activity only (Mount, 1994); van Noort says bacteria play a major role but chemical toxicity, for example from the low pH of some materials, may still be an important factor (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### S6. Recent developments and safety [link to Q3]
The developments in Q3 each raise their own safety question: antibacterial additives (chlorhexidine, triclosan, silver and zinc nanoparticles) must not harm strength or cells, and propolis was shown to be cytotoxic (Sikri, 2020); nano-hydroxyapatite (8% by weight) was incorporated to enhance fluoride release and mechanical properties and to inhibit residual bacteria at the margins (Sikri, 2020); experimental low-HEMA resin-modified formulas aim at cell safety, but only in vitro (Potiprapanpong et al, 2023); newer ion-releasing restoratives (alkasite and bioactive types) are mostly classed as glass ionomer-type by formulation, their behaviour over time is unclear and clinical evidence is limited (Melo et al, 2022).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 34 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Biocompatibility is the ability of a material to function without harming tissue. For GIC the concerns are acidity, monomer release from resin-modified types, and bacterial leakage; the cement is generally biocompatible when a layer of dentine separates it from the pulp (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 2. Pulp response to conventional GIC
- **pH:** about 1-2 when mixed, 4.18 at 20 minutes, 5.67-6.0 at 1 day (Bhat et al, 2019); Hilton says it tends to neutralise within 24 hours (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Response:** mild inflammation resolving within 10-20 days (Mount et al, 2016) to about 30 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- **Why mild:** weak polyacid, large molecules cannot enter tubules, dentine buffering, sealed tubules (Bhat et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Reparative dentine:** Mount reports a dentine bridge when GIC covers a healthy, mechanically exposed pulp, preferring calcium hydroxide if infection is doubtful (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Residual dentine thickness** moderates the response (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### 2A. How the pH changes (Bhat)
| Time after mixing | pH |
|---|---|
| mixing | about 1.7 (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| 2 minutes | 2.33 (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| 20 minutes | 4.18 (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| 1 hour | 4.55 (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| 1 day | 5.67-6.0 (Bhat et al, 2019) |
Bhat's curve; it varies with product and mix (Mount, 1994 gives 1.8 rising to 4.5 in 30 minutes for a luting cement).

### 2B. Compared with other cements
- Less pulp reaction than zinc phosphate but more than zinc oxide-eugenol (Phillips) (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Weaker cell-culture reaction than zinc oxide-eugenol or zinc polyacrylate (Craig 11th ed.) (Craig, 2002).
- Mild compared with its phosphoric-acid predecessors, though biocompatibility is described as controversial and varies by brand (Craig 14th ed.) (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Polyacid cements are considered less harmful than phosphoric-acid cements because of weaker acids and large immobile polyacid chains (McCabe) (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### 2C. Cytotoxicity and dentine thickness
Freshly prepared GIC is mildly cytotoxic in cell screening, and the effect falls with time after setting (Craig, 2002). On pulp contact freshly mixed cements cause mild transient inflammation, greatly reduced when at least 1 mm of dentine is present; thin (low powder:liquid) mixes raise the risk of post-operative sensitivity (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Fluoride released at high concentration may itself raise in-vitro cytotoxicity, and high fluoride release can damage pulp tissue (Craig, 2002; Berman et al, 2021).

### 3. Direct pulp contact
- Mount et al (2016) say GIC is quite toxic to an exposed pulp and prefer calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate cement first; van Noort and Barbour (2013) report localised necrosis in direct contact (Mount et al, 2016; van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- Mount (1994) and Mount et al (2016) say a healthy mechanically exposed pulp may heal under a GIC seal; Berman et al (2021) report toxicity and inferiority to calcium hydroxide in direct capping (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016; Berman et al, 2021).
- Protect dentine under 0.5 mm with calcium hydroxide (Sikri, 2020; Bhat et al, 2019; Singh, 2020; Garg and Garg, 2011), then an RMGI base (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 4. Resin-modified GIC and HEMA
| Concern | Finding |
|---|---|
| HEMA toxicity | hydrophilic; minimal toxicity once polymerised, but unconverted monomer can leach if conversion is low (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); damaging biological properties including allergic contact dermatitis (Hargreaves et al, 2012; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) |
| Pulp | moderate to intense inflammation and no bridge when capping exposures in human teeth (Hargreaves et al, 2012) |
| Cytotoxicity | RMGI tend to be more cytotoxic than conventional GIC in direct cell contact (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| Dentine needed | at least 0.2-0.5 mm under an RMGIC lining (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) |
| Staff | HEMA penetrates surgical gloves, so avoid touching (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) |

### 4A. Precautions with resin-modified GIC
- Sub-line deep cavities with calcium hydroxide, and avoid direct contact between RMGIC and dentine in very deep cavities (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).
- Cure in thin increments (an inference from Bonsor's heat and HEMA warning) (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); McCabe lines only very deep cavities (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Hargreaves et al (2012) report RMGIC capping of human exposures gave inflammation and no bridge (Hargreaves et al, 2012); adhesives, hydrophilic resins and RMGI gave favourable results in primates but not the same biocompatibility in humans (Berman et al, 2021).
- Use calcium silicate or calcium hydroxide on a pulp exposure, then seal with GIC (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Soft tissue, bone and plaque
High tissue tolerance; plaque does not thrive; derivatives used as bone substitutes in surgery (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Luting cements and post-operative sensitivity
Mount reports luting sensitivity similar to zinc phosphate (Mount et al, 2016), but Phillips rates luting GIC a greater pulpal hazard and Craig 11th notes possible prolonged hypersensitivity (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig, 2002). A low powder:liquid ratio keeps the pH acidic longer (Mount, 1994; Anusavice et al, 2013), though Mount calls early acid probably not the cause (Mount et al, 2016). Sealing dentine tubules before luting reduces it (Mount et al, 2016). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed and Goldstein et al link it to technique or dentine desiccation rather than the cement (Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein et al, 2018).

### 7. Recent developments (summary of Q3)
Resin-modified and tri-cure, high-viscosity, nano-fillers, antibacterial and remineralising additives, glass carbomer and zirconomer, and resin-based ion-releasing materials (Sikri, 2020; van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### 8. Biocompatibility of the new materials
- **Antibacterial additives:** chlorhexidine, triclosan, quaternary ammonium compounds; propolis shown cytotoxic (Sikri, 2020).
- **Low-HEMA RMGIC:** experimental materials gave 89-98% cell viability against 4% for a commercial RMGIC liner (cell culture only; HEMA not shown to be the sole cause) (Potiprapanpong et al, 2023).
- **Bioactive additives:** nano-hydroxyapatite added to enhance fluoride release, strength and inhibit residual bacteria (Sikri, 2020).
- **Alkasite and bioactive restoratives:** mostly glass ionomer-type by formulation; behaviour over time unclear; clinical evidence limited (Melo et al, 2022).

### 9. Clinical guidelines
Seal at once; leave dentine between cement and pulp; sub-line deep cavities when using RMGIC; for exposures see section 3 (Mount et al, 2016; Gulabivala and Ng, 2014; Berman et al, 2021).

### 10. Conclusion
Conventional GIC is among the more biocompatible restoratives because of its buffering, large polyacid molecules and seal; resin-modified types add HEMA-related risk near the pulp. New developments must be judged on both biological safety and clinical evidence.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Fresh pH 1-2, about 5.7-6 at 24 hours; response mild, resolves in 10-20 days to about 30 days depending on the book (Bhat et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016; McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Exposed pulp: Mount et al (2016) cover with calcium hydroxide or calcium silicate first yet allow GIC over a healthy mechanical exposure; Berman et al (2021) report toxicity and inferiority to calcium hydroxide (Mount et al, 2016; Berman et al, 2021).
- RMGIC: HEMA, tends to be more cytotoxic than conventional; keep 0.2-0.5 mm dentine (Hilton et al, 2013; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
