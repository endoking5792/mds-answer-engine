## STUDY MODE

### S1. Why GIC lutes at all [basic science]
Mount's reasons: fine film thickness, low solubility, tensile strength and abrasion resistance equivalent to zinc phosphate, continuing fluoride release, and high pulp and gingival tolerance [SRC:A305]. Against zinc phosphate it has similar tensile strength, much lower solubility, thixotropic flow and the same sensitivity potential [SRC:A310]. Sikri lists anticariogenic action, low oral solubility, pulp compatibility, thixotropic flow, low film thickness, adhesion and tooth colour [SRC:G53].

### S2. Limits [treatment relevance]
Its tensile strength (about 2-4 MPa) is no greater than zinc phosphate, so retention comes from preparation design and fit, not from the cement [SRC:A305] [SRC:A477]. Adhesion improves marginal seal but not retention [SRC:B450]. GIC luting cements have higher creep than zinc phosphate, and excess is harder to clean off than with zinc phosphate or polycarboxylate [SRC:C2279]. Early sensitivity worries are now thought unfounded: van Noort says there is no significant difference from zinc phosphate [SRC:B286].

### S3. Technique [treatment]
Powder:liquid about 1.5:1, with particles of 10 micrometres or less, give a film that seats [SRC:A306]. Mount advises against conditioning dentine on vital teeth because of hydraulic pressure, using a mineralising solution or tannic acid if needed; on non-vital teeth 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 seconds [SRC:A311]. Sikri describes a string test (about 3 cm), painting a thin layer on the prepared tooth, filling the crown, seating with occlusal force, and removing excess at initial set [SRC:G53].

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Type I glass ionomer cement is a low powder:liquid (about 1.5:1), fine-particle cement used to cement crowns, bridges, inlays, onlays and orthodontic bands [SRC:A187] [SRC:A306].

### 2. Indications
- Metal and metal-ceramic crowns, bridges, inlays and onlays [SRC:G11].
- Posts and pins; veneers in some systems [SRC:G11].
- Orthodontic bands and brackets; reduced white spot risk almost by half against one resin cement [SRC:G11] [SRC:B449].
- Temporary acrylic crowns: GIC is the preferred non-eugenol cement [SRC:C2277].
- Resin-modified GIC cements (self-etching) are popular for luting indirect restorations and posts [SRC:C275] [SRC:C278].

### 3. Advantages
Chemical adhesion, fluoride release, low solubility, thin film, thixotropic flow (no need to hold pressure), thermal expansion near dentine, radiopaque so residue shows on a radiograph, pulp compatible [SRC:G53] [SRC:A305] [SRC:A306].

### 4. Disadvantages
- Tensile strength 2-4 MPa; cannot make up for poor fit or retention [SRC:A305] [SRC:A477].
- Higher creep than zinc phosphate; excess harder to remove [SRC:C2279].
- Snap set; early moisture sensitivity (protected within about 5 minutes) [SRC:A475] [SRC:G53].
- Possible post-cementation sensitivity from hydraulic displacement of dentinal fluid [SRC:G53].
- Not for all-ceramic restorations (acid-based cements are avoided under them) [SRC:C2170].

### 5. Technique
1. Mix at low powder:liquid ratio (about 1.5:1) on a cool slab [SRC:A306].
2. Prepare the tooth; do not condition vital dentine; use a mineralising solution or tannic acid to seal tubules [SRC:A311] [SRC:A308].
3. String test: about 3 cm [SRC:G53].
4. Paint cement on the prepared surface and fill the crown; seat with occlusal force; vent long parallel walls [SRC:G53] [SRC:A306].
5. Remove excess at initial set [SRC:G53].

### 6. Comparison with other luting cements
| Cement | Compared with luting GIC |
|---|---|
| Zinc phosphate | similar tensile strength; GIC less soluble, thixotropic, fluoride release; phosphate needs positive pressure [SRC:A475] |
| Zinc polycarboxylate | little to choose; GIC somewhat easier to use [SRC:B289] |
| Resin cement | higher tensile strength and mechanical adhesion; no fluoride; film thickness issues [SRC:A476] |

### 7. Conclusion
Luting GIC is a reliable low-solubility cement for well-fitting restorations; retention must come from the preparation, and technique must protect against moisture and over-thin mixes.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Type I: P/L about 1.5:1, particles at or under 10 micrometres [SRC:A306].
- Tensile strength 2-4 MPa; do not rely on it for retention [SRC:A305].
- Vital tooth: do not condition dentine [SRC:A311].
- No significant sensitivity difference from zinc phosphate [SRC:B286].

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Expecting GIC to give retention.
2. Conditioning vital dentine before luting.
3. Using a high powder:liquid ratio that prevents full seating.
