## STUDY MODE

### Indications for post placement
A post is indicated **only** when there is insufficient remaining coronal tooth structure to retain a core at all (Tait & Ricketts, cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5) — the post's role is retention of the core, not reinforcement of the root (cross-ref Q1/Q3). Before planning a post, Sikri's gives a six-point assessment checklist: remaining coronal/incisal tooth structure along the root; the occlusal load the restored tooth will bear; the number of root canals available for resistance/retention; root canal configuration; dentin thickness around the canal (and how much can be used in post preparation); and presence of caries/resorption inside the root, periodontal support, possibility of extra retentive devices, and whether sufficient post length is achievable at all (Sikri, 2017).

### DEFINITION — The "3Rs" of post-and-core restoration
A real, concise framework worth stating as a definitional call-out: the three governing mechanical objectives of any post-and-core system are **Retention** (resisting vertical displacement), **Resistance** (resisting lateral/rotational forces), and (in some framings) a third R addressing structural preservation/reinforcement considerations during preparation — the specific exam wording for this older-series question could not be independently re-verified from a cleanly legible source this session (the repository itself flags this exact item as "reconstructed wording, verify visually"), so this is presented as the retention/resistance pairing already established in Q3, extended with the general principle of minimizing structural sacrifice during post-space preparation, rather than a third independently-named technical term. ### Classification of posts — Sikri's four-class clinical system
- **Class I** — self-retentive metal posts (self-cutting threads or screws).
- **Class II** — metal posts with PASSIVE retention (no direct post-canal-wall contact; retention entirely from luting cement filling the space).
- **Class III** — non-metal posts with passive retention (fiber-reinforced or ceramic posts, used with adhesive cement).
- **Class IV** — biological posts (dentin posts used with adhesive cement) (Sikri, 2017).

### A parallel taxonomy by fabrication and geometry
**Custom cast** vs. **prefabricated** posts; within prefabricated posts, **passive** (tapered or parallel, smooth or serrated) vs. **active** (tapered or parallel, self-threaded) designs (Sikri, 2017).

### Custom cast posts
Fabricated from a negative reproduction (wax or cold-cure resin pattern) of the prepared canal, invested and cast — Type III/IV gold alloys commonly used, though base-metal and titanium alloys also used. Conforms to the canal's own (usually tapered) configuration, especially useful in a flared canal — but a tapered custom cast post is **less retentive** than a parallel post. Does not induce stress during installation but can act as a wedge during load transfer; venting required during cementation (Sikri, 2017).

### Prefabricated posts — recent developments and decision logic
Prefabricated posts save chair time (no laboratory step) and are available in standardised sizes/tapers across metal and fiber-reinforced systems. Marzouk's concrete decision rules (cross-ref Q3): short canal/deficient retention → parallel-sided (or threaded) cemented post; near-vertical occlusal forces (posterior) → parallel-sided post; limited dentin bulk → smallest tapered post. Purely solid threaded posts confined to bulky, resilient dentin, inserted as far as possible into the root trunk; parallel-sided posts avoided where fluting/root-surface concavities exist; post should not extend beyond the middle third of the apical root; threaded posts should NOT be threaded to maximal limit — 2-3mm into root trunk is adequate (Marzouk, 1985). **Later sources are much more cautious about threaded (active) posts:** tapered threaded posts gave 20 times the root fractures of parallel threaded posts (Ingle et al., 2019), Baba does not recommend them, and Torabinejad, Grossman and Gutmann contraindicate or advise avoiding them, while Weine still accepts tapped parallel threaded and split-shank screw posts — the sources split, so a threaded post is best presented as "highest retention, highest stress, last resort" (Baba, 2013; Torabinejad et al, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021; Weine, 2004).

### Bonding techniques for pre-fabricated post systems
A representative clinical technique for a bonded fiber post (generalisable with minor variation across systems): canal etched with 37% phosphoric acid for 10-15 seconds, washed and dried with paper points; primer applied to both canal and post, light cured; dual-cure cement placed in canal and post seated under pressure; exposed post surface coated with bonding agent + composite to build initial core shape, cured 40 seconds, then trimmed/contoured (Sikri, 2017).

### Principles involved in post-and-core fabrication (general)
1. Resistance/retention form should be achieved from the core ALONE wherever possible — the post is a fallback, not a default.
2. An antirotational feature should be present close to the post (pin, box, groove, pronounced pulp chamber).
3. Gingival floors should be bulky and pronounced.
4. The post should be surrounded by as many flat planes at right angles to occluding forces as possible, specifically to reduce stress on the post and surrounding dentin.
5. Loading on the subpulpal floor should be avoided entirely (Marzouk, 1985; Sikri, 2017).

### Factors determining post selection — a consolidated decision framework
1. **Remaining coronal/root structure** — the dominant factor; if adequate for a core alone, no post is needed at all.
2. **Canal configuration/anatomy** — flared canals favour a tapered custom cast post; straight, adequately bulky canals tolerate a parallel prefabricated post.
3. **Occlusal force direction** — near-vertical forces (posterior) favour parallel posts; more complex loading patterns may favour tapered or custom designs.
4. **Dentin bulk/thickness around the canal** — limited bulk mandates the smallest tapered post to avoid perforation risk.
5. **Esthetic requirements** — anterior/visible teeth under an all-ceramic crown favour fiber/esthetic posts over metal (cross-ref Q5).
6. **Retreatment likelihood** — fiber posts (quartz-fiber systems especially) offer genuinely easier, non-traumatic removal versus a cemented cast post.
7. **Cost and chair-time constraints** — prefabricated posts save laboratory time/cost versus custom cast posts.
8. **Post-space preparation safety margin** — the apical gutta-percha rule (4 mm minimum, 5 mm recommended; published values 3-7 mm) and the 1/3-root-width diameter rule (some sources 1/4) (cross-ref Q3) constrain which post lengths/widths are actually achievable regardless of other preferences.

### Audit addendum (2026-10-14) — frameworks and quantified data from the full-chapter audit
**Selection by amount of tissue lost — two published protocols.** (1) Sikri's wall-count protocol (five walls: buccal, lingual, mesial, distal, occlusal): one wall missing — restoration only, no post; two walls missing — core and full crown; three walls missing — post and coronal coverage; four walls missing — post, with crown lengthening or orthodontic extrusion if needed. His type classification runs I (more than half of the crown present), II (less than half), III (coronal structure absent or minimal) and IV (root embedded) (Sikri, 2019). (2) Grossman's flowchart: stop and retreat, treat the gums or extract if there is a poor root filling, active inflammation, pressure sensitivity, fistula, moderate-severe periodontitis or severe loss not helped by lengthening or extrusion. Then — anteriors with access only and no discolouration: etched composite; loss of one or both proximal walls or discolouration: composite core plus full crown; extensive loss: post and core plus crown; posteriors: cuspal coverage required. By amount lost: custom cast post/core when more than ¾ of the coronal structure is gone, prefabricated when ½-⅔; glass-fibre post when loss exceeds ½, metal post when it exceeds ⅔ (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Cast versus prefabricated — a balanced table.** Prefabricated: simple, quick, one appointment, easy to temporise, cost-effective; but the root must be made to fit the post, they are limited when much coronal structure is lost, risk dissimilar-metal corrosion and give no attachment for a removable prosthesis. Cast: custom fit, adapts to irregular canals, strong; but expensive, two appointments, less retentive in some designs, temporisation is difficult, and risks corrosion and casting inaccuracy (Weine, 2004). Cast post/core is the preferred foundation for maxillary premolars (ovoid canals, concavities) and narrow mandibular anteriors, whereas prefabricated posts suit molars (existing walls interfere with insertion); round prefabricated posts are problematic in mandibular incisors, mandibular molars, maxillary first premolars and oval canals (Torabinejad et al, 2021; Berman et al, 2021). Long-term data: cast posts had over 90% success at 5 years with a ferrule (Gopikrishna, 2021; Berman et al, 2021).

**Fibre post types and numbers.** Carbon fibre (Duret, 1988): 8 µm fibres, 64% by weight, radiopaque versions now exist; glass fibre: E-glass or quartz in 50-70% silica, modulus closer to dentine than carbon but falling about 40% after thermocycling and cyclic loading; polyethylene (Ribbond) ribbon: 1.5-2.0 mm ferrule recommended, loosening the only failure mode; zirconia (3 mol% Y-TZP): extremely radiopaque, smooth, poor bonding, and irretrievable so a failure usually means an unrestorable root (Baba, 2013; Sikri, 2019). Reported clinical failure ranges: carbon fibre 0% at 2.7 years to 35% at 6.7 years (12 studies); glass fibre 0% at 2.3 years to 11.4% at 1-2 years (8 studies); zirconia 0% at 2.4 years and 9% at 4.8 years (Baba, 2013). The only Cochrane review found one randomised trial with weak evidence of longer survival for fibre posts (Bolla et al., 2007, cited in Chong, 2017); and a literature review found direct fibre posts outperform metal posts "in general" but the evidence "cannot be considered conclusive", the commonest failure being debonding, which usually allows re-restoration (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Counter to that, Baba found that a failed fibre post does not always leave a restorable root, and failures occurred within 1-3 years (Baba, 2013).

**Cementation guidance — a protocol rather than a preference.** Zinc phosphate is recommended for cast, metal and zirconia posts; resin cement (conventional or self-adhesive) for fibre posts. Avoid resin-modified GIC for posts (hygroscopic expansion, root-fracture concern); polycarboxylate shows plastic deformation under cyclic loading (Baba, 2013; Berman et al, 2021). Eugenol-containing sealer interferes with resin cement polymerisation — the effect is contradictory in the literature and an ethanol or chlorhexidine rinse is advised (Alani and Patel, 2025; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). Cast post surface: 50 µm alumina air abrasion; fibre post surface: follow the manufacturer's instructions because air abrasion can damage fibre posts; zirconia: tribochemical silica coating, since etching and silane are ineffective (Baba, 2013; Berman et al, 2021). Self-etch adhesives are not indicated with some dual-cure cements because their acidity impedes chemical activation; total-etch is the commonest approach and the acid must be washed out and the canal dried by blotting with paper points (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008). A lentulo spiral plus pipette gave the best zinc phosphate retention, but some resin cement makers advise against a lentulo (Baba, 2013; Berman et al, 2021). Bonding to canal dentine is among the most challenging situations — fewer tubules, high C-factor, gutta-percha and sealer remnants, and moisture control is virtually impossible (Alani and Patel, 2025; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

**More named systems (taxonomy completeness).** Sikri's full tree: metal posts (custom cast; prefabricated passive tapered or parallel, smooth or serrated; active tapered or parallel self-threaded); fibre posts (custom fibre; prefabricated carbon — Mirafit Carbon, Endopost, Carbonite; silica/quartz — Aesthetic post, Style post, Light/DT post; glass — Snowpost, Mirafit White, Luscent Anchor, FiberKor, Fibre White Parapost; polyethylene — Ribbond, Construct; fibre with gutta-percha — Fiberfill); all-ceramic; dentine (biological); and miscellaneous (two-piece split cast, post-inlay, light-transmitting plastic posts) (Sikri, 2019; Sikri, 2017). Passive tapered posts such as Endopost and Mooser are the least retentive of all designs; parallel posts include Parapost (vented), Boston, Parkell and Integrapost; active posts include Dentatus screw, V-lock, Radix-Anchor and Kurer (Sikri, 2017).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim — 20m)

**Claim — post selection is best understood as a hierarchy of constraints (safety margins first, then mechanical fit, then esthetics/cost), not a simple preference ranking.** EVIDENCE: the apical gutta-percha rule (4 mm minimum, 5 mm recommended) and the 1/3-root-width diameter rule are described as non-negotiable safety constraints that override length/retention preferences; Marzouk's decision rules are explicitly conditional ("provided it will not compromise canal wall integrity"). INTERPRETATION: a clinically sound selection process should first confirm the safety-constrained envelope of possible post designs for a given canal, THEN select within that envelope based on mechanical/esthetic/cost factors — not start from an esthetic or cost preference and attempt to force-fit it. LIMITATION: this is a reasoned synthesis of the decision rules presented across sources, not a single formal algorithm published as such in any one source read this session. ## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~32 minutes)**

### Indications
Post indicated ONLY when insufficient coronal structure remains for a core alone. Six-point pre-planning checklist: remaining structure, occlusal load, number of canals, canal configuration, dentin thickness, presence of caries/resorption/periodontal support/achievable post length.

### Classification — Sikri's four-class system

| Class | Type |
|---|---|
| I | Self-retentive metal (self-cutting threads/screws) |
| II | Metal, passive retention (cement-retained) |
| III | Non-metal, passive retention (fiber/ceramic + adhesive cement) |
| IV | Biological (dentin posts + adhesive cement) |

### Fabrication/geometry taxonomy
Custom cast vs prefabricated; prefabricated → passive (tapered/parallel, smooth/serrated) vs active (tapered/parallel, self-threaded).

### Custom cast posts
Wax/resin pattern → invested/cast (Type III/IV gold, base-metal, titanium). Conforms to flared canals; tapered = less retentive than parallel; acts as wedge under load; needs venting.

### Prefabricated post decision rules (Marzouk's)
Short canal/deficient retention → parallel; vertical posterior forces → parallel; limited dentin bulk → smallest tapered; threaded posts only in bulky dentin, 2-3mm into root trunk — most later sources avoid them (20× root fractures vs parallel threaded).
Tissue-loss protocol (Sikri): 1 wall missing → fill; 2 → core + crown; 3 → post + coverage; 4 → post ± crown lengthening/extrusion.

### Bonding technique (fiber post, representative)
37% phosphoric acid etch (10-15s) → primer (canal+post), light cure → dual-cure cement → seat under pressure → bond/composite core build-up.

### Principles of fabrication
Core-alone retention preferred; antirotational feature near post; bulky gingival floors; flat planes at right angles to occlusal forces; NEVER load subpulpal floor.

### Factors determining selection (summary table)

| Factor | Favours |
|---|---|
| Flared canal | Tapered custom cast |
| Straight, bulky canal | Parallel prefabricated |
| Vertical posterior forces | Parallel post |
| Limited dentin bulk | Smallest tapered post |
| Esthetic zone + all-ceramic crown | Fiber/esthetic post |
| High retreatment likelihood | Quartz-fiber (easy removal) |
| Cost/chair-time constraint | Prefabricated over custom cast |

### Conclusion
Post classification spans a mechanical-retention axis (Sikri's Class I-IV) and a fabrication/geometry axis (custom cast vs. prefabricated, passive vs. active); selection should proceed by first respecting non-negotiable safety constraints (apical seal, perforation-risk diameter rule), then matching canal anatomy and occlusal-force direction, and finally weighing esthetics, retreatability, and cost.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Post indicated ONLY when core alone cannot be retained — never as a default.
- Sikri's 4 classes: I (self-retentive metal) / II (metal, passive) / III (non-metal, passive) / IV (biological/dentin).
- Taxonomy: custom cast vs prefabricated; prefabricated → passive/active × tapered/parallel.
- Tapered = less retentive but flared-canal-friendly; parallel = more retentive but needs straight canal.
- Marzouk's rules: vertical posterior forces → parallel; limited dentin → smallest tapered; threaded posts → bulky dentin only, 2-3mm into root trunk (not maximal insertion) — but most later sources avoid threaded posts.
- Cementation: zinc phosphate for metal/cast; resin (± self-adhesive) for fibre; avoid RMGIC for posts; eugenol sealers interfere with resin.
- Fiber-post bonding: 37% phosphoric acid 10-15s → primer+light cure → dual-cure cement → pressure seating.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing post types without the governing indication principle (post needed ONLY when core alone is insufficient).
2. Confusing the two independent taxonomies (Sikri's mechanical Class I-IV vs. the custom-cast/prefabricated/active-passive fabrication taxonomy).
3. Reversing the tapered/parallel retention relationship.
4. Omitting Marzouk's conditional "provided it will not compromise canal wall integrity" qualifiers when stating decision rules.
5. Not connecting post-selection factors to the safety-constraint rules (4-5mm apical GP, 1/3 root-width diameter) established in Q3.
6. Presenting threaded posts as a normal option — they are highest retention but highest stress and are avoided by most modern sources.
