## STUDY MODE

### 1. Effect of endodontic treatment on the remaining tooth — basic science
A root canal treated tooth undergoes real, measurable structural change — not a generic "weakening." Tooth structure loss occurs both from the original disease process and from the endodontic access/negotiation itself, and because tooth strength is directly proportional to the bulk of remaining dentin, stiffness decreases measurably as that bulk decreases: a conservative access cavity decreases stiffness by **5%**; a Class II preparation causes **40%** stiffness loss; a full MOD preparation causes **60%** stiffness loss (Sikri, 2017). **These are one source's figures — the underlying experiment is Reeh et al.**, reported as stiffness reduction of about **20%** for an occlusal preparation, **46%** for MO and **63%** for MOD, with endodontic treatment itself adding only a further **5%** (Baba, 2013; Peters, 2025). The two sets are in the same range and are not contradictory in magnitude, but the Reeh figures show that it is the cavity preparation, not the endodontic treatment, that removes most of the stiffness. Ingle's independently corroborates this with a quantified figure of its own: an MOD cavity alone already reduces fracture resistance by **69%** (Ingle et al., 2019, cited in Module 2 SubModule 4.7).

**The "brittle tooth" myth, corrected independently by multiple sources**: it was once widely believed that endodontically treated teeth are inherently more brittle. Summitt's states directly that subsequent research shows the dentin of endodontically treated teeth has mechanical properties **equivalent** to untreated teeth — the real vulnerability comes from the **structure lost**, not any intrinsic change to the dentin substance (Hilton et al., cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5). Advanced Operative Dentistry offers a more precise mechanistic refinement: the traditional belief attributed brittleness to moisture loss and altered collagen cross-linking, but recent research instead attributes it to the **combination** of coronal structure loss AND the structural-integrity change caused specifically by the access preparation itself (Tait & Ricketts, cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5). A further, genuinely important nuance from Ingle's: dentin from endodontically treated teeth DOES show significantly lower shear strength/toughness than vital dentin, plausibly explained by more immature collagen cross-links forming after treatment, with collagen fibrils continuing to degrade over time in post-restored teeth (Ingle et al., 2019) — meaning the simple "dentin properties are unchanged" statement needs this qualification alongside it rather than standing alone.

**Loss of proprioception**: a real, clinically important consequence confirmed in an in-vivo study — a patient can inadvertently bite with MORE force on an endodontically treated tooth than a vital one, since impaired sensory feedback no longer signals excessive load the same way, plausibly explaining otherwise-unexpected failures of structurally sound-looking teeth (Hilton et al., cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5).

### 2. Large-cohort survival data and the coronal-sealing timing rule
Despite the structural concerns above, real large-scale data confirm endodontic treatment plus restoration is genuinely very successful: a US insurance cohort of roughly **1.1 million patients / 1,462,936 teeth** (Salehrabi & Rotstein) found **97% retained at 8 years**; a roughly 1.5-million-tooth Taiwanese cohort (Chen et al.) found **93% retention at 5 years** (Baba, 2013; Torabinejad et al, 2021). *Correction: an earlier version of this answer quoted the US cohort as "6 million patients" (via Hilton et al., 2013); none of the textbooks read in the audit support that figure, so the verified 1.1 million patients / 1.46 million teeth is used here.*

A real, practical timing rule: if the root canal filling is exposed to the oral environment, periapical contamination can occur within DAYS; no definitive data specifies an exact safe exposure window, so many clinicians use **2 weeks** as a conservative, arbitrary retreatment guideline — a long gap between completing endodontic treatment and placing the definitive restoration has been shown to produce a significantly higher failure rate (Hilton et al., cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5).

### 3. Purpose and terminology
Restorations of endodontically treated teeth serve three purposes: protecting remaining structure from fracture, preventing coronal reinfection of the canal system, and replacing missing tooth structure — and the **amount of remaining coronal tooth structure**, not the choice of post or core material, is the dominant factor governing long-term prognosis (Cohen et al., 2021, cited in Module 2 SubModule 8.4).

**DEFINITION — Core, Post/Dowel, Post-and-core**: the substitution of the crown portion of a tooth (with or without pins) is a CORE; root canal support for that core (metallic or plastic) is a POST or DOWEL; the combined system is the "post and core" system. A real, important general principle: resistance and retention form should, wherever possible, be achieved from the core ALONE, without root canal support — the post is a fallback, not a default (Sikri, 2017).

### 4. Classification of restorative modalities, by extent of tissue loss
- **Minimal loss** (intact anterior tooth, only endodontic access to restore): direct composite restoration, providing fracture resistance nearly equal to an intact tooth. Contraindicated once more than ~1/3 of coronal tissue is missing (Cohen et al., 2021).
- **Intermediate loss**: indirect onlays, overlays, or **endocrowns** (ceramic or resin composite, combining post-space anchorage, core, and crown into a single bonded unit) — detailed separately in Q7 (Cohen et al., 2021).
- **Extensive loss**: full-coverage crown, with or without a post-retained core, selected by the classification/selection criteria in Q4.
- **Posterior teeth with intact pulp chamber**: a Nayyar core (chamber-retained, no post) often avoids the need for a post entirely (cross-referenced from Module 1 SubModule 6.1.3).
- **Alternative to a full crown**: Sturdevant's adds that a molar/premolar endodontically treated tooth can, in the right circumstances, be restored with a cast metal **onlay** rather than a full crown, provided it distributes occlusal load to reduce fracture risk — not every endodontically treated posterior tooth automatically needs full coverage (Roberson et al., cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5).

### 5. Core materials — a structured comparison
**Composite resin**: Bis-GMA + filler (quartz/silica/glass), barium/strontium glass for radiopacity. Hybrid and newer low-shrink (silorane) composites are preferred over microfilled. Advantages: thinner sections than amalgam, immediate set (light-cure), bonds to tooth structure, mercury-free, tooth-coloured (ideal under all-ceramic crowns). Disadvantages: technique-sensitive (moisture/shrinkage control, rubber dam + incremental placement needed); hard to distinguish from tooth structure unless tinted. **Rule: composite is the material of choice when an all-ceramic crown is planned** (Tait & Ricketts, cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5).

**GIC/RMGI**: GIC is the weakest direct core and is suitable only where much sound tooth remains — Tait & Ricketts require ≥2 intact walls + 1-2mm remaining sound structure; Mount et al. require at least half the original crown plus a 3 mm collar of natural dentine and call a GIC "core" a misnomer; Pickard's Guide accepts HVGIC only when two opposing walls remain; and Cohen's states that composite has replaced GIC for core fabrication (Tait and Ricketts, 2011; Mount et al, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Berman et al, 2021). RMGI (adds HEMA resin) — **avoid under all-ceramic restorations** (hygroscopic expansion fractures ceramic); ideal for metal/metal-ceramic crowns instead. Advantages: quick-setting, adhesive, fluoride release (clinical significance controversial), low thermal expansion. Disadvantages: low compressive/tensile strength, moisture-sensitive, needs significant remaining structure (Tait & Ricketts, cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5).

**Amalgam**: high-copper alloys preferred. Real timing fact: standard amalgam needs 24 HOURS for max compressive strength (second appointment needed); spherical high-copper alloys usable in 10-15 MINUTES. Advantages: not technique-sensitive, strong in bulk, visually distinct from tooth structure. Disadvantages: long set, low initial strength, mercury concerns, galvanic corrosion risk, needs mechanical/bonded retention (not adhesive) (Tait & Ricketts, cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5).

### 6. Methods of core retention (vital teeth)
Once >50% tooth structure is lost, additional retention is usually needed — mechanical (amalgam) or adhesive (composite, now the more common route). **Adhesive**: acid-etch → hybrid layer → bonding resin; historical evolution from 3-step (etch/prime/bond separate) to modern 1-2-step systems (though 1-step systems etch enamel less effectively than separate phosphoric acid). **Amalgam bonding**: historically rooted in Baldwin's 1897 zinc-phosphate technique; modern products (All-Bond 2, Amalgambond Plus, Panavia) — specific technique sequence detailed in the source material. **Intradental pins**: self-threading, LAST RESORT due to shearing risk, perforation risk, and stress introduction — maximum 4 pins, 2mm into dentin, 1mm from EDJ. **Circumferential slots/grooves** (Outhwaite, 1979): equally retentive as 4 dentin pins, with a copper-band trade-off for matrix stability (Tait & Ricketts, cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5).

### 7. When to place a post — the central decision framework
**A post does NOT strengthen the tooth or root — its only real job is retaining the core (the consensus view; see the caveat below).** If sufficient coronal structure remains to retain a composite core after crown preparation, a post is neither essential nor indicated; a post is only genuinely indicated when there is insufficient remaining coronal structure to retain a core at all (Tait & Ricketts, cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5). This directly corroborates Cohen's independent statement that a post's mechanical role is fundamentally about RETENTION, not reinforcement — resistance to lateral/rotational forces depends far more on an adequate ferrule than on the post itself (Cohen et al., 2021, cited in Module 2 SubModule 4.7). **Caveat — this is a consensus, not unanimity.** Two RCTs on premolars found higher survival with bonded fibre posts (Ferrari et al., 2007 and Cagidiaco et al., 2008, both cited in Chong, 2017), and the Pickard's Guide table describes the fibre-post technique as one that "reinforces" remaining structure; the opposing and more widely cited position is that posts do not reinforce and that "once considered as a means of reinforcing roots, a post is now considered to have a major weakening effect" (Dietschi and Lasfargues, in Roulet et al, 2001). The accurate exam statement is "a post retains; any reinforcement is claimed only for bonded fibre-post systems and is not established."


### 8. Restorability assessment and timing — cross-reference
Restorability must be assessed BEFORE root canal treatment begins (Cohen et al., 2021) — detailed in full in Q6, including the ferrule's dominant role and crown-lengthening as a structural rescue option, cross-referenced here rather than repeated.

### 9. Should a crown always be placed? Anterior vs. posterior
**Anterior teeth**: a retrospective study of 1,273 teeth (1-25 years) found full coronal crowns did NOT significantly improve clinical success over conservative restorations — supporting an etched resin restoration in an otherwise intact/minimally restored anterior tooth (Sorensen and Martinoff: maxillary anteriors 85.4% success without a crown vs 87.5% with, mandibular 94.4% vs 97.5%); a 45-state US insurance dataset found **67%** of endodontically treated anterior teeth were restored WITHOUT a crown (Ingle et al., cited in Module 2 SubModule 8.4). **A counter-weight from the audit:** in the large Salehrabi & Rotstein dataset, of the anterior teeth that were extracted, 83% had no crown, 9.7% had a crown plus post and 7.3% a crown without a post, which Baba presents as stronger evidence that crowned anteriors last longer (Baba, 2013) — so "no benefit" holds for the intact or minimally restored anterior, not for the extensively broken-down one. **Posterior teeth**: a systematic review found crowned teeth had higher 10-year survival (81±12%) than uncrowned (63±15%), though 3-year survival without a crown was still acceptable (84±9%) — divergence emerges only after 3 years; older data (Vire) found crowned teeth lasted 87 months on average vs. 50 for uncrowned, and cuspal coverage gave a 6-FOLD survival advantage (Aquilino) (Module 2 SubModule 8.4). A genuine exception: Nagasiri et al. found a completely intact molar (except conservative access) can be successfully restored without cuspal coverage — though only 36% 5-year survival for molars left WITHOUT cuspal coverage once MORE structure was lost (Nagasiri, as cited in Berman et al, 2021) — the deciding factor is remaining structure, not a fixed rule. For the intact-except-access molar itself, Baba reports 5-year survival of 38% with composite versus 17% with amalgam, and 78% in teeth retaining at least 2.0 mm of axial wall (Baba, 2013): these are different cohorts and endpoints from the 36% above, and both are quoted with their own source.

### 10. Post-and-core vs. single-tooth implants — a current treatment-planning comparison
A systematic review of 55 single-tooth-implant studies found success rates 89-100% (largest study, 2,900 implants: 98% at 3 years); 13 studies of restored endodontically treated teeth found survival 81-100% (largest, 19,817 teeth: 97% at 72 months). **Conclusion: no significant difference in survival between the two modalities** (Hilton et al., cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5) — a genuinely current data point for the restore-vs-extract-and-implant decision.

### 11. Recent advances
Fiber-reinforced posts (detailed in Q5), bioceramic/calcium-silicate-based materials for foundation restorations (cross-ref Calcium Silicate Cements topic), endocrowns (Q7), and digital/CAD-CAM-fabricated custom posts and cores represent the current frontier — each addressing specific limitations (esthetics, stress distribution, retreatability, chairside efficiency) of the traditional cast-metal-post paradigm.

### Audit addendum (2026-10-14) — new quantified content from the full-chapter audit
*All items below are from textbooks read in full in the audit pass; page references are as reported by the readers (see Source Locators).*

**A. Survival, and what predicts it.** A pooled meta-analysis gave tooth survival of 93% at 2 years, 92% at 5 years and 88% at 10 years (Ng et al., cited in Berman et al, 2021). Survival odds ratios: a cast restoration/crown versus a plastic restoration 3.5; both proximal contacts present 2.6; non-molar versus molar 1.4; and teeth needing a cast post-and-core were about 2.6 times more likely to be extracted, with about 90% of those extracted being premolars or molars (Berman et al, 2021; Baba, 2013). A pre-operative periapical lesion did not affect survival, whereas pre-operative pain, a sinus tract, a periodontal defect, perforation or cervical resorption did. Caplan found endodontically treated teeth were 3 times more likely to be extracted than vital contralateral teeth, and molars 7 times more likely than premolars or anteriors; terminal-arch teeth carried about 96% higher risk of loss (Baba, 2013). Among extractions, 52% of molar losses were non-restorable fractures against 11.5% for endodontic failure (Peters, 2025).

**B. Is a full crown always needed? — the evidence, with its bias.** For posterior teeth, failure rates with versus without cuspal-coverage crown were: maxillary premolar 6% vs 44%, maxillary molar 2% vs 50%, mandibular premolar 6% vs 38%, mandibular molar 3% vs 42% (837 teeth; Baba, 2013). Sorensen and Martinoff's 1,273-tooth data: maxillary premolar success 56.0% without a crown vs 93.9% with; maxillary molar 50.0% vs 97.8%. Hansen's 20-year follow-up of 1,639 amalgam-restored posterior teeth without cuspal coverage found maxillary premolars with MOD cavities fractured in 28% at 3 years, 57% at 10 years and 73% at 20 years (Baba, 2013). **But the evidence is confounded:** teeth with periapical lesions were crowned less often (64% vs 76%), a Cochrane review found "no evidence to support or refute" crowns over fillings, and a randomised trial found post plus direct composite equivalent to a crown in premolars at 3 years (Mannocci); Ng described routine full-crown placement as "a large exaggeration" (Peters, 2025; Baba, 2013). Cuspal coverage with amalgam showed 88% survival at 100 months (Plasmans, 268 teeth), falling to 72% when re-treatments were counted; at least 2.0 mm of cuspal thickness is needed (Chong, 2017; Baba, 2013).

**C. Coronal seal and timing.** Defective restorations can allow reinfection within 19 days (Torabinejad); none of Cavit, IRM or TempBond prevented coronal leakage at 30 days; teeth restored within 2 weeks survived better (775 teeth); and teeth crowned within 4 months of root canal treatment survived 85% versus 68% for later crowning, with the late group extracted at 3 times the rate (Baba, 2013; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Peters, 2025). Coronal leakage carried an odds ratio of 10.7 against periapical healing, whereas a satisfactory coronal restoration had an odds ratio of 3.31 (Berman et al, 2021). Gillen's meta-analysis found a good root filling plus good restoration had about 2.7-2.8 times the odds of a good outcome versus either being poor (Peters, 2025). Practical timing rules vary: Harty puts a tender tooth on 2-3 weeks' probation and restores promptly if the periapical radiolucency is under 2 mm; Gulabivala and Ng say not to review for longer than 1 month; and Torabinejad and Weine advise not waiting for radiographic healing (Chong, 2017; Gulabivala and Ng, 2014; Torabinejad et al, 2021). **The "2 weeks" figure used earlier is therefore a convention, not a threshold.**

**D. Posts — what actually fails.** Across 11 studies the mean post-and-core failure rate was 9% (range 6-14%) over a mean of 8 years; loss of retention accounted for 56% of failures and root fracture 16%; root fracture occurred in 7% of threaded posts, 2% of tapered cemented and 1% of parallel cemented, and tapered threaded posts gave 20 times the root fractures of parallel threaded (Deutsch; Ingle et al., 2019, Ch.35). Cast posts survived 91% against 81% for threaded posts with resin core (Creugers). Post-space perforation occurred in 0-7% of cases (0.7%, 1.1%, 2.3% in general series; 5.4% in a referral practice) (Baba, 2013). Posts do not necessarily help: apical periodontitis was commoner in teeth with posts (Eckerbom, 636 teeth), and a post lowered survival under single crowns but improved it for removable-denture abutments (Sorensen and Martinoff, 1985; Baba, 2013).

**E. Decision protocols from the audit (useful framework answers).** Sikri's *Essentials* uses a wall-count protocol: one wall missing — restore directly with no post; two walls missing — core and full crown; three walls missing — post and coronal coverage; four walls missing — post, with crown lengthening or orthodontic extrusion if needed (Sikri, 2019). Grossman's stop criteria: do not begin definitive restoration with a poor root filling, active inflammation, a fistula or moderate-severe periodontitis, but retreat, treat or extract first (Gopikrishna, 2021). Thumb rule for a post: consider one when more than half the crown is lost; in anterior teeth when one or both proximal walls are missing; in posterior teeth when two adjacent proximal walls are missing (Sikri, 2017; Sikri, 2019). Torabinejad's five requirements for a definitive restoration — preserve structure, protect (cuspal coverage in posteriors), satisfy function and esthetics, give a coronal seal, and be completed promptly (Torabinejad et al, 2021).

**F. Direct restoration options under-represented in the earlier answer.** Posterior ETT with a large loss may be restored with a direct composite onlay for seal plus cuspal protection, and this is a legitimate choice when the endodontic prognosis is uncertain and a review period is needed (Alani and Patel, 2025). Where a deep proximal box is subgingival, a composite cervical margin relocation (deep margin elevation) lets the indirect margin sit supragingivally (Alani and Patel, 2025). Dietschi and Lasfargues argue the "fixed view that all ETT must be restored with a full crown has no scientific base" (Roulet et al, 2001), while Wiskott holds that an MOD cavity is sufficient indication for a full crown — a genuine disagreement between authors. Pins: Pickard's Guide says they "should not be used in modern dentistry" and Mount et al. say they are contraindicated in most cases (Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount et al, 2016); the pin topic remains excluded here per the earlier decision.

**G. Amalgam and composite cores — timing and limits.** Phillips' reports high-copper amalgam reaching at least 70% of its strength by 8 hours and being preparable for a crown soon after placement, and Bonsor & Pearson advise a fast-set alloy if the crown is prepared at the same visit; Baba states it "cannot be prepared right away even when fast-setting" — a mild disagreement; the earlier "24 h" figure is the conventional safe interval (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Baba, 2013). For composite cores, a flowable composite must be avoided for build-ups, and the C-factor in the canal is unfavourably high (Baba, 2013; Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1 — the "brittle endodontically treated tooth" belief is only partially correct; the mechanism is more nuanced than either "dentin is unchanged" or "dentin is simply brittle."** EVIDENCE: Summitt's and Sikri's state dentin mechanical properties are largely unchanged; Advanced Operative Dentistry attributes apparent brittleness to the COMBINATION of structure loss and access-preparation-induced structural change; Ingle's separately shows measurably lower shear strength/toughness in treated-tooth dentin, attributed to immature collagen cross-linking. INTERPRETATION: these are not fully contradictory — they describe different aspects (gross material property vs. structural geometry vs. molecular-level collagen maturity) of the same overall phenomenon. LIMITATION: the three sources were not reconciled into one unified mechanistic account in any single source read this session — this is the project's own synthesis across sources, not one textbook's integrated position. [Likely], not [Certain].

**Claim 2 — restoring an endodontically treated tooth and single-tooth implant placement are genuinely comparable options, not one a priori superior choice.** EVIDENCE: systematic review data show no significant survival difference (89-100% implants vs. 81-100% restored teeth, largest studies 98% vs 97%). INTERPRETATION: treatment planning should weigh case-specific factors (remaining structure, periodontal status, cost, patient preference) rather than defaulting to either modality. LIMITATION: survival is not the only outcome that matters (function, esthetics, cost, treatment time are not captured by survival percentages alone) — [Certain] on the survival-rate equivalence itself; [Likely] on how this should influence overall treatment-planning weight, since other outcome dimensions are not addressed by this comparison.

**Claim 3 — "always crown an endodontically treated posterior tooth" is not supported as a universal rule; remaining structure, not tooth type, is the deciding variable.** EVIDENCE: Nagasiri et al.'s intact-molar exception; the molar-specific ferrule-evidence weakness (detailed in Q6); the anterior-crown-no-benefit finding (67% restored without crowns). INTERPRETATION: blanket "crown every root-treated tooth" protocols are not evidence-aligned — case-specific structural assessment should drive the decision. LIMITATION: the "intact except for conservative access" scenario is a narrow exception, and the 36% five-year survival figure for molars without cuspal coverage when MORE structure was lost shows this exception does not generalise broadly — [Certain] on the structure-dependence principle; [Likely] on exactly where the threshold lies for any individual case.

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~150-160 minutes, 100-mark anchor)**

### Introduction
Restoration of the endodontically treated tooth encompasses assessing structural change, selecting an appropriate restorative modality (direct, indirect, post-and-core, or endocrown) matched to remaining tooth structure, and applying sound biomechanical principles (retention vs. resistance, ferrule) to maximise long-term survival.

### 1. Structural changes after endodontic treatment
- Stiffness loss by preparation: access 5%, Class II 40%, MOD 60% (Sikri); Reeh: occlusal 20% / MO 46% / MOD 63%, endodontic treatment adds only 5%; MOD alone 69% fracture-resistance reduction (Ingle's).
- "Brittle tooth" myth: dentin mechanical properties largely unchanged (Summitt's) — real vulnerability = structure loss + access-preparation geometry change (AOD) + lower shear strength/toughness from immature collagen cross-links (Ingle's).
- Proprioception loss → risk of excessive unconscious biting force.
- Survival data: 97% at 8yr (US, 1.46M teeth); 93% at 5yr (Taiwan, ~1.5M); pooled meta-analysis 93% (2yr) / 92% (5yr) / 88% (10yr) (Ng).
- Coronal seal timing: 2-week conservative retreatment guideline; delayed definitive restoration → higher failure.

### 2. Purpose and terminology
Three purposes: protect structure, prevent reinfection, replace missing structure. Core = crown substitution; Post/Dowel = root canal support; remaining structure (not material choice) = dominant prognosis factor.

### 3. Classification by extent of loss

| Tissue loss | Restoration |
|---|---|
| Minimal (intact, access only) | Direct composite |
| <1/3 missing | Direct composite (contraindicated beyond this) |
| Intermediate | Onlay/overlay/endocrown |
| Extensive | Full crown ± post-core |
| Posterior, intact chamber | Nayyar core (no post) |
| Alternative to crown | Cast metal onlay (Sturdevant's) |

### 4. Core materials

| Material | Key rule |
|---|---|
| Composite | Material of choice under all-ceramic crowns |
| GIC | Weakest core; ≥2 opposing walls + 1-2mm (Tait) / half the crown + 3mm collar (Mount) |
| RMGI | AVOID under all-ceramic (hygroscopic expansion fractures ceramic) |
| Amalgam | 24h to full strength (std.) / 10-15min (spherical high-Cu) |

### 5. Core retention methods
Adhesive (hybrid layer, evolved 3-step→1-step systems); amalgam bonding (Baldwin 1897 origin, modern resin adhesives); intradental pins (last resort, max 4, 2mm depth); slots/grooves (Outhwaite 1979, equal to 4 pins).

### 6. When to place a post
Post retains core ONLY — consensus is it does not strengthen root (bonded fibre posts: reinforcement claimed, not established). Indicated only when insufficient coronal structure remains for a core alone. Resistance depends on ferrule, not the post.

### 7. Crown decision — anterior vs posterior
Anterior: 67% restored without crown (no significant benefit in 1,273-tooth study for intact/minimal loss; but 83% of extracted anteriors in the 1.46M-tooth cohort had no crown). Posterior: crowned 81±12% vs uncrowned 63±15% at 10yr (divergence after 3yr); cuspal coverage = 6-fold survival advantage (Aquilino); exception — intact molar with conservative access can succeed without cuspal coverage, but only 36% 5yr survival if more structure lost.

### 8. Post-and-core vs implants
No significant survival difference (98% vs 97%, largest studies) — case-specific treatment planning, not a default choice.

### Conclusion
Remaining coronal tooth structure — not the specific post, core material, or crown design chosen — is the dominant determinant of long-term outcome for an endodontically treated tooth; restorative decision-making should therefore be structure-driven and individualised, informed by strong survival/ferrule evidence, rather than following a fixed "crown + post" default.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Stiffness loss by preparation: access 5% / Class II 40% / MOD 60% (Sikri) or Reeh 20/46/63%; MOD 69% fracture-resistance loss (Ingle's).
- Dentin mechanical properties largely unchanged — real vulnerability is STRUCTURE loss + access geometry, not intrinsic brittleness.
- Survival: 97% at 8yr (US cohort), 93% at 5yr (Taiwan cohort).
- 2-week conservative guideline for coronal-seal-exposure retreatment.
- A post retains the core ONLY — does not (by consensus) strengthen/reinforce the root.
- Post indicated ONLY when insufficient coronal structure remains for a core alone.
- Anterior teeth: 67% restored without a crown, no significant outcome difference.
- Posterior: crowned 81% vs uncrowned 63% at 10yr; cuspal coverage = 6-fold survival advantage.
- Post-and-core vs. implant: no significant survival difference (97-98%).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Stating a post "strengthens" or "reinforces" the root — the single most commonly tested misconception.
2. Treating "crown every endodontically treated tooth" as a universal rule rather than structure-dependent.
3. Omitting the quantified stiffness-loss figures (5%/40%/60%, or Reeh's 20/46/63%) when discussing structural change.
4. Confusing RMGI's suitability (fine for metal/metal-ceramic, contraindicated under all-ceramic) with GIC's.
5. Not citing real survival-comparison data (implant vs restored tooth) when asked about "recent advances" or treatment planning.
