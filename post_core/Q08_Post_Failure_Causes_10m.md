## STUDY MODE

### What "failure" means for a post-retained restoration
Failure may be **restorable** (the post, core or crown fails and the root is intact — loss of retention, post or core fracture, caries) or **non-restorable** (root fracture, perforation, uncontrollable infection). The distinction decides management, and most of the clinical data below separates the two (Ingle et al., 2019).

### How often, and how
Across 11 studies, mean post-and-core failure was **9%** (range 6-14%) over a mean follow-up of 8 years; Kaplan-Meier survival ranged from 78% (Robert, 1970; 5.2 years) to 99% (Weine; 10 years or more). **Loss of retention** occurred in about 5% of posts (144/2,980) and tooth fracture in about 2% (82/3,827); loosening accounted for **56%** of all failures and fractures for about **16%** (Ingle et al., 2019, Ch.35). In one cohort of cast tapered post-cores, 12.7% failed; 39% of failures were non-restorable, 36% were loss of retention and 58% root fracture (Berman et al, 2021). Of molars extracted after root canal treatment, 52% were lost to non-restorable fracture against 11.5% for endodontic failure (Peters, 2025).

### Causes — grouped
**1. Mechanical / design-related**
- **Insufficient length.** Only 34% of 327 posts in one series were as long as the crown; loosened posts used only 59% of the ideal length (Turner, cited in Baba, 2013). A 3 mm increase in length gave about 40% more retention (Sikri, 2017).
- **Excessive diameter or taper.** Fracture risk rose about sixfold for each mm of lost root diameter at 1.5 mm and above (Deutsch, cited in Baba, 2013); the diameter should not exceed one-third of the root (some sources one-quarter).
- **Post form.** Root fracture occurred in 7% of threaded posts, 2% of tapered cemented posts and 1% of parallel cemented posts, and tapered threaded posts caused 20 times the root fractures of parallel threaded posts (Ingle et al., 2019). Threaded posts were present in 64 of 95 vertically fractured teeth (Baba, 2013). Cast posts survived 91% against 81% for threaded posts with resin core (Creugers, cited in Ingle et al., 2019).
- **No ferrule, or inadequate coronal structure.** The lack of a ferrule raises the risk of post-crown dislodgement, post fracture and root fracture (Hilton et al., 2013; Ingle et al., 2019); cross-reference Q2.
- **Rigid-post stress concentration.** Stress concentrates at the post apex and root fracture follows (cross-reference Q3).

**2. Technical / procedural**
- **Post-space perforation.** Incidence 0-7% (0.7% of 420 posts, 1.1% of 3,178 teeth, 2.3% of 5,048, 5.4% in a referral practice); the mandibular first molar and maxillary first premolar are the commonest sites (Baba, 2013).
- **Over-preparation** with Peeso reamers or large Gates-Glidden drills (a No. 4 Gates-Glidden gave 7.3% furcal perforation in the distal root of mandibular molars) (Baba, 2013).
- **Disturbed apical seal.** Too little gutta-percha left — with no gap, 5-year success was 83% against 29% where a gap over 2 mm existed (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- **Hydraulic pressure during cementation** and **poor cementation** — inadequate venting, inadequate cement film, eugenol contamination of resin cement, moisture control, and inadequate smear-layer removal (Berman et al, 2021; Baba, 2013).
- **Delay in restoration.** Defective restorations can reinfect the canal within 19 days (Torabinejad, cited in Baba, 2013); teeth crowned within 4 months survived 85% against 68% (Baba, 2013).

**3. Biological**
- **Recurrent caries** at the margin — debonding of fibre posts is commonly accompanied by caries (Chong, 2017).
- **Persistent or recurrent periapical disease** from coronal leakage or an inadequate root filling; apical periodontitis was commoner in teeth with posts (Eckerbom, 636 teeth, cited in Baba, 2013).
- **Vertical root fracture**, which occurs almost only in endodontically treated teeth, in about 2-10% of restored teeth, usually faciolingual, and often seen with a deep narrow probing defect or a sinus tract (Baba, 2013; Berman et al, 2021).

**4. Occlusal and patient factors**
- Parafunction or bruxism raises failure of fibre posts (Mehta and Millar, cited in Baba, 2013); biting forces are 25-75 N anteriorly and 40-125 N posteriorly, but 1,000 N or more in parafunction (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Tooth position and function**: terminal-arch teeth carried about 96% higher risk of loss, and teeth with two proximal contacts had half the risk (Baba, 2013).
- **Poor case selection** — a post placed where none was needed (a post lowered survival under single crowns) (Sorensen and Martinoff, 1985, cited in Baba, 2013).

### Differences by post type
Fibre posts fail most commonly by **debonding/decementation**, often with caries, which usually allows re-restoration; rigid metal and zirconia posts fail by **root fracture**, which usually does not (Chong, 2017). Reported fibre-post failure rates range from 0% to 35% depending on the series and follow-up (Baba, 2013) — this is a wide and contested range, not an agreed figure.

### Management — by cause
A loosened post-crown should be removed and the root examined with magnification and transillumination for a split root; if a recementation fails within 12 months a split root is almost certain and the prognosis is hopeless (Mount et al, 2016). Otherwise: remove and replace the post (ultrasonics are effective for most metal and fibre posts; adhesively cemented posts and zirconia are very difficult); repair perforations with a calcium-silicate material; re-treat endodontically if apical disease is present; lengthen the crown if caries has extended subgingivally; and extract when the root is fractured, apart from selected hemisection or root resection cases (Baba, 2013; Mount et al, 2016).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim — 10m)

**Claim — most post failures are failures of the restoration, not of the root, but the failures that cost the tooth are the minority tied to post design and preparation.** EVIDENCE: loosening accounted for 56% of failures and root fracture for about 16%; root fracture rate was 1% for parallel cemented posts against 7% for threaded posts and 20× higher for tapered threaded than parallel threaded (Ingle et al., 2019). INTERPRETATION: prevention rests on a small set of controllable choices — a passive parallel (or fibre) post, adequate length with 4-5 mm of gutta-percha preserved, diameter within one-third of the root, a ferrule and timely coronal sealing. LIMITATION: pooled failure rates mix post types, tooth types and eras, many studies are retrospective, and the contrast between fibre and metal post failure modes comes largely from in-vitro and short-term clinical data — [Certain] on the ranking of failure causes; [Likely] on exactly how much each preventive measure reduces failure.

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes)**

### Introduction
Failure of a post-retained restoration may be restorable (loss of retention, post/core fracture, caries) or non-restorable (root fracture, perforation). Mean failure ~9% over 8 years; loosening is 56% of failures, root fracture ~16% (Ingle et al., 2019).

### Causes
1. **Mechanical/design** — short post (loosened posts used 59% of ideal length); excessive diameter (>⅓ root); threaded/tapered post form (root fracture 7% threaded, 2% tapered cemented, 1% parallel); no ferrule; rigid post stress at the apex.
2. **Technical** — perforation (0-7%); over-preparation with Peeso/Gates-Glidden; disturbed apical seal (<4-5 mm gutta-percha; 83% vs 29% success with/without gap); hydraulic pressure and poor cementation (venting, eugenol, moisture).
3. **Biological** — recurrent caries (often with fibre-post debonding); persistent apical periodontitis from leakage; vertical root fracture.
4. **Occlusal/patient** — parafunction; terminal tooth; single contact; poor case selection (post where none needed).

### By post type
Fibre posts: debonding, usually restorable. Metal/zirconia: root fracture, usually non-restorable.

### Management
Remove post-crown; examine root for split; remove and replace post; repair perforation; retreat endodontically; crown lengthening for caries; extract if root fractured.

### Prevention
Decide the need for a post first; parallel or fibre post; ≥4-5 mm gutta-percha; diameter ≤⅓ root; ferrule; vented cementation with correct cement; prompt coronal seal.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Mean post failure ~9% (6-14%) at ~8 years; loosening 56% of failures, root fracture ~16%.
- Root fracture: threaded 7% / tapered cemented 2% / parallel cemented 1%; tapered threaded 20× parallel threaded.
- Post-space perforation 0-7%.
- Loosened posts used only 59% of ideal length.
- No gap between post and gutta-percha: 83% vs 29% success at 5 years (gap >2 mm).
- Fibre posts fail by debonding (restorable); rigid metal by root fracture (not).
- Recementation failing within 12 months → suspect split root.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing causes without distinguishing restorable from non-restorable failure.
2. Omitting technical causes (perforation, over-preparation, poor cementation).
3. Not stating prevention.
4. Presenting fibre-post failure as rare — the range is wide.
5. Forgetting to examine for a split root before recementing.
