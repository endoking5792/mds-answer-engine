## STUDY MODE

### Why restorability must be assessed BEFORE treatment, not after
The clinical sequencing principle is unambiguous: restorability must be assessed **before** root canal treatment begins, not discovered as an unpleasant surprise afterward. A tooth committed to endodontic treatment only to be found unrestorable once access is complete represents a genuinely avoidable failure of the pretreatment workup (Cohen et al., 2021, cited in Module 2 SubModule 4.7).

A genuinely important, practice-relevant finding reinforcing this: endodontically treated teeth are more often extracted because of **restorative** failure than endodontic failure — and such failures generally trace back to errors made at the treatment-**planning** stage (Chugal & Lin, cited in Module 2 SubModule 4.7).

### The single most consequential structural factor — the ferrule (clinical/radiographic criterion)
The single most consequential structural factor in restorability assessment is the **ferrule** — the height of sound coronal dentin extending above the crown margin, encircled by the crown's own axial walls. The magnitude of its effect is genuinely striking: crown preparations with as little as **1mm** of coronal dentin extension above the margin **double** the fracture resistance compared with a preparation whose core material terminates flush at the margin with no ferrule at all; a minimum 1mm ferrule (ideally 2-3mm) is considered necessary to adequately stabilise the final restoration. Preparation shoulder width and crown margin design, by contrast, do **NOT** meaningfully influence fracture strength — ferrule HEIGHT specifically, not general margin geometry, is the variable to assess clinically and radiographically (Cohen et al., 2021).

### Clinical and radiographic criteria for assessing restorability
1. **Clinical probing**: direct assessment of remaining coronal tooth structure circumferentially, identifying how many sound dentin walls remain and their height relative to the anticipated crown margin.
2. **Radiographic assessment**: evaluating the extent of caries/fracture below the alveolar crest, root length/morphology, crown-to-root ratio, and periodontal bone support.
3. **Number of remaining dentin walls**: at least two, ideally three, walls should be present (cross-ref Q2) — fewer than this, restorability becomes genuinely questionable without a structural-rescue intervention.
4. **Crown-to-root ratio**: a poor ratio (short root relative to crown height) compromises long-term periodontal support even if a ferrule can technically be achieved.
5. **Periodontal status**: adequate biologic width must remain achievable after any structural-rescue procedure (below).

### Crown lengthening — the structural rescue option
When restorability is marginal because insufficient ferrule height is available, **crown lengthening** (surgical bone/soft-tissue recontouring, or orthodontic extrusion) is the structural rescue option that can convert an otherwise unrestorable tooth into a restorable one, by physically exposing additional sound coronal/root structure above the alveolar crest.

**The real trade-off**: both approaches genuinely improve restorability by creating more usable ferrule length, but at the real cost of a less favourable crown-to-root ratio and reduced root length/periodontal support (Sikri, 2017, cross-ref Q2) — crown lengthening is not a "free" fix; it trades periodontal support for restorative ferrule.

**The biologic-width constraint governing how much crown lengthening can achieve**: the supra-alveolar gingival apparatus comprises the gingival sulcus (0.7-1mm), junctional epithelium (~1mm), and connective-tissue attachment (~1mm), totalling ~2.7-3mm of biologic width (Ricketts, 2011) — a crown margin must not encroach on this, or the body will react by provoking alveolar bone loss, new connective-tissue attachment, apical migration of the junctional epithelium, and gingival recession to re-establish it. **The earlier shorthand "ferrule ~2 mm + biologic width ~2 mm = 4 mm" needs correcting.** Biologic width proper (Gargiulo) is about 2.04 mm — connective tissue 1.07 mm plus epithelial attachment 0.97 mm — and it *excludes* the sulcus (0.69-0.75 mm); Ingber and Nevins & Skurow require at least 3 mm from margin to bone crest; Dibart found 40% of mandibular molars with under 4 mm margin-to-bone developed furcation lesions at 5 years and none above 4 mm (Baba, 2013). Sikri's *Essentials* gives 3.5-4.0 mm from crest to remaining tooth; Gutmann and Lovdahl work from 2 mm of crown above the free gingival margin plus 2.5 mm of biologic width (Sikri, 2019; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). Hence **at least 4 mm of sound supra-alveolar structure is a minimum, and 4.5-5 mm is the ideal where a 1.5-2 mm ferrule sits on a ~3 mm margin-to-crest distance** — crown lengthening's practical goal is to create this combined envelope where it does not naturally exist.

### Two forms of crown lengthening
1. **Surgical crown lengthening**: osseous recontouring (removing alveolar bone) combined with apically repositioning the gingival flap, physically relocating the bone crest further from the intended crown margin and thereby exposing more sound tooth structure coronal to it. Allows the ferrule/biologic-width envelope to be re-established at the cost of reduced root support and a less favourable crown-to-root ratio.
2. **Orthodontic extrusion**: the tooth is orthodontically moved coronally (extruded) relative to the alveolar bone, bringing more sound tooth structure above the bone crest without surgically removing bone — the periodontal attachment apparatus migrates with the tooth over time (requiring either a stabilisation period or adjunctive fiberotomy to prevent full reattachment migration), achieving a comparable restorative benefit with a different biological mechanism and a different (slower) timeline than surgical crown lengthening. *(Previously [RECALL]; now sourced — see the addendum below: fiberotomy to about 3 mm apical to the crest with repeated intrasulcular incisions, light continuous force of 35-60 g, and 4-10 weeks of stabilisation (Baba, 2013).)*

### A genuine, honest qualification — the molar-specific evidence gap
As already established in Q2, the ferrule principle (and by extension the restorability-rescue logic for crown lengthening) is well-supported for premolars and anteriors, but the evidence specifically for molars is weaker — a meta-analysis found significant benefit only for premolars, and once shown the real additional tissue loss (3-45%) that crown lengthening/full coverage requires in molars specifically, over half of surveyed dentists changed their planned restoration design from full to partial coverage (Peters, ed., cited in Module 2 SubModule 4.7). This directly bears on the restorability decision: crown lengthening a molar specifically to achieve a textbook ferrule may not be evidence-justified if it sacrifices more periodontal support than the resulting restorative benefit is shown to deliver for that tooth type.

### When restorability assessment concludes "unrestorable"
If even one wall of sound dentin cannot be preserved, and crown lengthening/orthodontic extrusion cannot create an adequate ferrule/biologic-width envelope without compromising the crown-to-root ratio below a viable threshold, the tooth should be considered genuinely unrestorable — extraction (with or without implant replacement, cross-ref Q1's implant-comparison data) becomes the appropriate alternative rather than proceeding with endodontic treatment on a tooth that cannot ultimately be restored.

### Audit addendum (2026-10-14) — surgical technique, orthodontic eruption, restorability scoring
**1. Surgical crown lengthening — the technique (Baba, 2013, Ch.7; *Advances in Operative Dentistry* Ch.4).**
- **Indications**: deep subgingival caries or margins; cervical-third root fracture, perforation or resorption; short clinical crown with inadequate retention; a violated biologic width; and exposure to create a ferrule in an endodontically treated tooth (Baba, 2013).
- **Two types**: soft-tissue only (gingivectomy, where adequate attached gingiva and bone allow) and soft tissue plus bone (osseous resection) (Baba, 2013).
- **Technique**: inverse-bevel scalloped incision about 2-3 mm from the margin, intrasulcular incision, interproximal papilla release, a full-thickness (or partial-full-partial) flap, osteoplasty and ostectomy to re-create the required distance between margin and bone, thinning of papillae to about 1.5 mm, then apical repositioning with periosteal mattress sutures; vertical releasing incisions only if attached gingiva is insufficient (Baba, 2013; Roulet et al, 2001).
- **Healing**: the junctional epithelium extends to the apical level of root planing and a reduced, apically relocated biologic width is re-established (Caton and Nyman, cited in Roulet et al, 2001). Margins are not stable for at least 5 months; impressions at 6-8 weeks in non-esthetic areas and 8-12 weeks in esthetic areas; wait 3-6 months before final restoration in esthetic zones; provisional crowns must be highly polished (Baba, 2013; Roulet et al, 2001).
- **Pre-surgical checklist (referral)**: root canal treatment completed, caries removed, near-final margin and a complete foundation in contrasting material (Baba, 2013).
- **Limitations and contraindications**: reduced bone support, short roots, poor crown-root ratio, furcation position (a short-rooted molar with 1 mm above the crest cannot be rescued because bone reduction would reach the furcation), thin biotype, less than 2-3 mm attached gingiva, high muscle attachments, adjacent roots, and unacceptable esthetics; black triangles result when contact-to-crest distance exceeds 5 mm (Baba, 2013; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Torabinejad et al, 2021). Apical relocation of the finish line exposes a narrower root cross-section (weaker, furcation risk) and in-vitro studies showed reduced fracture resistance after simulated crown lengthening (Jain, 2016). Lasers: no solid evidence and unsuitable when bone removal is needed (Baba, 2013).

**2. Orthodontic (forced) eruption — protocol and comparison.**
- **Goals and thresholds**: bone levelling, usable tooth substance and restoration of crown-root ratio; a 1:1 crown-root ratio is acceptable; a fracture 1-2 mm below the CEJ is most favourable; at least 4 mm of tooth coronal to the crest is required (biologic width plus ferrule); extraction by orthodontic means becomes difficult if the fracture is more than 3 mm from the crest or beyond the coronal third of the root (Baba, 2013).
- **Technique**: light continuous force of 35-60 g through the centre of resistance (hook or wire in the post space); anchorage of two teeth each side; **fiberotomy** — a blade to about 3 mm apical to the crest with repeated intrasulcular incisions so that bone does not follow the tooth; extrusion can take as little as 2 months, followed by 4-10 weeks of stabilisation (Baba, 2013). In the rapid protocol (Goodacre), 1-3 weeks of activation then 8-12 weeks of retention, with supracrestal fiberotomy and root planing; slow extrusion causes coronal migration of bone and gingiva and gains no ferrule (Torabinejad et al, 2021).
- **Contraindications**: furcation exposure, moderate-severe bone loss, short or conical roots, and posterior teeth in most protocols (Baba, 2013; Sikri, 2019). A thinner, tapered root gives a smaller cervical diameter and poor emergence profile (Baba, 2013; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Why it may be preferred**: no bone cutting, no resorption or periodontal surgery and better esthetics; *Advances in Operative Dentistry* states "orthodontic extrusion is to be preferred over crown lengthening procedures that result in less favourable crown-root relationships and disharmonies in the gingival architecture" (Sikri, 2019; Roulet et al, 2001). Long-term predictability is not high (Torabinejad et al, 2021) and eruption is always accompanied by migration of the gingival margin and epithelial attachment, so soft tissue and bone often need recontouring (Mount et al, 2016). The earlier statement that both options carry "the same" crown-root cost is therefore too simple: it applies mainly to surgical lengthening.

**3. Surgical extrusion / intra-alveolar transplantation** (Baba, 2013, Ch.9): needs a 1:1 crown-root ratio and is contraindicated with short root trunks; sutures only or a non-rigid splint for about 1 week; PDL restitution by about 45 days; endodontic treatment at about 5 weeks and restoration at about 13 weeks; apical resorption in 17 of 58 roots (Kahnberg), with buccal plate collapse a risk (Baba, 2013).

**4. Restorability scoring and decision criteria.**
- **Tooth Restorability Index (TRI)**: six sextants (two proximal, two buccal, two lingual), each scored 0-3 (0 none, 1 inadequate — dentine under 1.5 mm thick or more than twice as high as thick, 2 questionable, 3 adequate), maximum 18 (McDonald and Setchell, cited in Sikri, 2019). The Dental Practicality Index (Dawood and Patel) is a second named index, cited without scoring in the sources read (Alani and Patel, 2025).
- **Cohen's thresholds**: a post is not needed if more than half the abutment height remains with walls over 1.5 mm; posterior walls should be over 3-4 mm high and 1.5-2 mm thick; a post is needed if less than half of the core height remains or walls are under 1 mm on more than ¾ of the circumference; and 4-5 mm height and 1 mm thickness of sound supra-bony structure are needed for biologic width plus ferrule (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Periodontal criteria (Pickard's Table 6.1)**: favourable = pocket depth 3-4 mm, minimal recession, more than 50% bone level; less favourable = active disease, loss of attachment, less than 50% bone, grade II-III mobility; favourable root-canal status = good-quality sealed filling, periapical resolution, straight canals, broad roots (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Stop criteria**: extraction/implant is considered when a ferrule cannot be created, lengthening gives a poor esthetic or furcation outcome, or the root is too short; "heroic" efforts are discouraged and abutment teeth need more ferrule (Baba, 2013; Berman et al, 2021).
- **Restorability must be decided before root canal treatment** — endodontists extracted before treatment in 37% of decisions for non-restorable caries (Peters, 2025).
- **Alternatives for one failing root**: hemisection, root amputation or bicuspidization (success 62-100% over 3-12 years, about 10% periodontal breakdown; a vertical fracture of the facial root of a maxillary molar can be managed by root resection) (Baba, 2013).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim — restorability assessment should prioritise ferrule height over margin/shoulder design, and crown lengthening should be considered a trade-off, not a routine fix.** EVIDENCE: 1mm ferrule doubles fracture resistance while margin/shoulder design has no measurable effect; crown lengthening/orthodontic extrusion improve ferrule availability but at a stated cost to crown-to-root ratio and root support. INTERPRETATION: the restorability decision should weigh the NET structural benefit (ferrule gained vs. periodontal support lost) rather than treating crown lengthening as an automatic solution whenever ferrule is marginal. LIMITATION: the molar-specific evidence gap means this calculus is genuinely less certain for molars than for premolars/anteriors — [Certain] on the premolar/anterior ferrule-benefit magnitude; [Likely], not settled, on exactly when crown lengthening's trade-off is worthwhile for a molar specifically.

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~40 minutes)**

### Why assess restorability before treatment
Restorative (not endodontic) failure is the more common reason treated teeth are extracted, and this traces back to planning-stage errors — restorability must be assessed BEFORE root canal treatment, not discovered afterward.

### Clinical and radiographic criteria

| Criterion | What to assess |
|---|---|
| Ferrule height | ≥1mm doubles fracture resistance; margin/shoulder design has NO effect |
| Remaining dentin walls | ≥2-3 walls ideal |
| Crown-to-root ratio | Must remain favourable after any rescue procedure |
| Radiographic extent of caries/fracture | How far subgingival/subosseous the defect extends |
| Periodontal bone support | Must tolerate any planned bone removal (surgical crown lengthening) |

### Crown lengthening as a restorability rescue
**Surgical**: osseous recontouring + apical flap repositioning → exposes more sound structure coronal to new bone crest. Cost: reduced root support, worse crown-to-root ratio.
**Surgical technique**: inverse-bevel scalloped incision 2-3mm from margin → full-thickness flap → osteoplasty/ostectomy → apical repositioning; stable margins at ≥5 months; impressions 6-8 wk (12 wk esthetic). Contraindicated: short roots, furcation near crest, poor crown-root ratio.
**Orthodontic extrusion**: light continuous force 35-60g, fiberotomy ~3mm below crest, 4-10 wk stabilisation; preferred by some (no bone loss) but not for posteriors/furcation-exposing cases.

### The combined structural requirement
Ferrule (1.5-2mm) + margin-to-crest (≥3mm; biologic width itself ≈2mm excluding sulcus) → **≥4mm sound supra-alveolar structure as a minimum, 4.5-5mm ideal.**
Assessment tool: Tooth Restorability Index (6 sextants × 0-3).

### Molar-specific caution
Ferrule/crown-lengthening benefit is proven mainly for premolars/anteriors; molar evidence is weaker — crown lengthening a molar purely to satisfy a textbook ferrule rule may not be justified given the real tissue cost (3-45% additional loss).

### When the tooth is unrestorable
If adequate ferrule/biologic-width cannot be created without destroying the crown-to-root ratio, the tooth is unrestorable — extraction (± implant) is the appropriate alternative.

### Conclusion
Restorability assessment is fundamentally a ferrule-height and biologic-width calculation performed BEFORE endodontic treatment, not a margin-design exercise — crown lengthening is a genuine but costly rescue option whose trade-off (periodontal support vs. restorative ferrule) must be weighed explicitly, with particular caution for molars where the evidence base is weaker.

- Restorability MUST be assessed BEFORE endodontic treatment — restorative (not endodontic) failure is the more common cause of extraction.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Treating margin/shoulder design as a restorability-determining factor — it has no measurable effect; ferrule height does.
2. Presenting crown lengthening as a cost-free fix rather than a genuine trade-off (periodontal support/crown-to-root ratio).
3. Omitting the combined ferrule + biologic-width calculation (≥4mm total) when discussing structural requirements.
4. Applying the crown-lengthening rescue indiscriminately to molars without noting the weaker evidence base/real tissue cost there.
5. Not distinguishing surgical crown lengthening from orthodontic extrusion as two mechanistically different rescue options.

## MNEMONIC
"**4mm for Function, 5 for Safety**" — the minimum sound supra-alveolar tooth for a restorable, periodontally healthy endodontically treated tooth is 4mm; 4.5-5mm is ideal.
