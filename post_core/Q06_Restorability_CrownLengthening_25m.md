## STUDY MODE

### Why restorability must be assessed BEFORE treatment, not after
The clinical sequencing principle is unambiguous: restorability must be assessed **before** root canal treatment begins, not discovered as an unpleasant surprise afterward. A tooth committed to endodontic treatment only to be found unrestorable once access is complete represents a genuinely avoidable failure of the pretreatment workup (Cohen et al., 2021, cited in Module 2 SubModule 4.7).

A genuinely important, practice-relevant finding reinforcing this: endodontically treated teeth are more often extracted because of **restorative** failure than endodontic failure — and such failures generally trace back to errors made at the treatment-**planning** stage (Chugal & Lin, cited in Module 2 SubModule 4.7).

### The single most consequential structural factor — the ferrule (clinical/radiographic criterion)
The single most consequential structural factor in restorability assessment is the **ferrule** — the height of sound coronal dentin extending above the crown margin, encircled by the crown's own axial walls. The magnitude of its effect is genuinely striking: crown preparations with as little as **1mm** of coronal dentin extension above the margin **double** the fracture resistance compared with a preparation whose core material terminates flush at the margin with no ferrule at all; a minimum 1mm ferrule (ideally 2-3mm) is considered necessary to adequately stabilise the final restoration. Preparation shoulder width and crown margin design, by contrast, do **NOT** meaningfully influence fracture strength — ferrule HEIGHT specifically, not general margin geometry, is the variable to assess clinically and radiographically (Cohen et al., 2021).

### Clinical and radiographic criteria for assessing restorability
1. **Clinical probing**: direct assessment of remaining coronal tooth structure circumferentially, identifying how many sound dentin walls remain and their height relative to the anticipated crown margin.
2. **Radiographic assessment**: evaluating the extent of caries/fracture below the alveolar crest, root length/morphology, crown-to-root ratio, and periodontal bone support.
3. **Number of remaining dentin walls**: at least two, ideally three, walls should be present (cross-ref Q2) — fewer than this, restorability becomes genuinely questionable without a structural-rescue intervention.
4. **Crown-to-root ratio**: a poor ratio (short root relative to crown height) compromises long-term periodontal support even if a ferrule can technically be achieved.
5. **Periodontal status**: adequate biologic width must remain achievable after any structural-rescue procedure (below).

### Crown lengthening — the structural rescue option
When restorability is marginal because insufficient ferrule height is available, **crown lengthening** (surgical bone/soft-tissue recontouring, or orthodontic extrusion) is the structural rescue option that can convert an otherwise unrestorable tooth into a restorable one, by physically exposing additional sound coronal/root structure above the alveolar crest.

**The real trade-off**: both approaches genuinely improve restorability by creating more usable ferrule length, but at the real cost of a less favourable crown-to-root ratio and reduced root length/periodontal support (Sikri, 2017, cross-ref Q2) — crown lengthening is not a "free" fix; it trades periodontal support for restorative ferrule.

**The biologic-width constraint governing how much crown lengthening can achieve**: the supra-alveolar gingival apparatus comprises the gingival sulcus (0.7-1mm), junctional epithelium (~1mm), and connective-tissue attachment (~1mm), totalling ~2.7-3mm of biologic width (Ricketts, 2011) — a crown margin must not encroach on this, or the body will react by provoking alveolar bone loss, new connective-tissue attachment, apical migration of the junctional epithelium, and gingival recession to re-establish it. **Combining the ~2mm ferrule requirement with this ~2mm biologic width means at least 4mm of sound supra-alveolar tooth structure is genuinely necessary** for both mechanical stability and periodontal health together (Hilton et al., cited in Module 1 SubModule 5.1.5) — crown lengthening's practical goal is to create this combined 4mm envelope where it does not naturally exist.

### Two forms of crown lengthening
1. **Surgical crown lengthening**: osseous recontouring (removing alveolar bone) combined with apically repositioning the gingival flap, physically relocating the bone crest further from the intended crown margin and thereby exposing more sound tooth structure coronal to it. Allows the ferrule/biologic-width envelope to be re-established at the cost of reduced root support and a less favourable crown-to-root ratio.
2. **Orthodontic extrusion**: the tooth is orthodontically moved coronally (extruded) relative to the alveolar bone, bringing more sound tooth structure above the bone crest without surgically removing bone — the periodontal attachment apparatus migrates with the tooth over time (requiring either a stabilisation period or adjunctive fiberotomy to prevent full reattachment migration), achieving a comparable restorative benefit with a different biological mechanism and a different (slower) timeline than surgical crown lengthening. ### A genuine, honest qualification — the molar-specific evidence gap
As already established in Q2, the ferrule principle (and by extension the restorability-rescue logic for crown lengthening) is well-supported for premolars and anteriors, but the evidence specifically for molars is weaker — a meta-analysis found significant benefit only for premolars, and once shown the real additional tissue loss (3-45%) that crown lengthening/full coverage requires in molars specifically, over half of surveyed dentists changed their planned restoration design from full to partial coverage (Peters, ed., cited in Module 2 SubModule 4.7). This directly bears on the restorability decision: crown lengthening a molar specifically to achieve a textbook ferrule may not be evidence-justified if it sacrifices more periodontal support than the resulting restorative benefit is shown to deliver for that tooth type.

### When restorability assessment concludes "unrestorable"
If even one wall of sound dentin cannot be preserved, and crown lengthening/orthodontic extrusion cannot create an adequate ferrule/biologic-width envelope without compromising the crown-to-root ratio below a viable threshold, the tooth should be considered genuinely unrestorable — extraction (with or without implant replacement, cross-ref Q1's implant-comparison data) becomes the appropriate alternative rather than proceeding with endodontic treatment on a tooth that cannot ultimately be restored.

## EXAM MODE

### Why assess restorability before treatment
Restorative (not endodontic) failure is the more common reason treated teeth are extracted, and this traces back to planning-stage errors — restorability must be assessed BEFORE root canal treatment, not discovered afterward.

### Clinical and radiographic criteria

| Criterion | What to assess |
|---|---|
| Ferrule height | ≥1mm doubles fracture resistance; margin/shoulder design has NO effect |
| Remaining dentin walls | ≥2-3 walls ideal |
| Crown-to-root ratio | Must remain favourable after any rescue procedure |
| Radiographic extent of caries/fracture | How far subgingival/subosseous the defect extends |
| Periodontal bone support | Must tolerate any planned bone removal (surgical crown lengthening) |

### Crown lengthening as a restorability rescue
**Surgical**: osseous recontouring + apical flap repositioning → exposes more sound structure coronal to new bone crest. Cost: reduced root support, worse crown-to-root ratio.
**Orthodontic extrusion**: tooth moved coronally relative to bone; periodontal apparatus migrates with it (may need fiberotomy/stabilisation). Slower, different biological mechanism, comparable restorative goal.

### The combined structural requirement
Ferrule (~2mm) + biologic width (~2.7-3mm, sulcus+junctional epithelium+connective tissue) → **≥4mm sound supra-alveolar structure needed** for both mechanical AND periodontal health.

### Molar-specific caution
Ferrule/crown-lengthening benefit is proven mainly for premolars/anteriors; molar evidence is weaker — crown lengthening a molar purely to satisfy a textbook ferrule rule may not be justified given the real tissue cost (3-45% additional loss).

### When the tooth is unrestorable
If adequate ferrule/biologic-width cannot be created without destroying the crown-to-root ratio, the tooth is unrestorable — extraction (± implant) is the appropriate alternative.

### Conclusion
Restorability assessment is fundamentally a ferrule-height and biologic-width calculation performed BEFORE endodontic treatment, not a margin-design exercise — crown lengthening is a genuine but costly rescue option whose trade-off (periodontal support vs. restorative ferrule) must be weighed explicitly, with particular caution for molars where the evidence base is weaker.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Treating margin/shoulder design as a restorability-determining factor — it has no measurable effect; ferrule height does.
2. Presenting crown lengthening as a cost-free fix rather than a genuine trade-off (periodontal support/crown-to-root ratio).
3. Omitting the combined ferrule + biologic-width calculation (≥4mm total) when discussing structural requirements.
4. Applying the crown-lengthening rescue indiscriminately to molars without noting the weaker evidence base/real tissue cost there.
5. Not distinguishing surgical crown lengthening from orthodontic extrusion as two mechanistically different rescue options.

## MNEMONIC
"**4mm for Function**" — the combined ferrule (2mm) + biologic width (2mm) minimum needed for a restorable, periodontally healthy endodontically treated tooth.
