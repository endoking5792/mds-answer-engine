## STUDY MODE

**What "without pulpal exposure" means.** The injury is an enamel fracture or an enamel-dentin fracture with the pulp intact. In the WHO-based classification it is an uncomplicated crown fracture, and in Ellis terms a Class 1 or Class 2 fracture (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234; Sikri, n.d., p.484). The question is therefore about dentin sealing, pulp monitoring and restoration in a growing child.

**Why a 12-year-old is different.** At 12 the tooth is usually immature or still developing, and the pulp is larger and more reactive than in an adult. The priorities are to protect the pulp, keep the tooth in function, and avoid over-treatment. Kohli and Lee stress that the prime objective in a developing dentition is to retain the tooth until growth is complete (2026, p.609-610). Hexem and Eisler note that teeth in children and young teenagers are restored to preserve the alveolar bone and gingiva until skeletal maturity (2026, Part 1, p.589).

**Immediate care.** Prevent bacterial ingress into the exposed dentin. Andreasen et al describe a glass-ionomer bandage over the fracture surface, which needs no etching and gives adequate bond, and a calcium hydroxide or glass-ionomer liner for a deep fracture (2007, p.304). Where the fracture lies close to the pulp horn, a provisional restoration left in place for about one month lets hard tissue deposit in the pulp horn before final restoration (p.306). Restore the crown anatomy and the occlusion at once to prevent drift and over-eruption (p.302).

**Definitive restoration.** Options are reattachment of an intact fragment, composite resin, veneer or, rarely, a crown. Andreasen et al prefer reattachment or composite to a temporary crown, because the temporary crown can leak and admits bacteria (2007, p.302). For a 12-year-old, a conservative restoration is favoured because it can be modified as growth proceeds. Reattachment requires an intact fragment with most of the enamel margin present (p.306). Berman's guide describes both composite build-up and reattachment as standard options for enamel-dentin fractures without pulp involvement (n.d., p.27).

**Pulp monitoring.** The pulp is tested at baseline and again at six to eight weeks, with radiographs (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320). Necrosis after an uncomplicated crown fracture is reported in 0 to 6% for enamel-dentin fractures without exposure (p.320). Kohli and Lee recommend sensibility testing at 3, 6 and 12 months, then yearly for five years, where a pulp injury is present (2026, p.612-613). Monitoring is needed even when the fracture is uncomplicated, because a concomitant luxation may be missed (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.302).

**Concomitant injury.** Look for luxation. A luxation with mobility and bleeding from the sulcus makes a dry operating field hard to achieve, and Berman's guide recommends a temporary restoration in that case (n.d., p.29). Crown fractures without luxation rarely cause canal obliteration or root resorption (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.319).

**Follow-up for a growing child.** Review until skeletal growth is complete. Kohli and Lee note that the follow-up in children continues until growth is complete, and that the treatment options are re-discussed at that point (2026, p.612-613). Radiographs check root development and the position of the tooth.

**Sealing and aesthetics.** A sealed or restored fracture protects the pulp and gives interim aesthetics. Hexem and Eisler note that sealing lacks a strong evidence base but remains recommended in most circumstances (2026, Part 1, p.596).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim — deferring definitive treatment in a child without pulp exposure.** [Likely]
- *Textbook:* a provisional seal for about one month, and definitive treatment once the pulp is judged well (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306).
- *DCNA:* the definitive restoration may be deferred for seven to 10 days where extensive restoration is needed, and monitoring continues (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.611).
- *Agreement:* both delay definitive work while the pulp is judged, and both keep the tooth under review.
- *LIMITATION:* the best interval before definitive restoration in a child is not established in the sources read.

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** A maxillary central incisor fracture in a 12-year-old that does not expose the pulp is an uncomplicated crown fracture, Ellis Class 1 or 2 [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234; Sikri, n.d., p.484]. The aims are to seal the dentin, protect the pulp, restore form and function, and follow the child until growth is complete.

**Timing.** Andreasen et al treat crown fractures as a subacute or delayed approach, which allows a careful history and examination before definitive treatment [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.288]. Temporary measures are used where the fracture is close to the pulp horn [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306].

**History and examination.** Ask about the mechanism and time of injury, previous injuries, spontaneous pain, sensitivity to temperature, sweet or sour foods, pain on chewing, and any disturbance in the bite [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.289]. Examine the fracture extent, the pulp exposure, mobility, displacement and the alveolus. Test sensibility on all anterior teeth as a baseline [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. Take radiographs at more than one angle to show the pulp size, the root stage and any root fracture [Berman et al, n.d., p.74].

**Immediate care.** Smooth sharp edges. Seal the exposed dentin with a glass-ionomer bandage, which needs no etch and bonds adequately, or place a calcium hydroxide base where the dentin is deep [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.304; Harty's, n.d., p.254] [Likely]. Where the fracture lies close to the pulp horn, a temporary restoration left for about a month allows hard tissue to deposit in the pulp horn [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306]. Where the exposed dentin is within 0.5 mm of the pulp, the IADT places a calcium hydroxide lining before the glass-ionomer or bonded cover [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.319].

**Definitive restoration.** Reattachment of an intact fragment with most of the enamel margin present gives exact morphology, and bonding raises fracture strength about threefold [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.303, p.306]. If the fragment is lost or unsuitable, use composite, which is conservative and can be modified as the child grows. A temporary crown is not the first choice, because it can leak [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.302]. A full crown is not indicated for a small fracture in a child. Fragment reattachment with fibre reinforcement or a protective restoration has favourable survival in the 2026 review [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.606] [Likely].

**Pulp monitoring.** Enamel-dentin fracture without exposure carries a necrosis rate of 0 to 6% [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320]. Test sensibility at 2 to 4 and 6 to 8 weeks, then at 3, 6 and 12 months and yearly [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. A negative response early is not proof of necrosis, because normal blood flow can take up to nine months to return to the coronal pulp of a traumatised tooth [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. Radiographs check the root development and the periapex.

**Follow-up schedule.** Harty's follow-up table for an uncomplicated crown fracture sets clinical and radiographic review at six to eight weeks, four months, six months and one year [Harty's, n.d., p.264] [Likely]. Canal obliteration and root resorption are rare after an isolated crown fracture [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.319]. The IADT review for an uncomplicated crown fracture is at six to eight weeks and one year [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.319].

**Growing patient.** Keep the tooth and follow the child until skeletal growth ends. Hexem and Eisler stress preserving alveolar bone and gingiva in teenagers until maturity [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 1, p.589]. Delayed treatment can lead to avoidable tooth loss [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.603].

**Records and communication.** Record the baseline, the immediate and definitive treatment, and the follow-up plan. Explain the options and the review schedule to the parents and the child [Berman et al, n.d., p.181-182; Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.604].

**Enamel infraction and infraction lines.** An enamel infraction on its own needs no treatment. Andreasen et al recommend pulpal sensibility control after six to eight weeks, and sealing of the infraction lines with resin if they are symptomatic or stain [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320; p.301]. Berman's guide adds that infractions are easily disclosed by directing the light beam perpendicular to the tooth, so an examiner should look for them before reporting the fracture as complete [Berman et al, n.d., p.29].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Uncomplicated crown fracture: enamel-dentin, pulp intact.
- Seal dentin first: glass-ionomer bandage or liner.
- Provisional restoration about one month where the fracture is close to the pulp horn.
- Reattach an intact fragment; otherwise composite, which can be modified as the child grows.
- Pulp monitoring at six to eight weeks; necrosis 0-6%.
- Follow until growth is complete.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Leaving exposed dentin unsealed.
2. Using a full crown for a small fracture in a child.
3. Stopping review once the restoration is placed.
4. Missing a concomitant luxation.

## AUDIT NOTE (IADT 2020, checked 2026-10-19)

**Audit note (IADT 2020; Andreasen et al, 2002, checked 2026-10-19).** The IADT review interval for an uncomplicated crown fracture (six to eight weeks, one year) and the calcium hydroxide rule for dentin within 0.5 mm of the pulp are added (Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.319). The IADT radiograph recommendation is one parallel periapical view, with more only if other injuries are suspected (p.319). The treatment-delay review (Andreasen et al, 2002, printed p.116) gives a subacute or delayed approach for crown fractures, which is consistent with the exam text.
