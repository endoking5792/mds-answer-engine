## STUDY MODE

**Ellis Class III.** Ellis Class 3 is an extensive crown fracture involving considerable dentin and exposing the pulp (Sikri, n.d., p.484). The answer therefore has two parts: the pulp treatment, and the aesthetic restoration.

**Pulp first.** The pulp decision comes before the restoration. A vital, freshly exposed pulp is capped or partially pulpotomised, with calcium hydroxide or MTA over the exposure, to encourage a hard-tissue barrier (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.630). A necrotic pulp needs root canal treatment. The choice depends on exposure size, time since injury and root stage (Berman et al, n.d., p.34).

**Aesthetic restoration.** Composite resin is the usual choice. Andreasen et al describe bonding to enamel as a micromechanical mechanism and show that bonding to dentin requires careful technique, because the strength depends on the adhesive, the technique and polymerisation stress (2007, p.734). The durability of anterior composites in trauma is limited: in one study of 253 restorations, composite fracture occurred in 9 to 14% and most failures were marginal leakage, bulk discolouration and fracture (p.732-734).

**Shade and form.** The technique that offers the most predictable result, according to Berman et al, uses a silicone reference guide made from a diagnostic wax-up or the intact tooth (n.d., p.30). Hexem and Eisler note that a putty index or custom template can be used with equivalent likelihood of success, and that freehand placement is acceptable for interim restorations (2026, Part 1, p.596).

**Interim measures.** Glass-ionomer cover may be placed first, with composite built over it. Hexem and Eisler point out that sealing with composite allows monitoring for pulp necrosis and avoids over-treatment in the early stages (2026, Part 1, p.596).

**Follow-up.** Review the pulp and the restoration at intervals. A pulp decision that is capping or pulpotomy needs the three-year follow-up described by Cvek and cited by Berman et al (n.d., p.37).

**Immediate response.** Where the patient presents with an exposure, the first priority is to control bleeding gently with a saline-soaked cotton pellet or sponge under light pressure, and to protect the exposed pulp from further contamination until the definitive pulp treatment is done (Harty's, n.d., p.256). The exposed tissue remains vital for a long time if it is not contaminated, and the inflammation is usually limited to the superficial two to four millimetres (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612) [Likely].

## EXAM MODE

**Classification.** Ellis Class III is an extensive crown fracture with considerable dentin loss and pulp exposure [Sikri, n.d., p.484]. It is a complicated crown fracture in the WHO-based scheme [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234]. The pulp decision therefore comes first, and the aesthetic restoration follows it.

**Assessment before treatment.** Record the time since injury, the size and bleeding of the exposure, and the response of the pulp to thermal and electrical tests on all anterior teeth from canine to canine, so that later tests have a baseline [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. Look for a concomitant luxation, because it changes the moisture control and the timing [Berman et al, n.d., p.29].

**Pulp management.** A vital, freshly exposed pulp in a mature tooth is treated by partial pulpotomy as the first option [Harty's, n.d., p.257]. Harty's describes a shallow cavity of about 2 mm in the pulp chamber, cut with a high-speed diamond bur under copious irrigation, which is then filled with a pulp-capping material [Harty's, n.d., p.256]. Andreasen et al treat an exposed pulp by capping or partial pulpotomy when the pulp is vital and bleeds freshly, using calcium hydroxide or MTA over the exposure [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.630]. Where the pulp is necrotic, root canal treatment is needed. Where the whole clinical crown has been lost, the usual plan is pulpal extirpation, root canal filling and a post-retained crown [Harty's, n.d., p.257]. IADT: partial pulpotomy preferred in complete roots, with calcium silicate or non-setting calcium hydroxide [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.320].

**Sealing and liner.** Harty's advises a calcium hydroxide base on the deepest dentin, where the pulp horn can be seen through a thin layer, because it protects the underlying odontoblasts [Harty's, n.d., p.254] [Likely]. Calcium hydroxide liners break down under restorations over time, and bonding is preferred by Andreasen et al for the permanent seal [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.303] [Likely]. A resin-modified glass-ionomer dome may be placed over a calcium hydroxide cap, with composite over it [Harty's, n.d., p.256].

**Aesthetic restoration.** Composite is the usual choice. Harty's shows a chamfered, acid-etched fracture line restored with nano-filled composite under dental-dam isolation [Harty's, n.d., p.255]. Andreasen et al describe enamel bonding as micromechanical, and note that bonding to dentin depends on the adhesive, the technique and polymerisation stress [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.734]. A silicone reference guide gives the most predictable anterior result [Berman et al, n.d., p.30]. A putty index or custom template gives equivalent results, and freehand placement is acceptable for an interim restoration [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 1, p.596] [Likely].

**Durability.** The chief failure modes are marginal leakage, bulk discolouration and fracture of the composite. Andreasen et al cite composite fracture in 9 to 14% of restorations in one study [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.732-734]. Fibre reinforcement and protective restorations improve the survival of reattached fragments, according to the 2026 DCNA review [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.606] [Likely].

**Follow-up.** Test the pulp at 2 to 4 and 6 to 8 weeks, at 3, 6 and 12 months and then yearly [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. Harty's table of follow-up after complicated crown fractures sets clinical and radiographic review at intervals up to one year [Harty's, n.d., p.264] [Likely]. Cvek's protocol requires at least three years of follow-up after capping or pulpotomy [Berman et al, n.d., p.37].

**Records and consent.** Record the baseline, the chosen pulp treatment and the restorative plan, and explain the options and their likely longevity before treatment [Berman et al, n.d., p.181-182].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Ellis III: extensive crown fracture with pulp exposure.
- Pulp decision first: cap or pulpotomy if vital; RCT if necrotic.
- Composite with a silicone reference guide for predictable form.
- Composite failure: marginal leakage, bulk discolouration, fracture.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Restoring before deciding on the pulp.
2. Freehand composite as the only option without discussing a guide.
3. Forgetting bonding and occlusion as failure causes.
