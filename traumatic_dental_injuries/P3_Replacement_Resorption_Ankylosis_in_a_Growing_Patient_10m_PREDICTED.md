## STUDY MODE

**What it is.** Replacement resorption, or ankylosis, is the fusion of the root surface with alveolar bone when the periodontal ligament cover of the root is absent. It is one of the four periodontal healing modalities (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.467-470).

**Two patterns.** Progressive replacement resorption gradually resorbs the whole root. Transient replacement resorption forms an ankylosis that is later resorbed by adjacent vital PDL and disappears within the first year (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.469-472). Transient ankylosis is recognised by the disappearance of the ankylosis and a return of a normal percussion tone (p.472).

**Diagnosis.** Percussion is often the first sign: a high or metallic note, and immobility (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.472). Radiographs show loss of the periodontal space, but changes usually appear six months to a year after replantation, and sometimes two months (p.470). In children the ankylosed tooth often sits in infraposition (p.472).

**Risk.** Ankylosis follows periodontal damage, especially intrusion and lateral luxation with socket damage (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.412; Berman et al, n.d., p.84). Rigid splinting is not recommended (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858). After intrusion, the fusion of an unerupted tooth can begin within a few weeks (Berman et al, n.d., p.84).

**Management.** Harty's states that there is currently no treatment for replacement resorption (n.d., p.266). Kohli and Lee recommend that parents be told of the risk to the permanent tooth, and that in a growing patient infraocclusion from ankylosis may need decoronation to keep the alveolar height for later implant placement (2026, p.619-620). Monitoring is the main tool, with percussion and radiographs over time.

**Growth.** Because the tooth fails to erupt with its neighbours, the adjacent teeth tip and the alveolar height is lost. Early decoronation preserves the ridge for later prosthetic treatment (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.621).

## EXAM MODE

**Definition.** Replacement resorption, or ankylosis, is the fusion of root and alveolar bone when the periodontal ligament cover of the root has been lost. It is one of the four periodontal healing modalities after injury [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.467-470].

**Two courses.** Progressive replacement resorption gradually resorbs the whole root. Transient replacement resorption forms an ankylosis that is later resorbed by adjacent vital periodontal ligament, and it disappears within the first year [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.469-472]. Tunnelling resorption along the root-canal filling can accompany advanced ankylosis [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.470-471].

**Diagnosis.** Percussion is often the first sign, producing a high or metallic note and immobility [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.472]. Radiographs show loss of the periodontal space, but changes usually appear six months to a year after injury, and sometimes two months [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.470]. Berman's guide notes that after intrusion the fusion can begin within a few weeks [Berman et al, n.d., p.84]. In children the ankylosed tooth often sits in infraposition [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.472].

**Risk.** Ankylosis follows loss of the ligament cover, which is most likely after intrusion and lateral luxation with socket damage [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.412; Berman et al, n.d., p.84]. In primary teeth, ankylosis affected 3% of luxated teeth, mainly intruded ones, and spontaneous resorption by the erupting successor can occur [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.19, p.553].

**Management.** No treatment is currently available for established replacement resorption [Harty's, n.d., p.266]. Prevention, avoidance of rigid splinting and monitoring are the tools [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858]. In a growing patient, infraocclusion from ankylosis may need decoronation to keep alveolar height for later implant placement [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619-620]. The IADT advises that parents be told decoronation or autotransplantation may be needed later if the replanted tooth ankyloses [Fouad et al, 2020, p.335-336]. Crowder et al [2024] describe decoronation and root submergence in 37 teeth, mostly in case reports, with limited evidence (PDF p.1). Kohli and Lee report that root surface treatment with 2% sodium fluoride for 20 minutes may slow it [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619].

**Growing patients.** Tell the parents of the risk to the permanent tooth, monitor until growth is complete, and plan any replacement with the alveolar growth in mind [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619-620] [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Ankylosis: loss of PDL cover; fusion with bone.
- Progressive vs transient; transient disappears within the first year.
- Percussion first; radiographs at six months to one year.
- No treatment for established replacement resorption.
- Decoronation in growing patients to preserve the ridge.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Waiting for a radiograph before diagnosing; percussion comes first.
2. Calling replacement resorption treatable once established.
3. Forgetting the parents' role in monitoring.
