## STUDY MODE

**Definition.** Replantation is replacing a tooth in its socket after avulsion, deliberate or traumatic. Intentional replantation is the surgical or deliberate removal of a tooth and its replacement in the socket (Harty's, n.d., p.225). The treatment is done outside the mouth, with the root end prepared and filled before the tooth is replaced (Johnson et al, n.d., p.1502). It is distinct from transplantation, which moves a tooth to another socket (Harty's, n.d., p.225).

**Indications: the textbooks differ.**
- *Harty's* is strict. Calcified canals, separated instruments, unnegotiable canals, perforations and closeness to the mandibular canal are "not valid indications". The only true indication is when "there is absolutely no other treatment available to maintain a strategic tooth" (Harty's, n.d., p.225-226).
- *Cohen's* is broader. Intentional replantation "may be an option when surgical access is limited or presents unacceptable risks". Mandibular second molars are the common example, because of thick buccal bone, shallow vestibular depth and root apices close to the mandibular canal. "Any tooth that can be atraumatically removed in one piece is a potential candidate" (Johnson et al, n.d., p.1500).

**Contraindications.** Flared or moderately curved roots and periodontal disease (Johnson et al, n.d., p.1500). Vertical root fracture has often been treated as a contraindication, although moderate success has been reported with dentine-bonded resin, with better results for incisors and for fractures under two-thirds of root length (Johnson et al, n.d., p.1500).

**Technique.** Minimise extraoral time, because prognosis depends mainly on avoiding trauma to the PDL and cementum during extraction and on keeping extraoral time short (Johnson et al, n.d., p.1502). Avoid elevators, and do not grip the root with forceps. Arrange all instruments for root-end preparation and filling before extraction. Keep the root moist with gauze soaked in a physiologic solution such as HBSS. Prepare and fill the root end, replant, and compress the buccal bone (Johnson et al, n.d., p.1502). Remove all debris from within the tooth once the decision is made (Harty's, n.d., p.226).

**Prognosis.** Resorption, inflammatory and replacement, decides the outcome. Long-term studies give mixed results, with 50% to 60% success over 5 to 10 years (Harty's, n.d., p.227).

**Opposing view.** Harty's bibliography lists "The case against intentional replantation" by Weine (Harty's, n.d., p.234). The title is cited here only to show that the opposing argument exists; its content is not in the extracted text.

## EXAM MODE

**Definition.** Intentional replantation is the surgical or deliberate removal of a tooth, treatment of the root outside the mouth, and replacement in its socket [Harty's, n.d., p.225; Johnson et al, n.d., p.225].

**Indications.** The sources differ in breadth. Harty's is strict: calcified canals, separated instruments, unnegotiable canals, perforations and closeness to the mandibular canal are not valid indications, and the only true indication is when no other treatment can maintain a strategic tooth [Harty's, n.d., p.225-226]. Cohen's is broader: intentional replantation may be an option where surgical access is limited or carries unacceptable risk, and any tooth that can be removed atraumatically in one piece is a potential candidate [Johnson et al, n.d., p.1500] [Likely]. The mandibular second molar is the common example, because of thick buccal bone, a shallow vestibule and root apices close to the mandibular canal [Johnson et al, n.d., p.1500].

**Contraindications.** Flared or moderately curved roots, and periodontal disease [Johnson et al, n.d., p.1500]. Vertical root fracture has often been considered a contraindication, although moderate success has been reported with dentine-bonded resin, better for incisors and for fractures less than two-thirds of root length [Johnson et al, n.d., p.1500] [Likely].

**Technique.** Keep the root moist with gauze soaked in a physiological solution such as HBSS. Arrange all instruments for root-end preparation and filling before extraction, to minimise extraoral time. Avoid elevators and do not grip the root with forceps. Prepare and fill the root end, replant, and compress the buccal bone [Johnson et al, n.d., p.1502]. Remove all tissue debris from within the tooth once the decision is made [Harty's, n.d., p.226].

**Pulpal reactions.** Pulpal reactions seen in human patients after intentional replantation support part of the experimental classification of pulp responses, though not all reactions are confirmed in humans [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.463] [Likely].

**Prognosis.** Long-term studies give mixed results, with 50 to 60% success over five to 10 years. Inflammatory and replacement resorption decide the outcome [Harty's, n.d., p.227]. Harty's bibliography cites an opposing view [Harty's, n.d., p.234].

**Case selection and consent.** Harty's weighs the tooth's strategic value, arch continuity, occlusion and restorability, and the patient is told the sequelae, prognosis and alternatives before consent [Harty's, n.d., p.225-226].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Intentional replantation: deliberate extraction, extraoral treatment, replacement.
- Harty's: strict indication, no other treatment for a strategic tooth.
- Cohen's: limited access or unacceptable risk; mandibular second molar typical.
- Contraindications: flared or curved roots, periodontal disease.
- Keep the root moist in HBSS; minimise extraoral time.
- Prognosis: 50-60% success over 5-10 years (mixed).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Confusing intentional replantation with transplantation or with avulsion replantation.
2. Listing calcified canals or perforations as indications.
3. Giving the mandibular second molar as the only example without noting Harty's strict indication.
4. Forgetting the extraoral working-time minimisation.
5. Quoting a success rate without its 5 to 10 year window.
