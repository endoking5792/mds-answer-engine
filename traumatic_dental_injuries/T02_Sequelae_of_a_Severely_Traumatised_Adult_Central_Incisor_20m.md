# T02 — Sequelae of a severely traumatised adult central incisor that is not fractured

**Marks:** 20 (Essay)
**Repository sitting:** Mar 2007 (END §14, PIII 07-15)
**Question wording (repository):** "Classify traumatic injuries to teeth. What are the possible sequelae of a severely traumatized adult central incisor which is not fractured? Discuss emergency and long term treatment."
**Status:** DRAFTED, audited against IADT 2020 on 2026-10-19 (see audit note).

**Interpretation of "severely traumatised, not fractured".** Without a fracture, a severe injury to an adult central incisor means a luxation: extrusive, lateral (with or without apical displacement) or intrusive. Avulsion is treated in T13–T17. The answer assumes a luxation and states this.

## STUDY MODE

**Why a non-fractured incisor can be seriously damaged.** A luxation injury damages the neurovascular bundle at the apex and the periodontal ligament, and sometimes the socket. The sequelae depend on the type of luxation, the stage of root development and how the tooth was managed (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.412). The main sequelae are pulp necrosis, pulp canal obliteration, root resorption of three kinds, loss of supporting bone and, in growing patients, ankylosis.

**Pulp necrosis.** Necrosis is the most important sequel. It follows extrusion in about 26%, lateral luxation in about 58% and intrusion in about 85% of teeth. Fully formed roots carry the greater risk (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.394). Necrosis can be silent: it is usually asymptomatic after luxation, so diagnosis rests on clinical and radiographic signs (p.394). Discolouration and periapical change, together with negative sensibility, are the usual signs (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610-611).

**Pulp canal obliteration.** Canal obliteration is a healing response in which the canal narrows with hard tissue. It is reported in 3 to 35% of luxated teeth, and is more common in mobile or displaced teeth with immature roots (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.413). A late complication is necrosis with periapical change, which Andreasen et al report in 7 to 16% of obliterated teeth (p.401).

**Root resorption.** Three types matter. Surface (repair-related) resorption is common and usually self-limiting; it is 1 to 18% overall and highest after intrusion. Inflammatory (infection-related) resorption follows pulp necrosis and needs urgent endodontic treatment with calcium hydroxide. Replacement resorption (ankylosis) is progressive and irreversible once established, and is the most serious long-term sequel in growing patients (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.412, p.466-472; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619).

**Loss of supporting bone.** Marginal bone is lost in about 31% of intruded teeth, 7% of lateral luxations, 6% of extrusions and 1% of subluxations (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.411). Delayed reduction and older age increase the loss (p.411).

**Colour change and root growth.** Crown discolouration usually follows pulp necrosis, but it can precede necrosis and is not diagnostic on its own (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.394). In immature roots, necrosis halts root development, which is why the root-stage question dominates treatment (Berman et al, n.d., p.74).

**Emergency treatment.** The emergency priorities are to exclude serious head and neck injury, reposition the tooth under local anaesthesia, splint it, and record a baseline. Lateral luxation with apical displacement needs local anaesthesia and controlled repositioning, with the apex released from the cortical plate (Berman et al, n.d., p.80). Extrusion is repositioned gently with axial pressure on the incisal edge (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.431). Intrusion is managed by waiting for spontaneous re-eruption in immature roots; mature roots, multiple intrusions and deep intrusions need surgical repositioning or orthodontic extrusion (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.458).

**Splinting.** A flexible or semi-rigid splint is used for luxation. Andreasen gives two to three weeks for extrusion and about three weeks for lateral luxation (six to eight weeks if bone breaks down). Ogle reports the IADT figures as two weeks for extrusion and four weeks for lateral and intrusive luxation (2024, p.158). The durations differ and both are shown.

**Endodontic decision.** Observation is acceptable for selected low-risk teeth for up to a year, when the patient is over 10, the root is complete, no intrusion has occurred and transient apical change is expected (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.399). Root canal treatment follows radiolucency, inflammatory resorption or discolouration (p.459). Kohli and Lee advise treatment once two signs of necrosis are present, and calcium hydroxide within seven to 10 days for a closed-apex tooth with necrosis (2026, p.610, p.619).

**Long-term treatment.** Replacement resorption has no reliable treatment once established; fluoride may slow it (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619), so management is prevention and early detection: avoid rigid splinting, time the endodontics to the resorption risk, and watch for infraocclusion (Harty's, n.d., p.266; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619-620). Where ankylosis causes infraocclusion in a growing patient, decoronation may maintain alveolar height for later implant placement (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619-620). Canal obliteration without necrosis can be a favourable healing response, so it is monitored rather than treated (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.616).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim — when to root-treat a severely luxated but vital-looking tooth.** [Likely]
- *Textbook:* Andreasen et al accept observation for up to a year in selected teeth, and treat on radiographic or clinical signs (2007, p.399, p.459).
- *DCNA:* Kohli and Lee treat on two signs and recommend root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide for a closed-apex necrotic tooth within seven to 10 days (2026, p.610, p.619).
- *Agreement and challenge:* the diagnostic principle is shared, but the threshold for acting differs.
- *LIMITATION:* neither source offers a trial comparing the two approaches; the decision remains a clinical judgement based on the sources' criteria.

**Kohli and Lee on ankylosis.** Kohli and Lee call ankylosis an acceptable outcome in some growing patients (2026, p.619), which differs from the textbook view that replacement resorption is the most serious sequel (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.412). The conflict should be stated rather than resolved.

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** A severely traumatised adult central incisor without a fracture has most likely sustained an extrusive, lateral or intrusive luxation. Avulsion is answered separately. The sequelae are pulp necrosis, canal obliteration, three kinds of root resorption, marginal bone loss and, in some teeth, ankylosis. The emergency response and the long-term plan are both needed [Andreasen et al, 2007; Kohli and Lee, 2026].

**Sequelae.** Pulp necrosis follows extrusion in about 26%, lateral luxation in about 58% and intrusion in about 85% of teeth, and is more common with fully formed roots [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.394]. Canal obliteration occurs in 3 to 35% of luxated teeth and can precede late necrosis in 7 to 16% [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.401, p.413]. Surface resorption is common and often self-limiting. Inflammatory resorption follows infection and needs urgent endodontic treatment. Replacement resorption is progressive once established. Marginal bone is lost in about 31% of intruded teeth and 7% of laterally luxated teeth [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.411].

**Diagnosis at the first visit.** Establish a baseline before any treatment. Test thermal and electrical responses on all anterior teeth from canine to canine, including the uninjured neighbours, and repeat the tests at 2 to 4 and 6 to 8 weeks, at 3, 6 and 12 months and yearly [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. A negative response early does not confirm necrosis, because normal blood flow can take up to nine months to return to a traumatised coronal pulp [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. Radiographs at more than one angle show the apical position and the width of the periodontal space. A displaced or wedged apex suggests lateral luxation with apical displacement [Berman et al, n.d., p.74-75].

**Emergency treatment.** Exclude serious head and neck injury first. Reposition the tooth under local anaesthesia. For extrusion, use gentle axial pressure on the incisal edge. For lateral luxation with apical displacement, release the apex from the labial plate and reposition. For intrusion, do not force the tooth: spontaneous re-eruption is expected in immature roots, while mature roots, multiple intrusions and deep intrusions need surgical repositioning or orthodontic extrusion [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.458, p.458; Berman et al, n.d., p.80, p.84].

**Splinting.** Use a passive flexible splint for the IADT period for each injury [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.323-328]: up to two weeks for subluxation only if mobility is excessive; two weeks for extrusion, plus four if marginal bone breaks down; four weeks for lateral luxation and after surgical repositioning of an intruded tooth. Andreasen et al give about three weeks for lateral luxation [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.442]. Rigid splints increase resorption (p.858).

**Endodontic decision.** Observation is acceptable for selected low-risk teeth for up to a year: the patient is over 10, the root is complete, there was no intrusion, and transient apical change is expected [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.399]. Treatment follows radiolucency, inflammatory resorption or discolouration [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.459]. Kohli and Lee treat once two signs of necrosis are present, and recommend root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide within seven to 10 days for a closed-apex tooth with necrosis [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610, p.619]. The two positions differ in threshold, and both are stated. IADT: intracanal calcium hydroxide 1 to 2 weeks after trauma, for up to one month, in intruded, severely extruded or laterally luxated fully developed teeth [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.317].

**Long-term complications.** Replacement resorption has no reliable treatment once established; fluoride may slow it [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619], so prevention is the aim: avoid rigid splinting, time endodontic treatment to the resorption risk, and check for infraocclusion [Harty's, n.d., p.266; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619-620]. In a growing patient, decoronation may maintain alveolar height for later implant placement [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619-620]. Canal obliteration without necrosis can be a favourable healing response and is monitored rather than treated [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.616]. The IADT notes high canal obliteration rates after extrusion, intrusion and lateral luxation (p.317); Abreu et al [2024] pool 27.6% in permanent teeth (PDF p.1).

**Follow-up.** Kohli and Lee recommend review at one week, six to eight weeks, six months and one year, then annually for five years, with sensibility at each visit [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.616]. Radiographs at two and three weeks show early resorption [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477]. IADT reviews for extrusive and lateral luxation: 2, 4, 8 and 12 weeks, then 6 months and one year [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.326-327].

**Conclusion.** Reposition early, splint flexibly for the period appropriate to the injury, decide on endodontic treatment by the balance of signs, and monitor for at least five years for necrosis, canal obliteration and replacement resorption.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Sequelae: pulp necrosis, canal obliteration, three kinds of resorption, bone loss, ankylosis.
- Necrosis by type: extrusion 26%, lateral 58%, intrusion 85%.
- Canal obliteration 3-35%; late necrosis 7-16%.
- Replacement resorption: no effective treatment once established; prevention matters.
- Splint flexibly; durations differ between sources.
- Observation up to a year in selected low-risk teeth; treat on signs.
- Monitor for at least five years.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Forgetting replacement resorption, which is the most serious long-term sequel in growing patients.
2. Treating canal obliteration as necrosis.
3. Quoting one splint duration for all luxations.
4. Not stating the five-year follow-up.
