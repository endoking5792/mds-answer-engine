## STUDY MODE

**Part 1: classification.** Traumatic dental injuries are classified in two complementary ways. The WHO-based clinical classification used by Andreasen et al groups them by tissue: hard tissue and pulp (enamel infraction, enamel fracture, enamel-dentin fracture, complicated and uncomplicated crown and crown-root fractures, root fracture), periodontal tissues (concussion, subluxation, luxation, avulsion) and supporting bone and soft tissues (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.233-234). Root fracture appears in that table as a fracture of dentin, cementum and pulp, and is subdivided by displacement of the coronal fragment under the luxation headings (p.234). The Ellis system gives class 6 for fracture of the root, with or without loss of crown (Sikri, n.d., p.484). Sikri also lists the root-fracture systems of Heithersay and Morile (five classes) and Feiglin (three zones for transverse fractures) (p.483). The clinically important division for a root fracture is by level and by healing outcome, set out below.

**Part 2: horizontal root fracture.** A horizontal root fracture crosses the root at right angles to its long axis. Root fractures make up 0.5 to 7% of injuries to the permanent dentition, and most occur in the apical or middle third in older studies, with more recent studies showing the middle third as most frequent (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.352-355).

**Diagnosis.** Root fractures escape the first radiograph in some cases and show later, so repeat films are needed (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.355). Take periapical radiographs at two angles, with a change of about 15 degrees in each direction, to show a fracture that is not visible on a single view (p.355). A horizontal fracture is often associated with luxation or extrusion of the coronal fragment, and the sensibility response and mobility of the fragment are recorded.

**Healing outcomes.** Four healing patterns are described (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.358-368):
- *calcified healing:* hard tissue bridges the fragments, sometimes with partial canal obliteration in the fracture region and apical fragment;
- *connective tissue interposition:* the fragments are separated by PDL-like tissue, and resorption at the fracture is common;
- *bone and connective tissue interposition:* bone grows into the gap, and the fracture line widens radiographically;
- *non-healing:* granulation tissue, often from infection in the avascular coronal pulp, separates the fragments and often leads to a periradicular radiolucency.

Hard-tissue union cannot be diagnosed radiographically before three months (p.358). Pulp canal obliteration is common in healing fragments, in 69 to 73% of teeth in clinical studies (p.379).

**Management.** The core principles are complete repositioning and firm, passive, immobile splinting until a hard-tissue callus has formed, which is usually about three months (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.369). Rigid splints gave the poorest results, and the length of splinting was not significant in one study, with four weeks considered acceptable (p.377). Ogle, citing the IADT, gives four weeks for root fracture and four months for a root fracture in the cervical third (2024, p.158).

Immature teeth with incomplete root fractures need no fixation and heal by hard-tissue union, although they may be splinted if other teeth are injured (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.368). A fracture in the cervical third close to or communicating with the gingival crevice heals less well; a conservative approach is still justified for fractures below the alveolar crest, with splinting of the coronal fragment to neighbouring teeth where the fracture is close to the gingival margin (p.368-369). Orthodontic bands on the coronal fragment are contraindicated because they injure the traumatised pulp (p.368).

**Pulp management during healing.** Test sensibility and radiograph the tooth at intervals. Pulp necrosis calls for root canal treatment of the coronal fragment, the apical fragment, or both, depending on the findings, and the same options apply as for T08 (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.355-368). Follow-up is at least several years, because healing can take years (p.358).

**Extraction.** Where the tooth cannot be saved, a careless extraction damages the alveolar process and can lead to severe atrophy, especially labio-lingually, compromising later aesthetic treatment (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.369).

## EXAM MODE

**Classification.** Injuries are classified in two complementary ways. The WHO-based clinical classification places root fracture among hard-tissue injuries, as a fracture of dentin, cementum and pulp [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234]. Sikri lists the Ellis class for root fracture as Class 6, with or without loss of crown, and also notes the root-fracture systems of Heithersay and Morile (five classes) and Feiglin (three zones for transverse fractures) [Sikri, n.d., p.483-484]. Root fractures make up 0.5 to 7% of injuries to the permanent dentition [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.353].

**Horizontal root fracture.** A horizontal fracture runs across the root at right angles to its long axis. Berman's guide describes the more horizontal fracture as more visible on a radiograph, and notes that the coronal fragment is often displaced and mobile after a few days, which makes the fracture more visible [Berman et al, n.d., p.52]. Fractures in the apical or middle third are associated with less coronal mobility and a more favourable course [Berman et al, n.d., p.58].

**Diagnosis.** Take one parallel periapical radiograph, two further radiographs at different vertical or horizontal angulations, and an occlusal view, as the IADT lists for luxation and root injuries [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.321, p.326]. Berman's guide recommends two or three radiographs at different vertical angulations [Berman et al, n.d., p.52]. Fractures can be missed on the first film and seen later [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.355]. CBCT is useful for root fractures where it is available [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.316].

**Prognosis by level.** Berman's guide records that the more apical the fracture, the less likely the pulp is to become non-vital. Coronal-third fractures have the worst prognosis, and in one series 44% of more horizontal fractures had to be extracted, against 8% of oblique coronal fractures [Berman et al, n.d., p.58]. Apical fractures have a more favourable prognosis, with success reported in the range of 24 to 34% in the cited series [Berman et al, n.d., p.59] [Likely].

**Healing.** Four healing patterns are described: calcified healing, connective tissue interposition, bone and connective tissue interposition, and non-healing. Hard-tissue union cannot be diagnosed before three months [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.358-368]. Pulp canal obliteration is common in healing fragments, in 69 to 73% of teeth in clinical studies [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.379].

**Management.** Reposition the coronal fragment completely under local anaesthesia and apply a passive flexible splint for four weeks, extended for a cervical fracture for up to four months [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.323]. Root canal treatment is contraindicated at the emergency visit [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.324]. Andreasen et al describe splinting until a hard-tissue callus forms, usually about three months, and report that rigid splints give the poorest results [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.369, p.377].

**Splint technique.** Avoid orthodontic bands on the coronal fragment, because they injure the already traumatised pulp. Use a passive splint that holds the fragments without forcing them [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.368]. Immature teeth with incomplete root fractures need no fixation and heal by hard-tissue union [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.368].

**Pulp management.** Test sensibility and radiograph the tooth at intervals. If the pulp becomes necrotic, treat the coronal fragment, the apical fragment, or both, as the findings dictate [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.355-368]. Calcium hydroxide and gutta-percha filling may be used for the coronal fragment once a hard-tissue barrier has formed apically [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.628-629].

**Extraction.** If the tooth cannot be saved, the extraction must be atraumatic, because careless extraction damages the alveolar process and can cause severe labio-lingual atrophy that compromises later aesthetic treatment [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.369].

**Follow-up.** Review clinically and radiographically at four weeks (splint removal), six to eight weeks, four months and six months, then at one year and yearly for at least five years [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.323].

**Diastasis.** The gap between the fragments, the diastasis, matters for healing. A larger diastasis favours necrosis of the coronal fragment and increases its mobility, which reduces the chance of healing [Berman et al, n.d., p.58]. The size of the diastasis is therefore recorded at each review and is a reason for careful repositioning [Berman et al, n.d., p.58] [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Root fracture: fracture of dentin, cementum and pulp (WHO table).
- Two angled radiographs (about 15 degrees each way); repeat films.
- Four healing patterns; non-healing usually infection.
- Complete repositioning and passive, immobile splint until callus (about 3 months).
- Rigid splints give poorest results; banding harms the pulp.
- Immature incomplete fractures need no fixation.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Relying on one radiograph to exclude root fracture.
2. Using rigid or orthodontic-band splints.
3. Quoting one healing outcome for all fractures.
4. Stopping follow-up too early.
