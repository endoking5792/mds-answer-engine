# T03 — Root healing following traumatic injuries

**Marks:** 20 (Essay)
**Repository sitting:** Jun 2017 (END §14, PIII 16-21)
**Question wording (repository):** "Root Healing following Traumatic injuries."
**Status:** DRAFTED, audited against IADT 2020 on 2026-10-19 (see audit note).

**Interpretation.** "Root healing" is read here as healing of the root's supporting and internal tissues after injury: the periodontal ligament and root surface, the dentine and canal, and the healing of root fractures. Pulp healing is included because the pulp determines the internal root.

## STUDY MODE

**Healing is a race between repair and infection.** Injury to the root damages the periodontal ligament (PDL), the root surface and the pulp. Healing depends on whether the injured surface is repaired by cells of the PDL, is replaced by bone, or is attacked by infection (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.466-473). The outcome is therefore a balance between the vitality of the surviving cells and the presence of bacteria at the injured surface.

**Periodontal healing: four modalities.** Histologically, replanted and injured teeth show four periodontal healing patterns (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.466).

*Normal PDL healing.* The PDL regenerates completely, usually in about four weeks including the nerve supply. It requires the innermost layer of cells on the root surface to be vital. After a true avulsion this is unlikely, because the injury damages that layer at least minimally (p.466). Radiographically the periodontal space is normal and there is no root resorption (p.466).

*Surface (repair-related) resorption.* Shallow lacunae are repaired by new cementum. The process is superficial, not progressive and self-limiting. It is usually invisible on radiographs because the lesions are small, and the tooth stays in position with normal percussion (p.467). Resorption lacunae of this type appear on non-traumatised teeth too, so their presence alone does not signal failure (p.467).

*Ankylosis (replacement resorption).* The root surface fuses with alveolar bone. It arises when the PDL cover of the root is absent, and it is progressive or transient depending on how much PDL survives (p.469-470). Radiographic signs usually appear at six months to a year, and percussion may show it earlier as a high note (p.470, p.472). Transient ankylosis can disappear within the first year, as adjacent vital PDL remodels the area (p.472). Progressive ankylosis gradually replaces the root with bone, and in children the tooth often sits in infraposition (p.470, p.472).

*Inflammatory (infection-related) resorption.* Bowl-shaped cavities in cementum and dentine are associated with inflammation in the adjacent PDL. It starts when small resorption cavities expose dentine tubules and infected necrotic pulp tissue releases toxins along the tubules. It is particularly frequent and aggressive after replantation in patients aged 6 to 10 years, and is first seen radiographically as early as two weeks after replantation (p.473). Simultaneous inflammatory and replacement resorption can occur (p.473).

**Pulpal healing.** The pulp responds to injury in one of several ways. In experimental replantation, pulpal reactions include regular tubular reparative dentine, irregular reparative dentine, osteodentine, immature bone, internal resorption and necrosis. The authors note that not all of these reactions have been confirmed in humans, although pulpal reactions after intentional replantation support part of the classification (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.462-463). A crown fracture with exposure heals if the vascular supply is intact and bacteria are kept out. Inflammation is then transient, and superficial in the first days (p.301). In luxation, the pulp can heal, revascularise, become partially necrotic or become totally necrotic, depending on the degree of interruption to its blood supply (p.412).

**Revascularisation.** In an immature tooth with an open apex, the pulp can re-vascularise after replantation. The chance depends on the size of the apical foramen and the length of the canal (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.484-485). Harty's reports revascularisation in 18 to 40% of open-apex teeth with a foramen larger than 1.1 mm (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, n.d., p.262). Revascularisation is more likely when the injury is treated early and the canal is not heavily contaminated (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.482).

**Root fracture healing.** Healing after a root fracture takes one of four routes: calcified healing with a dentinal callus across the fracture, interposition of connective tissue, interposition of bone and connective tissue, or non-healing with granulation tissue between the fragments (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.358-368). Hard-tissue union cannot be diagnosed radiographically before three months and may take years (p.358). Non-healing typically results from infection at the fracture line in the avascular coronal pulp (p.359).

**Pulp canal obliteration as healing.** Canal obliteration is a healing response in which hard tissue narrows the canal. It occurs in 3 to 35% of luxated teeth. It is compatible with pulp survival, and Kohli and Lee list it among favourable outcomes (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.413; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.616).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim — healing of the periodontal surface is largely governed by infection and by the survival of the innermost PDL cells.** [Likely]
- *Textbook:* Andreasen et al describe the four periodontal modalities as histological findings from experimental and human material, with normal healing unlikely after avulsion (2007, p.466).
- *DCNA:* Kohli and Lee sort outcomes into favourable and unfavourable, with inflammatory and replacement resorption as the unfavourable ones (2026, p.619).
- *Agreement:* both rest on the same two drivers: vital PDL cells and absence of infection.
- *LIMITATION:* the histological classification comes from animal and human case material of differing size; the relative frequencies of the modalities in humans are not reliably quantified in the sources read.

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** Root healing after trauma involves the periodontal ligament, the root surface, the pulp and, in fractures, the fragments. Healing may be complete, or may show surface resorption, ankylosis, inflammatory resorption or non-healing. Which pathway occurs depends on the survival of the PDL cells, the presence of infection and the stage of root development [Andreasen et al, 2007].

**Periodontal healing.** The four modalities are summarised below.

| Modality | Histology | Radiograph | Clinical sign | Usual course |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Normal PDL healing | full PDL regeneration in about four weeks | normal periodontal space, no resorption | normal position and percussion | requires vital innermost PDL; unlikely after avulsion |
| Surface (repair) resorption | shallow lacunae repaired with cementum | usually not visible | normal percussion | self-limiting, not progressive |
| Ankylosis (replacement) | fusion of bone and root surface | loss of periodontal space, seen at 6 months to 1 year | high percussion note, immobility | transient or progressive |
| Inflammatory (infection) | bowl-shaped cavities with inflammation | radiolucent cavities, first at two weeks in some | variable | aggressive in ages 6-10; needs endodontics |

[Andreasen et al, 2007, p.466-473]

**Pulpal healing.** The pulp responds by reparative dentine, osteodentine, immature bone, internal resorption or necrosis in experimental replantation, with the caution that human confirmation is incomplete (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.462-463). After a crown fracture with exposure, transient inflammation settles if the vascular supply stays intact and bacteria are excluded [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.301]. After luxation the pulp may heal, revascularise, or become partly or totally necrotic, depending on the interruption to its supply [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.412].

**Revascularisation.** In open-apex teeth, revascularisation after replantation depends on the foramen size and the canal length, and has been reported in 18 to 40% of teeth with a foramen larger than 1.1 mm [Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, n.d., p.262; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, n.d., p.262]. Early treatment and low contamination improve the chance [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.482].

**Root fracture healing.** Four routes are recognised: calcified healing with dentinal callus, connective tissue interposition, bone and connective tissue interposition, and non-healing. Hard-tissue union cannot be confirmed earlier than three months. Infection of the coronal pulp at the fracture line causes non-healing [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.358-368].

**Canal obliteration.** Canal obliteration is a healing response that occurs in 3 to 35% of luxated teeth. It is compatible with pulp survival and is listed among favourable outcomes, but it can precede late necrosis in 7 to 16% of cases [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.401, p.413]. The IADT says pulp canal obliteration is expected and can be recognised radiographically during the first year after injury [Fouad et al, 2020, p.338]. A 2024 meta-analysis gives a pooled prevalence of 27.6% in permanent teeth, with root fractures highest at 78.6% and concussion at 45.2% with high heterogeneity [Abreu et al, 2024, PDF p.1, p.6].

**Factors affecting healing.** The stage of root development is the strongest determinant of pulp healing. Dry time over 15 minutes, contamination and crown damage make resorption more likely and earlier. Rigid splinting harms healing, and the gingival fibres bond within about a week [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.481, p.865].

**Outcomes.** Favourable outcomes are normal PDL healing, continued root development, absence of resorption, and positive sensibility or canal obliteration. Unfavourable outcomes are aggressive inflammatory resorption, which needs immediate endodontic intervention, and replacement resorption with the risk of infraocclusion [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619].

**Why the distinction matters.** Surface resorption and transient ankylosis are the healing types that can be left alone, because they are self-limiting or resolve as adjacent PDL remodels the area. Inflammatory resorption and progressive replacement resorption are the types that damage the root and need action. The examiner should therefore name the type first, then the treatment: observation and radiographs for surface resorption, endodontic treatment with calcium hydroxide for inflammatory resorption, and early attention to the position of the tooth in a growing patient for ankylosis [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.467-473; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619].

**Clinical implications.** Monitor radiographically at two to three weeks and at longer intervals, because inflammatory resorption can appear early and replacement resorption can take six months to a year to show. Treat infection early, with calcium hydroxide where resorption is inflammatory. Splint briefly and flexibly. Decide on endodontics on the pulp signs, not on a single test.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Four periodontal modalities: normal, surface, ankylosis, inflammatory.
- Ankylosis shows on radiographs 6 months to 1 year; percussion can show it earlier.
- Inflammatory resorption is aggressive in ages 6-10; treat with endodontics.
- Revascularisation in 18-40% of open apices with foramen above 1.1 mm.
- Root fracture healing: calcified, connective, bone, or non-healing; union not before 3 months.
- Canal obliteration is a healing response, monitored not treated.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling all resorption the same; surface resorption is self-limiting and needs no treatment.
2. Missing ankylosis because the radiograph is taken too early.
3. Describing normal PDL healing as the expected result of avulsion.
4. Forgetting that inflammatory resorption needs endodontic treatment.
