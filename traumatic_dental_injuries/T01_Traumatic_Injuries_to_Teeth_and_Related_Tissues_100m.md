## STUDY MODE

**1. Scope and definition.** Traumatic dental injuries (TDI) are injuries to the teeth and their supporting tissues caused by a sudden physical impact. They are classified by the anatomical site of the damage and by any change in the tooth's position in its socket (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.609-610). The WHO-based clinical classification in Andreasen et al groups injuries into injuries to the hard tissues and pulp (enamel infraction, enamel fracture, enamel-dentin fracture, complicated crown fracture, crown-root fractures, root fracture), injuries to the periodontal tissues (concussion, subluxation, extrusive and lateral luxation, intrusive luxation, avulsion), and injuries to the supporting bone and the gingiva or oral mucosa (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.233-234). The Ellis classification used in older examinations groups crown fractures by severity and adds non-vital teeth, avulsion, root fracture and displacement without fracture as separate classes (Sikri, n.d., p.484). The two systems answer different questions. The WHO scheme is designed for clinical and epidemiological recording; Ellis is a grading system for crown fractures.

**2. Epidemiology.** Dental injuries are among the most common injuries to the head and neck. Oral injuries accounted for about 5% of all injuries in a Swedish population survey, although the oral region is about 1% of body area (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.239). Maxillofacial trauma makes up 9 to 33% of trauma seen in accident departments, depending on the setting (p.240). Prevalence in the permanent dentition is high: one in five British children has had a TDI to the permanent anterior teeth by the time they leave school (p.240). Boys are injured about twice as often as girls in the permanent dentition, a difference linked to contact sport, although the gap appears to be narrowing (p.242). Between 71 and 92% of lifetime TDI occur before the age of 19 (p.242). A first injury raises the risk of a second, with the risk of a second episode put at eight times higher after a first (p.241). Kohli and Lee put the proportion of adults who have had trauma to permanent teeth at about one third, and the peak incidence in childhood at 7 to 11 years (2026, p.609). Ogle reports that about one in ten children aged 2 to 18 has had significant tooth trauma (2024, p.151). The sources agree that trauma is common and concentrated in youth, but the prevalence figures vary with the population and the method, and the epidemiology is weak in many countries (Ogle, 2024, p.151-152).

**3. Aetiology and risk factors.** The main causes are falls and collisions, physical and leisure activities, traffic accidents, and inappropriate use of teeth such as biting hard objects (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.245-248). Intentional injury, including child physical abuse, is recognised (p.249). Iatrogenic injury during treatment is also documented (p.250). Protrusion of the maxillary incisors raises the risk: children with protruding incisors are about twice as likely to be injured as those with normal occlusion, and an overjet above 6 mm roughly doubles the extent of injury in some series (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.243; Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.24, p.685). Inadequate lip coverage has been linked to a threefold increase in risk for the maxillary incisors, although later work found the association weaker once the environment was controlled (p.243).

**4. Which teeth and which injuries.** The maxillary central incisors are the most often injured teeth in both dentitions, and the mandibular incisors come next (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.243; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.609). The commonest injury to permanent teeth is enamel fracture, followed by enamel-dentin fracture (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.243). Crown fractures make up 26 to 76% of dental injuries in the permanent dentition (p.319). Luxation injuries are 15 to 40% in the summary, or 15 to 61% in the text (p.388, p.411). Root fractures are uncommon, at 0.5 to 7% of injuries (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.353). Avulsion is 0.5 to 3% of injuries (p.460).

**5. Diagnosis.** The examination follows a sequence. The clinician first excludes serious injury to the head, neck and central nervous system, and refers at once where the mechanism suggests more force than the dental damage (Berman et al, n.d., p.74). The history records the time, the mechanism and any prior injury. The examination covers the soft tissues, the alveolus, the teeth (direction of displacement, mobility, percussion response, sensibility), and the occlusion (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.9; Berman et al, n.d., Ch.2). Radiographs are taken at several angles, because one view can miss a root fracture or apical displacement; a single periapical view is enough only for an obvious concussion or minor crown fracture (Berman et al, n.d., p.74). The stage of root development is recorded because it shapes the pulp prognosis (p.74). Sensibility tests have limits: negative results can persist for up to six months after injury, so the diagnosis of pulp necrosis depends on several signs, including discolouration, negative electrical or Doppler test, mobility and periapical change (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610-611; Andreasen et al, 2007, p.399).

**6. Principles of management.** Kohli and Lee set out three factors that decide treatment for every injury: the pulp status and its potential to heal, the stage of root development, and the amount of remaining tooth structure. The objectives are, in priority order, to relieve symptoms, preserve pulp vitality where possible, keep the tooth in its socket, and support alveolar growth, which matters most in developing dentitions (2026, p.609-610). Hexem and Eisler describe the management as phased: an acute phase for airway, infection and soft tissue, a post-acute phase for splinting and interim restoration, and a long-term phase for definitive treatment once healing and growth allow (2026, Part 1, p.589-590). Berman's guide gives the same logic for the individual injuries. Ogle stresses long-term follow-up and the unpredictable prognosis of avulsion (2024, p.151-165).

**7. Injury-by-injury summary.**

*Enamel infraction and enamel fracture.* Infractions usually need no treatment; enamel fractures are smoothed or restored (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.301-302). Pulp necrosis follows in 0 to 3.5% of infractions and 0 to 1% of enamel fractures (p.320).

*Enamel-dentin fracture (uncomplicated crown fracture).* The priority is to seal the exposed dentin. A glass-ionomer bandage or a calcium hydroxide or bonded liner is placed, followed by definitive restoration: reattachment of the fragment, composite, veneer or crown (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.302-306). Pulp necrosis is 0 to 6%.

*Complicated crown fracture (pulp exposure).* The pulp is treated by direct capping, partial pulpotomy, coronal or deep pulpotomy, or root canal treatment, depending on the exposure size, the time since injury and the root stage (Berman et al, n.d., p.34-37; Andreasen et al, 2007, p.304, Ch.22). Kohli and Lee report partial pulpotomy success above 95% when 2 to 4 mm of inflamed pulp is excised and a bioceramic material placed (2026, p.612). The textbook does not quote a single figure.

*Crown-root fracture.* Crown-root fractures extend into the root. In adults the tooth is often unrestorable and extraction with implant replacement is the usual end point. Forced or surgical extrusion can be considered where enough root remains; in growing patients the aim is to keep the tooth until growth is complete (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612-613). Andreasen et al describe removal of the coronal fragment with supragingival restoration, and surgical exposure of the fracture surface, as the two principal approaches (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.11, p.334-338).

*Root fracture.* Root fractures are classified by the site of the fracture (apical, middle or cervical third) and by the healing pattern: healing with calcified tissue, interposition of connective tissue, interposition of bone and connective tissue, and non-healing (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.356-367). Treatment is complete repositioning and firm, passive splinting until hard-tissue callus forms, usually after about three months, with rigid splints giving the poorest results (p.369, p.377). Pulp canal obliteration is common in the healing fragments, in 69 to 73% of teeth in clinical studies (p.379). Horizontal root fractures are treated in the same way; vertical fractures carry a different and worse prognosis.

*Luxation injuries.* Five luxation types are recognised in Andreasen et al (concussion, subluxation, extrusive, lateral and intrusive). Pulp necrosis follows concussion in about 3%, subluxation in 6%, extrusion in 26%, lateral luxation in 58% and intrusion in 85% (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.394). Intrusion carries the highest risk of resorption and bone loss, and lateral luxation with apical displacement is likely to need root canal treatment (Berman et al, n.d., p.75). Splinting is flexible for luxation injuries, and durations differ between sources (see below).

*Avulsion.* Avulsion is complete displacement from the socket. Immediate replantation after a brief rinse, storage in an appropriate medium if replantation is delayed, and a short flexible splint are the core steps (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.475-477). Extra-alveolar time and the storage medium decide the prognosis (p.486). The IADT guideline, as reported by Hexem and Eisler, requires an extra-oral dry time under 60 minutes for replantation (2026, Part 1, p.594).

*Alveolar bone injury.* Alveolar fractures are reported by Ogle, who gives a four-week fixation with no recommendation on splint type (2024, p.158). Andreasen's Ch.18 is summarised in the exam section.

**8. Pulpal management and endodontic timing.** The endodontic decisions follow the pulp status and root stage. A vital pulp in a fresh exposure is capped or partially pulpotomised. A necrotic pulp in a mature tooth needs root canal treatment, and Kohli and Lee recommend calcium hydroxide within seven to 10 days for a closed-apex tooth with necrosis (2026, p.619). Trope's 2010 review of immature teeth with necrotic pulps gives the disinfection and apical-barrier approach: the infected canal is cleaned without aggressive instrumentation, and a calcium hydroxide or MTA barrier is placed to allow a hard-tissue plug, after which the canal is filled (Trope, 2010, p.313-324). Revascularisation is possible in immature teeth with open apices, reported in 18 to 40% after replantation (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, n.d., p.262). Regenerative procedures are an option where revascularisation fails (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619).

**9. Splinting.** Splinting protects the healing periodontium but is not benign. Rigid splints increase resorption and can inhibit revascularisation (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858). The splint should be flexible for luxation injuries and semi-rigid or rigid for root fractures (p.859, p.865). Durations: Andreasen gives two to three weeks for extrusion and subluxation, about three weeks for lateral luxation (six to eight weeks if bone breaks down), four to six weeks for root fractures, and 7 to 10 days for replantation into an intact socket (p.864-865). The IADT, as reported by Ogle, gives two weeks for subluxation and extrusion, four weeks for lateral and intrusive luxation, four weeks for root fractures (four months for cervical-third fractures), and two weeks for avulsion, or four weeks where dry time exceeds 60 minutes (Ogle, 2024, p.158). Ogle also reports a 400-tooth study in which splinting for 0 to 20 days and 21 to 40 days gave similar results for avulsed teeth (p.159).

**10. Restorative and growth considerations.** Restore crown anatomy and occlusion early to prevent drift and over-eruption. Composite resin is the usual restoration, with reattachment where an intact fragment is available (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306). Implants and definitive fixed prostheses are normally delayed until growth is complete; decoronation and ridge preservation prepare the site for later treatment (Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Clinics care points). Where ankylosis causes infraocclusion in a growing patient, decoronation may be needed to keep alveolar height (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619-620).

**11. Long-term follow-up and complications.** Follow-up is essential. Kohli and Lee recommend monitoring for at least five years, with sensibility testing at each visit (2026, p.611). The late complications are pulp necrosis, pulp canal obliteration, external inflammatory resorption, replacement resorption (ankylosis) and loss of marginal bone (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.13, p.412-413). External inflammatory resorption needs urgent endodontic treatment with calcium hydroxide (p.477). Replanted teeth can function for 20 to 40 years in reported cases, which shows that long-term survival is possible even after a poor start (p.482).

**12. Evidence and controversies.** Four issues are contested. Pulp capping with calcium hydroxide versus bonding as the dentin barrier (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.303; Berman et al, n.d., p.38). The success figure for partial pulpotomy, which the 2026 review puts above 95% but the textbook does not quantify (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612). The splint duration for lateral luxation and for avulsion, where IADT and AAE differ (Ogle, 2024, p.158-159). The timing of root canal treatment after luxation: observation for up to a year in low-risk patients (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.399) against treatment on two signs (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.615). Each is discussed in its own answer.

*Confidence:* the WHO classification, the three-factor approach to management, the pulp-necrosis rates by luxation type and the need for long follow-up are well established in the sources [Certain]. The optimal splint duration, the best dentin liner and the precise endodontic timing are contested [Likely].

**13. Primary and developing teeth.** Trauma to primary teeth is common: about 20 to 40% of six-year-old children have had an injury to the primary dentition (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.19, p.553). Primary teeth are usually displaced away from the permanent successor, so the position of the displaced tooth in relation to the developing successor must be established on radiographs (p.553). Injuries to the primary dentition can disturb the permanent successor, which is a problem of the developing tooth rather than of the primary tooth; they affect 12 to 69% of the involved permanent teeth in the reported series (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.20, p.585). Avulsed primary teeth should not be replanted, because replantation risks injury to the permanent successor (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.19, p.555). Injuries to developing teeth produce discolouration, crown dilaceration, root angulation, arrested root formation and disturbed eruption, and later necrosis can follow even without decay (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.20, p.585).

**14. Alveolar fractures.** Alveolar fractures make up about 16% of injuries in the permanent dentition (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.18, p.527). Treatment is reduction, fixation with an acid-etch resin splint for four weeks, radiographic and sensibility control, and a minimum follow-up of one year. The prognosis for the involved permanent teeth is poor: reported pulp necrosis is 75%, canal obliteration 15%, progressive root resorption 11% and marginal bone loss 13% (p.527). Berman's guide recommends immediate reduction and fixation, and treats every effort to preserve alveolar bone and restorable teeth as a priority (Berman et al, n.d., p.127).

**15. Prevention.** Andreasen et al estimate that 7 to 25% of dental injuries have a cause that preventive measures could reduce, mainly contact sports, bicycle and motorcycle use, and intubation in patients with extensive restorations or weakened periodontium (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.30, p.846). Mouthguards are considered essential in some contact sports, and helmets reduce facial injury in cycling and motorcycling (Berman et al, n.d., p.195; Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.30, p.846).

**16. Medico-legal and psychological aspects.** Liability requires negligence, causation and injury together, and the standard of care is the usual measure of negligence (Berman et al, n.d., p.182). Records and informed consent matter: the examiner should document the findings, the options and the patient's decision (p.181). Psychological reactions can follow a dental injury even when it appears minor, and the rates of post-traumatic stress disorder after serious accidents have been reported between 11.6 and 23.6% (Berman et al, n.d., p.190-191).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1 — Crown fractures do not, by themselves, threaten the pulp; luxation does.** [Likely]
- *What the textbook states:* Andreasen et al found no pulp canal obliteration and little root resorption after crown fractures without luxation, and suggested that a crown fracture does not elicit those changes on its own (2007, p.319, Table 10.7). Bonded fragments showed necrosis in 0.5% and canal obliteration in 2%, and all affected teeth had a concomitant luxation (p.319).
- *What the 2026 DCNA reviews state:* Hexem and Eisler frame the pulp risk mainly in terms of bacterial ingress through exposed dentin rather than the fracture itself (2026, Part 1, p.596).
- *Agreement or challenge:* both accept that the fracture is less dangerous than the associated luxation, but they describe the mechanism differently. Andreasen's data point to luxation; the DCNA review points to the seal.
- *EVIDENCE:* the crown-fracture figures come from a few long-term clinical series cited by Andreasen; the DCNA point is mechanistic and largely drawn from animal work.
- *INTERPRETATION:* in a crown fracture, an examiner should look for a concomitant luxation and seal the dentin at once.
- *LIMITATION:* the two mechanisms are not tested against each other in a single study.

**Claim 2 — Dentin protection and pulp treatment: the evidence is strongest for the principle, weakest for the numbers.** [Likely]
- *What the textbook states:* the barrier principle is well supported, but calcium hydroxide is still the most recommended capping agent, while bonding is advocated for a permanent seal (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.302-303; Berman et al, n.d., p.38).
- *What the 2026 review states:* partial pulpotomy success above 95% with bioceramic sealing (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612).
- *Agreement or challenge:* the principle of sealing the dentin and preserving a vital pulp is agreed. The numerical success figures are not reconciled, and the review's figure has no stated denominator.
- *LIMITATION:* the most cited success data come from clinical follow-up series of different sizes and follow-up lengths, and none is a randomised trial in adult anterior fractures.

**Claim 3 — Splint length and endodontic timing are the least settled parts of management.** [Likely]
- *What the textbook states:* Andreasen et al give two to three weeks for extrusion and about three weeks for lateral luxation, and accept observation for up to a year in selected luxations (2007, p.399, p.442, p.865).
- *What the IADT position states, as reported by Ogle:* two weeks for subluxation and extrusion, four weeks for lateral and intrusive luxation, and two weeks for avulsion in most cases (2024, p.158). Ogle also cites an animal study in which 30 days of splinting led to more resorption than one week (p.159).
- *Agreement or challenge:* the sources agree on flexible splints for luxation, and disagree on the lateral and avulsion durations. The 400-tooth study cited by Ogle does not settle the lateral case.
- *LIMITATION:* the splint and timing figures rest on case series and animal work; none of the sources is a randomised comparison.

**Claim 4 — Long-term follow-up is necessary, and the prognosis of severe injury is not fixed.** [Certain]
- *What the sources state:* Kohli and Lee recommend five years of follow-up (2026, p.611). Andreasen et al report replanted teeth in service for 20 to 40 years in some cases, and document resorption and ankylosis that can appear late (2007, p.482, p.470).
- *Agreement:* all sources support long-term monitoring, and no source claims that the prognosis is settled at one year.

*Confidence:* the need for long-term follow-up and the broad principles of management are settled [Certain]. The quantitative claims for splinting, timing and pulpotomy are contested [Likely].

## EXAM MODE

**Definition and scope.** Traumatic dental injuries are injuries to the teeth and their supporting structures caused by a sudden physical impact. They are classified by the anatomical site of damage and by any change in tooth position. The WHO-based clinical classification used in Andreasen et al divides them into injuries to the hard tissues and pulp, injuries to the periodontal tissues, and injuries to the supporting bone and soft tissues [Andreasen et al, 2007; Kohli and Lee, 2026]. The classification serves two purposes: clinical recording and epidemiological comparison. The older Ellis grading remains in use in examinations and describes crown fractures by severity, with separate classes for non-vital teeth, avulsion, root fracture and displacement without fracture [Sikri, n.d.].

**Two classifications side by side.** An examiner who is asked to classify should present both schemes and say what each is for. The WHO-based clinical scheme records the injury site and the displacement, and is the basis for epidemiological work. The Ellis scheme grades crown fractures by severity, and is still used in examinations.

| WHO-based clinical category (Andreasen et al) | Closest Ellis class (Sikri) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Enamel infraction; enamel fracture | Class 1 (little or no dentin) | no pulp involvement |
| Uncomplicated crown fracture (enamel-dentin) | Class 2 (considerable dentin, pulp intact) | seal the dentin |
| Complicated crown fracture | Class 3 (considerable dentin, pulp exposed) | pulp decision |
| Uncomplicated and complicated crown-root fracture | no direct equivalent in the Ellis list shown | extraction or extrusion decision |
| Root fracture | Class 6 (fracture of the root) | level and healing pattern |
| Luxation injuries, avulsion | Class 7 (displacement without fracture) and Class 5 (teeth lost) | luxation or avulsion pathway |
| Non-vital tooth (status) | Class 4 | endodontic pathway |

The Ellis scheme is not a complete map of the WHO categories: crown-root fractures and luxation do not fit it neatly, which is one reason the WHO scheme is preferred for recording [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.233-234; Sikri, n.d., p.483-484].

**Epidemiology.** Dental injuries are common. Between 71 and 92% of lifetime injuries occur before the age of 19 [Andreasen et al, 2007]. Boys are injured about twice as often as girls in the permanent dentition, and a first injury raises the risk of a second. The maxillary central incisors are the most frequently injured teeth. Crown fractures make up 26 to 76% of permanent-dentition injuries, luxation 15 to 40% (summary) or 15 to 61% (text), root fracture 0.5 to 7% and avulsion 0.5 to 3%. Figures vary with population and method, and many studies are small and cross-sectional [Andreasen et al, 2007; Ogle, 2024].

**Aetiology and risk.** Falls, sport and play, traffic accidents, and misuse of teeth are the main causes. Child physical abuse and iatrogenic injury must be considered. Protrusion of the maxillary incisors is a strong predisposing factor: an overjet above 6 mm is reported to roughly double the extent of injury in some series, and inadequate lip coverage is linked to higher risk [Andreasen et al, 2007].

**Examination and diagnosis.** The first duty is to exclude serious head, neck or central nervous system injury, and to refer at once where the mechanism suggests more force than the dental damage. Take a history of the mechanism, the time and any previous injury. Examine the soft tissues, the alveolus, the teeth and the occlusion. Test mobility, percussion and sensibility, and record the direction of any displacement. Take periapical radiographs at more than one angle, and an occlusal view where needed [Berman et al, n.d.]. Pulp necrosis is diagnosed on several signs, because sensibility can be negative for up to six months [Kohli and Lee, 2026].

**Three factors and four objectives.** Treatment for any injury depends on three factors: pulp status and potential to heal, stage of root development, and remaining tooth structure. The objectives, in priority order, are to relieve symptoms, preserve pulp vitality, retain the tooth in its socket, and support alveolar growth [Kohli and Lee, 2026]. The management is phased: an acute phase for airway, infection and soft tissue; a post-acute phase for splinting and interim restoration; and a long-term phase for definitive treatment [Hexem and Eisler, 2026].

**Crown fractures.** Enamel infractions usually need no treatment, and enamel fractures are smoothed or restored. For enamel-dentin fractures, seal the exposed dentin at once, using a glass-ionomer bandage or a liner, and then restore. Reattachment of an intact fragment gives good morphology and bond strength of about three times that of enamel etch alone. For complicated fractures, choose the pulp treatment by exposure size, time and root stage: capping or partial pulpotomy for a vital pulp, root canal treatment for a necrotic pulp, and apexification or regenerative procedures for immature teeth. Partial pulpotomy success is reported above 95% in one 2026 review, but the textbook does not quote a single figure [Andreasen et al, 2007; Berman et al, n.d.; Kohli and Lee, 2026]. The IADT rehydrates a fragment for 20 minutes, places calcium hydroxide within 0.5 mm of the pulp, and prefers partial pulpotomy, even in complete roots [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.319-320].

**Crown-root fractures.** Adult crown-root fractures often end in extraction and implant replacement. Extrusion, orthodontic or surgical, may be considered where enough root remains. In growing patients the aim is to retain the tooth until growth finishes [Kohli and Lee, 2026]. IADT options: orthodontic or surgical extrusion, root submergence and intentional replantation [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.321-322]. Crowder et al [2024] report 37 decoronated teeth, on limited evidence (PDF p.1).

**Root fractures.** Root fractures are classified by level (apical, middle or cervical third) and healing pattern: calcified tissue, interposed connective tissue, interposed bone and connective tissue, or non-healing. Treatment is repositioning and firm, passive splinting until a callus forms, typically about three months. Rigid splints give the poorest results, and pulp canal obliteration is common in healing fragments [Andreasen et al, 2007].

**Luxation injuries.** Concussion needs no treatment; subluxation is splinted for up to two weeks only if mobility is excessive [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.325]. Extrusion is repositioned gently and splinted for two weeks, four if marginal bone breaks down (p.326). Lateral luxation is repositioned digitally and splinted for four weeks (p.327). Intruded immature teeth are allowed to re-erupt, with orthodontic repositioning if none within four weeks; mature teeth under 3 mm are observed, with surgical repositioning if none within eight weeks; 3 to 7 mm is repositioned surgically (preferably) or orthodontically, and beyond 7 mm surgically (p.328). Andreasen et al give pulp necrosis after concussion in about 3%, subluxation 6%, extrusion 26%, lateral luxation 58% and intrusion 85% [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.394].

**Avulsion.** Replant at once, after a gentle rinse if the tooth is dirty (IADT). If replantation is delayed, store the tooth in milk, HBSS, saliva or saline, in that order; water is poor but better than drying [Fouad et al, 2020, p.333]. After 30 minutes most PDL cells are non-viable, and 60 minutes is the IADT management boundary (p.333-334). Splint for two weeks, and start root canal treatment of a closed apex within two weeks (p.334). The IADT gives avulsion as 0.5% to 16% of dental injuries (p.332).

**Alveolar injuries.** Fractures of the alveolar socket require repositioning and fixation. The IADT figure reported by Ogle is four weeks; no splint type is recommended [Ogle, 2024]. A fuller account of alveolar, soft-tissue and primary-tooth injuries is outside this draft.

**Pulp management and endodontic timing.** A vital, freshly exposed pulp is capped or partially pulpotomised. A necrotic pulp in a closed-apex tooth needs root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide within seven to 10 days. In immature teeth, revascularisation after replantation is reported in 18 to 40% of open-apex teeth. Where revascularisation fails, the aim is disinfection with a calcium hydroxide or MTA apical barrier to allow hard-tissue closure, and then canal filling. Observation for up to a year is accepted in selected low-risk luxations [Kohli and Lee, 2026; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, n.d.; Trope, 2010; Andreasen et al, 2007]. The IADT advises calcium hydroxide intracanal for up to one month in intruded, severely extruded or laterally luxated fully developed teeth [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.317].

**Splinting.** Splints should be flexible for luxation and semi-rigid or rigid for root fracture; rigid splinting harms healing [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858]. The IADT durations are given in the luxation and root-fracture answers. Andreasen et al give two to three weeks for extrusion, about three weeks for lateral luxation, and 7 to 10 days for replantation into an intact socket (p.864-865).

**Restoration and growth.** Restore the crown and occlusion promptly to prevent drift and over-eruption. Use composite resin as the usual restoration, and reattachment where an intact fragment allows. Delay implants and fixed prostheses until growth is complete. Decoronation and ridge preservation protect the site where ankylosis causes infraocclusion in a growing patient [Andreasen et al, 2007; Hexem and Eisler, 2026; Kohli and Lee, 2026].

**Complications.** The late complications are pulp necrosis, pulp canal obliteration, external inflammatory resorption, replacement resorption (ankylosis) and marginal bone loss. External inflammatory resorption needs urgent endodontic treatment with calcium hydroxide. Replacement resorption has no effective treatment once established, and in growing patients it can cause infraocclusion [Andreasen et al, 2007; Kohli and Lee, 2026].

**Follow-up and prognosis.** Follow-up should last at least five years, with sensibility and radiographs at each review. Long-term outcomes can be good: replanted teeth have been reported in service for 20 to 40 years with a normal periodontium. Prognosis is nonetheless unpredictable for avulsion, and some older patients may need implants [Kohli and Lee, 2026; Andreasen et al, 2007; Ogle, 2024].

**Evidence and disagreement.** Four matters are contested and should be stated in any answer. First, the dentin liner beneath a restoration: bonding is favoured by Andreasen et al, while Berman et al retain calcium hydroxide for capping. Second, the success figure for partial pulpotomy, which the 2026 review places above 95% without a denominator. Third, the splint duration for lateral luxation and for avulsion, where IADT and AAE recommendations differ. Fourth, the timing of root canal treatment after luxation, where Andreasen accepts observation and Kohli and Lee favour treatment on two signs [Andreasen et al, 2007; Berman et al, n.d.; Kohli and Lee, 2026; Ogle, 2024].

**The first visit as a working sequence.** A reliable sequence protects both the patient and the outcome. First, exclude serious head, neck or central nervous system injury, and refer if the mechanism calls for it [Berman et al, n.d., p.74]. Second, take the history, including the exact time since injury and, for an avulsed or displaced tooth, the storage conditions [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.475]. Third, examine and record the injuries with photographs and a diagram. Fourth, take radiographs at several angles before any reduction, so that the baseline is known [Berman et al, n.d., p.74]. Fifth, give local anaesthesia for forceful repositioning, control bleeding, and deal with soft-tissue lacerations first where they threaten the airway or the repair [Hexem and Eisler, 2026]. Sixth, reposition, splint, and plan the pulp and restorative care. Tetanus status should be checked, because most avulsed teeth and many luxations have been in contact with soil or the mouth [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477]. The question of systemic antibiotics at reimplantation is open: a recent meta-analysis, cited by Hexem and Eisler, found no clear consensus, and the textbooks differ on the drug and the regimen [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, p.592; Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, n.d., p.263].

**Pulp decisions by clinical state.** The pulp decision is best laid out as a grid. A vital pulp in a fresh exposure is capped or partially pulpotomised, with calcium hydroxide or MTA over the exposure [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.630]. Where degenerative or inflammatory change is likely, capping and pulpotomy are contraindicated and pulpectomy is the option for mature teeth [Berman et al, n.d., p.35; Andreasen et al, 2007, p.618]. Where the exposure is old, or the pulp is necrotic, the mature tooth needs root canal treatment, and the immature tooth needs apexification or a regenerative procedure [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.629; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612]. A tooth with negative sensibility but no radiographic or clinical sign of necrosis is observed, and treatment is deferred [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.399]. The conservative criteria in Andreasen et al require no symptoms, no radiographic pathology, continued root development in immature teeth, and a verified vital pulp (p.626).

**Interpreting sensibility.** Sensibility is a poor single test after trauma. Immediately after injury about half the luxated teeth do not respond, and the response recovers in many (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.395). Lack of sensibility on its own, or discolouration on its own, is not enough to diagnose necrosis (p.394). The clinician therefore records a baseline on the uninjured teeth, repeats the tests at fixed intervals, and waits for the pattern (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610-611; Berman et al, n.d., p.74). The practical point for an examiner is that a student who orders endodontic treatment on one negative test has misapplied the sources.

**Immature teeth.** An open apex changes everything. Revascularisation is possible if the foramen is wide and the tooth is replanted early; the reported rate is 18 to 40% after replantation (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, n.d., p.262). Where the pulp is necrotic, Trope describes a disinfection that does not use aggressive instrumentation, followed by a calcium hydroxide or MTA barrier at the apex to allow a hard-tissue plug, and then canal filling (Trope, 2010, p.313-324). Berman's guide describes apexification with calcium hydroxide in which the second appointment falls two weeks to a month after the first (Berman et al, n.d., p.40). Kohli and Lee advise monitoring and treatment only if infection appears, and note regenerative procedures as an option (2026, p.619). The examiner should explain that the aim in an immature tooth is root development as well as pulp health.

**Adult injuries.** In adults the aims are the same, but the consequences are different. Kohli and Lee note that adults may present with extensive crown-root fractures that are unrestorable, and that extraction and implant placement is often the outcome; forced or surgical extrusion is an option where enough root remains (2026, p.612-613). Ogle describes replantation of an avulsed adult tooth as having an unpredictable prognosis, and suggests endosseous implants as an alternative in older patients (2024, p.151). Cone-beam imaging can reveal fractures that a periapical view misses, and Kohli and Lee treat CBCT as a key component of assessment where it is available (2026, p.610).

**Fracture-specific pitfalls.** Several pitfalls recur. A probe can create a pulp exposure in a thin roof of dentin, so probing should be gentle and the examiner should avoid probing a fracture line (Berman et al, n.d., p.28). Crown fractures are sensitive to thermal change, which is a useful diagnostic cue but not a measure of pulp survival. Root fractures can escape the first radiograph and appear later, so a repeat radiograph at four weeks is sensible (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.355-357). A non-healing root fracture typically shows a radiolucency at the fracture line and granulation tissue, and the sequence after splinting should be watched. Rigid splints and orthodontic bands on the coronal fragment can injure the already traumatised pulp (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.368).

**Comparative summary of injury types.**

| Injury | Most important decision | Typical first step | Typical definitive step | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enamel infraction | none usually | smooth or seal lines | none | stain |
| Enamel-dentin fracture | seal the dentin | GIC bandage, liner | reattach, composite, veneer or crown | bacterial leakage |
| Complicated crown fracture | pulp decision | capping or pulpotomy | restore; RCT if necrotic | pulp necrosis |
| Crown-root fracture | extract or extrude | retain if possible | extrusion, restoration or implant | loss of tooth |
| Root fracture | repositioning and splint | passive splint about 3 months | observe healing pattern | non-healing, canal obliteration |
| Luxation | type of displacement | reposition and splint | endodontics on signs | necrosis, resorption, bone loss |
| Avulsion | replant at once | rinse, replant, splint | root canal treatment, storage choice | ankylosis, resorption |

**Documentation and communication.** Because late complications are common, the record should be complete. It should contain the mechanism and time, the storage of any avulsed tooth, the baseline sensibility and radiographs, photographs, the splint and its date, and the advice given (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.475; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.609-611). Parents and patients should be told that splinting will be needed, that pulp changes may appear months or years later, and that monitoring must continue (Kohli and Lee, 2026; Ogle, 2024).

**Prognosis figures in one place.** The sources give the following reported figures for pulp and supporting-tissue outcomes. Pulp necrosis after luxation ranges from 15 to 52% overall, and pulp canal obliteration from 3 to 35%; root surface resorption from 1 to 18%, greatest after intrusion; internal resorption about 2%; and loss of supporting bone about 5% [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.413]. Pulp necrosis after crown fracture is 0 to 3.5% for enamel infraction, 0 to 1% for enamel fracture and 0 to 6% for enamel-dentin fracture without exposure [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320]. Replanted and autotransplanted teeth show success rates in the range of 87 to 93% for absence of progressive root resorption in one review of anterior transplants, with 10 to 20% later showing pulpal or periodontal complications [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.770]. These are reported rates, not guarantees, and they come from clinical series of different sizes.

**A timetable for review.** At the injury visit, record, radiograph, reposition and splint. Review at two weeks, when the splint is removed, and start endodontic treatment of a closed apex within two weeks [Fouad et al, 2020, p.334, p.337]. Then review at four weeks, three and six months, one year, and yearly for five years; open apices at 1, 2, 3 and 6 months (p.337-338).

**Primary and developing teeth.** In the primary dentition, displaced teeth are usually pushed away from the permanent successor, and the successor must be checked radiographically [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.553]. A concussed primary tooth needs no active treatment and a review at eight weeks; an extruded primary tooth is repositioned and splinted for about three weeks, or extracted if the extrusion is severe; an intruded primary tooth is usually left to realign and reviewed at one and two months; and an avulsed primary tooth is not replanted [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.555]. Injuries to developing teeth can cause enamel discolouration, crown dilaceration, arrested root formation and disturbed eruption, and necrosis can follow years later without decay [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.20, p.585] [Likely].

**Alveolar fractures.** Alveolar fractures are reduced and fixed, usually with an acid-etch splint for four weeks, and followed for at least one year. The reported outcomes for the involved permanent teeth are unfavourable: pulp necrosis in 75%, canal obliteration in 15%, progressive root resorption in 11% and marginal bone loss in 13% [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.18, p.527].

**Soft tissue and infection.** Soft-tissue injuries are managed in four steps: cleansing, debridement, haemostasis and closure. Intraoral treatment is done before the lips are sutured, and tetanus prophylaxis is considered for contaminated wounds [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.21, p.595, p.610]. Routine antibiotics for soft-tissue wounds have little supporting evidence, so they are restricted to specific situations such as heavy contamination, delayed debridement and open reduction of a jaw fracture [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.21, p.610] [Likely].

**Timing.** The sources give a graded approach. Crown and crown-root fractures are subacute or delayed; extrusion and lateral luxation are acute, although the evidence is weak; avulsion is acute if the tooth has not been replanted at the time of injury [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.9, p.288] [Likely]. Crown and crown-root fractures are managed subacutely or in a delayed way [Andreasen et al, 2002, printed p.116].

**Prevention and legal aspects.** About 7 to 25% of dental injuries have a cause that preventive measures could reduce, so mouthguards, helmets and seat restraints matter [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.30, p.846]. Liability requires negligence, causation and injury together [Berman et al, n.d., p.182].

**Conclusion.** Traumatic dental injuries are common, concentrated in young people, and varied in their management. Good outcomes depend on accurate classification, a three-factor decision about the pulp, periodontium and remaining tooth, prompt and appropriate splinting, timely endodontic treatment, and at least five years of follow-up. The examiner should show the classification, the reasoning for each injury, and the points on which the evidence is unsettled.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- WHO classification: hard tissue and pulp; periodontal; bone and soft tissue.
- Crown fractures 26-76%; luxation 15-40% (or 15-61%); avulsion 0.5-3%; root fracture 0.5-7%.
- Three factors: pulp status, root development, remaining tooth structure.
- Pulp necrosis by luxation type: 3%, 6%, 26%, 58%, 85%.
- Splint flexible for luxation; rigid splinting harms healing.
- Avulsion: replant at once; storage medium and dry time decide prognosis.
- Closed-apex necrosis: root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide within 7-10 days.
- Follow up for at least five years.
