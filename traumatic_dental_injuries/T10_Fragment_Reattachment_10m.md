## STUDY MODE

**Definition.** Fragment reattachment is bonding the original, retrieved crown fragment back to the tooth after a crown fracture. The fragment must be retrieved at the time of injury, so public information matters (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306).

**Indications.** The sole indication is an intact enamel-dentin fragment with most of the enamel margin present, so that it sits firmly against the fracture surface. Small defects can be restored with composite at the same visit (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306). Berman's guide adds that larger fragments are easier to bond, and small or multiple fragments are difficult to manage (Berman et al, n.d., p.31).

**Technique.** The fracture surfaces are cleaned and the fragment tried for fit. Earlier technique bevelled enamel on both surfaces or hollowed the dentin of the fragment; with modern dentin-bonding agents, the bond is made to dentin and enamel with a bonding system used as the manufacturer directs (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306). Beveling along the fracture line may increase fracture resistance in in vitro testing (p.306). Berman's guide notes that reattachment is attractive today for patient acceptance, reduced chair time and accurate morphology (n.d., p.31).

**Bond and pulp.** Reattachment roughly tripled the fracture strength compared with acid-etch enamel alone (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.303). Animal data show that the pulp stays safe while at least 0.2 mm of dentin remains between the pulp and the bonding surface, and that an early adverse response at eight days was not present at 90 days (p.306).

**Outcome.** In a long-term clinical study of bonded fragments, 1 of 198 teeth (0.5%) developed necrosis and four (2%) developed canal obliteration, all with concomitant luxation (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.319). Hexem and Eisler note that fragment loss is a recognised risk and cite a systematic review (2026, Part 1, ref 24).

## EXAM MODE

**Definition and indication.** Fragment reattachment rebonds the retrieved crown fragment to the tooth. The sole indication is an intact enamel-dentin fragment with most of the enamel margin present, so that it seats firmly against the fracture surface. Small defects can be restored with composite at the same visit [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306]. Berman's guide notes that larger fragments are easier to bond, and that small or multiple fragments are difficult to manage [Berman et al, n.d., p.31].

**Retrieval.** Successful reattachment depends on finding the fragment at the time of injury, which is why public information about retrieval matters [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306].

**Technique.** Etch the enamel of both fracture surfaces, check the fit and bond with a composite system. Harty's describes this sequence and a chamfered, acid-etched surface restored under dental-dam isolation [Harty's, n.d., p.254-255]. Bevelling the fracture line may increase fracture resistance in in vitro testing [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306] [Likely]. Reattachment can be achieved with different dentin-bonding agents as long as the manufacturer's directions are followed closely [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306]. Rehydrate 20 minutes [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.319].

**Bond strength and pulp.** Reattachment roughly tripled fracture strength compared with acid-etch enamel alone [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.303]. Animal data show the pulp stays safe while at least 0.2 mm of dentin remains between the pulp and the bonding surface [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306]. A clinical study reported reattachment without hazard to vitality at the time of injury [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306] [Likely].

**Outcome.** In a long-term series of 198 bonded incisors, one tooth (0.5%) developed necrosis and four (2%) developed canal obliteration; all five had a concomitant luxation [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.319]. The 2026 review describes predictable reattachment with modern bonding, favourable survival especially with fibre reinforcement or protective restorations, and lower cost than full coverage [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.606] [Likely]. Fragment loss is a recognised risk, supported by a systematic review cited in the Part 1 article [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 1, ref 24]. Favourable, case-level [Khandelwal et al, 2021].

**Limits.** Reattachment is harder with a concomitant luxation, because the dry field is difficult to keep. An unruly patient or a fracture that needs time to settle may favour a provisional restoration first [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Intact enamel-dentin fragment with most of enamel margin is the sole indication.
- Retrieval at the scene is essential.
- Bonding roughly triples fracture strength (vs acid-etch enamel).
- 0.2 mm of dentin above the pulp for safety.
- Fragment loss is a recognised risk.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Reattaching a small, damaged or multiple fragment.
2. Ignoring the 0.2 mm dentin safety threshold.
3. Forgetting fragment loss as a complication.
