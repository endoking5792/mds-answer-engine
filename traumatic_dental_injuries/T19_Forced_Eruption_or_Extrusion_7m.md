## STUDY MODE

**Definition.** Forced eruption (orthodontic extrusion) moves a tooth coronally out of its socket, usually slowly over weeks, so that a fracture line that lies below the gingival margin is brought into the open where it can be restored. Extrusion can be orthodontic (brackets and wires) or surgical.

**Indication.** Crown-root fractures that extend below the marginal level are the usual indication. Andreasen et al note that different fracture types are amenable to orthodontic extrusion (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.700-701). A rapid extrusion technique was introduced by Heithersay in 1973 and developed by Ingber in 1976 (p.702).

**Biology.** A non-vital tooth can be extruded about 3 to 5 mm over three to four weeks, because the periodontal fibres are stretched and then remodelled as the bone follows (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.702). The gingival margin shifts coronally by growth of the attached gingiva rather than by movement of the mucogingival junction (p.702). Relapse is common because the stretched gingival fibres pull the tooth back (p.702).

**Choice of method.** For a complicated crown-root or cervical root fracture, orthodontic or surgical extrusion is available. Surgical extrusion is considered where the fracture lies palatally below the marginal bone (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.703). The choice follows the fracture level and the remaining root.

**Follow-up.** Radiographs and clinical review during extrusion, then a period of stabilisation so that the tissue settles before definitive restoration (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.702-703). Weine's chapter describes wire and bracket placement, the balance between force and tissue lag, and a stabilisation period of around six weeks (Weine et al, n.d., Ch.12).

## EXAM MODE

**Definition.** Forced eruption is coronal movement of a tooth out of its socket to expose a fracture line that lies below the gingival margin, so that the fracture can be restored [Andreasen et al, 2007].

**Indication.** Crown-root or cervical root fractures that extend below the margin. Orthodontic extrusion is used where the fracture level and the remaining root allow it [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.700-703].

**Method.** Orthodontic extrusion uses brackets and wires; a rapid technique was described by Heithersay (1973) and developed by Ingber (1976). A non-vital tooth can move about 3 to 5 mm in three to four weeks [Likely]. Surgical extrusion is an alternative where the fracture is palatally below the marginal bone [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.702-703]. The IADT lists orthodontic extrusion, surgical extrusion and root submergence as options for complicated crown-root fractures [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.321-322].

**Problems.** Relapse, because stretched gingival fibres pull the tooth back. Gingival shift is caused by growth of attached gingiva. Monitor the periodontal condition throughout [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.702].

**Conclusion.** Forced eruption exposes a sub-marginal fracture for restoration. It is orthodontic or surgical, is best planned with the fracture level and the remaining root, and needs a stabilisation period afterwards.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Forced eruption exposes sub-marginal fractures.
- Non-vital tooth moves about 3-5 mm in three to four weeks.
- Relapse from stretched gingival fibres.
- Orthodontic or surgical extrusion, chosen by fracture level.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Confusing forced eruption with intrusion.
2. Forgetting relapse.
3. Extruding without a stabilisation period.
