## STUDY MODE

**Purpose.** A splint stabilises a traumatised tooth so that the periodontium can heal and the tooth is not re-injured. It does not treat the pulp. Andreasen et al note that splinting has a history: until the 1970s, splints for traumatised teeth were borrowed from jaw-fracture methods, such as cap splints, arch bars and wires, and that acid-etch techniques from the late 1960s changed the approach (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858).

**Requirements of an optimal splint.** Andreasen et al list the features an optimal splint should have: direct intraoral application; construction from materials available in practice; no increase in periodontal injury and no promotion of caries; no soft-tissue irritation; passive, exerting no orthodontic force; versatile enough to be rigid, semi-rigid or flexible; easy to remove without permanent damage; allowing pulp testing and endodontic treatment; hygienic and aesthetic (p.859).

**Types.** Splints are flexible (more mobility than an uninjured tooth), semi-rigid (equal to normal tooth mobility) or rigid (less than normal mobility) (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.859). Acid-etch composite splints are common. Ogle reports that composite and wire splints are the most used flexible splints, and that arch bars create large compression zones (2024, p.158-159).

**Rigidity and healing.** Rigid splinting has been shown to impair periodontal healing and to reduce pulp revascularisation in monkey models, compared with no splint (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858). Rigid splints also gave the poorest results in root fracture (p.377). Flexible splints are therefore the usual choice for luxation, and semi-rigid or rigid splints are reserved for root fracture (p.859, p.865).

**Duration.** Andreasen gives two to three weeks for extrusion and subluxation, three weeks for lateral luxation (six to eight weeks if bone breaks down), four to six weeks for root fracture, and seven to 10 days for replantation into an intact socket to prevent ankylosis (p.864-865). The IADT, as reported by Ogle, gives two weeks for subluxation and extrusion, four weeks for lateral and intrusive luxation and root fracture, and four months for a cervical-third root fracture (2024, p.158).

**Practical technique.** Berman's guide describes splints fixed to the teeth with composite across the labial surfaces after etching, and notes that the splint must be removed carefully with support of the tooth (n.d., p.80-84). Hexem and Eisler describe a 2026 shift toward reinforced composites designed for splints, 3D-printed and CAD/CAM splints and the choice of splint colour (2026, Part 1, p.594-596).

## EXAM MODE

**Purpose.** A splint holds the injured tooth in position while its periodontal ligament heals. It does not treat the pulp, and it must allow pulp testing and root canal access [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.859].

**Requirements.** The splint should be placed directly, be passive and exert no orthodontic force, be easy to remove without damage, be hygienic and aesthetic, and permit pulp testing and endodontic treatment [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.859].

**Choosing rigidity.** Flexible splints suit luxation injuries. Semi-rigid or rigid splints suit root fracture. Rigid splinting has been shown in a monkey model to decrease pulp revascularisation and increase necrosis and inflammatory resorption, compared with no splint [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858] [Likely]. For root fracture, rigid splints gave the poorest results [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.377].

**Durations.** IADT durations [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.325-328]: subluxation up to two weeks, only if mobility is excessive; extrusion two weeks, plus four if marginal bone breaks down; lateral luxation four weeks; intrusion four weeks after surgical repositioning; root fracture four weeks, up to four months cervically. Avulsed teeth: two weeks [Fouad et al, 2020, p.334]. Andreasen et al give two to three weeks for extrusion and about three weeks for lateral luxation [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.442].

**Materials.** The IADT specifies a passive flexible wire of up to 0.4 mm, or nylon of 0.13 to 0.25 mm bonded with composite, and warns against nylon in children with few permanent teeth [Fouad et al, 2020, p.333-334].

**Technique.** Harty's advises that the patient bite into softened pink wax, double or triple rolled, to hold the teeth in the optimal position while the splint is applied [Harty's, n.d., p.263]. Berman's guide describes splint application after etching and careful removal with support of the tooth [Berman et al, n.d., p.80-84]. Kumari et al [2025] found very low-quality evidence, favouring flexible, short splints (PDF p.1).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Splint does not treat the pulp; allow pulp testing and root canal access.
- Flexible for luxation; semi-rigid or rigid for root fracture.
- Rigid splints impair healing.
- Durations differ: two to four weeks for most luxations; 7-10 days for replantation into an intact socket.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Using a rigid splint for luxation.
2. Quoting one duration for all injuries.
3. Forgetting that the splint must allow pulp testing and endodontic access.

## AUDIT NOTE (IADT 2020, checked 2026-10-19)

**Audit note (IADT 2020; Kumari et al, 2025, checked 2026-10-19).** Corrections: the avulsion splint is two weeks (Fouad et al, 2020, p.334), not one week; the IADT luxation durations are in Bourguignon et al (2020, p.323-328) and differ from the Andreasen figures for lateral luxation. The Ogle (2024) figure of four weeks for avulsion with dry time over 60 minutes is not in the IADT avulsion text, which gives two weeks and four weeks only for an alveolar or jawbone fracture (Fouad et al, 2020, p.334-335). The nylon and wire specifications are as in the IADT text (p.333-334). The 2025 splinting review found very low-quality evidence and supports the IADT's flexible, short-term approach (PDF p.1).
