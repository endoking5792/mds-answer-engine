# T08 — Treatment options for a Class IV fracture of a maxillary central incisor in a 25-year-old

**Marks:** 20 (Long Essay)
**Repository sitting:** Feb 2019 (END §14, ABS PII)
**Question wording (repository):** "Describe in detail the treatment options for a class IV fracture of a maxillary central incisor in a 25 year old patient."
**Status:** DRAFTED, audited against IADT 2020 on 2026-10-19 (see audit note).

**Scope note (important).** In the Ellis classification, Class IV is a traumatised tooth that has become non-vital, with or without loss of crown structure. It is a pulp status, not a fracture pattern (Sikri, n.d., p.484). The question's wording is therefore read as a non-vital, fractured central incisor in an adult. If the examiner means Class 3 (fracture with pulp exposure), the answer for T05 applies. This ambiguity should be stated in the exam.

## STUDY MODE

**What a non-vital traumatised incisor means.** Pulp necrosis may follow a crown fracture, a luxation or both. In adults the tooth often shows discolouration and a negative response to sensibility testing. A diagnosis of necrosis needs two or more signs, such as discolouration, negative electrical or cold testing and a periapical change, and a single negative test is not enough (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.394, p.399; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610-611).

**Confirming the diagnosis.** Take the history and examine for mobility, percussion, discolouration and sinus tract. Take periapical radiographs at more than one angle, and consider CBCT where fracture extent or periapical change is unclear (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610). A fracture that extends into the root can be missed on a single radiograph, and it changes the treatment (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.355).

**Endodontic treatment.** For a mature tooth with a closed apex, pulp necrosis is treated by root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide within seven to 10 days (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619). Where the fracture involves the root, Andreasen et al describe three options. Root canal treatment of the coronal fragment alone is indicated where there is no periapical pathology and the fracture site shows a vital pulp in the apical fragment. Root canal treatment of both fragments is used when the entire pulp is necrotic, which is technically more difficult. Root canal treatment of the coronal fragment with surgical removal of the apical fragment is indicated when the apical fragment cannot be treated or the prognosis is poor (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.628-629).

**Restoration.** A non-vital incisor has lost its blood supply and is more brittle, so the restoration must protect the remaining tooth. The options are:

*Direct composite.* Used where the remaining crown is adequate. Adhesion to dentin is the main issue, because the endodontic access has altered the dentin, and the fractured surface needs careful bonding (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.25).

*Laminate veneer.* Suits large fractures with sound enamel, with contraindications and selection problems described in Andreasen et al (2007, p.313-317).

*Post and core with a crown.* Where the coronal structure is inadequate, a post and core is used, and a full crown protects the tooth. The choice of post, the ferrule and the root filling are dealt with in the endodontic restoration answers of Module 1; they are not repeated here.

**Adult considerations.** Kohli and Lee point out that in adults, extensive crown-root fractures often end in extraction and implant placement, while orthodontic or surgical extrusion may be considered where enough root remains (2026, p.612-613). Ogle notes that in older patients an implant may be a reasonable alternative to a poor-prognosis tooth, and that avulsion prognosis is unpredictable (2024, p.151). A tooth with a non-vital pulp and a fracture into the root therefore needs an early decision on retention versus extraction, which depends on the root remaining and the periodontal condition.

**Follow-up.** Review radiographically and clinically. Monitor for periapical healing, which may take years, and for late root fracture or periodontal breakdown (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.628-631). Kohli and Lee recommend long-term monitoring of at least five years (2026, p.611).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim — retention or extraction of a non-vital incisor with a root fracture.** [Likely]
- *Textbook:* root canal treatment of the coronal fragment, both fragments or the coronal fragment with surgical removal of the apical fragment, depending on the findings (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.628-629).
- *DCNA:* in adults with extensive crown-root fracture, extraction and implant placement is often the end point; extrusion may be considered where enough root remains (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612-613).
- *Agreement and challenge:* the textbook describes the options for a tooth that can be saved, and the DCNA review notes that many adult cases are not saveable. The decision turns on the remaining root and the periodontal condition.
- *LIMITATION:* the sources do not quantify survival of these options in adults.

## EXAM MODE

**Interpretation.** Ellis Class 4 is a traumatised tooth that has become non-vital, with or without loss of crown structure. It describes pulp status, not a fracture pattern [Sikri, n.d., p.484]. The question is therefore read as a non-vital, fractured maxillary central incisor in a 25-year-old, and the answer says so.

**Diagnosis of necrosis.** A response at the first examination does not show a healthy pulp, and no response does not show necrosis, because the pulp can respond later [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. Blood flow to the coronal pulp can take up to nine months to return after a traumatic injury [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. Diagnosis therefore rests on two or more signs, such as discolouration, negative electrical or cold testing and periapical change [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.399; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610-611]. CBCT is a key component where it is available [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.611].

**Root fracture.** A fracture into the root must be looked for. Radiographs at two or three vertical angulations improve detection, because a more horizontal fracture is more visible on radiograph [Berman et al, n.d., p.52]. Berman's guide notes that the coronal fragment of an intra-alveolar root fracture becomes non-vital in only 20 to 24% of cases, and that the prognosis is worse for coronal-third fractures [Berman et al, n.d., p.52, p.58]. The IADT recommends CBCT where available for root and crown-root fractures [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.316], and lists orthodontic extrusion, surgical extrusion and root submergence among the options for crown-root fractures (p.321-322).

**Endodontic options.** For a closed-apex tooth with necrosis, root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide begins within seven to 10 days [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619]. Where the fracture involves the root, root canal treatment of the coronal fragment alone is indicated when there is no periapical pathology and the fracture site shows a vital apical fragment. Root canal treatment of both fragments is used when the whole pulp is necrotic. Root canal treatment of the coronal fragment with surgical removal of the apical fragment is used when the apical fragment cannot be treated or the prognosis is poor [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.628-629].

**Extraction or retention.** Berman's guide reports that a more horizontal fracture led to extraction in 44% of teeth, compared with 8% for a more oblique coronal fracture [Berman et al, n.d., p.58]. Retention depends on the remaining root, the periodontal condition and the patient's wishes. In an adult, extensive crown-root fracture often ends in extraction and implant placement [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612-613].

**Restoration.** A non-vital incisor is more brittle. Where the crown is intact, a direct composite is used, with bonding that accounts for the altered dentin of the endodontic access [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.25]. A laminate veneer suits a large fracture with sound enamel [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.313-317]. Where the whole clinical crown is lost, Harty's describes pulpal extirpation, root canal filling and a post-retained crown as the usual plan [Harty's, n.d., p.257]. Post selection, ferrule and root filling are dealt with in the Module 1 restoration answers.

**Implants in adults.** The adult patient has finished growing, so an implant is an option where the root cannot be kept. Hexem and Eisler stress that implants should be delayed until growth is complete, and that ridge preservation and augmentation prepare the site [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.602-603]. Ogle suggests an implant may be an alternative in older patients with a poor-prognosis tooth [Ogle, 2024, p.151] [Likely].

**Follow-up.** Review clinically and radiographically to monitor periapical healing, which can take years, and for late root fracture or periodontal breakdown [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.628-631]. Kohli and Lee recommend at least five years of monitoring [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.616].

**Crown-root fractures with a retained fragment.** Harty's notes that in an uncomplicated crown-root fracture the coronal fragment is usually retained by the periodontal ligament, and that such teeth have a poor prognosis when the fracture line runs well below the gingival margin on the palatal aspect. The fragment is removed so that the extent of the fracture can be assessed and sealed [Harty's, n.d., p.257]. This step matters before any endodontic plan is fixed, because it changes whether the tooth can be kept [Harty's, n.d., p.257] [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Ellis Class IV = non-vital tooth, not a fracture pattern.
- Confirm necrosis on two or more signs.
- Fracture into the root: coronal fragment, both fragments, or coronal fragment with apical surgery.
- Non-vital incisors are brittle; protect the restoration.
- Adult extensive crown-root fracture often ends in extraction and implant.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling Ellis Class IV a fracture pattern.
2. Diagnosing necrosis on a single negative test.
3. Missing a root fracture on a single radiograph.
4. Restoring a non-vital incisor with a brittle restoration that does not protect the root.
