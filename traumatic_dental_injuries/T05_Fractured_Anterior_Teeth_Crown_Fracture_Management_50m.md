## STUDY MODE

**Definitions and classification.** A crown fracture is a break of the crown. The WHO-based clinical classification used by Andreasen et al separates enamel infraction (an incomplete crack with no tooth loss), uncomplicated crown fracture (loss of enamel and dentin without pulp exposure), complicated crown fracture (exposure of pulp), uncomplicated crown-root fracture and complicated crown-root fracture (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234). The older Ellis classification remains in use in exam questions. Sikri lists Ellis Class 1 as a simple fracture with little or no dentin involvement, Class 2 as an extensive fracture involving considerable dentin without the pulp, and Class 3 as an extensive fracture with pulp exposure (Sikri, n.d., p.484). Ellis Class 4 is a traumatised tooth that has become non-vital, with or without loss of crown, which is a status rather than a fracture pattern (Sikri, n.d., p.484). Sikri also lists the later Ellis and Davey and Andreasen modifications (p.483-484).

**Frequency.** Crown fractures make up 26 to 76% of dental injuries in the permanent dentition (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.319). Most involve the maxillary central incisors. Fractures without pulp involvement are more common than those with exposure (Berman et al, n.d., p.28).

**Clinical and radiographic assessment.** The history records the time since injury, which affects treatment. Examination covers extent of the fracture, pulp exposure, mobility, displacement and sensibility. Radiographs assess pulp size, root development, and any concomitant root fracture or luxation (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320). Enamel infraction lines are easier to see with a light beam directed perpendicular to the tooth (Berman et al, n.d., p.29). Pulp sensibility testing is done at baseline, and again at 6 to 8 weeks (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320).

**Prognosis by type.** For enamel infraction, Andreasen et al report pulp necrosis in 0 to 3.5%; for enamel fracture with no dentin exposed, 0 to 1.0%; for enamel-dentin fracture without pulp exposure, 0 to 6%; and for fractures with pulp exposure, prognosis depends on the pulp treatment chosen (p.320). In one long-term study of 198 bonded incisors, only one developed necrosis (0.5%) and four developed canal obliteration (2%), and all five affected teeth had a concomitant luxation (p.319).

**Pulpal response.** Exposed dentin causes only slight pulpal change that resolves if the vascular supply stays intact and bacteria are kept out (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.301). Pulp exposure starts an inflammatory response that is initially superficial, with fibrin covering the exposure. Untreated exposures show extensive granulation tissue after prolonged periods (p.301).

**Treatment of uncomplicated crown fractures (enamel and dentin, no pulp exposure).** Enamel infractions usually need no treatment, unless multiple lines stain and need sealing (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.301). Enamel fractures are smoothed or corrected by grinding, or restored with composite, with radiographic and sensibility follow-up (p.302, p.320). For enamel-dentin fractures, the goal is to seal the exposed dentin so that bacteria cannot reach the tubules. Andreasen et al describe a glass-ionomer bandage for provisional coverage, which needs no etch and bonds adequately to the fracture surface. Acrylic or celluloid crowns used as moulds may be used for esthetics, with calcium hydroxide or glass-ionomer beneath (p.304). The restoration should restore the crown anatomy and occlusion at once, because unrestored fractures allow drift and eruption of opposing teeth (p.302). Immediate reattachment of the fragment or composite restoration is generally preferred to a temporary crown, because a temporary crown carries leakage risk (p.302).

**Dentin protection: liner or bonding.** Andreasen et al note that hard-setting calcium hydroxide liners disintegrate under restorations and harbour bacteria, so they do not form a permanent barrier (p.303). The authors argue that a dentin bonding agent, applied with enamel etch, reduces microleakage and gives a larger bonding area (p.303). Berman et al take a more traditional line: calcium hydroxide is still the most frequently recommended capping agent, but hybridisation with a dentin-bonding agent has been advocated for a permanent seal (p.38). Hexem and Eisler note that the evidence for sealing exposed dentin is weak, but that sealing is still recommended in most circumstances to protect the pulp and improve interim aesthetics (2026, p.596).

**Treatment of complicated crown fractures (pulp exposure).** The pulp treatment is chosen by the size of the exposure, the time since injury and the stage of root development (Berman et al, n.d., p.34). Options are direct pulp capping, partial pulpotomy (Cvek), coronal or deep pulpotomy, or root canal treatment (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.304; Berman et al, n.d., p.34-37). Treatment aims to keep a vital, non-inflamed pulp walled off by a continuous hard-tissue barrier. Pulp capping or pulpotomy should not be done where degenerative or inflammatory change is expected, and no age limit applies per se (Berman et al, n.d., p.35). Cvek's follow-up protocol requires review over at least three years, with no symptoms, no radiographic pathology and continued root development in immature teeth (Berman et al, n.d., p.37). Kohli and Lee report partial pulpotomy success above 95% when 2 to 4 mm of inflamed pulp is excised and a bioceramic sealer is placed (2026, p.612). Andreasen et al do not quote a single success rate, and they describe the outcome as depending on exposure and time (p.624-630). Where the pulp is necrotic, root canal treatment follows (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.304).

**Restoration after pulp treatment.** Restoration options are composite resin (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.25), laminate veneers, or full crown coverage (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.304-305, p.313-317). Composite restoration is generally preferred for adults with modest fractures because it is conservative and reversible.

**Fragment reattachment.** Reattachment of the original fragment gives exact crown morphology, minimal chair time and a material that wears like the neighbouring teeth (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306). An intact enamel-dentin fragment with most of the enamel margin present is the main indication (p.306). Bonding the fragment increased fracture strength about threefold compared with acid-etch enamel alone (p.303). Animal data show the pulp stays safe while at least 0.2 mm of dentin remains between pulp and bonding surface (p.306). Berman et al add that the technique is becoming more attractive with modern bonding agents because of patient acceptance and reduced chair time (p.31). Hexem and Eisler mention that fragment loss after bonding is a recognised risk, citing a systematic review (2026, ref 24).

**Veneers.** Laminate veneers are indicated when the fracture is large, the aesthetics are demanding and the remaining enamel is sound. Contraindications and patient-selection problems are discussed in Andreasen et al (p.313-317).

**Adults and the 2024 question.** An adult central incisor with pulp exposure requires the same pulp decision, but the surrounding tissues change the plan. Kohli and Lee frame adult trauma around the same aims (pulp, periodontium and tooth retention) and recommend a monitoring period of at least five years (2026, p.609-611). Immature roots do not apply, so the closed-apex rule is the important one: a closed-apex tooth with necrosis needs root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide within seven to 10 days (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619).

**Interim and follow-up.** Provisional measures may be kept in place for about a month to let hard tissue deposit in the pulp horn, when the fracture is close to the pulp (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306). Pulp sensibility and radiographs are repeated at 3, 6 and 12 months, then annually for five years in Kohli and Lee's schedule (2026, p.612).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1 — Pulp capping versus pulpotomy, and the place of calcium hydroxide.** [Likely]
- *What the textbook states:* Andreasen et al treat exposed pulp by capping or partial pulpotomy when the pulp is vital and bleeds freshly, and do not give a single success figure (2007, p.630). Berman et al say calcium hydroxide remains the most recommended capping agent, while MTA is increasingly used and white MTA addresses discolouration (n.d., p.38-39).
- *What the 2026 DCNA review states:* partial pulpotomy with bioceramic sealing gives success above 95% (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612).
- *Agreement or challenge:* the sources agree that the pulp must be vital and that the barrier matters. The 95% figure is higher than any percentage in the textbooks read, and it comes from a narrative review that cites Cvek's work rather than a new trial.
- *EVIDENCE:* Berman's follow-up protocol requires three years of review (n.d., p.37). The 95% figure has no denominator in the review.
- *INTERPRETATION:* partial pulpotomy is a reasonable first choice for a vital, freshly exposed pulp, but a single figure should not be quoted as settled.
- *LIMITATION:* the choice between calcium hydroxide and MTA rests on different evidence bases; neither source gives a head-to-head trial for adult anterior fractures.

**Claim 2 — Dentin protection: calcium hydroxide liner or bonding agent.** [Likely]
- *What the textbook states:* Andreasen et al argue that calcium hydroxide liners break down and harbour bacteria, so bonding is preferred for microleakage (2007, p.303). Berman et al state that hard-setting calcium hydroxide gives no long-term seal but can induce a bridge, and that hybridisation with a bonding agent is advocated (n.d., p.38).
- *What the DCNA review states:* sealing lacks strong evidence but is still recommended to protect the pulp and interim aesthetics (Hexem and Eisler, 2026, p.596).
- *Agreement or challenge:* the sources agree that sealing matters and that calcium hydroxide alone is not a permanent seal. They differ on whether a bonding agent should replace the liner, and no source gives a trial comparing the two in adults.
- *LIMITATION:* the in vitro and animal basis of the bonding argument is acknowledged by Andreasen et al, who describe 1970s and 1980s experimental work (p.302-303).

**Claim 3 — Reattachment versus composite.** [Likely]
- *What the textbook states:* reattachment restores morphology and wears like enamel, with strength improved by bonding and a safe pulp at 0.2 mm of residual dentin (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306). Berman et al call it attractive with modern agents (n.d., p.31).
- *What the DCNA review states:* fragment loss is a recognised complication, supported by a systematic review (Hexem and Eisler, 2026).
- *Agreement or challenge:* the textbooks favour reattachment where the fragment is intact and large; the 2026 review adds a caution about loss. The two positions are compatible.
- *LIMITATION:* the strength and survival figures from Andreasen's in vitro and animal work are not matched by long-term clinical trials in the books read.

*Confidence:* the classification, the pulp-exposure principles and the need to seal exposed dentin are well established [Certain]. The superiority of partial pulpotomy over calcium hydroxide capping and of reattachment over composite in adults is not established [Guessing].

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** Crown fractures are the most common form of dental trauma in the permanent dentition, accounting for 26 to 76% of injuries [Andreasen et al, 2007]. The management question has three parts: whether the pulp is exposed, how much of the crown is lost, and what the patient needs aesthetically and functionally. Each choice is shaped by the patient's age and by the stage of root development. The aims are to protect the pulp, restore form and function, and monitor the tooth for late complications. A central incisor in an adult follows the same principles, but the long-term plan is built around the periodontium and around a monitoring period of at least five years [Andreasen et al, 2007; Kohli and Lee, 2026].

**Classification.** The WHO-based classification used in Andreasen et al names enamel infraction, uncomplicated and complicated crown fracture, uncomplicated and complicated crown-root fracture, and root fracture. Enamel infraction is an incomplete crack with no loss of tooth substance. An uncomplicated crown fracture loses enamel and dentin without exposing the pulp. A complicated crown fracture exposes the pulp. The Ellis grading still used in examinations runs from Class 1 (little dentin involved) through Class 2 (considerable dentin, pulp intact) and Class 3 (considerable dentin with pulp exposure) to Class 4 (a non-vital tooth). Class 4 describes pulp status, so a student who calls it a fracture pattern has made a conceptual error [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234; Sikri, n.d., p.484].

**Assessment.** Record the time since injury, because it determines whether a freshly exposed pulp can be saved. Examine the extent of the fracture, the pulp exposure (a light probe can create one, so avoid probing the dentin), the mobility, the displacement and the sensibility response. Look for a concomitant luxation, because it changes the plan and makes a dry operating field harder to achieve. Take periapical radiographs covering the pulp size, the root stage and any root fracture. Test sensibility at baseline and again at 6 to 8 weeks [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320; Berman et al, n.d., p.28-29].

**Uncomplicated fractures (no pulp exposure).** Enamel infractions usually need no treatment. Multiple infraction lines may be sealed with unfilled resin to prevent staining. Enamel fractures can be smoothed or corrected by grinding, or restored with composite, with radiographic and sensibility review. For enamel-dentin fractures the priority is to seal the dentin at once. The glass-ionomer bandage is a simple provisional option: no etch is needed, the glass ionomer adheres adequately to the fracture surface, and it can be built up in increments in emergency settings. An acrylic or celluloid crown can be moulded over a calcium hydroxide or glass-ionomer layer where aesthetics are urgent. Where the periodontium is also injured, the splint itself can cover the exposed dentin, with a liner beneath [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.301-304]. The IADT rehydrates an intact fragment for 20 minutes and places calcium hydroxide where dentin is within 0.5 mm of the pulp [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.319].

**Restoring the crown.** Restore the crown anatomy and the occlusion at once. Unrestored fractures allow the adjacent teeth to drift or tilt into the gap, and the opposing incisor may over-erupt [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.302]. Immediate reattachment or composite is preferred over a temporary crown, because a temporary crown can leak, and leakage admits bacteria to the dentinal tubules [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.302].

**Liner or bonding.** The dentin beneath a restoration must be sealed. Andreasen et al argue that hard-setting calcium hydroxide liners break down with time and are infiltrated by cultivable bacteria, so they cannot serve as a permanent barrier. A dentin-bonding agent used with enamel etching gives a larger bonding area and reduces microleakage, and the authors advocate it on that basis [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.303]. Berman et al note that calcium hydroxide remains the most recommended capping material, while hybridisation with a bonding agent is advocated for a permanent seal [Berman et al, n.d., p.38]. The clinician should therefore choose the liner by the depth of the exposed dentin: calcium hydroxide where the dentin is deep and a bridge is wanted, and bonding where the dentin is more superficial and the bonding area matters.

**Complicated fractures (pulp exposure).** The pulp choice depends on three factors: the size of the exposure, the time since injury and the stage of root development [Berman et al, n.d., p.34]. A vital pulp that is freshly exposed and bleeds is treated by direct capping or partial pulpotomy, with calcium hydroxide or MTA over the exposure to encourage a hard-tissue barrier [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.630; Berman et al, n.d., p.36-38]. Coronal or deep pulpotomy is used for larger or older exposures. Capping and pulpotomy are not appropriate where degenerative or inflammatory change is expected [Berman et al, n.d., p.35]. Where the pulp is necrotic, root canal treatment is indicated. An immature tooth with a necrotic pulp is managed by apexification or, in some protocols, by regenerative endodontics [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.304; Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612]. The IADT prefers partial pulpotomy, including in complete roots, with non-setting calcium hydroxide or calcium silicate [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.320]; pulp removal is usually indicated in mature crown-root fractures (p.322).

**Outcome figures.** Andreasen et al report pulp necrosis of 0 to 3.5% after enamel infraction, 0 to 1% after enamel fracture, and 0 to 6% after enamel-dentin fracture without exposure [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320]. In a long-term series of 198 bonded fragments, one tooth (0.5%) developed necrosis and four (2%) developed canal obliteration; all five had a concomitant luxation [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.319]. The 2026 DCNA review reports success above 95% for partial pulpotomy in complicated fractures, citing Cvek's work [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612]. The textbook does not quote a single figure, so the examiner should present the review's figure as a reported success rate rather than a fact. Krastl et al [2022] report pulp survival of 90-100% after pulpotomy in immature and 70-100% in mature teeth, on observational data [p.1007].

**Restoration options.** Reattachment is the first choice where one intact fragment is available and most of the enamel margin is present. It restores exact morphology, needs little chair time, and wears like the neighbouring enamel. Bonding roughly triples the fracture strength compared with acid-etch enamel alone [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.303]. Animal data indicate that the pulp remains safe while at least 0.2 mm of dentin separates the pulp from the bonding surface [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306]. Composite resin is the conservative default when the fragment is lost or unsuitable; it can be built up in increments and modified later [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.304]. Laminate veneers suit large fractures with sound enamel and high aesthetic demands, and their indications, contraindications and patient-selection problems are set out in Andreasen et al (p.313-317). Full crown coverage is reserved for extensive loss of tooth structure.

**Fragment loss.** The 2026 DCNA review recognises that bonded fragments can be lost, and cites a systematic review of bonded-fragment loss in crown-fractured anterior teeth [Hexem and Eisler, 2026]. Loss is a reason to keep the fragment safe, to counsel the patient about activity and to plan a re-bonding or a composite if the fragment fails.

**Interim management.** Where the fracture lies close to the pulp, a provisional restoration may be left in place for about a month. This allows a hard-tissue barrier to form in the pulp horn before final restoration [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306]. Temporary crowns carry the leakage risk noted above, so a glass-ionomer or composite interim is often preferred when the periodontium is also injured [Berman et al, n.d.].

**Adult considerations.** An adult central incisor is managed for pulp, periodontium and retention. The aims are the same as in children, but the time frame for monitoring is at least five years, and the restorative plan must respect the periodontal condition [Kohli and Lee, 2026]. Where the pulp becomes necrotic in a closed-apex tooth, root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide is indicated within seven to 10 days [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619]. Complicated crown-root fractures in adults often end in extraction and replacement, and orthodontic or surgical extrusion may be considered where enough tooth remains [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612-613].

**Follow-up.** Test pulp sensibility at 3, 6 and 12 months, then yearly for five years, and check radiographs at each visit. Cvek's protocol for pulp capping and pulpotomy requires follow-up over at least three years, with no symptoms, no radiographic pathosis and continued root development in immature teeth [Berman et al, n.d., p.37]. Check restorations for marginal leakage, composite fracture and discolouration [Andreasen et al, 2007; Kohli and Lee, 2026]. The IADT review is at six to eight weeks and one year [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.319].

**Summary by situation.**

| Situation | First step | Definitive step | Key check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enamel infraction | none, or seal lines | none | review for stain and sensitivity |
| Enamel fracture | smooth edges | grind or composite | pulp sensibility and radiograph |
| Enamel-dentin, pulp intact | seal dentin (GIC bandage, liner) | reattach, composite, veneer or crown | sensibility at 6-8 weeks, then 3, 6, 12 months |

| Immature, pulp necrotic | apexification or regenerative procedure | restore | root development |

**Points of disagreement.** Three points should be stated as contested. First, the best liner beneath a restoration: Andreasen et al prefer bonding, while Berman et al retain calcium hydroxide for capping [Andreasen et al, 2007; Berman et al, n.d.]. Second, the success figure for partial pulpotomy, which the 2026 review puts above 95% but the textbooks do not quantify [Kohli and Lee, 2026]. Third, the need for definitive restoration in adults, where the 2026 review favours a phased approach [Kohli and Lee, 2026; Hexem and Eisler, 2026].

**Uncomplicated crown-root fractures.** Harty's notes that in an uncomplicated crown-root fracture the coronal fragment is usually retained by the periodontal ligament, and that such teeth have a poor prognosis when the fracture line runs well below the gingival margin on the palatal side. The fragment is removed so that the extent can be assessed and the fracture sealed [Harty's, n.d., p.257] [Likely].

**Follow-up schedule.** Harty's table of follow-up for crown fractures sets clinical and radiographic review at intervals up to one year after injury, with splint removal where a splint was used [Harty's, n.d., p.264] [Likely]. Cohen's sensibility schedule is 2 to 4 and 6 to 8 weeks, then 3, 6 and 12 months and yearly [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560].

**Adhesive advances.** Hexem and Eisler report that improved bonding systems allow predictable reattachment of fractured fragments when they are available, preserve natural structure and shade, and are generally less costly than full-coverage restorations. They note favourable long-term survival, especially with fibre reinforcement or protective restorations, and advise patients to bring any fragment to the appointment [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.606] [Likely].

**Conclusion.** Management of a fractured central incisor is driven by pulp status, fragment integrity and the patient's age and aesthetic demands. Seal exposed dentin at once, choose the pulp treatment by exposure size, time and root stage, reattach where an intact fragment allows, and otherwise restore with composite or veneer. Monitor the pulp for at least five years. The sources differ on the best dentin liner and on the success figures for partial pulpotomy, and the examiner should state both.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Crown fractures are 26-76% of permanent dentition injuries.
- Ellis Class 3 = extensive fracture with pulp exposure; Class 4 = non-vital tooth (status, not fracture).
- Seal exposed dentin at once; GIC bandage for provisional cover.
- Calcium hydroxide gives no long-term seal; bonding offers a permanent barrier in theory.
- Vital, freshly exposed pulp: capping or partial pulpotomy; necrotic pulp: root canal treatment.
- Reattachment: intact fragment, about threefold bond strength, at least 0.2 mm dentin.
- Closed-apex necrosis: root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide within 7-10 days.
- Monitor for at least five years.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling Ellis Class 4 a fracture type rather than a non-vital status.
2. Leaving exposed dentin unsealed while waiting for definitive treatment.
3. Quoting a partial pulpotomy success rate without its source and denominator.
4. Reattaching a fragment that is small, damaged or without most of its enamel margin.
5. Forgetting the three-year-plus follow-up for capping and pulpotomy.
