## STUDY MODE

**The older view.** Replantation was traditionally treated as a temporary measure, because many replanted teeth eventually succumb to root resorption (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.482). Extended dry time was often a reason to refuse replantation outright.

**The newer view, in four parts.**

1. *Replant even when the prognosis looks poor.* The time of injury is often not the moment to make definitive decisions. Even an extended extra-oral period, including dry storage, is "not an absolute contraindication to replantation". The tooth can be replanted and treated as a temporary restoration until definitive planning is done (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.475).

2. *Delay the replantation to protect the socket and the root.* For a tooth with prolonged dry time and a contused socket, Andreasen et al describe delayed replantation; their example was kept dry for 24 hours. The sequence is: heal the contused socket, treat the root surface to resist ankylosis, then complete endodontic treatment to prevent inflammatory resorption (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.478). A tooth with an avital PDL may heal only by ankylosis, so the aim becomes slowing resorption rather than restoring a ligament (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.479).

3. *Treat the root surface chemically.* Sodium fluoride 2.4%, pH 5.5, for 20 minutes, after scraping away the necrotic PDL, is reported to cut progression of root resorption by about 50% in replanted human teeth. The effect is attributed to fluoride incorporated into cementum and dentine (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477-478).

4. *Replantation can be a long-term option.* Cases have been reported in which replanted teeth stayed in service for 20 to 40 years with a normal periodontium, clinically and radiographically (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.482). Rigid splinting is avoided, since it increases resorption, and endodontic timing is matched to the resorption risk: delayed pulp extirpation beyond 20 days accelerates inflammatory resorption (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477, 488).

**Sequence of replantation (the 5m repeat).** Andreasen's sequence is: rinse the tooth with saline if it is contaminated, without scrubbing; rinse the socket to remove the coagulum; replant with light digital pressure and reposition any socket wall; splint with an acid-etch resin splint for about one week; give tetanus prophylaxis; take a radiograph to confirm position; begin endodontic treatment about one week after replantation; review radiographs at 2 and 3 weeks (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.475-477).

## EXAM MODE

**Principle.** Replantation has moved from a temporary, last-resort measure to a deliberate treatment that is planned around the healing biology. The clinician protects the PDL and the socket, slows resorption where it cannot be prevented, and times the endodontics to the resorption risk [Andreasen et al, 2007].

**Replant despite poor prognosis.** Extended dry time is not an absolute contraindication. The tooth can be replanted as a temporary restoration while definitive planning is carried out. Decisions made at the scene are often provisional [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.475].

**Delayed replantation.** Andreasen et al heal a contused socket first, then treat the root surface and complete endodontic treatment [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.478]. The IADT regards delayed replantation as having a poor long-term prognosis, with ankylosis expected [Fouad et al, 2020, p.334].

**Root-surface treatment.** Sodium fluoride 2.4% for 20 minutes is reported to halve root resorption progression [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477]. The IADT does not mention fluoride.

**Long-term outcome.** Replanted teeth can function for decades. Reports cite 20 to 40 years with normal periodontal status [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.482] [Likely].

**Endodontic timing.** Delayed pulp extirpation beyond 20 days accelerates inflammatory resorption [Likely]. Treat early, with calcium hydroxide if resorption starts [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477, 488].

**Splinting.** Use a passive flexible splint for two weeks, or four weeks if the alveolus or jaw is fractured [Fouad et al, 2020, p.334-335]. Andreasen et al note that rigid splinting increases resorption and that one week is normally sufficient [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477].

**Sequence at the chair.** Replant gently, splint flexibly for two weeks, give amoxicillin or penicillin, and check tetanus status [Fouad et al, 2020, p.334-336]. A closed apex needs root canal treatment within two weeks; Andreasen starts it at about one week [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477]. Review at 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 3 and 6 months, one year, then yearly (p.337).

**Why the change.** Earlier practice treated a long dry time as a reason to discard the tooth. The current approach weighs the likely survival of the PDL, the condition of the socket and the patient's options. Andreasen et al note that the time of injury is often the wrong moment for definitive decisions, so a poor prognosis is a reason to replant provisionally, not to refuse replantation [Andreasen et al, 2007].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Extended dry time is not an absolute contraindication to replantation.
- Delayed replantation: socket healing, then root-surface treatment, then endodontics.
- Sodium fluoride 2.4% pH 5.5, 20 min: about 50% less resorption progression.
- Avital PDL: ankylosis is the only possible healing mode.
- Replanted teeth in service 20 to 40 years in reported cases.
- Splint one week; delayed extirpation beyond 20 days accelerates resorption.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling replantation a temporary procedure without acknowledging long-term survival.
2. Replanting a long-dry tooth at once when the textbook plan is delayed replantation.
3. Quoting the fluoride reduction without its animal-to-human context and the 50% figure.
4. Splinting for weeks.
5. Starting endodontics too late, after resorption is established.

## AUDIT NOTE (IADT 2020, checked 2026-10-19)

**Audit note (IADT 2020; Kumari et al, 2025, checked 2026-10-19).** Corrections to the earlier draft: the IADT splint is two weeks, not one week (Fouad et al, 2020, p.334-335); the root canal window for a closed apex is two weeks, not one week (p.334); the first antibiotic choice is amoxicillin or penicillin (p.336). The IADT does not mention fluoride root treatment (no match in the guideline text), and it states that delayed replantation has a poor long-term prognosis (p.334). The Andreasen delayed-replantation and fluoride protocol is kept as a textbook option. The 2025 splinting review (Kumari et al, 2025, PDF p.1) supports flexible, short splints but rates the evidence very low.
