# T06 — Diagnosis, treatment plan and management of traumatic fracture of maxillary anterior teeth in an 18-year-old

**Marks:** 20 (Long Essay)
**Repository sitting:** May 2014 (END §14, RG PII Cons RS-3)
**Question wording (repository):** "Diagnosis, treatment plan and management of traumatic fracture of maxillary anterior teeth in a 18 years old patient"
**Folded repeats:** none
**Status:** DRAFTED, audited against IADT 2020 on 2026-10-19 (see audit note).

**Assumption stated.** The case is an uncomplicated or complicated crown fracture of a maxillary central incisor. The answer covers both possibilities because the diagnosis decides the plan.

## STUDY MODE

**The patient.** An 18-year-old is close to skeletal maturity, but the sources do not give a single age at which growth ends, and the clinician should check for growth potential. Hexem and Eisler note that teenagers still have ongoing craniofacial growth, which complicates restorative planning, and that restorative work in this group should aim to preserve alveolar bone and gingiva until maturity is reached (2026, Part 1, p.589). Andreasen et al add that in trauma of the permanent dentition the first question is whether any growth potential remains, especially where skeletal deviations are present (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.24, p.686).

**Clinical examination.** Record the extent of the fracture (enamel, enamel-dentin, pulp exposure), mobility, displacement, percussion and sensibility response at baseline. Examine the adjacent teeth and the opposing arch, because the occlusion and any concomitant luxation change the plan (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320; Berman et al, n.d., p.28-29). Measure the overjet. An overjet above 6 mm raises the risk of injury and the severity of injury (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.24, p.685). Assess the lip seal, because inadequate lip coverage is linked to higher risk (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.243).

**Radiographs.** Take periapical views at more than one angle, and an occlusal view where needed. The radiographs establish the pulp size, the root stage, any root fracture and any concomitant luxation (Berman et al, n.d., p.74; Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320). The radiograph also serves as a baseline for later comparison (Berman et al, n.d., p.33).

**Diagnosis.** The diagnosis is either uncomplicated crown fracture (enamel-dentin, pulp intact) or complicated crown fracture (pulp exposed). Use the WHO-based classification and, if asked, the Ellis grade (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234; Sikri, n.d., p.484). Pulp vitality is judged on the sensibility response and the bleeding at any exposure. A fresh, bleeding exposure favours capping or pulpotomy; an old exposure or a non-responding tooth points to root canal treatment (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.630; Berman et al, n.d., p.34).

**Options and choice.**

*Reattachment.* If an intact fragment with most of the enamel margin is available, reattachment gives exact morphology, short chair time and a material that wears like enamel (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306). Berman's guide notes that big fragments are easier to bond and that small or multiple fragments are difficult (Berman et al, n.d., p.31).

*Composite resin.* This is the usual definitive restoration for crown fractures. Andreasen et al give a survival record, with fracture of the composite in 9 to 14% of restorations in one study, and state that the commonest failures are marginal leakage, bulk discolouration and fracture (2007, p.732-734). Berman's guide recommends a silicone reference guide as the most predictable technique for an anterior composite (n.d., p.30).

*Laminate veneer.* Indicated for larger fractures with sound enamel and a high aesthetic demand, with contraindications and patient-selection problems (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.313-317).

*Full crown.* Reserved for extensive loss of tooth structure, and it involves more tooth removal in a young patient. The choice should follow the remaining structure and the patient's wishes.

**Timing and sequence.** Andreasen et al state that for crown fractures without pulp involvement, an observation period of about three months before orthodontic or definitive treatment is sufficient (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.24, p.687). Provisional sealing, with glass ionomer or a temporary crown, bridges the interval. Immediate reattachment or composite is preferred to a temporary crown in most cases (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.302).

**Orthodontic and occlusal review.** The occlusion must be checked. Adjacent teeth drift or tilt into an unrestored gap, and the opposing incisor can over-erupt (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.302). Where the overjet is large, orthodontic treatment may be needed before a final restoration, and the sequence should be coordinated (Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.24, p.685-687).

**Follow-up.** Test pulp sensibility at baseline and at six to eight weeks, and review the restoration and the periodontium. Kohli and Lee recommend sensibility testing at 3, 6 and 12 months, then yearly for five years in pulp-injured teeth (2026, p.612-613).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim — choosing between reattachment and composite in an 18-year-old.** [Likely]
- *Textbook:* reattachment is preferred where the fragment is intact and large, because of morphology and wear (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306); composite is the usual alternative (Berman et al, n.d., p.27).
- *DCNA:* fragment reattachment is a recognised option, and the 2026 review notes the risk of fragment loss (Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 1, ref 24).
- *Agreement:* both favour reattachment for a suitable fragment and composite otherwise.
- *LIMITATION:* the long-term survival of reattached fragments in adolescents is not quantified in the sources read.

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** An 18-year-old with a maxillary anterior fracture needs a diagnosis that covers the pulp, the fracture, the occlusion and the growth status, followed by a staged plan that protects the tooth and restores aesthetics and function. The answer assumes a maxillary central incisor, uncomplicated or complicated [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234].

**History.** Take the medical and dental history, the medications and allergies, and the mechanism and time of injury. Ask about previous injuries, spontaneous pain, sensitivity to temperature and sweet or sour foods, pain on touch or chewing, and any disturbance in the bite [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.289]. Exclude serious injury first, and refer at once where the mechanism points to more force than the dental damage [Berman et al, n.d., p.74].

**Clinical examination.** Record the extent of the fracture, pulp exposure, mobility, displacement and sensibility at baseline. Examine the adjacent and opposing teeth. Measure the overjet: an overjet above 6 mm raises the risk and severity of injury [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.24, p.685]. Assess the lip seal, because inadequate lip coverage is linked to a higher risk [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.243].

**Radiographs.** For an uncomplicated crown fracture the IADT recommends one parallel periapical radiograph, with more only if other injury is suspected [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.319]. The films show pulp size, root stage, any root fracture and luxation, and serve as the baseline for review [Berman et al, n.d., p.33, p.74; Andreasen et al, 2007, p.320].

**Diagnosis.** Uncomplicated (enamel-dentin, pulp intact) or complicated (pulp exposed), with the Ellis grade where examined [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234; Sikri, n.d., p.484]. Judge pulp vitality on sensibility and on bleeding at any exposure. Sensibility should be repeated because a negative early result does not prove necrosis, and blood flow can take up to nine months to return [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560].

**Treatment-planning factors.** Pulp status, root development and remaining tooth structure decide the plan. The objectives are symptom relief, pulp preservation, retention and, in a growing patient, support of alveolar growth [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.609-610]. At 18, most growth is complete, but the clinician should check growth potential, since teenagers still grow [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 1, p.589].

**Restorative options in order of preference.** Reattachment of an intact fragment with most of the enamel margin present, which gives morphology and wear like enamel [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.306]. Composite, the usual definitive restoration, with a silicone reference guide for the most predictable anterior result [Berman et al, n.d., p.30]. Laminate veneers for larger fractures with sound enamel [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.313-317]. Full crowns for extensive loss, as a last choice in a young patient [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.304].

**Timing.** For crown fractures without pulp involvement, observation for about three months before definitive or orthodontic treatment is sufficient [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.24, p.687]. Provisional sealing bridges the interval.

**Orthodontic and occlusal review.** Adjacent teeth drift into an unrestored gap, and the opposing incisor can over-erupt [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.302]. A large overjet may need orthodontic management before the final restoration, and the sequence should be coordinated [Andreasen et al, 2007, Ch.24, p.685-687].

**Delay and its cost.** Delayed or neglected trauma can lead to avoidable tooth loss, and later endodontic treatment is more complicated [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.603]. Cost and access matter; patients from under-resourced households are more likely to delay definitive care [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.604].

**Follow-up.** Sensibility at baseline and at six to eight weeks. Harty's table for an uncomplicated fracture sets review at six to eight weeks, four months, six months and one year [Harty's, n.d., p.264] [Likely]. Kohli and Lee recommend sensibility at 3, 6 and 12 months and yearly for five years where pulp injury is present [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.612-613]. IADT review: six to eight weeks and one year [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.319].

**Communication and psychology.** Explain the diagnosis, the options, their longevity and costs, and the review plan. Hexem and Eisler advise trauma-informed language, the risks of no treatment, and telephone follow-up to improve compliance [Hexem and Eisler, 2026, Part 2, p.604]. Berman's guide notes that a physical injury need not be severe to produce distress, and that a visible injury in a young person can cause lasting psychological effects, so the clinician should ask about anxiety and self-consciousness [Berman et al, n.d., p.190-191] [Likely].

**Conclusion.** Diagnose the pulp, the fracture, the occlusion and the growth status. Protect the pulp and seal the dentin. Reattach an intact fragment; otherwise restore with composite or veneer. Review the pulp for at least five years.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Check growth potential first; teenagers still grow.
- Overjet above 6 mm raises injury risk; check lip seal.
- Pulp decision by exposure size, time and root stage.
- Reattachment for an intact fragment; composite otherwise.
- Crown fractures without pulp involvement: about three months' observation before definitive treatment.
- Sensibility at six to eight weeks; long follow-up.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Starting definitive treatment before the pulp has been assessed over time.
2. Forgetting the overjet and lip seal.
3. Choosing a full crown first without considering reattachment or composite.
4. Ignoring growth in a teenager.
