## STUDY MODE

**What it is.** Avulsion is total displacement of the tooth from its socket (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.460). It forms 0.5 to 3% of traumatic injuries in the permanent dentition and most often affects maxillary central incisors in children aged 7 to 9 (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.460).

**Why time and medium decide the outcome.** Periodontal healing depends on the PDL left on the root staying hydrated. Dry storage leads to cell necrosis and compromised healing (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.486). Speed of replantation is the single most important factor for a good outcome (Kahler, n.d., p.2635). Pulpal necrosis is expected after every avulsion (Kahler, n.d., p.2634).

**Emergency care.** Replant at once. If the tooth is dirty, rinse it for 10 seconds under cold running water (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.475). If replanting is impossible, store it in milk, saline or saliva; water is the least desirable medium (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.475; Kahler, n.d., p.2636).

**Clinical care.** Record the extra-alveolar time and storage. Do not scrub or sterilise the root. Rinse with saline, flush the socket of coagulum, and reposition any displaced socket wall with light digital pressure before replanting (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.475-477). Splint with an acid-etch resin splint for about one week, since rigid splinting increases resorption (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477). Give tetanus prophylaxis (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477).

**Endodontic timing.** Closed-apex teeth need root canal treatment, because pulp necrosis can be anticipated (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477). Andreasen et al delay treatment about one week after replantation, to avoid ankylosis and to let the PDL fibres splint. Trope (n.d., p.341) starts the clock from the injury: treat within 7 to 10 days, before the necrotic pulp becomes infected. Paranjpe and Noblett (2021, p.220) give 7 to 10 days after replantation. The sources agree on about a week but not on the starting point.

**Antibiotics.** Andreasen calls the value of systemic antibiotics questionable (p.477). Harty's records the IADT position: doxycycline twice daily for one week for patients over 12, and penicillin V for younger patients (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, n.d., p.263).

**Follow-up.** Radiograph at 2 and 3 weeks for early resorption (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.477). Resorption is more likely with crown damage, contamination, or dry time over 15 minutes (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.481).

## EXAM MODE

**Principle.** Two things decide the outcome: how long the tooth was out of the socket, and whether the PDL on the root stayed alive. The management aims to keep the PDL viable, prevent infection of the necrotic pulp, and limit root resorption.

**Definition and frequency.** Avulsion is complete displacement of a tooth out of its socket. It makes up 0.5 to 3% of traumatic injuries in permanent teeth and most often affects maxillary central incisors in children aged 7 to 9 [Andreasen et al, 2007] [Likely].

**Emergency phase.** Replant at once. If the tooth is dirty, rinse it gently in milk, saline or saliva, then replant it [Fouad et al, 2020, p.333]. If replantation is delayed, use milk, HBSS, saliva or saline in that order; water is poor but better than drying (p.333).

**Clinical phase.** Record the dry time and medium. After 30 minutes most PDL cells are non-viable; 60 minutes is the IADT management boundary [Fouad et al, 2020, p.333-334]. Splint with a passive flexible wire of up to 0.4 mm, or nylon, for two weeks; use four weeks if the alveolus or jaw is fractured (p.334-335). Check tetanus status (p.334).

**Endodontic phase.** A closed apex needs root canal treatment within two weeks of replantation [Fouad et al, 2020, p.334]. An open apex aims for revascularisation; treat only when necrosis and infection are confirmed (p.335). Calcium hydroxide may be used intracanal for up to one month (p.337). Amoxicillin or penicillin are the first antibiotic choices; tetracycline and doxycycline are generally not recommended under 12 years (p.336).

**Follow-up.** Review at 2 weeks (splint removal), 4 weeks, 3 and 6 months, one year, and yearly for at least five years [Fouad et al, 2020, p.337]. An open apex is reviewed at 1, 2, 3 and 6 months as well (p.338).

**Prognosis.** Depends on dry time, medium and root stage. Delayed replantation has a poor long-term prognosis: the ligament becomes necrotic and replacement resorption is expected [Fouad et al, 2020, p.334].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Replant immediately; speed is the most important factor.
- Rinse 10 s under cold running water; never scrub the root.
- Storage: milk, saline or saliva. Water is least desirable.
- Over 60 min dry: marked resorption and poor PDL survival.
- Splint for about one week only.
- Closed apex: root canal treatment about one week after replantation (or 7 to 10 days from injury, per Trope).
- Radiographs at 2 and 3 weeks; tetanus prophylaxis.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Scrubbing or sterilising the root, which destroys PDL and cementum.
2. Storing the tooth in water.
3. Splinting for many weeks; the textbook figure is about one week.
4. Starting root canal treatment immediately instead of after about one week.
5. Forgetting tetanus prophylaxis and the 2- and 3-week radiographs.
