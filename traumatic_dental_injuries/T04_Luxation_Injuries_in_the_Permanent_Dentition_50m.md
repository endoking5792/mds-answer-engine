## STUDY MODE

**Definition and classification.** A luxation injury is damage to the tooth-supporting structures in which the tooth is loosened or displaced, but the tooth is not out of its socket. The WHO-based clinical classification used by Andreasen et al lists concussion, subluxation, extrusive luxation, lateral luxation and intrusive luxation, and treats avulsion separately (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.233-234). Concussion is an injury with percussion sensitivity but no abnormal mobility or displacement. Subluxation is abnormal loosening without displacement. Extrusive luxation is partial displacement out of the socket. Lateral luxation is displacement in a direction other than axial, usually with comminution or fracture of the socket. Intrusive luxation is displacement into the alveolar bone, again usually with socket fracture (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.234). Lateral luxation with apical displacement, where the apex has broken through the labial cortical plate, is a distinct presentation (Berman et al, n.d., p.75).

**Epidemiology.** Andreasen et al give luxation as 15 to 61% of traumatic injuries in permanent teeth in their text (p.388), but the chapter's essentials give 15 to 40% (p.411). The two figures are not reconciled in the book, and the answer should cite the range as "15 to 40% in the summary, 15 to 61% in the text" rather than choose one. Maxillary central incisors are most often involved (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.233-234; Ogle, 2024). Kohli and Lee describe a peak incidence in children aged 7 to 11 and note that many injuries occur in adults as well (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.609).

**Diagnosis.** The clinical examination records the direction of displacement, mobility, percussion response and sensibility response. Radiographs are taken at several angles, because a single view can miss root fracture or apical displacement (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.388-389, p.411; Berman et al, n.d., p.74). Berman's guide stresses that a periapical radiograph set should be taken at different angulations and that the root stage of formation affects pulp prognosis (Berman et al, n.d., p.74). Andreasen et al note that the clinical diagnosis of luxation depends on combining clinical and radiographic findings and that a film holder reduces measurement error (p.388-389).

**Pulp status and pulp necrosis.** Pulp necrosis after luxation is common. The frequency in permanent teeth ranges from 15 to 59% across the studies Andreasen et al cite (p.393). Two factors predict necrosis: the type of luxation and the stage of root development (p.393-394). By type, pulp necrosis was 3% after concussion, 6% after subluxation, 26% after extrusive luxation, 58% after lateral luxation and 85% after intrusive luxation (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.394, Table 13.3). The stage of root development matters too: necrosis is more frequent when the root is fully formed (p.394).

Kohli and Lee caution that sensibility tests give negative results for up to six months after a luxation injury, and that endodontic treatment should not follow a single negative test. They advise a diagnosis of pulp necrosis when at least two signs are present, such as negative sensibility plus discolouration, mobility, periapical change or a resorptive defect, with CBCT where available (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610-611). Andreasen et al give a similar rule: primarily two or more of crown discolouration, negative electrical or laser Doppler testing and periapical radiolucency, with transient negative sensibility considered over two to three months (p.399). Andreasen et al also state that a pulp that appears necrotic may still revascularise, and that a small error rate of endodontic treatment on revascularising pulps is accepted (p.399).

**Management by type.**

*Concussion.* No splint is needed. Examination and sensibility testing at baseline and follow-up are the core. The follow-up interval is four to six weeks in Andreasen et al (p.421, p.426). Kohli and Lee advise review at one week, six to eight weeks and one year (p.611).

*Subluxation.* Splinting is usually not needed; slight occlusal reduction helps if chewing is painful. Splint only for significant mobility or in very young or compromised patients (Berman et al, n.d., p.80). Kohli and Lee suggest a flexible splint for up to two weeks where mobility affects comfort (p.611). IADT (as reported by Ogle, 2024) recommends two weeks.

*Extrusive luxation.* Reposition the tooth gently with axial finger pressure on the incisal edge, then splint (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.431, Fig 15.5; p.442). Repositioning should be done early: after three to four days, extruded or laterally luxated teeth are harder to reposition (p.431). Andreasen et al give a splint period of two to three weeks for extrusion (p.442). IADT (Ogle, 2024) gives two weeks. A flexible acid-etch resin splint is the usual choice (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.442; p.428).

*Lateral luxation.* Local anaesthesia is needed before forceful repositioning (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.442). The repositioning movement is traumatic, so the tooth is moved back gently. Andreasen et al describe repositioning the labial cortical plate and then the tooth, and splinting for three weeks; if marginal bone breaks down, extend fixation to six to eight weeks (p.442). IADT (Ogle, 2024) gives four weeks. Berman's guide adds a clinical point: if the apex is displaced through the labial plate, the tooth may need to be pushed or pulled coronally to release the apex before repositioning, and a wedged tooth may need the socket manipulated (Berman et al, n.d., p.80). Lateral luxation with apical displacement is very likely to lose pulp vitality. Mature teeth are likely to need root canal treatment, while immature teeth should be given time to revascularise and monitored (Berman et al, n.d., p.75).

*Intrusive luxation.* Spontaneous re-eruption is expected in incompletely formed roots (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.458). Spontaneous eruption has been seen up to about 17 years of age; beyond that, active repositioning is advised (p.449). Surgical repositioning is the treatment of choice for multiple or deep intrusions (more than 7 mm) (p.458). Orthodontic extrusion is preferred to surgical repositioning because marginal bone healing is slightly better (p.458). Berman's guide suggests that teeth intruded less than 3 mm may re-erupt on their own, and that spontaneous eruption is unusual beyond 6 mm; orthodontic extrusion, if chosen, should start within three to four weeks (Berman et al, n.d., p.84). Andreasen et al expect the tooth to be repositioned sufficiently within two to three weeks (p.449). Radiographic controls are needed during re-eruption, and periapical radiolucency or inflammatory resorption calls for endodontic treatment (p.459).

*Avulsion* is covered in T13–T14 and is not repeated here.

**Splinting.** Andreasen et al state that splinting should be semi-rigid or rigid, and last four to six weeks, for root fractures (p.865). For luxation injuries, the splint length depends on the injury and on socket involvement: about two to three weeks when ankylosis is not a risk, and one to two additional weeks when there is a socket or bone fracture (p.865). The gingival fibres heal within about one week (p.865). Andreasen et al also say that replantation into an intact socket needs no more than seven to 10 days to prevent ankylosis (p.864). Flexible splints are preferred because rigid splinting has been associated with poorer healing and more resorption (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858; resorption link also in Ch.17, p.477). The IADT (as reported by Ogle, 2024) is two weeks for subluxation and extrusion, and four weeks for lateral and intrusive luxation. Ogle notes that where the AAE recommends two weeks for avulsion regardless of dry time, the IADT and AAE differ (Ogle, 2024, p.158-159).

**Endodontic decision.** Two approaches are in tension. Andreasen et al accept observation for up to about one year in selected cases: where the patient is over 10 years old, the root is fully formed, the injury was not an intrusion, and transient apical change is expected (p.399). Active treatment is indicated when radiolucency, inflammatory resorption or crown discolouration appears (p.459), and prophylactic pulp extirpation is considered for fully formed roots after lateral luxation or intrusion, to prevent discolouration or progressive resorption (p.399). Kohli and Lee take a firmer line: treatment is indicated on two signs of necrosis, and for a mature tooth with a closed apex, root canal treatment with calcium hydroxide within seven to 10 days is their recommendation (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610, p.619). The sources agree on the diagnostic criteria but differ on how quickly to treat.

**Follow-up.** Long-term review is essential. Kohli and Lee recommend five years of monitoring, with sensibility tests at baseline and at each visit (2026, p.611). Andreasen et al note that pulp canal obliteration is a late sequel, reported in 3 to 35% of luxated teeth, and that late pulp necrosis can follow canal obliteration in 7 to 16% (p.401, p.413).

**Complications.** The prognostic table in Andreasen et al lists pulp necrosis at 15 to 52%, pulp canal obliteration at 3 to 35%, root surface resorption at 1 to 18% (greatest after intrusion), internal root resorption at 2%, and loss of supporting bone at 5% (p.413). Bone loss by type is 0% after concussion, 1% after subluxation, 6% after extrusion, 7% after lateral luxation and 31% after intrusion (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.411). Delayed reduction increases the risk of supporting-tissue damage (p.411). Andreasen et al also note that loss of marginal bone increases with the patient's age, the duration of splinting and the extent of displacement (p.411).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1 — How often and how early to root-treat a luxated tooth.** [Likely]
- *What the textbook states:* Andreasen et al accept observation for up to a year in low-risk patients and treat on radiographic or clinical signs (p.399, p.459).
- *What the 2026 DCNA review states:* root canal treatment is indicated once two signs of necrosis are present, with CBCT where available (Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.610-611).
- *Agreement or challenge:* both use a multi-sign rule, but the textbook accepts a longer observation period. The difference is partly one of era: Andreasen's chapter is from 2007, and the DCNA review draws on newer imaging.
- *EVIDENCE:* the 2007 prevalence figures are from cohort studies cited by Andreasen et al (p.394). The 2026 review is a narrative review, not a trial (Kohli and Lee, 2026).
- *INTERPRETATION:* a tooth with one sign and a fully formed root may be observed. A tooth with two signs should be treated.
- *LIMITATION:* sensibility tests are unreliable for up to six months, and no source gives a validated threshold for CBCT findings.

**Claim 2 — Splint duration.** [Likely]
- *What the textbook states:* lateral luxation three weeks, longer if the marginal bone breaks down (six to eight weeks), extrusion two to three weeks (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.442). Harty's Table 12-2 gives two weeks for lateral luxation and two weeks for extrusion (Harty's, n.d., p.262).
- *What the IADT position states (as reported by Ogle, 2024):* lateral and intrusive luxation four weeks, extrusion and subluxation two weeks.
- *Agreement or challenge:* the textbook gives a shorter lateral splint. Ogle reports a systematic analysis and a 400-tooth study in which splinting for two weeks and four weeks gave similar periodontal outcomes, and an animal study in which 30 days of splinting gave more resorption than one week (Ogle, 2024, p.158-159).
- *INTERPRETATION:* the splint should be as short as the injury allows, but the evidence for a specific duration is weak.
- *LIMITATION:* the 400-tooth study concerns avulsed teeth, not luxation injuries, so it does not settle the lateral luxation figure.

**Claim 3 — Intrusion: spontaneous or active re-eruption.** [Likely]
- *What the textbook states:* spontaneous eruption for immature roots and for injuries under 3 mm, active repositioning above 7 mm or in older patients, and orthodontic extrusion preferred to surgery (Andreasen et al, 2007, p.449, p.458; Berman et al, n.d., p.84).
- *Agreement or challenge:* the two books agree on the broad pattern. They differ on the cut-off points (Berman's 6 mm for unusual spontaneous eruption against Andreasen's 7 mm for surgical repositioning).
- *LIMITATION:* the figures come from case series and experimental work; the sources say this themselves.

*Confidence:* the broad management pattern for luxation is well established in the textbooks [Certain]. The exact splint durations and the timing of root canal treatment are contested between sources [Likely].

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** A luxation injury damages the periodontal ligament and the supporting bone while the tooth stays in its socket, or is displaced without leaving it altogether. These injuries are common in permanent teeth. Management has four aims: restore the tooth to its correct position, preserve the pulp and the supporting tissues where possible, decide early whether endodontic treatment is needed, and monitor for late complications for several years. The decisions depend on three factors: the type and direction of displacement, the stage of root development, and the state of the pulp [Andreasen et al, 2007; Kohli and Lee, 2026].

**Classification.** The clinical classification has five luxation types, and avulsion is handled separately. Concussion is tenderness to percussion with no abnormal mobility or displacement. Subluxation is abnormal mobility without displacement. Extrusive luxation is partial displacement out of the socket. Lateral luxation is displacement off the long axis, usually with socket comminution or fracture, and it may be accompanied by apical displacement through the labial plate. Intrusive luxation is displacement into the alveolar bone [Andreasen et al, 2007; Berman et al, n.d.].

**Clinical and radiographic diagnosis.** Record the direction of displacement, mobility, the percussion note and the response to sensibility testing. A metallic sound on percussion suggests a wedged apex. Take periapical radiographs at more than one angle, and an occlusal view where apical displacement is suspected, because a single view can miss a root fracture or an apex displaced through the cortical plate [Berman et al, n.d.; Andreasen et al, 2007]. Sensibility tests are unreliable early: negative responses can persist for up to six months after injury, so they cannot by themselves confirm pulp necrosis [Kohli and Lee, 2026].

**Pulp prognosis.** Necrosis is most likely after intrusion, followed by lateral and extrusive luxation. The reported figures in Andreasen et al are: concussion 3%, subluxation 6%, extrusion 26%, lateral luxation 58% and intrusion 85%. Fully formed roots carry the greater risk. Overall, necrosis after luxation ranges from 15 to 59% across the studies cited [Andreasen et al, 2007].

**Concussion and subluxation.** These need observation rather than active treatment. Reduce any occlusal interference for patients who find chewing painful. Subluxation with clinically significant mobility, or injuries in very young or compromised patients, justify a short flexible splint. Follow-up for four to six weeks in Andreasen, or at one week, six to eight weeks, six months and one year in the DCNA review, is the practical schedule [Andreasen et al, 2007; Berman et al, n.d.; Kohli and Lee, 2026]. The IADT advises no treatment for concussion, and for subluxation a passive flexible splint for up to two weeks only if mobility or tenderness is excessive [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.325].

**Extrusive luxation.** The IADT repositions the tooth gently by pushing it back into the socket under local anaesthesia, then stabilises it with a passive flexible splint for two weeks, adding four weeks if the marginal bone breaks down [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.326]. The IADT radiographs are one parallel periapical view, two further views at different angulations, and an occlusal view [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.326]. Andreasen et al give two to three weeks [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.442].

**Lateral luxation.** Local anaesthesia is needed. The IADT repositions the tooth digitally, releasing it from its locked position, and stabilises it with a passive flexible splint for four weeks, with additional splinting if the marginal bone or socket wall breaks down [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.327]. Andreasen et al give about three weeks, extended to six to eight weeks if bone breaks down [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.442], and Harty's table gives two weeks [Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, n.d., p.262]. Lateral luxation with apical displacement is likely to need root canal treatment [Berman et al, n.d., p.75].

**Intrusive luxation.** Intrusion carries the highest risk of pulp necrosis, bone loss and resorption. For immature teeth the IADT allows spontaneous re-eruption for all intruded teeth, whatever the degree of intrusion; if there is no re-eruption within four weeks, orthodontic repositioning starts [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.328]. For mature teeth, an intrusion of less than 3 mm may re-erupt without intervention; if there is no re-eruption within eight weeks the tooth is repositioned surgically and splinted for four weeks. For 3 to 7 mm the IADT prefers surgical repositioning, with orthodontic as the alternative; beyond 7 mm it recommends surgical repositioning [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.328]. Andreasen et al prefer orthodontic extrusion to surgical repositioning [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.458], so the two sources disagree on the preferred method for 3 to 7 mm.

**Endodontic decision.** The decision to root-treat turns on the number of signs present. Andreasen accepts observation for up to about one year in low-risk patients: over 10 years of age, completed roots, no intrusion, and transient apical change expected. Active treatment follows radiolucency, inflammatory resorption or discolouration. Kohli and Lee are firmer: root canal treatment is indicated once two signs of necrosis are present, and a mature tooth with a closed apex is best treated with calcium hydroxide within seven to 10 days [Andreasen et al, 2007; Kohli and Lee, 2026].

**Splinting.** Splints should be flexible or semi-rigid. The IADT durations are: concussion, no splint; subluxation up to two weeks only if mobility is excessive; extrusion two weeks, plus four weeks if the marginal bone breaks down; lateral luxation four weeks; intrusion four weeks after surgical repositioning; root fracture four weeks, up to four months for a cervical fracture; alveolar fracture four weeks [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.323-328; Fouad et al, 2020, p.335]. Andreasen et al give two to three weeks for extrusion and subluxation, and about three weeks for lateral luxation [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.442]. The IADT figures are the current guideline figures.

**Complications and sequelae.** Pulp necrosis (15 to 52% overall) and pulp canal obliteration (3 to 35%) are the main pulpal complications. Root surface resorption affects 1 to 18%, greatest after intrusion. Internal resorption occurs in about 2%. Loss of supporting bone is about 5% overall, and 31% after intrusion. Delayed reduction raises the risk of supporting-tissue loss, and bone loss rises with the patient's age, the length of splinting and the extent of displacement [Andreasen et al, 2007].

**Follow-up.** Review for at least five years, with pulp sensibility at each visit, because canal obliteration and necrosis may appear late. Pulp canal obliteration can be followed by late necrosis in 7 to 16% of cases [Andreasen et al, 2007; Kohli and Lee, 2026]. The IADT schedule for extrusive and lateral luxation is 2, 4, 8 and 12 weeks, then 6 months, one year, and yearly for at least five years [Bourguignon et al, 2020, p.326-327]. Andreasen et al note late complications, including pulp canal obliteration [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.412-413].

**The emergency visit.** Before any dental treatment, exclude serious injury to the head, neck or central nervous system; refer at once if the mechanism suggests more force than the dental injury alone [Berman et al, n.d., p.74]. Take a full history of the injury, including the time elapsed and any loss of consciousness. Record the findings with photographs and a diagram of the displacement, because later changes are judged against this baseline. Obtain periapical radiographs at several angles, and an occlusal view where needed. Give local anaesthesia before any forceful reduction, and check tetanus status [Andreasen et al, 2007; Berman et al, n.d.].

**Choosing a splint.** Andreasen et al list the requirements of an optimal splint. It should be placed directly in the mouth, be passive and exert no orthodontic force, allow pulp testing and endodontic access, be easy to remove without damage, and be hygienic and aesthetic. Splints may be rigid, semi-rigid or flexible, and the choice of rigidity follows the injury: flexible for luxation, semi-rigid or rigid for root fracture [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858-859]. Acid-etch composite splints meet most of these requirements, but etching can stain labial enamel, which matters where composite is to be placed later [Andreasen et al, 2007, p.858]. Wire-and-composite or bracket splints are common alternatives, and an overly rigid splint can itself injure the periodontium [Andreasen et al, 2007].

**Evidence and its limits.** The prevalence and prognosis figures in Andreasen et al come from clinical series and follow-up studies that are often small and from earlier decades. Ogle notes that most luxation and avulsion studies have small samples and that prevalence estimates are imprecise [Ogle, 2024]. Kohli and Lee's 2026 review is narrative and draws on guideline statements rather than trials. The management principles are therefore well established, while the exact durations and cut-offs are not.

**Growing patients.** In children and adolescents, the goal is to keep the tooth until skeletal growth is complete, because early loss leads to alveolar bone resorption that compromises later replacement [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.609-610]. Replacement resorption (ankylosis) can follow luxation, especially intrusion and lateral luxation with socket damage. When ankylosis produces infraocclusion, decoronation may be needed to keep alveolar height for later implant placement [Kohli and Lee, 2026, p.619-620; Andreasen et al, 2007].

**Communication.** Parents or patients should be told the likely course: that pulp necrosis is common after some injuries, that splinting will be needed, that monitoring is long, and that late problems can appear years later. This matters because follow-up is often poor [Kohli and Lee, 2026; Ogle, 2024].

**Sensibility schedule and a third splint figure.** Cohen's Ch.21 recommends thermal and electrical testing of all anterior teeth from canine to canine at the first visit, repeated at two to four and six to eight weeks, at three, six and twelve months and yearly [Cohen's Pathways, Kahler, n.d., p.2560]. **Conclusion.** Diagnose on several signs, reposition gently and early, splint flexibly for as short a period as the injury allows, treat pulp necrosis on the balance of signs, and monitor for at least five years. The sources differ on the splint length for lateral luxation and on the timing of root canal treatment, and both positions should be stated.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Five luxation types; concussion and subluxation need no or minimal splinting.
- Pulp necrosis by type: concussion 3%, subluxation 6%, extrusion 26%, lateral 58%, intrusion 85%.
- Negative sensibility can last six months; diagnose necrosis on two or more signs.
- Extrusion: early gentle repositioning, flexible splint 2-3 weeks.
- Lateral luxation: local anaesthesia, splint about 3 weeks (6-8 if bone breaks down).
- Intrusion: spontaneous re-eruption in immature roots; surgical or orthodontic extrusion for deep or multiple intrusions.
- Bone loss highest after intrusion (31%).
- Follow up for at least five years.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Treating a single negative sensibility test as proof of pulp necrosis.
2. Rigid splinting, which increases root resorption.
3. Quoting one splint duration for all luxations.
4. Starting root canal treatment at once in a mature tooth with one equivocal sign.
5. Giving only the surgical option for intrusion without mentioning orthodontic extrusion and spontaneous re-eruption.

## AUDIT NOTE (IADT 2020, checked 2026-10-19)

**Audit note (IADT 2020, Bourguignon et al, 2020, checked 2026-10-19).** Tables 11 and 12 (PDF p.13-14, printed p.326-327) and the intrusion rules (PDF p.15, printed p.328) were read in full. Corrections: (1) lateral luxation splint is four weeks (IADT), against three weeks (Andreasen) and two weeks (Harty's), so the IADT figure now leads; (2) extrusion splint is two weeks, plus four weeks if marginal bone breaks down, which agrees with Harty's and not with Andreasen's two to three weeks; (3) intrusion: for 3 to 7 mm the IADT prefers surgical repositioning, against Andreasen's preference for orthodontic extrusion (p.458), which remains a visible disagreement; (4) the IADT allows spontaneous re-eruption for all intruded immature teeth, and for mature teeth less than 3 mm; (5) the Ogle (2024) avulsion figure of four weeks for dry time over 60 minutes is not in the IADT avulsion text, which gives two weeks and four weeks only for an alveolar or jawbone fracture (Fouad et al, 2020, p.334-335). The sensibility schedule (Cohen's Ch.21) is unchanged, with the IADT lateral figure added for comparison.
