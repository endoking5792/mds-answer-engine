## STUDY MODE

### S1. What "bioactive" means here [basic science]
Glass ionomer is described as a natural bioactive material because it releases fluoride, sodium and phosphate ions, more in acid, and takes up calcium and phosphate from saliva, hardening its surface as it matures (Sikri, 2020). Mount says that before GIC all restoratives were biologically inert, with little potential for bioactivity apart possibly from fluoride release (Mount et al, 2016). Ion exchange needs water, and GIC is water-based, which is why it suits remineralisation (Mount et al, 2016). A DCNA review says most commercial ion-releasing restoratives are glass-ionomer type (with more resin and acid-reactive fillers), on the market from 2013, with behaviour over time unclear and no clinical evidence at the time (Melo et al, 2022).

### S2. Newer ion-releasing materials [classification]
Alkasite (Cention N), bioactive resins (ACTIVA type) and giomers release ions from resin matrices (see Q13). In primary molars a bioactive resin performed comparably to compomer or composite for up to 2 years in three RCTs, but the review cannot show a caries-preventive advantage (Popa et al, 2026); a 24-month RCT of 22 patients (44 Class I restorations) found no failures or secondary caries in either group, so the caries outcome is uninformative, with better surface luster, occlusal wear, form and fracture scores for the bioactive resin than for bulk-fill composite (Raghip et al, 2025; abstract only). In-vitro bond strength is reported in two directions: lower than a resin-modified GIC (Fuji II LC) after 24 hours and two months (Al-Hasan et al, 2022), and 4.29 against 2.47 MPa for the resin and a resin-modified GIC respectively in the in-vitro arm of one review (Popa et al, 2026; abstract only, order as listed).

### S3. Glass hybrid systems [treatment relevance]
A glass hybrid (EQUIA-type) combines a high-viscosity GIC with a resin-based surface coat; Pickard describes such coating systems (G-Coat Plus) as regulating long-term water uptake and improving abrasion resistance (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). In a 5-year RCT of moderate to large two-surface Class II cavities, survival was 94.5% for the glass hybrid and 94.4% for nano-hybrid composite; success was 81.9% against 90.7%, but the authors report no significant difference in overall survival or success (p>0.05) (Miletic et al, 2024; abstract only).

### S3A. Other bioactive routes in the textbooks [classification]
Adding bioactive glass to GIC produces an apatite layer, but more bioactive glass lowers strength, and aluminium in the glass raises strength while lowering bioactivity (De Caluwe et al, 2017, as tabulated in Hill and Gillam, 2024). Craig describes a calcium aluminate/glass ionomer cement that sets partly by dissolution and reprecipitation, has a high pH, was bioactive in vitro (hydroxyapatite precipitation) and performs like self-adhesive cements for crown retention (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### S4. What the pooled evidence says [prognosis]
A meta-analysis of 40 RCTs (permanent teeth, at least 12 months) found bioactive materials reduced secondary caries risk by 45% (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.46-0.65), but the benefit came from GICs (RR 0.36) and not giomers (RR 1.09), and the pooled comparator mixed amalgam and composite (Zailai et al, 2026). This is the reviewer's inference, not a finding of Zailai: Ge et al (2023) found lower secondary caries than amalgam but no significant difference from composite, so the pooled effect may depend on the comparator (Ge et al, 2023).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
A bioactive restorative releases ions that interact with tooth and plaque (remineralisation, buffering, antibacterial action); glass ionomer is the classic bioactive restorative, since earlier restoratives were inert apart possibly from fluoride release (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Glass ionomer as the bioactive standard
- Releases fluoride, sodium and phosphate; takes up calcium and phosphate from saliva (Sikri, 2020).
- Strontium enters partly demineralised dentine (Mount et al, 2016).
- Ion exchange needs water; GIC is water-based (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3. Ion-releasing alternatives
| Material | Matrix | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Alkasite (Cention N) | resin with alkaline glass | hydroxide, calcium, fluoride; properties comparable or superior (Bonchev et al, 2025); clinically comparable to composite (Laporte et al, 2026) |
| Bioactive resin (ACTIVA) | resin | comparable to compomer or composite in primary molars to 2 years (Popa et al, 2026); no failures, better wear and fracture scores than composite (Raghip et al, 2025) |
| Giomer | resin with pre-reacted glass | no secondary caries benefit in the meta-analysis (Zailai et al, 2026) |
| Compomer | resin | not bioactive in the GIC sense (Mount et al, 2016) |
A DCNA review calls most such products GI-type (more resin, acid-reactive fillers), with behaviour over time unclear and clinical evidence lacking (Melo et al, 2022).

### 4. Glass hybrid (EQUIA-type) system
High-viscosity GIC restoration plus a resin coat (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); 5-year survival 94.5% against 94.4% for nano-hybrid composite in moderate two-surface Class II cavities; success 81.9% against 90.7%, no significant difference overall (Miletic et al, 2024).

### 5. Evidence on secondary caries
- Bioactive materials overall: RR 0.55 against conventional restorations; GIC RR 0.36; giomer RR 1.09 (Zailai et al, 2026).
- GIC against amalgam: lower secondary caries; against composite: no significant difference (Ge et al, 2023); the pooled comparator mixed both (Zailai et al, 2026).

### 6. Limitations
Short follow-up; remineralisation mainly laboratory evidence; bioactive-resin bond strength in vitro differs: lower than RMGIC (Al-Hasan et al, 2022), higher in one review's in-vitro arm (Popa et al, 2026); term "bioactive" loosely used (Melo et al, 2022).

### 6A. Telling a true glass ionomer from a look-alike
The critical difference is that glass ionomers and resin-modified glass ionomers set in the dark in a clinically acceptable time through the acid-base reaction, which compomers do not; Mount offers a dark-set test on a glass slab under a light-proof cover (Mount et al, 2016). A compomer cannot bond by ion exchange and has no remineralising ion exchange (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6B. When to choose which
GIC or glass hybrid for high caries risk, moisture problems, root and cervical lesions and primary teeth (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020); composite under occlusal or incisal load, where Mount says composites are stronger than GIC (Mount et al, 2016); ion-releasing resins are an option pending long-term evidence (Laporte et al, 2026; the cavity-size limit is this answer's own inference).

### 7. Conclusion
GIC remains the benchmark bioactive restorative; glass hybrid systems extend its use to moderate Class II; resin-based ion-releasing materials look promising but are not proven to prevent caries.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- GIC = natural bioactive material (fluoride, sodium, phosphate release; calcium and phosphate uptake) (Sikri, 2020).
- Glass hybrid: survival 94.5% against 94.4% for composite over 5 years in moderate Class II (Miletic et al, 2024).
- Bioactive materials RR 0.55 for secondary caries, driven by GIC (0.36), not giomer (Zailai et al, 2026).
- Resin-based ion-releasing materials: trials last up to 2 years (Popa et al, 2026; Raghip et al, 2025); clinical evidence lacking (Melo et al, 2022).
