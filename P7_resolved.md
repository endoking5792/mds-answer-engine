## STUDY MODE

### S1. What "bioactive" means here [basic science]
Glass ionomer is described as a natural bioactive material because it releases fluoride, sodium and phosphate ions, more in acid, and takes up calcium and phosphate from saliva, hardening its surface as it matures (Sikri, 2020). Mount says that before GIC all restoratives were biologically inert and the profession's interest in bioactivity was small (Mount et al, 2016). Ion exchange needs water, and GIC is water-based, which is why it suits remineralisation (Mount et al, 2016). A DCNA review says most commercial ion-releasing restoratives are glass-ionomer type (with more resin and acid-reactive fillers), on the market from 2013, with behaviour over time unclear and no clinical evidence at the time (Melo et al, 2022).

### S2. Newer ion-releasing materials [classification]
Alkasite (Cention N), bioactive resins (ACTIVA type) and giomers release ions from resin matrices (see Q13). In primary molars a bioactive resin performed comparably to compomer or composite for up to 2 years in three RCTs, but the review cannot show a caries-preventive advantage (Popa et al, 2026); in vitro it had a lower bond strength to dentine than a resin-modified GIC (Al-Hasan et al, 2022); a 24-month RCT of 22 patients found it similar to bulk-fill composite (Raghip et al, 2025).

### S3. Glass hybrid systems [treatment relevance]
A glass hybrid (EQUIA-type) combines a high-viscosity GIC with a nano-filled resin coat. In a 5-year RCT of moderate to large two-surface Class II cavities, survival was 94.5% for the glass hybrid and 94.4% for nano-hybrid composite, while success was lower for glass hybrid (81.9% against 90.7%) (Miletic et al, 2024).

### S4. What the pooled evidence says [prognosis]
A meta-analysis of 40 RCTs (permanent teeth, at least 12 months) found bioactive materials reduced secondary caries risk by 45% (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.46-0.65), but the benefit came from GICs (RR 0.36) and not giomers (RR 1.09), and the pooled comparator mixed amalgam and composite, so the effect may reflect the amalgam comparison (Zailai et al, 2026; Ge et al, 2023).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
A bioactive restorative releases ions that interact with tooth and plaque (remineralisation, buffering, antibacterial action); glass ionomer is the original bioactive restorative (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Glass ionomer as the bioactive standard
- Releases fluoride, sodium and phosphate; takes up calcium and phosphate from saliva (Sikri, 2020).
- Strontium enters partly demineralised dentine (Mount et al, 2016).
- Ion exchange needs water; GIC is water-based (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3. Ion-releasing alternatives
| Material | Matrix | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Alkasite (Cention N) | resin with alkaline glass | hydroxide, calcium, fluoride; comparable to composite (Bonchev et al, 2025; Laporte et al, 2026) |
| Bioactive resin (ACTIVA) | resin | comparable to composite or compomer in primary molars to 2 years (Popa et al, 2026; Raghip et al, 2025) |
| Giomer | resin with pre-reacted glass | no secondary caries benefit in the meta-analysis (Zailai et al, 2026) |
| Compomer | resin | not bioactive in the GIC sense (Mount et al, 2016) |
A DCNA review calls most such products GI-type and says clinical evidence was lacking (Melo et al, 2022).

### 4. Glass hybrid (EQUIA-type) system
High-viscosity GIC restoration plus a resin coat; 5-year survival 94.5% against 94.4% for nano-hybrid composite in moderate two-surface Class II cavities; success 81.9% against 90.7% (Miletic et al, 2024).

### 5. Evidence on secondary caries
- Bioactive materials overall: RR 0.55 against conventional restorations; GIC RR 0.36; giomer RR 1.09 (Zailai et al, 2026).
- GIC against amalgam: lower secondary caries; against composite: no significant difference (Ge et al, 2023).

### 6. Limitations
Short follow-up; remineralisation mainly laboratory evidence; bioactive resins have lower bond strength to dentine than RMGIC in vitro (Al-Hasan et al, 2022); term "bioactive" loosely used (Melo et al, 2022).

### 6A. Telling a true glass ionomer from a look-alike
The critical difference is that glass ionomers and resin-modified glass ionomers set in the dark in a clinically acceptable time through the acid-base reaction, which compomers do not; Mount offers a dark-set test on a glass slab under a light-proof cover (Mount et al, 2016). A compomer cannot bond by ion exchange and has no remineralising ion exchange (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6B. When to choose which
GIC or glass hybrid for high caries risk, moisture problems, root and cervical lesions and primary teeth (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020); composite where strength and polish matter (Mount et al, 2016); ion-releasing resins as an option in moderate cavities pending long-term evidence (Laporte et al, 2026).

### 7. Conclusion
GIC remains the benchmark bioactive restorative; glass hybrid systems extend its use to moderate Class II; resin-based ion-releasing materials look promising but are not proven to prevent caries.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- GIC = natural bioactive material (fluoride, sodium, phosphate release; calcium and phosphate uptake) (Sikri, 2020).
- Glass hybrid: survival 94.5% against 94.4% for composite over 5 years in moderate Class II (Miletic et al, 2024).
- Bioactive materials RR 0.55 for secondary caries, driven by GIC (0.36), not giomer (Zailai et al, 2026).
- Resin-based ion-releasing materials: short-term only (Melo et al, 2022).
