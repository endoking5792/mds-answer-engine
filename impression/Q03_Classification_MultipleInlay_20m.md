## STUDY MODE

### Classification of impression materials
(DEFINITION, Sikri, 2017) By setting behaviour:
- **Rigid (non-elastic)**: impression plaster (Type I gypsum), impression compound, zinc oxide-eugenol paste — cannot be withdrawn past an undercut without fracturing/permanently distorting; used mainly for edentulous/denture-border work or bite registration, not for dentate multi-unit cast work.
- **Elastic**:
  - *Hydrocolloids*: Agar (reversible — heat/cool sol-gel cycle); Alginate (irreversible — calcium cross-linking of alginate salts).
  - *Elastomers*: Polysulfide; Condensation silicone; Addition silicone (polyvinylsiloxane); Polyether.

By viscosity/consistency (elastomers specifically): light body, medium body, heavy body, putty — the basis of the double-mix "putty-wash" technique used for multi-unit impressions.

By use: preliminary/study (alginate) vs. final/working impression (elastomers — required for the dimensional accuracy multiple cast restorations demand).

### Why multiple inlays specifically change the clinical problem
A single-inlay impression only has to capture one preparation's fine detail accurately. When **several inlay preparations exist in the same arch** (e.g., multiple posterior Class II inlays), the impression must simultaneously: (a) record fine marginal/internal detail (slots, grooves, proximal boxes) at **every** preparation without a void or drag at any one of them; (b) remain dimensionally accurate across the **whole arch span**, since a die relationship error between two inlay preparations (not just within one) will cause the laboratory-fabricated restorations to fail to seat together correctly; and (c) still record the full remaining dentition accurately enough for the opposing cast/occlusion to be checked (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011). This is precisely why the **two-phase (putty + light-body wash) technique in a stock or custom tray** — rather than a single-consistency or copper-band technique (suited to one tooth) — is the technique of choice for multiple inlays.

### Technique for multiple-inlay impression (double-mix / putty-wash, in a stock tray with spacer — Sikri, 2017)
1. **Preliminary assessment**: verify each inlay preparation for adequate retention form, finish-line clarity, and absence of undercuts that would lock the rigid set elastomer in place; block out any with soft wax if present.
2. **Tray selection**: a stock tray (metal or rigid plastic) of correct arch size, covering all preparations with adequate peripheral clearance; tray adhesive applied and allowed to dry/become tacky (3-5 minutes).
3. **Primary (putty) impression with spacer**: equal scoops of putty base and catalyst are hand-mixed (doughed and rolled — not with latex gloves, to avoid inhibiting addition silicone) until streak-free, and loaded into the tray. A polyethylene sheet spacer is placed over the arch (full arch if the whole arch is needed, partial if only the inlay-bearing segment is required) before the loaded tray is seated, creating a uniform relief space for the wash at every preparation site simultaneously — critical when multiple inlays are present, since each site needs its own adequate wash thickness.
4. The tray is held immobile under uniform pressure until the putty sets (confirmed when a fingernail indentation on the excess putty rebounds completely); removed with minimal lateral movement; the spacer is peeled off and the impression checked for gross defects (if defective, the primary impression is repeated before proceeding, since a flawed primary stage cannot be corrected at the wash stage).
5. **Final (wash) impression**: light-body material (hand-mixed in equal parts, or — preferably for multiple preparation sites — dispensed through an automix gun for a consistently proportioned, bubble-free mix) is syringed into **each** inlay preparation in turn, working systematically around the arch so no site is missed and the material does not begin to set before the tray is reseated. The tray (with its spacer-created relief space) is reseated over the wash-coated arch and held with minimal, even pressure until both layers set together.
6. **Removal**: tray removed rapidly along the path of withdrawal (exploiting elastic recovery); peripheral seal broken with air/finger pressure first; impression rinsed and dried, oral cavity checked for and cleared of any retained chips (elastomer debris can injure interdental tissue if missed).
7. **Alternative — scraping variant**: where a spacer is not used/available, the primary putty impression is instead taken without a spacer and the putty **around each prepared tooth and interdentally** is scraped out with a putty cutter or surgical blade to create relief space at every inlay site before the wash stage — more operator-dependent (risk of uneven relief depth between sites) but avoids needing pre-cut spacer sheets.
8. **Evaluation**: for multiple inlays specifically, each preparation's margin must independently show no demarcation line between wash and putty, no voids, and full ≥0.5mm coverage beyond its own finish line — a single acceptable site does not mean the impression is acceptable overall; **any one** failed site requires a retake (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011).
9. **Disinfection and pouring** as per standard elastomer protocol (brief immersion/spray; delayed pour for addition silicone if no hydrogen absorber is present).

### Why addition silicone/polyether (rather than polysulfide or alginate) is preferred specifically for multiple inlays
Alginate's poor dimensional stability and inability to form metal dies rule it out for any cast-restoration work. Among the elastomers, the **superior dimensional stability** of addition silicone (0.05-0.2% permanent deformation) and polyether (0.1-0.2%) is particularly valuable across a multi-unit arch impression, since any shrinkage/distortion error compounds across the span between preparations, whereas polysulfide's and condensation silicone's larger, by-product-driven deformation would risk a progressively worse inter-abutment relationship error the further apart the inlay preparations are (Sikri, 2017; Craig's 14th ed., via Module 3 study material).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim)

**Claim — the spacer technique is preferable to the scraping technique for multiple-site impressions.** Textbook position: Sikri (2017) presents both as valid variants of the double-mix technique but implicitly favours the spacer method by describing it first and in more procedural detail, while flagging that scraping is manual and site-by-site. EVIDENCE: a pre-cut, uniform-thickness spacer mechanically guarantees equal relief space at every preparation site, whereas scraping depends on operator judgement repeated at each of several sites in the same impression — more sites means more chances for an inconsistent relief depth. INTERPRETATION: for multiple inlays specifically (as opposed to one single-tooth impression), the spacer technique's reproducibility advantage is proportionally more valuable, since an error at any one site invalidates that unit's restoration. LIMITATION: this is a reasoned inference from the mechanics described, not a cited comparative study measuring actual relief-space uniformity between the two variants — [Likely], not [Certain].

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~36 minutes)**

### Classification of impression materials
**Rigid**: impression plaster, impression compound, ZOE paste.
**Elastic — Hydrocolloids**: Agar (reversible), Alginate (irreversible).
**Elastic — Elastomers**: Polysulfide, Condensation silicone, Addition silicone (PVS), Polyether — each further graded light/medium/heavy body/putty.

### Impression technique for multiple inlays
**Material of choice**: addition silicone or polyether (superior dimensional stability across a multi-unit arch span) in a double-mix (putty-wash) technique.

**Steps**:
1. Assess all preparations; block out any undercuts.
2. Select stock tray (or custom tray if medium-body technique used); apply adhesive.
3. Mix putty (hand-doughed, non-latex gloves); load tray; place polyethylene spacer over arch (or omit and plan to scrape later).
4. Seat tray, hold immobile till set; remove; peel spacer; inspect primary impression.
5. Syringe light-body wash into **every** inlay preparation systematically (automix gun preferred for consistency across multiple sites).
6. Reseat tray over wash before it sets; hold with even pressure till both set together.
7. Remove rapidly along withdrawal path; rinse, inspect oral cavity for retained chips.
8. **Scraping alternative**: no spacer used; putty scraped out at each prep + interdentally with a putty cutter before wash stage.
9. Evaluate **each** preparation independently (margin coverage, no demarcation line, no voids) — any single failed site = retake.
10. Disinfect (brief immersion/spray) and pour (delay ≥1h for addition silicone without hydrogen absorber).

### Why this material/technique for multiple inlays
Superior dimensional stability of addition silicone/polyether prevents cumulative inter-abutment distortion across the arch span between multiple preparations — a risk that polysulfide/condensation silicone's larger shrinkage and alginate's poor stability would worsen with distance between units.

### Conclusion
Classification of impression materials rests on setting behaviour (rigid vs. elastic) and, within elastics, on chemistry (hydrocolloid vs. elastomer) and viscosity. For multiple inlays, the double-mix (putty-wash) technique using a dimensionally stable elastomer, with systematic per-site wash placement and independent per-site evaluation, is the technique of choice.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Classification: Rigid (plaster/compound/ZOE) vs. Elastic (Hydrocolloid: agar/alginate; Elastomer: polysulfide/condensation silicone/addition silicone/polyether).
- Multiple inlays → double-mix/putty-wash technique, addition silicone or polyether preferred (dimensional stability across arch span).
- Spacer technique = uniform relief space at every site; scraping = manual, site-by-site, more variable.
- Each preparation evaluated INDEPENDENTLY — one bad site invalidates the whole impression for that unit.
- Automix gun preferred for wash material when multiple sites must be syringed systematically.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Giving a generic single-tooth impression technique without addressing what changes when **multiple** preparations are present (cumulative distortion risk, per-site independent evaluation).
2. Omitting the classification part of the question entirely and jumping straight to technique.
3. Not naming which specific elastomer is preferred and why (dimensional-stability rationale) for a multi-unit case.
4. Confusing the spacer and scraping variants of the double-mix technique.
5. Forgetting to mention per-site evaluation of the final impression (treating the impression as pass/fail as a whole rather than per-preparation).

## MNEMONIC
"Classify, then Clear a Space" — **C**lassify materials → **C**hoose addition silicone/polyether → **S**pacer (or scrape) → **S**yringe wash at every site → **S**eat → check **E**ach site.
