## STUDY MODE

### 1. Basic concepts and classification

An impression is the negative replica of the teeth, alveolar ridge, and adjoining tissues; the positive replica of a single tooth is a **die**, and of a whole arch or part of it, a **cast/model** (Sikri, 2017). Impression making is distinguished clinically from a passive "taking": because oral soft tissues are variably thick and displaceable under pressure, the operator actively **manipulates** vital tissue during the procedure, and the material choice itself depends on how much soft-tissue variability must be captured (Sikri, 2017).

**Classification of impression materials** (DEFINITION framework, Sikri 2017; Bhat, cited in the existing Module 3 study material):
- **By setting mechanism** — (a) rigid/non-elastic: impression plaster, impression compound, zinc oxide-eugenol paste; (b) elastic: hydrocolloids (agar — reversible; alginate — irreversible) and elastomers (polysulfide, condensation silicone, addition silicone/polyvinylsiloxane, polyether).
- **By use** — preliminary/primary (alginate, impression compound) vs. final/working (elastomers, for cast/indirect restorations).
- **By viscosity** (elastomers specifically) — light body, medium body, heavy body, and putty, based on flow characteristics under a given load (Sikri, 2017).

### 2. Ideal requirements of an impression material

A real, structured requirement framework (Sikri, 2017; corroborated independently by Bhat's five-category ideal-requirements list already built into Module 3's study material) covers: pleasant odour/taste/colour; adequate shelf life; easy manipulation; sufficient consistency/texture; sufficient elasticity with **complete elastic recovery** (no permanent deformation under strain); adequate tear strength (material should tear only after the 0.04% critical permanent-deformation point, not before — a material tearing before this point is paradoxically the more suitable one, since the alternative is permanent distortion); dimensional stability across temperature/humidity; compatibility with die material (lower contact angle = fewer voids); non-toxicity; and cost-effectiveness. Properties of specific clinical significance include **wettability** (fewer voids, better detail), **hydrophilic/hydrophobic nature** (moisture tolerance), and **flexibility** (ease of withdrawal from undercuts) (Sikri, 2017).

### 3. Armamentarium — impression trays

**Stock trays** (metal or plastic, perforated or plain, various sizes) suit alginate and putty/light-body techniques; **custom (special) trays**, fabricated in self-cure acrylic (or thermoplastic) over a primary cast with a wax/plaster spacer, are required when the medium-body/single-consistency technique is used (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011). A custom tray must be rigid, moisture-resistant, dimensionally stable, and must bond durably (chemically/mechanically) to the impression material; it uses less material, needs no pre-selection, and gives a uniform material thickness that minimises polymerisation-shrinkage distortion — but takes time to fabricate, must "age" at least 24 hours before use (to let the 0.06%/24h acrylic polymerisation shrinkage stabilise), and carries a residual-monomer irritation risk (Sikri, 2017). Tray selection itself is clinically consequential: an oversized tray risks pharyngeal contact of material (nausea) and greater distortion; too narrow a tray lets teeth/tissue touch the tray walls directly, producing a faulty impression (Sikri, 2017). Clear rigid polycarbonate trays suit viscous elastomers; opaque flexible plastic trays suit alginate; metal trays, while retentive via a rolled rim, need adhesive too and are harder to decontaminate between patients (Ricketts, 2011).

### 4. Gingival management before the working impression

Where a preparation finish line is subgingival, the sulcus must be exposed so a low-viscosity wash can enter it without tearing — **gingival retraction**: the reversible lateral/apical displacement of the marginal gingiva (Sikri, 2017). A real quantified anatomic constraint governs how far this can go: the supra-alveolar "biological width" comprises the gingival sulcus (0.7-1mm) + junctional epithelium (~1mm) + connective-tissue attachment (~1mm) ≈ 3mm total (Ricketts, 2011); encroaching on it (e.g., a deeply subgingival margin) triggers bone loss, new connective-tissue attachment, apical migration of the junctional epithelium, and gingival recession as the body re-establishes the width. Margins should ideally be supragingival; where aesthetics force a subgingival margin, it should not extend beyond 0.5mm from the free gingival margin, and a minimum sulcus width of ≥0.2mm is needed to record the margin accurately with an adequately durable material thickness (Ricketts, 2011).

Retraction methods are classified as **mechanical, chemo-mechanical, surgical/electrosurgical, and miscellaneous** (Sikri, 2017):
- **Mechanical**: retraction cord (single or double-cord technique — a narrow first cord stays in the sulcus while a wider second cord, soaked in hemostatic agent, is placed above it and removed before the impression, leaving the first cord's retraction intact); wooden wedges; cotton twills (plain, or with fast-setting ZOE); rubber dam strip; copper band with modelling wax.
- **Chemo-mechanical**: cords or pastes soaked in a vasoconstrictor (epinephrine — real absorption data: ~71pg absorbed from 2.5cm of cord over 5-15min in the sulcus, with systemic cardiovascular caution) or an astringent/coagulant (alum, ferric sulfate — considered superior to epinephrine but can stain gingiva; aluminium chloride — the least traumatic astringent per Ricketts, 2011, and used in Expasyl/Gingitrac systems).
- **Surgical/electrosurgical**: local curettage ("gingitage," for sulcus <3mm), gingivectomy, and electrosurgery (electrosectioning/electrocoagulation/fulguration/desiccation) — contraindicated with cardiac pacemakers, and requiring a dry field, light rapid strokes with a 5-second cooling gap between strokes, and avoidance of metallic restorations (short-circuit risk) (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011).
- **Miscellaneous/newer agents**: Magic Foam Cord (an expanding, effervescing polyvinylsiloxane held under a Comprecap, mechanically displacing gingiva without a hemostatic action — Ricketts, 2011); Merocel strips; Expasyl (a kaolin/aluminium-chloride paste, syringed, retracting ~0.5mm in one minute); Gingitrac; soft-tissue lasers (CO2, Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, Argon — photoablation producing a blood-free incision, but with variable hemostasis by wavelength and no tactile feedback).

### 5. The impression technique itself — step by step

**(a) Hydrocolloid (alginate) technique** (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011): correct powder:water ratio measured with the manufacturer's scoop/cylinder (never altered to change setting time, since this changes tear strength/elasticity instead); powder fluffed, then added to water (not reversed) in a flexible rubber bowl; figure-of-eight spatulation against the bowl wall for 45s-1min to express air; tray loaded to flange level (not overloaded, to avoid airway obstruction/patient discomfort); teeth "buttered" with a finger-smeared layer first to reduce bubble entrapment in fine fissures; patient seated upright (critical for the upper arch specifically, to direct flow anteriorly and prevent posterior/pharyngeal flow and retching); tray seated with lip retraction, then held immobile under even pressure for the ~3-5min gelation; removed with a single rapid snap (not a to-and-fro rocking) to exploit elastic recovery, after breaking the peripheral seal with air/finger pressure; rinsed, disinfected briefly, and cast within 30-60 minutes (never allowed to dry — syneresis — or sit in excess water — imbibition — since both distort it, and cast removal must also be prompt since prolonged plaster/stone contact degrades the cast surface).

**(b) Elastomeric (final) impression technique** — the dominant technique for crown/bridge and cast-restoration work (Ricketts, 2011; Sikri, 2017):
- **Two-phase, single-stage ("putty-wash"/"twin-mix") technique**: light-body wash is syringed around the prepared tooth/teeth (including into the gingival sulcus, with care over air-pressure to avoid dislodging the wash) while a heavier-body/putty material is simultaneously mixed and loaded into the tray; the loaded tray is seated over the unset wash before it begins to gel, so both set together as one impression. This is the most reliable, least distortion-prone technique and is the one recommended with addition-silicone putty in a rigid tray (Sikri, 2017).
- **Two-phase, two-stage technique**: putty/heavy body is seated first, allowed to set, and removed (primary impression); wash is then syringed into the tooth preparation and the primary impression reseated over it before the wash sets (secondary "wash" impression). Because the primary impression is already an accurate "fit," there is little room for the wash to escape, generating **hydrostatic recoil**: the tray walls flex outward on seating and rebound on removal, producing an undersized die. Space for the wash can be deliberately created by (i) taking the primary impression **before** tooth preparation, (ii) inserting a polyethylene/cellophane spacer over the teeth before the primary impression, or (iii) cutting relief/escape grooves in the primary impression. A rigid metal tray minimises this distortion; the two-stage technique should generally be avoided with addition silicone specifically (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011).
- **Single-mix/monophase technique**: one consistency (regular-body or monophase, classically polyether) used both in the tray and the syringe — the only technique polyether should be used with, since it is supplied in one viscosity (Ricketts, 2011).
- **Triple-tray/dual-arch (closed-mouth) technique**: for single-unit, less extensive restorations — putty loaded on both sides of a pre-shaped triple tray with light body syringed on prepared and opposing teeth, the patient closing into maximal intercuspation; it eliminates the need for an articulator, minimises mandibular-flexure error from wide opening, and uses less material (Sikri, 2017).
- **Copper-band technique**: for single-tooth (or a few vague-margin) impressions — an annealed copper band trimmed/fitted 1mm beyond the finish line with ~1mm circumferential clearance, filled with softened green-stick compound or putty and seated; it is cooled, removed, trimmed, and a Type IV gypsum die poured and transferred back to the main cast for proximal-contact/occlusal adjustment (Sikri, 2017).

Before seating any elastomeric material, the field must be checked for **undercuts** (buccal sulcus, bone-loss defects, pontic recesses) that would lock a rigid set material in place — blocked out with soft wax beforehand — and kept **dry** (elastomers are inherently hydrophobic; the patient is reclined, teeth isolated with cotton rolls/saliva ejector, and dried immediately before syringing) (Ricketts, 2011).

### 6. Evaluating the impression

A completed impression is checked for: no tray show-through in the restoration area; no voids, especially over the preparation; no tears or fractured margins; impression material extending ≥0.5mm beyond the finish line with no thin unsupported zones (which distort under stone weight); no shiny smooth patches (moisture contamination); and, for the two-phase single-stage technique specifically, that the wash has merged **imperceptibly** with the putty with no demarcated interface (a visible line means the wash had already begun to set before the putty was seated — the impression must be retaken) (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011). The impression is also checked well beyond the prepared tooth, since a drag or defect elsewhere in the arch can prevent the opposing models from seating correctly in the patient's true occlusion even if the preparation margins themselves are perfect (Ricketts, 2011).

### 7. Pouring/casting the impression

For addition silicone specifically, hydrogen gas released after setting can produce voids in the gypsum cast unless mitigated: a hydrogen absorber (palladium black, polymer fibres) in the material, a ≥1-hour pouring delay (≥overnight/12h for epoxy dies, which set far more slowly), vacuum storage for 5 minutes, or a hot-water-then-cool step before pouring (Sikri, 2017). The cast should not be left seated in the impression beyond setting, since this produces a rough, chalky cast surface from prolonged water exchange between stone and impression material (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011).

### 8. Disinfection (cross-referenced in full in Q5)

Every impression must be disinfected before reaching the laboratory, since organisms such as *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, and *C. albicans* survive on impression surfaces (Sikri, 2017). Immersion in 2.5% sodium hypochlorite or 2% glutaraldehyde for a brief (3-10 minute) period is standard; prolonged immersion distorts polyether/alginate (expansion) and hydrophilic silicones (reduced dimensional stability) specifically, so these are generally sprayed rather than immersed, or immersed for under 10 minutes (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011).

### 9. Common technical errors (cross-cutting)

Distortion: tray movement/uneven pressure during set, premature or improper (rocking) removal, inadequately aged custom tray, poor tray-adhesive bond, delayed tray seating after mixing. Bubbles: air incorporated during mixing, premature gelation of syringe material blocking flow. Rough cast surface: inadequate impression cleaning, excess residual water/wetting agent, premature cast separation (Sikri, 2017).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1 — the two-phase, two-stage putty-wash technique is intrinsically more distortion-prone than the single-stage technique.** Textbook position: both Sikri (2017) and Ricketts (2011) independently state that the two-stage approach risks a smaller, distorted die via hydrostatic recoil of the reseated primary impression, and both recommend the single-stage technique with a rigid tray as the more reliable default. [Certain] — this is internally consistent, mechanistically explained (compression of the primary impression on reseating + plastic/elastic rebound on removal), and corroborated independently by two different textbook lineages (an Indian clinical text and a UK-authored advanced-operative text) rather than one source's isolated opinion. EVIDENCE: the mechanism is a straightforward physical consequence of reseating an already-accurate negative mould over a space-filling wash with nowhere to escape. INTERPRETATION: clinicians should default to single-stage putty-wash (or true monophase polyether) whenever feasible. LIMITATION: neither source cites a quantified clinical trial comparing marginal-fit outcomes of single- vs. two-stage technique in this chapter — the claim rests on a well-reasoned mechanical argument, not a cited RCT, so the *direction* of the effect is well-supported but its *magnitude* in actual crown-margin discrepancy (in micrometres) is not given here. [Likely, not Certain, on magnitude specifically.]

**Claim 2 — latex gloves inhibit the set of addition-silicone (polyvinylsiloxane) impression material, but not other elastomers.** Both sources agree this is a real, specific phenomenon: sulfur compounds from latex vulcanisation migrate onto the material and poison the platinum catalyst specifically used in addition-silicone cross-linking (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011; and independently corroborated in the Module 3 Dental Materials study material from Craig's 14th ed.). EVIDENCE: three independently-authored sources converge on the identical catalyst-poisoning mechanism. INTERPRETATION: vinyl or nitrile gloves (or thorough detergent-washed latex gloves) should be used specifically when handling addition silicone, both for mixing and for touching the prepared tooth before the wash is syringed. LIMITATION: this is presented as settled mechanistic fact across all sources consulted, with no contested evidence found — a case where the textbook and the supplementary source genuinely agree, so no further hedging is warranted here (per the "do not manufacture controversy" rule).

**Claim 3 — contact-angle (hydrophobicity) values for polyether vs. addition silicone differ numerically between the two sources consulted.** Ricketts (2011) gives polyether a contact angle of 49° vs. addition silicone's 98°; the Module 3 study material (citing Powers & Wataha, 10th ed.) instead describes plain addition silicone's contact angle as ~90° falling to 20-40° once modern surfactants are added. **This is flagged explicitly as a genuine point of numeric divergence rather than smoothed into one figure** — the two sources are measuring materials from different eras/formulations (Ricketts' figure may reflect an unmodified product; Powers & Wataha's range reflects deliberately surfactant-modified "hydrophilic" addition silicones now dominant in practice), and the underlying qualitative point (addition silicone is markedly more hydrophobic than polyether in its base form, and both have been chemically modified to narrow this gap) is itself undisputed across all sources. ## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~150 minutes)**

### Introduction
An impression is the negative replica of teeth and adjoining tissues made for fabricating casts/dies in indirect restorative and prosthetic treatment. Impression procedures in restorative dentistry comprise material/tray selection, gingival management, the impression technique itself, evaluation, disinfection, and pouring.

### 1. Classification of impression materials
- **Rigid/non-elastic**: impression plaster, impression compound, zinc oxide-eugenol paste.
- **Elastic**:
  - Hydrocolloids — Agar (reversible); Alginate (irreversible).
  - Elastomers — Polysulfide, Condensation silicone, Addition silicone (PVS), Polyether.
- By consistency (elastomers): light, medium, heavy body, putty.
- By use: preliminary (alginate) vs. final/working (elastomers, for cast restorations).

### 2. Ideal requirements
Pleasant organoleptic properties; adequate shelf life; easy manipulation; sufficient working/setting time; complete elastic recovery; adequate tear strength; dimensional stability; compatibility with die material (low contact angle); non-toxic; cost-effective; good wettability and flexibility for undercut removal.

### 3. Armamentarium
- **Stock trays**: metal/plastic, perforated/plain — for alginate and putty-wash techniques.
- **Custom trays**: self-cure acrylic over a primary cast + spacer — for single-consistency/medium-body technique; must be rigid, dimensionally stable, aged ≥24h.

### 4. Gingival retraction (where margins are subgingival)
*Definition*: reversible lateral/apical displacement of the gingival margin to expose the finish line for impression recording.
- Biological width ≈3mm (sulcus 0.7-1mm + junctional epithelium 1mm + connective tissue 1mm); margins should stay ≥0.5mm supragingival where possible; minimum sulcus width 0.2mm needed for accurate margin recording.
- **Methods**:
  - *Mechanical*: single/double retraction cord, wedges, cotton twill, copper band.
  - *Chemo-mechanical*: cord/paste + astringent (aluminium chloride, ferric sulfate, alum) or vasoconstrictor (epinephrine — caution in cardiac/hypertensive patients).
  - *Surgical/electrosurgical*: gingivectomy, gingitage, electrosurgery (dry field, metal-free instruments, short strokes with cooling gaps).
  - *Newer*: Magic Foam Cord, Expasyl, Gingitrac, lasers.

### 5. Impression technique
**A. Hydrocolloid (alginate)**: correct powder:water ratio (never varied to adjust set time) → mix in rubber bowl, figure-8 spatulation 45s-1min → load tray to flange level → butter occlusal surfaces → seat with patient upright (esp. maxillary arch) → hold immobile during ~3-5min gelation → remove with single rapid snap → rinse, brief disinfection, pour within 30-60min (avoid syneresis/imbibition).

**B. Elastomeric techniques (for cast/crown-bridge work)**:
| Technique | Procedure | Key risk |
|---|---|---|
| Two-phase, single-stage (putty-wash/twin-mix) | Wash syringed on prep + putty loaded in tray, seated together, set together | Most reliable; least distortion |
| Two-phase, two-stage | Putty set first, removed, wash syringed, putty reseated | Hydrostatic recoil → undersized die; needs relief space |
| Single-mix/monophase | One viscosity (e.g., polyether) in tray and syringe | Must be single-stage only |
| Triple-tray/dual-arch | Putty + wash, patient bites into intercuspation | For single-unit restorations only |
| Copper band | Annealed band + compound/putty, single tooth | For single crowns / vague margins |

- Block out undercuts with soft wax beforehand; keep field dry (reclined patient, cotton rolls, dry tooth) since all elastomers are hydrophobic to some degree.
- Latex gloves inhibit addition-silicone set (sulfur poisons platinum catalyst) — use vinyl/nitrile.

### 6. Evaluating the impression
No voids/tears/show-through; margins covered ≥0.5mm beyond finish line; wash-putty junction imperceptible (a visible line = retake); check remote areas of the arch for occlusal accuracy.

### 7. Pouring
Addition silicone: delay pouring ≥1h (hydrogen gas) or use a palladium absorber. Do not leave cast seated in impression after setting (rough, chalky surface).

### 8. Disinfection
Immerse 2.5% NaOCl or 2% glutaraldehyde, 3-10min (brief, to avoid distorting alginate/polyether/hydrophilic silicones); spray for moisture-sensitive materials instead of prolonged immersion.

### 9. Common errors
Distortion (tray movement, premature/improper removal, under-aged custom tray); bubbles (air incorporation, premature gelation); rough cast (inadequate cleaning, excess water, premature separation).

### Conclusion
A systematic sequence — correct material/tray selection → effective, atraumatic gingival management where needed → a technique matched to the material's handling properties → careful evaluation before the patient is dismissed → prompt, appropriate disinfection and pouring — is what converts impression making from a "taking" into the accurate "making" of a negative replica that indirect restorative success depends on.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Classification: rigid (plaster/compound/ZOE) vs. elastic (hydrocolloids: agar reversible, alginate irreversible; elastomers: polysulfide/condensation silicone/addition silicone/polyether).
- Biological width ≈3mm; margin ≥0.5mm supragingival ideally; minimum sulcus width 0.2mm for accurate recording.
- Two-phase single-stage (putty-wash) > two-stage for accuracy (avoids hydrostatic recoil/undersized die).
- Latex gloves inhibit ONLY addition silicone (sulfur poisons Pt catalyst) — not polyether/polysulfide/condensation silicone.
- Addition silicone releases H₂ gas after set — delay pour ≥1h or use palladium absorber.
- Triple-tray/dual-arch technique: single-unit restorations only, no articulator needed.
- Copper band technique: single-tooth/vague-margin impressions, Type IV gypsum die.
- Disinfect briefly (3-10min) — prolonged immersion distorts alginate, polyether, hydrophilic silicones.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing only the materials (hydrocolloids/elastomers) and skipping the actual *technique* sequence (tray selection → gingival management → impression → evaluation → pouring) that the command word "procedures" specifically asks for.
2. Confusing single-stage and two-stage putty-wash technique, or failing to explain *why* two-stage causes distortion (hydrostatic recoil, not just "it's less accurate").
3. Omitting the biological-width rationale when discussing gingival retraction — examiners expect the 3mm figure and its three components, not just "retract the gingiva."
4. Forgetting that latex-glove inhibition is specific to addition silicone, not a universal elastomer problem — a common generalisation error.
5. Not mentioning hydrogen-gas evolution and the pour-delay rule for addition silicone when discussing pouring/casting errors.
