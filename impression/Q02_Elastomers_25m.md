## STUDY MODE

### Why elastomers were developed
Alginate (irreversible hydrocolloid), though widely used for preliminary impressions, is inadequate for cast/crown-and-bridge work mainly because of its poor dimensional stability (distorts if not poured within 5-10 minutes; cannot produce metal dies; inaccurate for long-span fixed prosthodontics) (Sikri, 2017). Elastomers — rubber-like polymers formed by polymerisation/vulcanisation of a base polymer with a catalyst — were developed specifically to overcome these weaknesses, in a real historical sequence: polysulfide (1950) → condensation silicone (1955) → polyether (1965) → addition silicone (1975) (Craig's 14th ed., cited in Module 3 study material). All four are sub-classified by viscosity into light, medium, heavy body, and putty.

### 1. Polysulfide
Two-paste system: base = polysulfide polymer, titanium dioxide, zinc sulfate, silica; catalyst = lead dioxide with dibutyl phthalate, sulfur, magnesium stearate. Setting reaction: oxidation of terminal –SH groups causes chain lengthening (cross-linking), producing elastomeric rubber (Sikri, 2017). **Merits**: excellent surface detail; good flexibility and tear resistance; easiest of the elastomers to remove from undercuts (not rigid); unaffected by latex gloves. **Demerits**: unpleasant odour/taste, stains linen; long setting time (8-12min, patient discomfort); hydrophobic; 3-5% permanent deformation improving only with a 30-minute delay before pouring; some cellular toxicity; does not self-adhere, so unusable for border moulding (Sikri, 2017).

### 2. Condensation silicone
Base paste: α-ω-hydroxyl-terminated polydimethylsiloxane; catalyst: tri-/tetra-functional alkyl silicates + stannous octoate catalyst. The cross-linking reaction releases **ethyl alcohol** as a by-product — the direct chemical cause of this material's comparatively poor long-term dimensional stability, since continued evaporation of the alcohol after setting keeps shrinking the material (Philips', cited in Module 3 study material; Ricketts, 2011). **Merits**: pleasant taste/odour/colour, no staining; shorter set (6-8min) than polysulfide; excellent surface detail; stiffer, better tear resistance than polysulfide. **Demerits**: must be poured **immediately** (low dimensional stability, up to 3% permanent deformation); hydrophobic (field must be completely dry); some cellular toxicity (Sikri, 2017).

### 3. Addition silicone (polyvinylsiloxane, PVS)
Base paste: low-molecular-weight polymethylhydrosiloxane (pendant Si-H groups) + filler; accelerator paste: vinyl-terminated dimethylsiloxane polymer + filler + a platinum catalyst (the "Karstedt type" — platinum/divinyltetramethyldisiloxane complex) (Craig's 14th ed.). Unlike condensation silicone, the addition reaction normally produces **no** low-molecular-weight by-product, giving it the best dimensional stability of all elastomers (permanent deformation only 0.05-0.1% per Sikri, 2017; ANSI/ADA Spec No.19 gives ~99.8% elastic recovery, i.e. only 0.2% permanent deformation, per Powers & Wataha 10th ed., cited in Module 3 study material) — allowing **multiple pours**. **A real secondary reaction**, however, releases **hydrogen gas** if -OH groups (commonly water) are present, requiring a hydrogen absorber (palladium black, polymer fibres) in the material or a pour delay of ≥1 hour (overnight for epoxy dies). **Merits**: pleasant flavoured products; excellent dimensional stability; good tear strength (better than hydrocolloid, less than polyether); can be electroplated; fairly inert chemically once set. **Demerits**: low flexibility (removal from deep undercuts can be difficult); hydrophobic (field must be dry, though "hydrophilic" surfactant-modified versions now exist — contact angle falling from ~90° to 20-40° with added surfactants, Powers & Wataha); setting is **inhibited by latex-glove sulfur compounds poisoning the platinum catalyst** (vinyl/nitrile gloves preferred); occasional foreign-body reactions reported; does not self-adhere (unusable for border moulding) (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011).

### 4. Polyether
Two tubes: base = polyether copolymer + fillers/plasticisers; catalyst = cross-linking agent + filler/plasticiser. Classic chemistry is real **cationic ring-opening polymerisation** of terminal aziridine rings via an aromatic-sulfonate-ester initiator — not a condensation reaction — producing no by-product and therefore good dimensional stability (Craig's 14th ed.; Philips'). A second, "hybrid" (condensation-type) polyether chemistry also exists, releasing alcohol by-products but behaving similarly because the ether-dominated backbone still predominates. **Merits**: the **most hydrophilic** elastomer (moisture in the field least disruptive); dimensionally stable (0.1-0.2% permanent deformation, multiple pours over ~2 weeks); does not tear easily (good subgingival detail without tearing); self-adheres (can be used in border moulding); can be electroplated; unaffected by latex gloves. **Demerits**: very stiff when set (final stiffness roughly double that of addition silicone — difficult removal from undercuts, and may absorb water on storage, compromising die accuracy at high humidity); short working/setting time (3-5min); the classic manual-mix tubes are messy, which is why automixing (Pentamix) systems were originally designed for this material (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011).

### 5. Comparative data and manipulation
A real comparative table (Philips', cited in Module 3 study material) gives working time (polysulfide 4-7min > condensation silicone 2.5-4min > addition silicone 2-4min > polyether 3min) and tear strength (polysulfide highest, 2500-7000 N/m, down to addition silicone's 1500-4300 N/m — though Sikri (2017) separately ranks polyether as having the single highest tear strength and hydrocolloids the lowest, a classification difference worth noting rather than treating as contradictory, since the two sources rank slightly different material sets). Clinically, putty/heavy-body materials are mixed by hand-doughing (equal scoops of base/catalyst), while light-body wash materials are now almost universally dispensed through automix cartridges with a double-helix mixer tip, giving a bubble-free, precisely-proportioned mix (Ricketts, 2011).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, one claim)

**Claim — addition silicone has superseded the older elastomers (polysulfide, condensation silicone) as the material of choice for cast-restoration impressions.** Textbook position: Sikri (2017) and Ricketts (2011) both state addition silicone (with polyether) now dominates clinical use. Supporting evidence across sources: addition silicone's quantified permanent-deformation figures (0.05-0.2%) are an order of magnitude better than condensation silicone's (~3%) and clearly better than polysulfide's (3-5%), directly explaining the shift (Sikri, 2017; Powers & Wataha, via Module 3 study material). INTERPRETATION: the laboratory-measured dimensional-stability advantage translates into a genuine clinical advantage (multiple accurate pours, more forgiving shipping/pour-delay logistics). LIMITATION: this is in-vitro/manufacturer-standard (ANSI/ADA Spec No.19) deformation data, not a clinical trial of crown-margin fit outcomes — the claim about *handling superiority* is well-supported, but neither source cited here supplies a clinical-outcome study directly linking the lower lab-measured deformation to fewer ill-fitting crowns in practice. ## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~40 minutes)**

### Introduction
Elastomers (rubber-base impression materials) were developed to overcome alginate's poor dimensional stability for cast/crown-bridge impressions. Four types exist, introduced in historical order: polysulfide (1950) → condensation silicone (1955) → polyether (1965) → addition silicone (1975).

### Classification
By chemistry: polysulfide, condensation silicone, addition silicone (PVS), polyether.
By viscosity: light body, medium body, heavy body, putty.

### Comparative table

| Property | Polysulfide | Condensation silicone | Addition silicone (PVS) | Polyether |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Setting time | 8-12 min | 6-8 min | 4-5 min | 3-5 min |
| By-product | None (oxidation cross-link) | Ethyl alcohol | None (secondary H₂ possible) | None (classic type) |
| Dimensional stability | Poor (pour in 30min) | Poor (pour immediately) | Excellent (0.05-0.2% deformation) | Excellent (0.1-0.2%) |
| Hydrophilicity | Hydrophobic | Hydrophobic | Hydrophobic (surfactant-modified now available) | Most hydrophilic |
| Tear strength | High | Moderate | Good | Highest |
| Flexibility/removal | Easiest | Stiffer | Low flexibility | Very stiff |
| Latex-glove effect | None | None | Inhibited (Pt catalyst poisoned) | None |
| Border moulding | Cannot self-adhere | — | Cannot self-adhere | Self-adheres — usable |
| Multiple pours | No | No | Yes | Yes (≈2 weeks) |

### Merits of elastomers as a class (over alginate, for cast restorations)
Superior dimensional stability and detail reproduction; elastic recovery from undercuts without permanent distortion; ability to produce metal dies; compatible with crown-and-bridge-grade accuracy and long-span fixed prosthodontics.

### Demerits of elastomers as a class
Costlier than alginate; technique-sensitive (moisture control, latex-glove inhibition for PVS, mixing precision); some (polysulfide, condensation silicone) require immediate/near-immediate pouring; putty-wash techniques carry hydrostatic-recoil distortion risk if the two-stage method is used; special/custom trays often needed.

### Clinical selection
Addition silicone and polyether now dominate for cast/crown-bridge impressions because of superior, quantified dimensional stability; polysulfide is largely obsolete in contemporary practice (odour, long setting time, special-tray requirement); condensation silicone persists mainly where cost is the overriding constraint.

### Conclusion
Elastomers, as a class, solved alginate's core limitation (dimensional instability) for cast-restoration work, but each of the four types carries its own distinct chemistry-linked trade-off (by-product-driven shrinkage, catalyst-poisoning sensitivity, hydrophobicity, or stiffness) — material selection must therefore be matched to the clinical situation (undercut severity, moisture control achievable, need for border moulding, logistics of pouring delay) rather than treated as one uniform "rubber base" choice.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Historical order: Polysulfide (1950) → Condensation silicone (1955) → Polyether (1965) → Addition silicone (1975).
- Addition silicone: Pt (Karstedt) catalyst, no by-product, best dimensional stability, but H₂ gas evolution + latex-glove catalyst poisoning.
- Condensation silicone: ethyl alcohol by-product → poor dimensional stability, pour immediately.
- Polyether: most hydrophilic, self-adheres (usable in border moulding), but very stiff.
- Polysulfide: best flexibility/easiest removal, but worst odour/taste/setting time, obsolete clinically.
- Only addition silicone is inhibited by latex gloves — a frequently-tested discriminator.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Stating latex-glove inhibition applies to "silicones" generically — it is specific to **addition** silicone (platinum catalyst), not condensation silicone.
2. Forgetting condensation silicone's alcohol by-product as the *mechanistic reason* for its poor stability, instead just asserting "less stable" without explanation.
3. Omitting the viscosity-based sub-classification (light/medium/heavy/putty) when asked to "discuss elastomers."
4. Missing polyether's unique ability to self-adhere/be used in border moulding, which PVS/polysulfide cannot do.
5. Not critically weighing merits against demerits (the command word is "critically discuss") — a pure list of properties without a selection/trade-off discussion loses marks.
