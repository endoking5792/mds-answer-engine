## STUDY MODE

### S1. The design problem GIC was invented to solve [basic science]
Every direct restorative has to answer three questions: does it stay on the tooth, does it seal the margin against bacteria, and does it survive load? Before GIC the two acid-base cements each answered half of that. Silicate cement released fluoride and was translucent, and recurrent caries around it was unusually rare, but it dissolved in saliva and had poor strength (Hilton et al, 2013). Zinc polycarboxylate (developed in the 1960s) was the first cement with real adhesion to tooth, but its low tensile strength meant the adhesion did not last (Mount, 1994) and it was too soluble and weak to restore teeth (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Replacing the zinc oxide of polycarboxylate with the ion-leachable glass of silicate cement gave a stronger, less soluble, more translucent material (McCabe and Walls, 2008). That is why GIC is described as combining the adhesion of polycarboxylate with the fluoride release of silicate (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). The whole later history of GIC is a series of attempts to keep those two strengths while fixing its two weaknesses, brittleness and water sensitivity.

### S2. PAST: how the material developed

**The dates are not agreed.** Bhat and van Noort give Wilson and Kent, 1972 (Bhat et al, 2019; van Noort and Barbour, 2013); Powers also gives 1972 (Powers and Wataha, 2013); a DCNA review says 1969 (Hill, 2007); Craig's 14th edition says "the 1960s" on one page but "the 1970s" on another (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). The sensible reading is that discovery and first publication fall between 1969 and 1972, and that the 1972 date used in Indian textbooks is the safe one to write. [Likely] First clinical use is attributed to McLean and Wilson in 1975, and the first product was ASPA (aluminosilicate polyacrylate) cement with a citric acid conditioner (Bhat et al, 2019).

**Why the early cements failed, and what fixed them** (this chain is a good model for a "past" answer):
- The original liquid, a 50% polyacrylic acid solution, gelled within months, probably by hydrogen bonding between chains; copolymers (itaconic, maleic acid) prevent this by steric hindrance (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount, 1994).
- Early formulations used high-fluoride, opaque glasses to get a fast set; tartaric acid let manufacturers use lower-fluoride, more translucent glasses and sharpened the set (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
- Water-hardening ("anhydrous") cements, with freeze-dried polyacid blended in the powder, first appeared commercially in 1981 (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- Early ASPA restorations failed mainly because they were left unsealed, so they took up water or dried out and cracked; properly sealed replacements lasted at least eight years (Mount et al, 2016). Handling, not chemistry, was the lesson.
- Wilson and McLean's 1988 classification (Type I luting, II restorative, III lining/base) is the one still used in Mount's texts (Mount, 1994).
- Resin-modified GIC (RMGIC) arose in the 1980s, when water-soluble polymer or polymerisable resin was added to the liquid (Hill, 2007); dates quoted range from the late 1980s (Sakaguchi et al, 2019*) to the early 1990s (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- High-viscosity GICs (Fuji VIII, IX) arrived in the 1990s for minimal-preparation restorations (Bhat et al, 2019); compomers were introduced from about 1995 (Powers and Wataha, 2013) and are not glass ionomers (Mount et al, 2016); nano-ionomers (RMGIC with nanomers and nanoclusters) have been available since 2007 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### S3. PRESENT: chemistry, mechanism and why it behaves as it does

**Composition.** A powder of ion-leachable calcium (or strontium) fluoro-alumino-silicate glass and a liquid that is a 40-55% solution of a 2:1 acrylic/itaconic acid copolymer (or a maleic/acrylic copolymer), with tartaric acid 5-15% (Mount et al, 2016). A typical glass (weight %): SiO2 29.0, Al2O3 16.6, CaF2 34.2, Na3AlF6 5.0, AlF3 5.3, AlPO4 9.9 (Mount et al, 2016). The glass is fused (1000-1500 deg C), quenched in water so it cracks, then ground (Bhat et al, 2019); particle size runs from 4 to 50 micrometres, finer for luting and lining and coarser for restoratives (Mount et al, 2016). Fluoride is in the glass as a flux and oxide scavenger, not as part of the matrix (Mount et al, 2016). The glass must be reactive enough: the alumina:silica ratio has to exceed 1:2 by mass for the acid to attack it (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

**Setting reaction (three overlapping stages).**
1. Dissolution: the polyacid attacks the glass surface; roughly 20-30% of the glass decomposes, releasing calcium/strontium, aluminium and fluoride ions (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Precipitation and gelation: calcium/strontium ions cross-link the polyacid chains first, giving a clinically hard surface within about 4-10 minutes, then aluminium ions cross-link later and form the strong, insoluble matrix (Mount et al, 2016).
3. Maturation: progressive hydration of the matrix salts sharply improves properties over the next day and beyond (Mount et al, 2016).
The set cement is unreacted glass cores in a siliceous gel inside a cross-linked polysalt matrix (Mount et al, 2016). About 24% of it is water, some loosely bound (lost easily early on) and some tightly bound (part of the hydration shell) (Mount et al, 2016).

**Why water balance matters.** Calcium cross-links form first, are fragile and wash out if the cement takes up water early; aluminium cross-links form later (Mount et al, 2016). Early water uptake dissolves the matrix, early dehydration cracks it. The cement is most vulnerable during Stage 2 and stays vulnerable to water loss for some time afterwards (Mount et al, 2016). Modern auto-cure cements, made with calcium removed from the glass surface, tolerate early water better, but waterproof sealing immediately after placement is still recommended (Mount et al, 2016). This one principle explains almost every handling rule below.

**Adhesion.** The polyacid's carboxyl groups displace phosphate ions from the apatite, each phosphate taking a calcium ion with it, and an ion-enriched interface layer forms between cement and tooth (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). Brief conditioning with 10% polyacrylic acid (10-15 s) removes the smear layer, wets the surface and pre-activates calcium and phosphate ions (Mount, 1994); left longer than about 20 s it begins to demineralise (Mount, 1994). The tooth must never be acid-etched before GIC (Mount et al, 2016). **A real disagreement about bond strength:** Mount says the bond strength has not been measured, because the failure is cohesive in the cement, so bond tests actually measure the cement's tensile strength (Mount et al, 2016). Craig's 11th edition gives a dentine bond of 2-3 MPa (Craig, 2002). Both statements are in textbooks; the Mount reading is the more careful one. [Likely]

**Fluoride.** Release is high in the first days, falls over the first weeks and settles by 2-3 months at a low steady level that continues for years (Mount et al, 2016); the cement takes fluoride back up from toothpaste or topical agents and so acts as a reservoir (Mount et al, 2016). Summitt's key point is that the ability to be recharged matters more than the size of the initial release (Hilton et al, 2013), and that GICs and RMGICs recharge best (Hilton et al, 2013). Release is higher at lower pH (Hilton et al, 2013). Acidic topical fluorides (APF) can roughen and degrade the cement (Hilton et al, 2013).

**Properties, in numbers.** Seven-day strengths (MPa) in Mount & Hume's Table 9.2, with large variation between products: Type II.1 compressive 70-220, diametral tensile 12-20; Type II.2 compressive 140-220; Type I luting compressive 70-150 (Mount et al, 2016). ISO 9917 minimums: compressive strength 130 MPa for restoratives and 70 MPa for luting and lining; film thickness 25 micrometres for luting; setting time 2-6 min for restoratives; acid erosion 0.05 mm/h (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Flexural strength is only 15-20 MPa against more than 70 MPa for composites (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Thermal expansion is close to tooth (10-11 against 11.4), which is why the cement stays bonded through temperature change (Mount et al, 2016). In one reported comparison, one-year wear was 73 micrometres for GIC against under 10 micrometres per year for composites (Hilton et al, 2013). In practice GIC is not for marginal ridges, incisal corners or sole support of a crown (Mount, 1994).

**Biocompatibility.** Fresh cement is very acidic (pH about 0.9-1.6) but dentine buffers it, and the pulp response is mild and resolves in 10-20 days as the pH rises within the first hour (Mount et al, 2016). Poly(alkenoic) acid chains are large and do not readily enter the tubules (Mount, 1994). Pulpal inflammation is driven by bacteria rather than by the cement, so a complete seal is what protects the pulp (Mount, 1994). Direct contact with an exposed pulp is different: without intervening dentine GIC is quite toxic to exposed pulp, so calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate material goes first (Mount et al, 2016). RMGIC tends to be more cytotoxic than conventional GIC (Hilton et al, 2013), consistent with HEMA release in laboratory studies (Agha et al, 2021) [Likely]. Post-cementation sensitivity with GIC luting is no worse than with zinc phosphate (Mount et al, 2016).

### S4. PRESENT: classification and which type for which job [clinical decision-making]

| Type (Wilson & McLean 1988, Mount) | Use | Powder:liquid | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| I luting | crowns, bridges, inlays, orthodontic bands | about 1.5:1 | film thickness 25 micrometres or less, fast set (Mount, 1994) |
| II.1 restorative, aesthetic | any aesthetic restoration without undue load | 2.8:1 to 6.8:1 | auto-cure or dual-cure (Mount, 1994) |
| II.2 restorative, reinforced | non-aesthetic use needing fast set and strength | 3:1 to 4:1 | metal-modified or high-strength (Mount, 1994) |
| III lining/base | lining 1.5:1; base or dentine substitute 3:1 or more | | only difference between lining and base is the P/L ratio (Mount, 1994) |

Phillips uses the same idea with different labels (Type I luting; IIa aesthetic restorative; IIb reinforced restorative; III lining/base) (Anusavice et al, 2013), and Bhat adds fissure sealant, orthodontic cement and core build-up types (Bhat et al, 2019).

**Decision logic** used in Mount's system: GIC is the material of choice where the lesion is not under heavy occlusal load (Mount et al, 2016); for cervical (Site 3) lesions a Type II.1 cement, auto-cure or resin-modified depending on light access (Mount et al, 2016); for proximal tunnels, a radiopaque high-strength auto-cure cement (Mount et al, 2016). Where load is high, use a composite or amalgam laminate over a GIC base (Mount et al, 2016).

### S5. PRESENT: modified materials and what is, and is not, a glass ionomer
- **Metal-reinforced:** silver cermet (about 40% by weight micro-fine silver sintered to glass, with up to 5% titanium dioxide to restore colour) and amalgam-alloy admixes. They improve abrasion resistance but not other properties and may reduce the ion-exchange adhesion (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). They are no longer popular (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Resin-modified GIC:** liquid with 15-25% resin (HEMA and other methacrylates) (Mount et al, 2016); sets partly by acid-base reaction and partly by light and chemical polymerisation, and still sets in the dark (Mount et al, 2016). Gains: immediate set on light, higher strength. In one manufacturer comparison, setting time fell from 4 min to 20 s and diametral tensile strength rose from 16 to 37 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Costs: residual HEMA of 4.5% to possibly 15% (Mount et al, 2016), water uptake and swelling (Mount et al, 2016), and (some sources) polymerisation shrinkage of 3-4% (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Mount's counter-point: acid-base shrinkage is small and the water uptake offsets it (Mount et al, 2016).
- **High-viscosity GIC:** finer glass (about 3 micrometres) and polyacid added to the powder give amalgam-like handling; strength changes little but abrasion resistance seems better (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Used for ART (Bhat et al, 2019); not for load-bearing areas of permanent teeth (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Nano-ionomer:** nanoscale fillers in an RMGIC to improve polish and optics; data are sparse and the benefits are laboratory claims (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Not glass ionomers:** compomers (will not set without light; no acid-base dark set) (Mount et al, 2016); giomers (pre-reacted glass-ionomer particles in a resin matrix, so no ion exchange) (Mount et al, 2016). A simple clinical test: a true GIC reaches a reasonable set within about 7-10 minutes under a light-proof cover, while imitation materials stay soft or rubbery (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994). (Sources disagree on 5-7 versus 10 minutes: (Mount, 1994*).) [Likely]

### S6. PRESENT: technique, because it decides outcome [treatment]
1. Isolate, clean the surface (pumice and water), condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 s, wash, dry lightly without dehydrating (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Use a capsule if possible: standardised P/L ratio and mixing; hand mixing is possible but powder content varies (Mount, 1994). Mix for about 10 seconds; placement must be in the working window, judged by loss of gloss (Mount, 1994).
3. Place under a matrix with pressure (Mount et al, 2016).
4. **Seal at once** with a single-component, very low-viscosity light-cured unfilled resin; a varnish is less ideal because the solvent leaves porosity (Mount, 1994).
5. Delay final finishing, ideally 24 hours (a week for best translucency) (Mount, 1994); fast-set Type II.2 can be finished from about 6 minutes (Mount, 1994).
**Cavity design.** Because the bond is chemical, the preparation can be minimal: no mechanical undercuts (Mount et al, 2016). For Class V, a butt joint (cavosurface margin near 90 degrees) with about 1 mm minimum depth, no bevel, because GIC is brittle and needs bulk (Hilton et al, 2013). Minor undercuts left by caries removal can be filled with GIC rather than cutting more tooth (Mount et al, 2016).
**Sandwich (bi-layered) technique.** GIC replaces dentine, composite replaces enamel. Mount's logic: composite shrinks and its dentine bond is uncertain, whereas GIC bonds dentine reliably and, being water-based, does not hydrolyse (Mount, 1994). Closed sandwich covers the GIC with composite; open sandwich leaves GIC exposed at the gingival margin to release fluoride (Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013). Cement must be at least 1 mm thick at the highest P/L available (Mount, 1994).

### S7. PRESENT: outcome and prognosis
Failures are mostly handling failures: ditching from a low-powder mix or early water contact, cracks from dehydration, bulk fracture from overload (Mount et al, 2016). Reported figures vary with the study: in Summitt's summary, Class I and Class II GIC restorations showed 8% and 40% failure at 24 months (Hilton et al, 2013); a Class V study showed 80% retention at 10 years with no mechanical retention (Hilton et al, 2013); GIC survives better than amalgam in irradiated xerostomic patients without topical fluoride, but not with it (Hilton et al, 2013). Randomised-trial evidence is set out in the synthesis below.

### S8. FUTURE: where the field is going
- **Aim of research:** higher fracture resistance without losing the ion-exchange adhesion and fluoride release, which are the whole point of the material (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Antibacterial additives** (chlorhexidine, chitosan) and remineralising agents (CPP-ACP): results are equivocal (Mount et al, 2016); metallic nano-additives (silver, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) improve antibacterial action in laboratory work, but clinical benefit is unproven (Danelon et al, 2025; Guo et al, 2024); hydroxyapatite-modified GIC is promising in the laboratory (Zhang et al, 2025).
- **Ion-releasing resin restoratives (alkasite, bioactive resins)** are the new competitors, not true GICs (Laporte et al, 2026; Popa et al, 2026).
- **Glass hybrid (EQUIA-type)** systems pair high-viscosity GIC with a resin coat, with 5-year survival comparable to composite in Class II cavities (Miletic et al, 2024).
- **Predicted but unverified:** the intended direction is bioactivity with composite-level strength. [Guessing]


## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
Four claims where textbook statements and published evidence do not line up. Study types are named because laboratory data cannot be read as clinical proof.

### Claim 1. Does the fluoride a GIC releases prevent recurrent (secondary) caries?
- **Textbook position.** Mount's Atlas says chemical union plus fluoride gives "almost total prevention of recurrent caries" (Mount, 1994). More cautious texts call it "a matter of some debate" (McCabe and Walls, 2008) or "disputed", citing retrospective and heterogeneous studies (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Summitt describes the evidence as "modest but growing" and notes that in two retrospective reports GIC developed recurrent caries within 5 years and almost half of GIC restorations were replaced (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Retrieved evidence.** A meta-analysis of 64 RCTs (8310 GIC against 5857 amalgam or composite restorations, 1-10 years) found secondary caries was lower with GIC than amalgam (primary teeth RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.41-0.72; permanent teeth RR 0.20, 0.11-0.38) but not lower than composite (primary RR 0.92, 0.77-1.10; permanent RR 0.77, 0.39-1.51) (Ge et al, 2023). A second meta-analysis of 40 RCTs pooled bioactive materials against composite and amalgam and found RR 0.55 (0.46-0.65) with moderate certainty, GICs alone RR 0.36 and giomers no benefit (RR 1.09) (Zailai et al, 2026). The ADA evidence review summarised by Summitt reached 65% lower chance of recurrent caries against amalgam in permanent teeth (Hilton et al, 2013). In the laboratory, GICs still released the most fluoride of all direct materials (Justen et al, 2024), and GI sealants showed a burst in the first 24-48 hours then slower release and better recharge than resin sealants (Dobrzynski et al, 2025).
- **Interpretation.** The advantage over amalgam is consistent across sources. The Atlas's "almost total prevention" is not supported, because there is no advantage over composite in the RCT meta-analysis. The bioactive-material benefit depends on the comparator: the pooled control group mixes amalgam and composite, so the amalgam contrast can drive it (this is a reviewer's inference, not stated in the abstract). [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Abstract-level reading only. The fluoride data are in vitro; no study isolated fluoride release as the mechanism. Certainty for Ge 2023 is not stated in the abstract.
- **Conclusion and implication.** What can be concluded: GIC restorations have fewer secondary caries than amalgam; fluoride release is highest in GICs in the laboratory. What cannot: that fluoride release itself is the reason, or that GIC beats composite. Clinical use: pick GIC for high-caries-risk or moisture-compromised situations, and do not promise caries-free margins.

### Claim 2. How long do GIC and high-viscosity (ART) restorations last?
- **Textbook position.** Mount, Summitt and van Noort all restrict GIC to areas without heavy occlusal load; high-viscosity GICs are "still inferior to composite" in wear and not for load-bearing permanent areas (Hilton et al, 2013); van Noort does not recommend GIC for adult Class II (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). A DCNA review notes high-viscosity GI Class II restorations were satisfactory at 3 years despite many failures, and cites a retrospective failure rate of 60% at 72 months (Vaderhobli, 2011).
- **Retrieved evidence.** A systematic review of 14 RCTs (only 2 at low risk of bias) found no significant difference in survival between ART/high-viscosity GIC and traditional restorations for single- and multiple-surface cavities in primary molars and single-surface cavities in permanent posterior teeth at 1, 2, 3 and 5 years; at 4.3 and 6.3 years the difference favoured ART/HVGIC; no significant difference for multiple-surface permanent restorations (Frencken et al, 2021). A second review (67 articles, non-randomised designs included) found the conventional technique better than ART for single-surface occlusal and multi-surface GIC restorations, with higher annual failure rates for multi-surface restorations (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018). An umbrella review of 13 reviews graded the evidence "conflicting" and the medium- and long-term performance "still questionable" (Panetta et al, 2024). In a 5-year randomised trial of two-surface Class II molar restorations, glass hybrid and nano-hybrid composite had the same survival (94.5% against 94.4%) although the success rate was numerically lower for glass hybrid (81.9% against 90.7%) (Miletic et al, 2024); in molar-incisor hypomineralisation molars, 5-year survival was 59.2% for glass hybrid against 75.5% for fibre-reinforced composite (Dokumacigil et al, 2026).
- **Interpretation.** The textbooks' caution is partly refined, not overturned: modern high-viscosity and glass-hybrid materials do acceptably in single-surface cavities and primary teeth, and in one good trial in moderate Class II cavities. The reviews disagree on ART versus conventional technique, and the textbooks' blanket exclusion of Class II is too strong for the newer materials. [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Most trials are small or at high risk of bias; review abstracts do not report certainty ratings; the MIH result is population-specific.
- **Conclusion and implication.** Can conclude: no proven survival penalty for ART/HVGIC in single-surface and primary-tooth cavities up to 5 years. Cannot conclude equivalence for multi-surface permanent-tooth restorations. Clinical use: reserve GIC for single-surface, low-load or high-risk situations; use composite where load is high.

### Claim 3. Does the sandwich (bi-layered) technique improve restoration success?
- **Textbook position.** Mount strongly recommends a GIC base under composite where a margin lies on dentine, citing composite shrinkage and an uncertain dentine bond (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016), while admitting early sandwich studies showed more bulk fractures (Mount et al, 2016). A DCNA article states routine GI liners under composite are not evidence-supported (Hayashi, 2020). Summitt reports an open sandwich with RMGI successful at 5 years in moderate-to-high caries-risk patients, but a 9-year compomer trial found no difference in secondary caries (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Retrieved evidence.** A meta-analysis of RCTs in non-carious cervical lesions found only loss of retention differed: lower with the sandwich technique at 3 years (RR 7.5, 95% CI 2.1-27.2), but the review contained only 4 studies, all at unclear risk of bias (de Paula et al, 2019). An umbrella review also states that the sandwich technique with RMGIC bases improved retention in non-carious cervical lesions (Fernandez et al, 2025). Laboratory reviews show composite-to-GIC bonding depends on surface treatment (Ismail et al, 2025).
- **Interpretation.** Mount's rationale is supported only for cervical lesions and only for retention, on thin evidence. For Class I and II, and for open versus closed sandwich, no trial evidence was found in this retrieval. [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Wide confidence interval; the exact figure should not be quoted as a precise effect.
- **Conclusion and implication.** Use the sandwich technique where dentine margins or caries risk make composite bonding unreliable, and say that the evidence is limited.

### Claim 4. Are the new ion-releasing and bioactive materials better than GIC?
- **Textbook position.** Mount judges antibacterial and remineralising additives "equivocal" in value (Mount et al, 2016) and nano-ionomer claims are laboratory-only (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Retrieved evidence.** In a meta-analysis of clinical studies, alkasite materials performed comparably to resin composite and "superior" to GIC, with no pooled numbers in the abstract and moderate risk of bias in several studies (Laporte et al, 2026). The laboratory network meta-analysis found alkasite properties similar to composite and stronger than GIC, while GICs still released the most fluoride (Justen et al, 2024). One small non-randomised pilot (36 restorations, 6 months) reported dislodgement in 1 of 28 alkasite against 6 of 8 Fuji IX restorations (Padilla-Ocampo et al, 2025). ACTIVA BioACTIVE in primary molars performed comparably to composite and compomer at 12-24 months (Popa et al, 2026).
- **Interpretation.** Resin-based ion-releasing materials win on strength; GIC wins on fluoride release. Bioactivity has not been shown to give a clinical caries-preventive advantage. [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Short follow-up, small trials; the pilot is hypothesis-generating only.
- **Conclusion and implication.** Alkasite and bioactive resins are credible alternatives where strength matters; none replaces GIC's chemical adhesion to dentine.

### 1. Introduction
- **Definition.** A water-based cement formed by acid-base reaction between a polyalkenoic acid and an ion-leachable, fluoride-containing calcium or strontium aluminosilicate glass; official ISO name "glass polyalkenoate cement" (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Credit.** Wilson and Kent, early 1970s (1972 in most textbooks; some give 1969) (Bhat et al, 2019; Hill, 2007) [Likely].
- **Parentage.** Glass from silicate cement (fluoride, translucency) plus polyacid from zinc polycarboxylate (adhesion) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Key claims to fame.** Chemical bond to enamel and dentine, fluoride release and uptake, biocompatibility; weakness is brittleness and water sensitivity (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. PAST: historical evolution
| Period | Event |
|---|---|
| Before 1970 | Silicate cement: fluoride and translucency, but soluble (Hilton et al, 2013). Polycarboxylate (1960s): first adhesive cement, but weak (Mount, 1994). |
| 1969-1972 | Wilson and Kent: ASPA cement (aluminosilicate polyacrylate) (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| 1975 | First clinical use reported by McLean and Wilson (Bhat et al, 2019) |
| 1970s | Liquid gelled; copolymers (itaconic, maleic) and tartaric acid solved storage and set (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount, 1994) |
| 1981 | Water-hardening ("anhydrous") cements marketed (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| 1988 | Wilson and McLean classification: Types I, II, III (Mount, 1994) |
| 1980s-early 1990s | Resin-modified GIC (Hill, 2007; Powers and Wataha, 2013) |
| 1990s | High-viscosity GIC (Fuji VIII, IX) for ART (Bhat et al, 2019); compomers (about 1995), not true GIC (Powers and Wataha, 2013; Mount et al, 2016) |
| 2007 | Nano-ionomer (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) |
| Today | Glass hybrid systems; ion-releasing resins (alkasite, bioactive) (Laporte et al, 2026) |
- **Lesson of the early years:** most early failures were unsealed restorations that took up water or dried out and cracked (Mount et al, 2016).
- **A useful observation (Mount and Hume):** while dentine bonding agents went through eight "generations", glass ionomer chemical adhesion stayed at one generation and was reliable throughout (Mount et al, 2016). GIC derivatives have also been used as bone replacement in surgery (Mount, 1994).

### 3. PRESENT: composition and manufacture
**Powder:** ion-leachable fluoro-alumino-silicate glass; typical weight %: SiO2 29.0, Al2O3 16.6, CaF2 34.2, Na3AlF6 5.0, AlF3 5.3, AlPO4 9.9 (Mount et al, 2016).
- Silica gives translucency and hardness; alumina forms the polyacrylate matrix; fluorides act as flux and are released (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Strontium or lanthanum add radiopacity (Mount et al, 2016).
**Liquid:** 40-55% aqueous 2:1 acrylic/itaconic acid copolymer (or maleic/acrylic) (Mount et al, 2016); tartaric acid 5-15% speeds the set and sharpens it (Mount et al, 2016).
**Manufacture:** ingredients fused at 1000-1500 deg C, plunged into water, ground (Bhat et al, 2019); particle size 4-50 micrometres: finer for luting and lining, coarser for restoratives (Mount et al, 2016); glass reactivity is controlled by fusion temperature and heat history (Mount et al, 2016).
**Why each component is there.** Tartaric acid does two things: it first binds released calcium (extending working time) and then speeds aluminium cross-linking, so the set is sharper (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). The alumina:silica ratio of the glass is higher than in silicate cement because polyacrylic acid is a much weaker acid than phosphoric acid; the cost is a shorter working time (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Polyacid molecular weight and concentration raise strength but also viscosity, which is why dehydrated polyacid in the powder became popular (Mount, 1994).
**Presentations:** powder and liquid; water-hardening (polyacid dried into powder, water or dilute tartaric acid as liquid); capsules; paste-paste (Mount et al, 2016).

### 4. Setting reaction
1. **Dissolution:** acid attacks the glass; about 20-30% decomposes, releasing Ca/Sr, Al and F ions (Mount et al, 2016).
2. **Gelation:** Ca/Sr ions cross-link polyacid chains (early set, fragile, water-soluble); initial hard set at about 4-10 min (Mount et al, 2016).
3. **Hardening/maturation:** Al ions cross-link slowly (strong, insoluble); hydration of the matrix continues for 24 hours and longer (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Set structure:** unreacted glass cores, siliceous gel, polysalt matrix; about 24% water, loosely and tightly bound (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Water balance:** early water uptake dissolves the matrix; early drying cracks it (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Resin-modified GIC:** acid-base plus light/chemical polymerisation; the material hardens on light but full set takes days (Mount et al, 2016).
**Factors that alter the rate of set:** glass composition and fusion temperature, particle size, tartaric acid content, powder:liquid ratio and mixing temperature; a chilled slab and powder lengthen the working time by up to 25%, but the liquid must never be refrigerated because it can gel (Mount et al, 2016). High ambient temperature speeds the reaction (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Properties
**(a) Adhesion.** Ionic exchange with apatite: carboxyl groups displace phosphate; an ion-enriched layer forms (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). Condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 s; do not acid-etch (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). Failure is cohesive, within the cement, so the stronger the cement the better the "bond" (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Why polyacrylic acid is the conditioner:** it is the cement's own acid, so residue does not interfere with setting; it lowers the surface energy of tooth and improves wetting; it is mild and does not unduly open tubules, because plugs remain after 10 seconds (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

**(b) Fluoride.** High release for the first weeks, falling to a low steady level over 2-3 months and continuing for years (Mount et al, 2016); recharged from toothpaste and topical fluoride, so it is a reservoir (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013). Clinical value is debated (see section 10). For remineralisation to start, salivary fluoride should reach at least 10 ppm, a level GICs can provide (Mount et al, 2016); Summitt recommends long-term release of at least 2-3 micrograms per mL per day, achievable only with supplemental fluoride recharge (Hilton et al, 2013). Fluoride also suppresses *Streptococcus mutans* growth on the cement surface (Mount, 1994).
**(c) Biological.** Fresh pH 0.9-1.6, buffered by dentine; mild pulp response resolving in 10-20 days (Mount et al, 2016). On an exposed pulp, cover with calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate material first (Mount et al, 2016). Resists plaque; gingival tolerance high (Mount, 1994). The aim of a lining is to seal the dentine against bacteria, not to deliver a therapeutic effect through sound dentine (Mount, 1994).
**(d) Mechanical.** Compressive strength 70-220 MPa by type; flexural 15-20 MPa (composite over 70) (Mount et al, 2016; McCabe and Walls, 2008). ISO 9917 minimum compressive strength 130 MPa (restorative), 70 MPa (luting, lining) (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Brittle; poor shear resistance; low wear resistance (Mount, 1994).
**(e) Thermal.** Expansion 10-11 against tooth 11.4, diffusivity close to dentine: good insulation and little stress at the interface (Mount et al, 2016).
**(f) Optical.** Translucent; translucency develops over days; dehydration or early water ruins it (Mount et al, 2016).
**(g) Solubility and erosion.** Low in lactic acid; vulnerable to acidic fluorides (APF) and fresh cement in acid (Mount et al, 2016). ISO limit 0.05 mm/h (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
**(h) Radiopacity.** Most restorative GICs are more radiopaque than dentine, but some auto-cure Type II.1 cements are radiolucent (Mount et al, 2016).

**By setting:** auto-cure (acid-base only), dual-cure (plus light), tri-cure (plus chemical initiator) (Mount et al, 2016).
**Phillips:** Types I, IIa, IIb, III (Anusavice et al, 2013).
**Bhat:** adds fissure sealant (IV), orthodontic cement (V) and core build-up (VI) (Bhat et al, 2019).

### 7. Modifications and newer materials
1. **Metal-reinforced:** silver cermet (about 40% silver by weight sintered to glass, up to 5% TiO2) and amalgam-alloy admix; better abrasion resistance, no other gain, less ion-exchange (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
2. **Resin-modified:** 15-25% resin (HEMA); sets on light and in the dark; higher strength and immediate finishing; drawbacks are residual HEMA (4.5% to 15%), water uptake, some shrinkage (Mount et al, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
3. **Tri-cure:** adds redox chemical initiator (Mount et al, 2016).
4. **High-viscosity:** finer glass (about 3 micrometres), higher P/L; packable; ART; not for load-bearing permanent areas (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Hilton et al, 2013).
5. **Nano-ionomer:** nanomers and nanoclusters in RMGIC for polish and optics; clinical data sparse (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
6. **Glass hybrid:** high-viscosity GIC plus resin coat; 5-year survival in Class II comparable to composite in one RCT (Miletic et al, 2024).
7. **Not true GIC:** compomer, giomer, alkasite (resin-based) (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Dark-set test** to tell a true GIC from an imitation: sets within about 7-10 min under light-proof cover (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).

### 7A. Resin-modified, high-viscosity and delivery variants in more detail
- **RMGIC gains:** set on light, more strength and toughness, early resistance to water, good translucency from the start (Mount et al, 2016; van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **RMGIC losses:** HEMA is hydrophilic, so the cement takes up water and swells in the first 5-7 days and may stain (Mount et al, 2016); residual HEMA is released into dentine (Mount et al, 2016); some powders are sensitive to ambient light and should be dispensed only at the moment of mixing (Mount et al, 2016); it is more cytotoxic than conventional GIC (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Auto-cure RMGIC** (resin catalyst, no light needed) is useful where light cannot reach, as in minimal cavities (Mount et al, 2016).
- **High-viscosity advantages:** packable, non-sticky, reduced early moisture sensitivity, quicker finishing, better wear resistance, low solubility (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Delivery:** capsules remove porosity and standardise the ratio; automixed RMGIC avoids air entrapment; granulated powder mixes in under half the time (Mount et al, 2016); paste-paste systems (film thickness about 5 micrometres, set in 3 min) are used for luting and orthodontic work but lose some fluoride release and properties (Mount et al, 2016).

### 8. Clinical uses and technique
**Uses:**
- Luting: crowns, bridges, bands; reasons are low solubility, fluoride, thixotropic flow; retention comes from the preparation, since cement tensile strength is only 2-4 MPa (Mount et al, 2016).
- Restorative: Class III, Class V (including erosion and abrasion), root caries, deciduous teeth, small posterior lesions not under load (Mount et al, 2016).
- Lining and base: best long-term seal to dentine; under amalgam and composite (Mount, 1994).
- Sealants: partially erupted teeth, caries-active children (Mount et al, 2016).
- ART and minimal intervention: sealing the lesion can arrest caries; high-viscosity GIC (Mount et al, 2016; Bhat et al, 2019).
- Core: only where at least half the crown and a 3 mm sound collar remain (Mount et al, 2016).
- Indirect pulp capping and transitional restoration (Mount et al, 2016).
**Technique:**
1. Isolate; clean with pumice; condition 10% PAA 10 s; wash; dry lightly (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Capsule mix about 10 s; place in the working window (Mount, 1994).
3. Matrix and pressure (Mount et al, 2016).
4. Seal at once with unfilled light-cured resin (varnish second best) (Mount, 1994).
5. Finish at 24 hours or later (Mount, 1994).
**Cavity design.** Adhesion removes the need for undercuts; minimal tunnels and slots possible (Mount, 1994). Class V: butt joint, no bevel, about 1 mm depth (Hilton et al, 2013). Leave affected dentine if the margin is sealed (Mount, 1994).
**Sandwich technique (bi-layered):**
- GIC replaces dentine, composite replaces enamel.
- Rationale: composite shrinks and its dentine bond is uncertain; GIC is water-based and bonds reliably (Mount, 1994).
- Steps: place high-P/L GIC (at least 1 mm), cut back to expose enamel margins, etch enamel (and cement 15 s for auto-cure), bond, add composite incrementally (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
- Open sandwich leaves GIC at the gingival margin for fluoride release (Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013).

### 8A. Site-wise cavity design and placement (Mount and Hume system)
- **Occlusal and proximal lesions (Sites 1 and 2):** GIC is the best initial restoration; use the strongest, preferably auto-cure, high-P/L cement and laminate with composite or amalgam if the load is heavy; no sub-lining (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Tunnel (occlusal approach):** enter through the fossa medial to the marginal ridge, remove infected dentine only, leave affected axial dentine (Mount, 1994); Type II.2 radiopaque capsulated cement in two increments tamped with a plastic sponge under a mylar strip, trimmed at about 5-6 minutes (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Cervical (Site 3) lesions:** Size 2 gets a Type II.1 cement, auto-cure or resin-modified (Mount et al, 2016); Size 3 (approximal root-surface or recurrent caries) gets a radiopaque, high-strength auto-cure cement, conditioned 10 s, placed in two increments and sealed with low-viscosity resin (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Large cavities:** a high-strength GIC base as a thermal barrier with ion exchange; calcium hydroxide sub-lining unnecessary (Mount et al, 2016); if the floor is very near the pulp, a thin high-fluoride auto-cure sub-lining may be added (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Undercuts left by caries removal** are filled with GIC rather than cut away (Mount et al, 2016).

### 8B. Special uses
- **Luting.** Vital tooth: do not condition dentine with polyacrylic acid because of hydraulic pressure; use a mineralising solution or tannic acid if needed and vent the crown. Non-vital tooth: condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 s (Mount, 1994). Post cementation: condition the posthole, dry with alcohol, wind the cement in with a Lentulo spiral (Mount, 1994).
- **Fissure sealant.** Pumice, condition with 10-20% polyacrylic acid for 10 s, dry field, protect with unfilled resin (Mount et al, 2016); best in partially erupted teeth and caries-active individuals (Mount et al, 2016). Resin sealants remain the first choice in many texts because of retention (Mount et al, 2016) [Likely].
- **Paediatric.** Many deciduous teeth can be restored with GIC alone (Mount et al, 2016); hypoplastic permanent molars are held with GIC while they erupt (Mount et al, 2016); stainless steel crowns on primary molars are cemented with GIC (Mount et al, 2016).
- **ART and high-viscosity GIC:** minimum-preparation restorations with hand instruments (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Root caries and high-risk patients:** root lesions need more fluoride than enamel to remineralise (Hilton et al, 2013); for xerostomic and high-risk patients, RMGIC is an aesthetic choice for Class V (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Indirect pulp capping and transitional restoration:** GIC (or RMGIC over calcium hydroxide) seals residual caries; it is relatively insoluble and releases ions (Hilton et al, 2013; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Orthodontics:** bands cemented with GIC show better retention and less demineralisation than zinc phosphate (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Cervical resorption:** after removal of resorptive tissue the defect is restored with GIC protected by unfilled composite resin (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Elderly patients:** repair rather than replace; GIC preferred for its anti-caries value (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Therapeutic coating:** a long-lasting protective coat on susceptible surfaces where hygiene is poor (Mount et al, 2016).

### 9. Advantages, disadvantages, failure
**Advantages:** chemical adhesion; fluoride release and recharge; biocompatibility; thermal expansion close to tooth; conservative cavity; useful where isolation is difficult (Mount et al, 2016; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
**Disadvantages:** brittle, low tensile and shear strength; low wear resistance; water sensitivity; staining and loss of gloss; slow maturation; not for marginal ridge, incisal corner or heavy load (Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013).
**Failure causes:** low-powder mix or early water contact (ditching), dehydration (cracks), overload (bulk fracture), contaminated surface (loss in erosion lesions) (Mount et al, 2016).
**Reported clinical performance (textbook-quoted):** Class V GIC without mechanical retention, 80% retention at 10 years (Hilton et al, 2013); Class I and Class II failure 8% and 40% at 24 months (Hilton et al, 2013); ADA review: 65% lower chance of recurrent caries than amalgam in permanent teeth (Hilton et al, 2013); Class V resin-modified GIC retained in 96.4% at 5 years against 51.5% for bonded composite in one study (Hilton et al, 2013); in retrospective reports almost half of GIC restorations were replaced for recurrent caries, which shows fluoride release does not remove caries risk (Hilton et al, 2013). These figures come from different study designs and should not be merged into one success rate [Likely].
**Special cautions:** xerostomia (Sjogren): auto-cure may disintegrate; acidic topical fluoride degrades GIC (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013); irradiated xerostomic patients lose the GIC survival advantage when topical acidic fluoride is used (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 9A. Repair and maintenance
- A failed GIC is normally replaced after removing all the old material, because the union between old and new GIC is not strong; partial removal with a composite laminate is the alternative (Mount et al, 2016). (One chapter says the opposite for sealants (Mount et al, 2016).)
- Minor marginal ditching can be reopened with a fine diamond and repaired with GIC (Mount et al, 2016).
- Beneath indirect restorations, a low-solubility luting cement is chosen because dissolution of the lute opens the margin (Mount et al, 2016).

### 10. Present evidence (critical appraisal)
1. **Fluoride and secondary caries.** RCT meta-analysis (64 trials): GIC has fewer secondary caries than amalgam (permanent teeth RR 0.20, primary RR 0.55) but not fewer than composite (RR 0.77, 0.92) (Ge et al, 2023). So the benefit is real against amalgam; "almost total prevention" is not supported [Likely].
2. **Longevity and ART.** Review of 14 RCTs: ART with high-viscosity GIC did not differ significantly from traditional restorations in primary teeth and single-surface permanent cavities; only 2 of 14 trials had low risk of bias (Frencken et al, 2021). Evidence overall is "conflicting" (Panetta et al, 2024). Multi-surface permanent restorations remain unproven [Likely].
3. **Sandwich technique.** Meta-analysis in cervical lesions: lower loss of retention at 3 years, wide CI, 4 small trials (de Paula et al, 2019). No trial evidence found here for Class I/II [Likely].
4. **Fluoride release is not the same as clinical benefit.** Laboratory release is highest for GIC (Justen et al, 2024) but this has not been shown to be the mechanism of any clinical advantage [Likely].

5. **Conventional GIC against RMGIC.** In the same meta-analysis, secondary caries did not differ significantly between conventional GIC and RMGIC (primary teeth RR 1.12, 0.67-1.87; permanent teeth RR 1.63, 0.34-7.84) (Ge et al, 2023). Neither type is proven better for caries prevention [Likely].

### 11. FUTURE
- **Research aim:** more fracture resistance without losing ion exchange and fluoride release (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Antibacterial and remineralising additives:** chlorhexidine, chitosan, CPP-ACP, silver, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide; promising in vitro, not proven clinically (Mount et al, 2016; Danelon et al, 2025; Guo et al, 2024).
- **Hydroxyapatite and nano-fillers:** laboratory improvement in strength and fluoride release (Zhang et al, 2025).
- **Ion-releasing resins (alkasite, bioactive):** composite-like strength, short-term clinical performance comparable to composite and better than GIC (Laporte et al, 2026; Popa et al, 2026).
- **Glass hybrid and high-viscosity systems:** moving GIC into moderate Class II (Miletic et al, 2024).
- **Other directions mentioned in the DCNA literature:** amino-acid-modified, non-HEMA GICs and ultrasound-assisted hardening (Vaderhobli, 2011); bioactive orthodontic RMGIC needing further study (Zhang et al, 2017); and self-etch and self-adhesive materials that borrowed from the GIC interface (Seghi and Leyva del Rio, 2019).
- **Likely direction:** bioactivity with composite-level strength [Guessing].

### 11A. Role of GIC in modern (biological, minimal-intervention) restorative dentistry
- **Sealing the lesion rather than removing it all.** Infected dentine is removed, affected dentine may be left if the margin is fully sealed, and the lesion is expected to arrest and remineralise (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Transitional restoration.** A high-P/L GIC over affected dentine releases fluoride, strontium and phosphate ions; in a high-caries-risk mouth it may be kept as the permanent restoration and laminated later (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Dentine sealing under other materials.** GIC gives the most effective long-term seal to dentine, so it is used as the base under composite and amalgam (Mount, 1994).
- **High-risk patients.** Fluoride-releasing materials are described as essential for the high-caries-risk patient (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Moisture and technique tolerance.** Useful where isolation is difficult, though durable restorations still need moisture control, with dentine moist but not wet (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Limit of the role.** Minimal-cavity designs apply to new lesions with early dentine involvement; they are a first stage that may later be replaced by a conventional design (Mount, 1994).

### 12. Conclusion
GIC is the only restorative with a reliable chemical bond to tooth and a fluoride reservoir. Its past was a story of fixing handling (copolymers, tartaric acid, sealing); its present is a family of materials for luting, lining, sealing and low-load restoration; its future is stronger, bioactive materials that must not lose the ion exchange that defines the group (Mount et al, 2016) [Likely]. For an examiner, the three points to leave on the page are the chemistry (acid-base, three-stage set, water balance), the clinical rules that follow from it (condition, seal, delay finishing, choose by load) and the honest limit of the evidence (benefit against amalgam, not proven against composite) [Likely].


## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Wilson and Kent, early 1970s (1972 in most books); ISO name "glass polyalkenoate cement"; first product ASPA.
- Powder = fluoro-alumino-silicate glass; liquid = 40-55% polyacrylic/itaconic copolymer + tartaric acid 5-15% (Mount et al, 2016).
- Setting = dissolution, gelation (Ca/Sr, hard set 4-10 min), maturation (Al, 24 h and beyond); about 24% water (Mount et al, 2016).
- Adhesion = ion exchange; condition 10% PAA 10 s; never acid-etch; failure is cohesive (Mount et al, 2016).
- Fluoride = early burst, low steady level, rechargeable reservoir; benefit shown against amalgam, not against composite (Ge et al, 2023).
- Types I (luting, 1.5:1), II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced (3-4:1), III lining/base; only P/L separates lining from base (Mount, 1994).
- Seal immediately, finish at 24 h or later (Mount, 1994).
- RMGIC has 15-25% resin; compomer, giomer and alkasite are not glass ionomers (Mount et al, 2016).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling compomers, giomers or alkasite materials "glass ionomers"; they are resin-based.
2. Writing "acid-etch the tooth before GIC"; the conditioner is 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds.
3. Leaving the restoration unsealed or finishing it at once, then wondering why it cracked.
4. Writing "fluoride release prevents recurrent caries" without qualification; the RCT evidence shows benefit against amalgam only.
5. Quoting one date for the invention, or a precise "bond strength" figure, without noting that sources disagree on both.
