## STUDY MODE

### S1. The design problem GIC was invented to solve [basic science]
Every direct restorative has to answer three questions: does it stay on the tooth, does it seal the margin against bacteria, and does it survive load? Before GIC the two acid-base cements each answered half of that. Silicate cement released fluoride, and recurrent caries around it was unusually rare, but it dissolved in saliva and had poor strength (Hilton et al, 2013). Zinc polycarboxylate (developed in the 1960s) was the first dental cement for which an inherent adhesion to tooth could be demonstrated (McCabe and Walls, 2008), but its low tensile strength meant the adhesion did not last (Mount, 1994) and it was too soluble and weak to restore teeth (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Replacing the zinc oxide of polycarboxylate with the ion-leachable glass of silicate cement gave a stronger, less soluble, more translucent material (McCabe and Walls, 2008). That is why GIC is described as combining the adhesion of polycarboxylate with the fluoride release of silicate (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). The whole later history of GIC is a series of attempts to keep those two strengths while fixing its two weaknesses, brittleness and water sensitivity.

### S2. PAST: how the material developed

**The dates are not agreed.** Wilson and Kent, 1972, is reported in most textbooks (Mount et al, 2016; Bhat et al, 2019; van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Powers and Wataha, 2013; Sikri, 2020, Ch.20); Sikri's Chapter 1 gives 1971 (Sikri, 2020, Ch.1); a DCNA review says 1969 (Hill, 2007); Craig's 14th edition says "the 1960s" on one page but "the 1970s" on another (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Taken together, the books place discovery and first publication between 1969 and 1972. [Likely] First clinical use is attributed to McLean and Wilson in 1975 (Bhat et al, 2019); the first commercial product was ASPA (aluminosilicate polyacrylate) cement, made available in 1976 (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Mount et al, 2016), with a citric acid conditioner (Bhat et al, 2019).

**Why the early cements failed, and what fixed them** (this chain is a good model for a "past" answer):
- The original liquid, a 50% polyacrylic acid solution, gelled within months, probably by hydrogen bonding between chains; copolymers (itaconic, maleic acid) prevent this by steric hindrance (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount, 1994).
- Early formulations used high-fluoride, opaque glasses to get a fast set; tartaric acid let manufacturers use lower-fluoride, more translucent glasses and sharpened the set (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
- Water-hardening ("anhydrous") cements, with freeze-dried polyacid blended in the powder, first appeared commercially in 1981 (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- Early ASPA restorations often failed because they were left unsealed, so they took up water or dried out and cracked: in Mount's two illustrated cases both were faulty from failure to seal, and the properly sealed replacements had been in place for eight years when photographed (Mount et al, 2016); van Noort likewise attributes high failure rates in older studies partly to techniques inappropriate for the early materials (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Handling, not chemistry, was the lesson.
- Wilson and McLean's 1988 classification (Type I luting, II restorative, III lining/base) is the one still used in Mount's texts (Mount, 1994).
- Resin-modified GIC (RMGIC) arose in the 1980s, when water-soluble polymer or polymerisable resin was added to the liquid (Hill, 2007); dates quoted range from the late 1980s (Sakaguchi et al, 2019*) to the early 1990s (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- High-viscosity GICs (Fuji VIII, IX) arrived in the 1990s for minimal-preparation restorations (Bhat et al, 2019); compomers were introduced as Dyract in 1993 (Sikri, 2020, Ch.1) or from about 1995 (Powers and Wataha, 2013) and are not glass ionomers (Mount et al, 2016); nano-ionomers (RMGIC with nanomers and nanoclusters) have been available since 2007 (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### S3. PRESENT: chemistry, mechanism and why it behaves as it does

**Composition.** A powder of ion-leachable calcium (or strontium) fluoro-alumino-silicate glass and a liquid that is a 40-55% solution of a 2:1 acrylic/itaconic acid copolymer (or a maleic/acrylic copolymer), with tartaric acid 5-15% (Mount et al, 2016). A typical glass (weight %): SiO2 29.0, Al2O3 16.6, CaF2 34.2, Na3AlF6 5.0, AlF3 5.3, AlPO4 9.9 (Mount et al, 2016). The glass is fused (1000-1500 deg C, Bhat et al, 2019; 1100-1500 deg C, Anusavice et al, 2013), quenched in water so it cracks, then ground (Bhat et al, 2019); particle size runs from 4 to 50 micrometres, finer for luting and lining and coarser for restoratives (Mount et al, 2016). Fluoride is in the glass as a flux and oxide scavenger, not as part of the matrix (Mount et al, 2016). The glass must be reactive enough: the alumina:silica ratio has to exceed 1:2 by mass for the acid to attack it (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

**Setting reaction (three overlapping stages).**
1. Dissolution: the polyacid attacks the glass surface; roughly 20-30% of the glass decomposes, releasing calcium/strontium, aluminium and fluoride ions (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Precipitation and gelation: calcium/strontium ions cross-link the polyacid chains first, giving a clinically hard surface within about 4-10 minutes, then aluminium ions cross-link later and form the strong, insoluble matrix (Mount et al, 2016).
3. Maturation: progressive hydration of the matrix salts sharply improves properties over the next day and beyond (Mount et al, 2016).
The set cement is unreacted glass cores in a siliceous gel inside a cross-linked polysalt matrix (Mount et al, 2016). About 24% of it is water in Mount's account, some loosely bound (lost easily early on) and some tightly bound (part of the hydration shell) (Mount et al, 2016); a DCNA review gives somewhere between 11% and 24% (Ngo, 2010).

**Why water balance matters.** Calcium cross-links form first, are fragile and wash out if the cement takes up water early; aluminium cross-links form later (Mount et al, 2016). Early water uptake dissolves the matrix, early dehydration cracks it. The cement is most vulnerable during Stage 2 and stays vulnerable to water loss for some time afterwards (Mount et al, 2016). Modern auto-cure cements, made with calcium removed from the glass surface, tolerate early water better, but waterproof sealing immediately after placement is still recommended (Mount et al, 2016). The windows differ by type in the Atlas. Fast-set Type I, II.2 and III cements resist water uptake from about 5 minutes after the start of the mix but stay liable to water loss, cracking and crazing for about 2 weeks, and the Atlas says no protective seal is needed while they remain in a wet environment (Mount, 1994). Auto-cure Type II.1 snap-sets at about 4 minutes but is then extremely susceptible to both water uptake and loss, so it needs a waterproof resin seal at once and should not be challenged by dehydration for up to 6 months (Mount, 1994). In dual-cure Type II.1, light seals the cement to the depth of penetration and the unlit part matures like an auto-cure cement for at least a week (Mount, 1994). The two Mount books therefore differ slightly on whether every fast-set cement must be sealed. This one principle explains almost every handling rule below.

**Adhesion.** The polyacid's carboxyl groups displace phosphate ions from the apatite, each phosphate taking a calcium ion with it, and an ion-enriched interface layer forms between cement and tooth (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). Brief conditioning with 10% polyacrylic acid (10-15 s) removes the smear layer, wets the surface and pre-activates calcium and phosphate ions (Mount, 1994); left longer than about 20 s it begins to demineralise (Mount, 1994). Other books give other conditioners: 10% for 10 s (Mount et al, 2016) to 20% for 10-20 s (Sikri, 2020, Ch.20), 10-25% for 15-30 s (Torres, 2020), with van Noort calling a 20% solution the best conditioner (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). The tooth must never be acid-etched before GIC (Mount et al, 2016). **Books differ on how to quote bond strength:** Mount says the bond strength has not been measured, because the failure is cohesive in the cement, so bond tests actually measure the cement's tensile strength (Mount et al, 2016). Craig's 11th edition gives a dentine bond of 2-3 MPa (Craig, 2002), McCabe quotes around 1-3 MPa (McCabe and Walls, 2008) and van Noort 2-7 MPa, but van Noort too says the fracture runs through the cement (cohesive failure) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). So Mount et al (2016), McCabe and Walls (2008) and van Noort and Barbour (2013) all describe cohesive failure; they differ on whether the number is called a bond strength. [Likely]
 Nakabayashi and Pashley give the pKa of phosphoric acid as 2.12 and of polyacrylic acid as 4.25, so phosphoric acid has the higher potential to demineralise dentine, and acidic cements such as GIC leave a slightly demineralised dentine subsurface (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).

**Fluoride.** Release is high in the first days, falls rapidly over the first week and settles by 2-3 months at a low steady level that continues for years (Mount et al, 2016); other books put the plateau earlier, at about 3 weeks (Forsten, quoted by Hilton et al, 2013), or describe the high release as falling away 8-10 weeks after placement (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); Garg and Garg give a peak in the first 24 hours and a constant level at 3-4 months (Garg and Garg, 2011), and Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed (adapted from the 7th ed) gives a burst completed within 24 hours, then a low sustained release thought well below the level needed to prevent secondary caries unless the cement is re-exposed to fluoride about three times a day (Ritter et al, 2019). The cement takes fluoride back up from toothpaste or topical agents and so acts as a reservoir (Mount et al, 2016). Summitt's key point is that the ability to be recharged matters more than the size of the initial release (Hilton et al, 2013), and that GICs and RMGICs recharge best (Hilton et al, 2013). Release is higher at lower pH (Hilton et al, 2013). Acidic topical fluorides (APF) can roughen and degrade the cement (Hilton et al, 2013).

**Properties, in numbers.** Seven-day strengths (MPa) in Mount & Hume's Table 9.2, with large variation between products: Type II.1 compressive 70-220, diametral tensile 12-20; Type II.2 compressive 140-220; Type I luting compressive 70-150 (Mount et al, 2016). Other books give other ranges, so say that values depend on product and test: Sikri quotes 16,000-22,000 psi (about 110-152 MPa) and hardness 48 KHN for traditional GIC (Sikri, 2020), whereas Phillips Table 14-8 gives 196-251 MPa compressive, 18-26 MPa diametral tensile and 87-177 KHN for Type II restorative GIC (Anusavice et al, 2013). ISO 9917 limits: compressive strength at least 130 MPa for restoratives and 70 MPa for luting and lining; film thickness at most 25 micrometres for luting; setting time 2-6 min for restoratives; acid erosion at most 0.05 mm/h (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Flexural strength is quoted as only 15-20 MPa against more than 70 MPa for composites (McCabe and Walls, 2008), but Mount's Table 9.2 gives 8-40 MPa for conventional cements and 60-80 MPa for resin-modified and the new reinforced versions (Mount et al, 2016). Fracture toughness is about 0.7 MPa.m1/2 for restorative GIC and about 1.4 for resin-modified GIC, against 1.75-1.92 for hybrid composite (Anusavice et al, 2013). Thermal expansion is close to tooth (10-11 against 11.4) (Mount et al, 2016), and a good match is thought to be one of the parameters that help the seal (McCabe and Walls, 2008). In one reported comparison of a high-viscosity GIC, one-year wear was 73 micrometres against under 10 micrometres per year for composites (Hilton et al, 2013); Mount disagrees that wear is a general problem, because a well-placed cement stood heavy abrasion better than the remaining tooth structure in his long-term cases (Mount, 1994). In practice GIC is not for marginal ridges, incisal corners or sole support of a crown (Mount, 1994).

**Biocompatibility.** Fresh cement is very acidic (pH about 0.9-1.6) but dentine buffers it, and the pulp response is mild and resolves in 10-20 days as the pH rises within the first hour (Mount et al, 2016); other books say within about 30 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008) or a few days (Mount, 1994). Poly(alkenoic) acid chains are large and do not readily enter the tubules (Mount, 1994). Pulpal inflammation is driven by bacteria rather than by the cement, so a complete seal is what protects the pulp (Mount, 1994). Direct contact with an exposed pulp is different: without intervening dentine GIC is quite toxic to exposed pulp, so calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate material goes first (Mount et al, 2016). Sources differ here: another chapter of the same book says GIC can cover a mechanical or traumatic exposure of a healthy pulp, with calcium hydroxide if there is any doubt about infection (Mount et al, 2016); the Atlas reports healing when a healthy exposure is sealed (Mount, 1994); and Phillips advises a calcium hydroxide liner when the preparation is closer than 0.5 mm to the pulp chamber (Anusavice et al, 2013). RMGIC tends to be more cytotoxic than conventional GIC (Hilton et al, 2013), consistent with HEMA release in laboratory studies (Agha et al, 2021) [Likely]. Post-cementation sensitivity with GIC luting is no worse than with zinc phosphate (Mount et al, 2016).

### S4. PRESENT: classification and which type for which job [clinical decision-making]

| Type (Wilson & McLean 1988, Mount) | Use | Powder:liquid | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| I luting | crowns, bridges, inlays, orthodontic bands | about 1.5:1 | film thickness 25 micrometres or less, fast set (Mount, 1994) |
| II.1 restorative, aesthetic | any aesthetic restoration without undue load | 2.8:1 to 6.8:1 | auto-cure or dual-cure (Mount, 1994) |
| II.2 restorative, reinforced | non-aesthetic use needing fast set and strength | 3:1 to 4:1 | metal-modified or high-strength (Mount, 1994) |
| III lining/base | lining 1.5:1; base or dentine substitute 3:1 or more | | only difference between lining and base is the P/L ratio (Mount, 1994) |

The table follows the Atlas; the later Mount text gives somewhat different figures, namely Type I film thickness of 20 micrometres or less and a Type II.1 powder:liquid ratio of 3:1 or greater (Mount et al, 2016), and 3.5:1 or greater for a restorative or base cement (Mount et al, 2016).

Phillips uses the same idea with different labels (Type I luting; IIa aesthetic restorative; IIb reinforced restorative; III lining/base) (Anusavice et al, 2013), and Bhat adds fissure sealant, orthodontic cement and core build-up types (Bhat et al, 2019).

**Decision logic** used in Mount's system: GIC is the material of choice where the lesion is not under heavy occlusal load (Mount et al, 2016); for cervical (Site 3) lesions a Type II.1 cement, auto-cure or resin-modified depending on light access (Mount et al, 2016); for proximal tunnels, a radiopaque high-strength auto-cure cement (Mount et al, 2016). Where load is high, use a composite or amalgam laminate over a GIC base (Mount et al, 2016).

### S5. PRESENT: modified materials and what is, and is not, a glass ionomer
- **Metal-reinforced:** silver cermet (about 40% by weight micro-fine silver sintered to glass, with up to 5% titanium dioxide to restore colour) and amalgam-alloy admixes. They improve abrasion resistance but not other properties and may reduce the ion-exchange adhesion (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). They are no longer popular (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Resin-modified GIC:** liquid with 15-25% resin (HEMA and other methacrylates) (Mount et al, 2016); sets partly by acid-base reaction and partly by light and chemical polymerisation, and still sets in the dark (Mount et al, 2016). Gains: immediate set on light, higher strength. In one manufacturer comparison, setting time fell from 4 min to 20 s and diametral tensile strength rose from 16 to 37 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Costs: residual HEMA of 4.5% to possibly 15% (Mount et al, 2016), water uptake and swelling (Mount et al, 2016), and (some sources) polymerisation shrinkage of 3-4% (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Mount's counter-point: acid-base shrinkage is small and the water uptake offsets it (Mount et al, 2016).
- **High-viscosity GIC:** finer glass (about 3 micrometres) and polyacid added to the powder give amalgam-like handling; strength changes little but abrasion resistance seems better (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Used for ART (Bhat et al, 2019); not for load-bearing areas of permanent teeth (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Nano-ionomer:** nanoparticles (nanomers and nanoclusters) added to an RMGIC are said to improve polishability and optical characteristics (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); data are sparse and the claimed gains in wear resistance and fluoride release are laboratory claims (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Not glass ionomers:** compomers (will not set without light; no acid-base dark set) (Mount et al, 2016); giomers (pre-reacted glass-ionomer particles in a resin matrix, so no ion exchange) (Mount et al, 2016). A simple clinical test: a true GIC under a light-proof cover shows clear signs of set after 5-7 minutes in the Atlas (Mount, 1994), is reasonably hard to touch within 7 minutes in Mount's later text (Mount et al, 2016) and reaches a reasonable set within 10 minutes in the Atlas chapter on linings (Mount, 1994), while imitation materials stay soft or rubbery; an RMGIC kept from light eventually sets in some 15-20 minutes (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). ### S6. PRESENT: technique, because it decides outcome [treatment]
1. Isolate, clean the surface (pumice and water), condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 s (10-15 s in the Atlas; other books differ, see Adhesion above), wash, dry lightly without dehydrating (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
2. Use a capsule if possible: standardised P/L ratio and mixing, although encapsulation can trap porosities, which automixing of resin-modified cement avoids; hand mixing is possible but powder content varies (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994). Mix for about 10 seconds; placement must be in the working window, judged by loss of gloss (Mount, 1994). Working time is about 1.5 minutes after a 30 s mix (Bhat et al, 2019) to 2-3 minutes (Sikri, 2020), depending on product and book.
3. Place under a matrix with pressure (Mount et al, 2016).
4. **Seal at once** (essential for auto-cure Type II.1) with a single-component, very low-viscosity light-cured unfilled bonding resin, flowed on as the matrix is removed and trimmed through; pre-mixed solvent-containing or chemically cured bonds and manufacturers' varnishes stay porous because the vehicle evaporates, and a thick layer leaves an overhang to be cut away with a sharp blade (Mount, 1994). Books differ on varnish: McCabe says it is necessary to varnish the surface and van Noort calls the varnish extremely important, though both accept a resin bonding agent as the longer-lasting protection (McCabe and Walls, 2008; van Noort and Barbour, 2013); if a varnish is used the Atlas wants two coats, each dried for about 30 s (Mount, 1994).
5. Finishing depends on the type. Auto-cure Type II.1 is not finished before 24 hours, ideally a week for best translucency (Mount, 1994; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); fast-set Type II.2 can be finished from about 6 minutes (Mount, 1994), although the Atlas's own case captions use 4 minutes (Mount, 1994); dual-cure and resin-modified cements can be contoured at once under air/water spray, working from restoration towards tooth, with the final polish delayed as long as possible (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). Summitt advises a 2-5 minute delay before polishing a resin-modified Class V (Hilton et al, 2013), and Sikri prefers initial contouring after 24 hours for conventional cement (Sikri, 2020). Always use air/water spray, and keep reshaping to a minimum because the surface formed under the matrix is the smoothest (Mount, 1994).
**Cavity design.** Because the bond is chemical, the preparation can be minimal: no mechanical undercuts (Mount et al, 2016). For Class V, a butt joint (cavosurface margin near 90 degrees) with about 1 mm minimum depth, no bevel, because GIC is brittle and needs bulk (Hilton et al, 2013). Minor undercuts left by caries removal can be filled with GIC rather than cutting more tooth (Mount et al, 2016).
**Sandwich (bi-layered) technique.** GIC replaces dentine, composite replaces enamel. Mount's logic: composite shrinks and its dentine bond is uncertain, whereas GIC bonds dentine reliably and, being water-based, does not hydrolyse (Mount, 1994). Closed sandwich covers the GIC with composite; open sandwich leaves GIC exposed at the gingival margin to release fluoride (Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013). Cement must be at least 1 mm thick at the highest P/L available (Mount, 1994). Timing in the Atlas: an auto-cure Type II.1 base is left undisturbed for 20 minutes before the composite stage, while Type II.2 or III bases allow it at 6 minutes from the start of the mix (Mount, 1994). Etching the set cement: Mount etches the auto-cure cement for 15 s (Mount, 1994), Torres 5 s with phosphoric acid after the initial set (Torres, 2020), Bonsor and Pearson say etching freshly set GIC removes the matrix and works only after about 24 hours (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013), and McCabe says etch only if the restoration has been in place for some time (McCabe and Walls, 2008). The open sandwich uses a resin-modified cement, since conventional glass ionomers are less successful in it (Hilton et al, 2013). Phillips recommends the sandwich technique for Class II and V composites in moderate to high caries-risk patients (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### S7. PRESENT: outcome and prognosis
Failures are mostly handling failures: ditching from a low-powder mix or early water contact, cracks from dehydration, bulk fracture from overload (Mount et al, 2016). Reported figures vary with the study: in Summitt's summary, Class I and Class II GIC restorations showed 8% and 40% failure at 24 months (Hilton et al, 2013); a Class V study showed 80% retention at 10 years with no mechanical retention (Hilton et al, 2013); GIC survives better than amalgam in irradiated xerostomic patients without topical fluoride, but not with it (Hilton et al, 2013). For ART with high-viscosity GIC, Craig's 14th edition quotes two-year survival of 93% (single-surface) and 62% (multiple-surface) in primary teeth and five-year survival of 80% for single-surface permanent restorations (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); Phillips quotes 99% at 1 year and 88% at 3 years for single-surface permanent restorations, and 66.1% for ART against 57.0% for amalgam at 6.3 years in children (Anusavice et al, 2013). Randomised-trial evidence is set out in the synthesis below.

### S8. FUTURE: where the field is going
- **Aim of research:** higher fracture resistance without losing the ion-exchange adhesion and fluoride release, which are the whole point of the material (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Antibacterial additives** (chlorhexidine, chitosan) and remineralising agents (CPP-ACP): results are equivocal (Mount et al, 2016); metallic nano-additives (silver, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) improve antibacterial action in laboratory work, but clinical benefit is unproven (Danelon et al, 2025; Guo et al, 2024); hydroxyapatite-modified GIC is promising in the laboratory (Zhang et al, 2025). Silver diamine fluoride is a separate caries-arresting option rather than a GIC additive: Craig's 14th edition describes a solution of about 30% SDF (about 25% silver, 5% fluoride, pH about 10) that stains tooth black and so needs an aesthetic cover (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); see the dedicated answer P10 for remineralisation and SDF.
- **Ion-releasing resin restoratives (alkasite, bioactive resins)** are the new competitors, not true GICs (Laporte et al, 2026; Popa et al, 2026).
- **Glass hybrid (EQUIA-type)** systems pair high-viscosity GIC with a resin coat, with 5-year survival comparable to composite in Class II cavities (Miletic et al, 2024).
- **Other material changes:** a calcium-aluminate glass ionomer hybrid for luting sets by the GIC acid-base reaction plus hydration of calcium aluminate, with a basic pH during curing and a reported in-vitro setting expansion of 0.4% (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Predicted but unverified:** the intended direction is bioactivity with composite-level strength. [Guessing]


## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
Four claims where textbook statements and published evidence do not line up. Study types are named because laboratory data cannot be read as clinical proof.

### Claim 1. Does the fluoride a GIC releases prevent recurrent (secondary) caries?
- **Textbook position.** Mount's Atlas says chemical union plus fluoride gives "almost total prevention of recurrent caries" (Mount, 1994). More cautious texts call it "a matter of some debate" (McCabe and Walls, 2008) or say there is "some disagreement as to the long-term effect of the fluoride release" (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Summitt describes the evidence as "modest but growing" and notes that in two retrospective reports GIC developed recurrent caries within 5 years and almost half of GIC restorations were replaced (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Retrieved evidence.** A meta-analysis of 64 RCTs (8310 GIC against 5857 amalgam or composite restorations, 1-10 years) found secondary caries was lower with GIC than amalgam (primary teeth RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.41-0.72; permanent teeth RR 0.20, 0.11-0.38) but not lower than composite (primary RR 0.92, 0.77-1.10; permanent RR 0.77, 0.39-1.51) (Ge et al, 2023). A second meta-analysis of 40 RCTs pooled bioactive materials against composite and amalgam and found RR 0.55 (0.46-0.65) with moderate certainty, GICs alone RR 0.36 and giomers no benefit (RR 1.09) (Zailai et al, 2026). The ADA evidence review summarised by Summitt reported a 65% lower chance of recurrent caries around single-surface GIC restorations than amalgam in permanent teeth, and no difference in primary teeth (Hilton et al, 2013). In the laboratory, GICs still released the most fluoride of all direct materials (Justen et al, 2024), and GI sealants showed a burst in the first 24-48 hours then slower release and better recharge than resin sealants (Dobrzynski et al, 2025).
- **Interpretation.** Against amalgam in permanent teeth, Ge et al (2023) and the ADA review summarised by Summitt (Hilton et al, 2013) both report lower recurrent caries with GIC; for primary teeth Ge et al report lower risk (RR 0.55) while the ADA review reports no difference. The Atlas's "almost total prevention" (Mount, 1994) is not matched by the RCT meta-analysis, which found no advantage over composite (Ge et al, 2023). The bioactive-material benefit depends on the comparator: the pooled control group mixes amalgam and composite, so the amalgam contrast can drive it (this is a reviewer's inference, not stated in the abstract). [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Abstract-level reading only. The fluoride data are in vitro; no study isolated fluoride release as the mechanism. Certainty for Ge 2023 is not stated in the abstract.
- **Conclusion and implication.** What can be concluded: GIC restorations have fewer secondary caries than amalgam; fluoride release is highest in GICs in the laboratory. What cannot: that fluoride release itself is the reason, or that GIC beats composite. Clinical use: pick GIC for high-caries-risk or moisture-compromised situations, and do not promise caries-free margins.

### Claim 2. How long do GIC and high-viscosity (ART) restorations last?
- **Textbook position.** Mount, Summitt and van Noort all restrict GIC to areas without heavy occlusal load; high-viscosity GICs are "still inferior to resin composites" in wear and not for load-bearing permanent areas (Hilton et al, 2013); van Noort does not recommend GIC for adult Class II (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). A DCNA review notes high-viscosity GI Class II restorations were satisfactory at 3 years despite many failures, and cites a retrospective failure rate of 60% at 72 months (Vaderhobli, 2011).
- **Retrieved evidence.** A systematic review of 14 RCTs (only 2 at low risk of bias) found no significant difference in survival between ART/high-viscosity GIC and traditional restorations for single- and multiple-surface cavities in primary molars and single-surface cavities in permanent posterior teeth at 1, 2, 3 and 5 years; at 4.3 and 6.3 years the difference favoured ART/HVGIC; no significant difference for multiple-surface permanent restorations (Frencken et al, 2021). A second review (67 articles, non-randomised designs included) found the conventional technique better than ART for single-surface occlusal and multi-surface GIC restorations, with higher annual failure rates for multi-surface restorations (Ruengrungsom et al, 2018). An umbrella review of 13 reviews graded the evidence "conflicting" and the medium- and long-term performance "still questionable" (Panetta et al, 2024). In a 5-year randomised trial of two-surface Class II molar restorations, glass hybrid and nano-hybrid composite had the same survival (94.5% against 94.4%) although the success rate was numerically lower for glass hybrid (81.9% against 90.7%) (Miletic et al, 2024); in molar-incisor hypomineralisation molars, 5-year survival was 59.2% for glass hybrid against 75.5% for fibre-reinforced composite (Dokumacigil et al, 2026).
- **Interpretation.** The textbooks' caution is partly refined, not overturned: modern high-viscosity and glass-hybrid materials do acceptably in single-surface cavities and primary teeth, and in one good trial in moderate Class II cavities. The reviews disagree on ART versus conventional technique, and the textbooks' restriction of GIC to low-load areas (Hilton et al, 2013; van Noort and Barbour, 2013) was written before the newer trial evidence cited above. [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Most trials are small or at high risk of bias; review abstracts do not report certainty ratings; the MIH result is population-specific.
- **Conclusion and implication.** Can conclude: no proven survival penalty for ART/HVGIC in single-surface and primary-tooth cavities up to 5 years. Cannot conclude equivalence for multi-surface permanent-tooth restorations. Clinical use: reserve GIC for single-surface, low-load or high-risk situations; use composite where load is high.

### Claim 3. Does the sandwich (bi-layered) technique improve restoration success?
- **Textbook position.** Mount strongly recommends a GIC base under composite where a margin lies on dentine, citing composite shrinkage and an uncertain dentine bond (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016), while admitting early sandwich studies showed more bulk fractures (Mount et al, 2016). A DCNA article states routine GI liners under composite are not evidence-supported (Hayashi, 2020). Summitt reports an open sandwich with RMGI successful at 5 years in moderate-to-high caries-risk patients, but a 9-year compomer trial found no difference in secondary caries (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Retrieved evidence.** A meta-analysis of RCTs in non-carious cervical lesions found only retention differed: it was higher with the sandwich technique at 3 years (RR 7.5, 95% CI 2.1-27.2 as reported), but the review analysed only 4 studies (6 reports, three being follow-ups of one RCT), all at unclear risk of bias (de Paula et al, 2019). An umbrella review also states that the sandwich technique with RMGIC bases improved retention in non-carious cervical lesions (Fernandez et al, 2025). Laboratory reviews show composite-to-GIC bonding depends on surface treatment (Ismail et al, 2025).
- **Interpretation.** Mount's rationale is supported only for cervical lesions and only for retention, on thin evidence. For Class I and II, and for open versus closed sandwich, no trial evidence was found in this retrieval. [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Wide confidence interval; the exact figure should not be quoted as a precise effect.
- **Conclusion and implication.** Use the sandwich technique where dentine margins or caries risk make composite bonding unreliable, and say that the evidence is limited.

### Claim 4. Are the new ion-releasing and bioactive materials better than GIC?
- **Textbook position.** Mount judges antibacterial and remineralising additives "equivocal" in value (Mount et al, 2016) and nano-ionomer claims are laboratory-only (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Retrieved evidence.** In a meta-analysis of clinical studies, alkasite materials performed comparably to resin composite and "superior" to GIC, with no pooled numbers in the abstract and moderate risk of bias in several studies (Laporte et al, 2026). The laboratory network meta-analysis found alkasite properties similar to composite and stronger than GIC, while GICs still released the most fluoride (Justen et al, 2024). One small non-randomised pilot (36 restorations, 6 months) reported dislodgement in 1 of 28 alkasite against 6 of 8 Fuji IX restorations (Padilla-Ocampo et al, 2025). ACTIVA BioACTIVE in primary molars performed comparably to composite and compomer at 12-24 months (Popa et al, 2026).
- **Interpretation.** Resin-based ion-releasing materials win on strength; GIC wins on fluoride release. Bioactivity has not been shown to give a clinical caries-preventive advantage. [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Short follow-up, small trials; the pilot is hypothesis-generating only.
- **Conclusion and implication.** Alkasite and bioactive resins are credible alternatives where strength matters; none replaces GIC's chemical adhesion to dentine.

### 1. Introduction
- **Definition.** A water-based cement formed by acid-base reaction between a polyalkenoic acid and an ion-leachable, fluoride-containing calcium or strontium aluminosilicate glass; official ISO name "glass polyalkenoate cement" (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Credit.** Wilson and Kent, 1972 in most textbooks (Mount et al, 2016; Bhat et al, 2019), 1971 in Sikri Ch.1 (Sikri, 2020), 1969 in Hill (2007); first commercial product ASPA, 1976 (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Mount et al, 2016) [Likely].
- **Parentage.** Glass from silicate cement (fluoride, translucency) plus polyacid from zinc polycarboxylate (adhesion) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Key claims to fame.** Chemical bond to enamel and dentine, fluoride release and uptake, biocompatibility; weakness is brittleness and water sensitivity (Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. PAST: historical evolution
| Period | Event |
|---|---|
| Before 1970 | Silicate cement: fluoride release, but soluble (Hilton et al, 2013). Polycarboxylate (1960s): first cement with demonstrable inherent adhesion, but weak (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount, 1994). |
| 1969-1972 | Wilson and Kent: ASPA cement (aluminosilicate polyacrylate) (Bhat et al, 2019); 1972 in most books, 1971 in Sikri Ch.1, 1969 in Hill |
| 1975-1976 | First clinical use reported 1975 by McLean and Wilson (Bhat et al, 2019); ASPA first commercial product, 1976 (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| 1970s | Liquid gelled; copolymers (itaconic, maleic) and tartaric acid solved storage and set (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount, 1994) |
| 1981 | Water-hardening ("anhydrous") cements marketed (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| 1988 | Wilson and McLean classification: Types I, II, III (Mount, 1994) |
| 1980s-early 1990s | Resin-modified GIC (Hill, 2007; Powers and Wataha, 2013) |
| 1990s | High-viscosity GIC (Fuji VIII, IX) for ART (Bhat et al, 2019); compomers (1993 in Sikri, 2020; about 1995 in Powers and Wataha, 2013), not true GIC (Mount et al, 2016) |
| 2007 | Nano-ionomer (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) |
| Today | Glass hybrid systems; ion-releasing resins (alkasite, bioactive) (Laporte et al, 2026) |
- **Lesson of the early years:** Mount's illustrated early failures were unsealed restorations that took up water or dried out and cracked (Mount et al, 2016); van Noort adds inappropriate technique (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **A useful observation (Mount and Hume):** while dentine bonding agents went through eight "generations", glass ionomer chemical adhesion stayed at one generation and was reliable throughout (Mount et al, 2016). GIC derivatives have also been used as bone replacement in surgery (Mount, 1994).

### 3. PRESENT: composition and manufacture
**Powder:** ion-leachable fluoro-alumino-silicate glass; typical weight %: SiO2 29.0, Al2O3 16.6, CaF2 34.2, Na3AlF6 5.0, AlF3 5.3, AlPO4 9.9 (Mount et al, 2016).
- Silica gives translucency and hardness; alumina forms the polyacrylate matrix; fluorides act as flux and are released (Bhat et al, 2019).
- Strontium or lanthanum add radiopacity (Mount et al, 2016).
**Liquid:** 40-55% aqueous 2:1 acrylic/itaconic acid copolymer (or maleic/acrylic) (Mount et al, 2016); tartaric acid 5-15% retains the working time and sharpens the set (Mount et al, 2016).
**Manufacture:** ingredients fused at 1000-1500 deg C, plunged into water, ground (Bhat et al, 2019); particle size 4-50 micrometres: finer for luting and lining, coarser for restoratives (Mount et al, 2016); glass reactivity is controlled by fusion temperature and heat history (Mount et al, 2016).
**Why each component is there.** Tartaric acid does two things: it first binds released calcium (extending working time) and then speeds aluminium cross-linking, so the set is sharper (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). The alumina:silica ratio of the glass is higher than in silicate cement because polyacrylic acid is a much weaker acid than phosphoric acid; the cost is a shorter working time (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Polyacid molecular weight and concentration raise strength but also viscosity, which is why dehydrated polyacid in the powder became popular (Mount, 1994).
**Presentations:** powder and liquid; water-hardening (polyacid dried into powder, water or dilute tartaric acid as liquid); capsules; paste-paste (Mount et al, 2016).

### 4. Setting reaction
1. **Dissolution:** acid attacks the glass; about 20-30% decomposes, releasing Ca/Sr, Al and F ions (Mount et al, 2016).
2. **Gelation:** Ca/Sr ions cross-link polyacid chains (early set, fragile, water-soluble); initial hard set at about 4-10 min (Mount et al, 2016).
3. **Hardening/maturation:** Al ions cross-link slowly (strong, insoluble); hydration of the matrix continues for 24 hours and longer (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Set structure:** unreacted glass cores, siliceous gel, polysalt matrix; about 24% water, loosely and tightly bound (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Water balance:** early water uptake dissolves the matrix; early drying cracks it (Mount et al, 2016). Types I, II.2, III resist uptake from about 5 min but can dehydrate for about 2 weeks; auto-cure II.1 needs a resin seal at once and stays water-sensitive for up to 6 months (Mount, 1994).
- **Resin-modified GIC:** acid-base plus light/chemical polymerisation; the material hardens on light but full set takes days (Mount et al, 2016).
**Factors that alter the rate of set:** glass composition and fusion temperature, particle size, tartaric acid content, powder:liquid ratio and mixing temperature; a chilled slab and powder lengthen the working time by up to 25%, but the liquid must never be refrigerated because it can gel (Mount et al, 2016). High ambient temperature speeds the reaction (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Properties
**(a) Adhesion.** Ionic exchange with apatite: carboxyl groups displace phosphate; an ion-enriched layer forms (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016). Condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 s (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016), or 20% for 10-20 s (Sikri, 2020), or 10-25% for 15-30 s (Torres, 2020); do not acid-etch (Mount et al, 2016). Failure is cohesive, within the cement, so bond tests mainly measure the cement's own tensile strength (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Why polyacrylic acid is the conditioner:** it is the cement's own acid, so residue does not interfere with setting; it lowers the surface energy of tooth and improves wetting; it is mild and does not unduly open tubules, because plugs remain after 10 seconds (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

**(b) Fluoride.** High release in the first days, falling over the first week to a low steady level by 2-3 months and continuing for years (Mount et al, 2016); plateau at about 3 weeks (Forsten, in Hilton et al, 2013); falling away at 8-10 weeks (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); constant level at 3-4 months (Garg and Garg, 2011); burst complete in 24 h (Ritter et al, 2019); recharged from toothpaste and topical fluoride, so it is a reservoir (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013). Clinical value is debated (see section 10). For remineralisation to start, salivary fluoride should reach at least 10 ppm, a level GICs can provide (Mount et al, 2016); Summitt recommends long-term release of at least 2-3 micrograms per mL per day, achievable only with supplemental fluoride recharge (Hilton et al, 2013). Fluoride also suppresses *Streptococcus mutans* growth on the cement surface (Mount, 1994).
**(c) Biological.** Fresh pH 0.9-1.6, buffered by dentine; mild pulp response resolving in 10-20 days (Mount et al, 2016) or about 30 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008). On an exposed pulp, Mount's pulp chapter says cover with calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate material first, while his GIC chapter allows GIC over a healthy mechanical exposure, with calcium hydroxide if infection is doubted (Mount et al, 2016). Resists plaque; gingival tolerance high (Mount, 1994). The aim of a lining is to seal the dentine against bacteria, not to deliver a therapeutic effect through sound dentine (Mount, 1994).
**(d) Mechanical.** Compressive strength 70-220 MPa by type (Mount et al, 2016); 16,000-22,000 psi, about 110-152 MPa, hardness 48 KHN (Sikri, 2020); 196-251 MPa, 87-177 KHN for Type II (Anusavice et al, 2013); depends on product and test. Flexural 15-20 MPa, composite over 70 (McCabe and Walls, 2008); 8-40 MPa conventional and 60-80 MPa resin-modified (Mount et al, 2016). Fracture toughness about 0.7 (restorative GIC), 1.4 (RMGIC), 1.75-1.92 (hybrid composite) MPa.m1/2 (Anusavice et al, 2013). ISO 9917 minimum compressive strength 130 MPa (restorative), 70 MPa (luting, lining) (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Brittle; poor shear resistance (Mount, 1994). Wear: 73 micrometres a year in one high-viscosity GIC report (Hilton et al, 2013); a well-placed cement withstood heavy abrasion better than remaining tooth structure (Mount, 1994).
**(e) Thermal.** Expansion 10-11 against tooth 11.4, diffusivity close to dentine: good insulation and little stress at the interface (Mount et al, 2016).
**(f) Optical.** Translucent; translucency develops over days; dehydration or early water ruins it (Mount et al, 2016).
**(g) Solubility and erosion.** Low in lactic acid (Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed: poor in 24-h in vitro testing, best of the conventional non-resin cements in 6-month in vivo testing, Ritter et al, 2019); vulnerable to acidic fluorides (APF) and fresh cement in acid (Mount et al, 2016). ISO limit 0.05 mm/h (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
**(h) Radiopacity.** Most restorative GICs are more radiopaque than dentine, but some auto-cure Type II.1 cements are radiolucent (Mount et al, 2016); Garg and Garg say conventional glass ionomer cements are radiolucent and that metal-modified and resin-modified types are radiopaque (Garg and Garg, 2011).

### 7. Modifications and newer materials
1. **Metal-reinforced:** silver cermet (about 40% silver by weight sintered to glass, up to 5% TiO2) and amalgam-alloy admix; better abrasion resistance, no other gain, less ion-exchange (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
2. **Resin-modified:** 15-25% resin (HEMA); sets on light and in the dark; higher strength and immediate finishing; drawbacks are residual HEMA (4.5% to 15%), water uptake, some shrinkage (Mount et al, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
3. **Tri-cure:** adds redox chemical initiator (Mount et al, 2016); Craig's 14th edition: acid-base plus light plus redox dark cure (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
4. **High-viscosity:** finer glass (about 3 micrometres) and polyacid in the powder; packable; ART; not for load-bearing permanent areas (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Hilton et al, 2013).
5. **Nano-ionomer:** nanomers and nanoclusters in RMGIC for polish and optics; clinical data sparse (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
6. **Glass hybrid:** high-viscosity GIC plus resin coat; 5-year survival in Class II 94.5% against 94.4% for composite, success 81.9% against 90.7%, no significant difference, in one RCT (Miletic et al, 2024).
7. **Not true GIC:** compomer and giomer (Mount et al, 2016); alkasite is an ion-releasing restorative compared against GIC in the literature (Laporte et al, 2026).
- **Dark-set test** to tell a true GIC from an imitation under a light-proof cover: signs of set at 5-7 min (Mount, 1994), reasonably hard within 7 min (Mount et al, 2016), reasonable set within 10 min (Mount, 1994); an unlit RMGIC sets in some 15-20 min (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### 7A. Resin-modified, high-viscosity and delivery variants in more detail
- **RMGIC gains:** set on light, more strength and toughness, early resistance to water, good translucency from the start (Mount et al, 2016; van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **RMGIC losses:** HEMA is hydrophilic, so the cement takes up water and swells in the first 5-7 days and may stain (Mount et al, 2016); residual HEMA is released into dentine (Mount et al, 2016); some powders are sensitive to ambient light and should be dispensed only at the moment of mixing (Mount et al, 2016); it is more cytotoxic than conventional GIC (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Auto-cure RMGIC** (resin catalyst, no light needed) is useful where light cannot reach, as in minimal cavities (Mount et al, 2016).
- **High-viscosity advantages:** packable, non-sticky, reduced early moisture sensitivity, quicker finishing, better wear resistance, low solubility (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Delivery:** capsules standardise the ratio but can trap porosities; automixed RMGIC avoids them; granulated powder mixes in under half the time (Mount et al, 2016); paste-paste systems (film thickness about 5 micrometres, set in 3 min) are used for luting and orthodontic work but lose some fluoride release and properties (Mount et al, 2016).

### 8. Clinical uses and technique
**Uses:**
- Luting: crowns, bridges, bands; reasons are low solubility, fluoride, thixotropic flow; retention comes from the preparation, since cement tensile strength is only 2-4 MPa (Mount et al, 2016).
- Restorative: Class III, Class V (including erosion and abrasion), root caries, deciduous teeth, small posterior lesions not under load (Mount et al, 2016).
- Lining and base: best long-term seal to dentine; under amalgam and composite (Mount, 1994).
- Sealants: partially erupted teeth, caries-active children (Mount et al, 2016).
- ART and minimal intervention: sealing the lesion can arrest caries; high-viscosity GIC (Mount et al, 2016; Bhat et al, 2019).
- Core: only where at least half the crown and a 3 mm sound collar remain (Mount et al, 2016); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says reinforced GIs should not be used for core build-ups (Ritter et al, 2019); Goldstein, block-out only for conventional GI (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Indirect pulp capping and transitional restoration (Mount et al, 2016).
**Technique:**
1. Isolate; clean with pumice; condition 10% PAA 10 s; wash; dry lightly (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Capsule mix about 10 s; place in the working window (Mount, 1994).
3. Matrix and pressure (Mount et al, 2016).
4. Seal at once with unfilled light-cured low-viscosity resin (Mount, 1994); McCabe and van Noort teach varnish, with resin as the longer-lasting seal (McCabe and Walls, 2008; van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
5. Finish: auto-cure Type II.1 at 24 hours or later; Type II.2 from about 6 min; dual-cure and RMGIC at once, polishing last (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016); Summitt 2-5 min delay for RMGI (Hilton et al, 2013).
**Cavity design.** Adhesion removes the need for undercuts; minimal tunnels and slots possible (Mount, 1994). Class V: butt joint, no bevel, about 1 mm depth (Hilton et al, 2013). Leave affected dentine if the margin is sealed (Mount, 1994).
**Sandwich technique (bi-layered):**
- GIC replaces dentine, composite replaces enamel.
- Rationale: composite shrinks and its dentine bond is uncertain; GIC is water-based and bonds reliably (Mount, 1994).
- Steps: place high-P/L GIC (at least 1 mm), cut back to expose enamel margins, etch enamel (and cement 15 s for auto-cure, Mount; Bonsor: freshly set cement loses its matrix and etches only after about 24 h; McCabe: etch only if matured), bond, add composite incrementally (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Open sandwich leaves GIC at the gingival margin for fluoride release (Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013), using RMGI because conventional GIC is less successful (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 8A. Site-wise cavity design and placement (Mount and Hume system)
- **Occlusal and proximal lesions (Sites 1 and 2):** GIC is the best initial restoration; use the strongest, preferably auto-cure, high-P/L cement and laminate with composite or amalgam if the load is heavy; no sub-lining (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Tunnel (occlusal approach):** enter through the fossa medial to the marginal ridge, remove infected dentine only, leave affected axial dentine (Mount, 1994); Type II.2 radiopaque capsulated cement in two increments tamped with a plastic sponge under a mylar strip, trimmed at about 5-6 minutes (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed does not support the tunnel technique and reports GI tunnel restorations inadequate in trials (Ritter et al, 2019), and Goldstein calls tunnel preparations ideal for composite or glass ionomer (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- **Cervical (Site 3) lesions:** Size 2 gets a Type II.1 cement, auto-cure or resin-modified (Mount et al, 2016); Size 3 (approximal root-surface or recurrent caries) gets a radiopaque, high-strength auto-cure cement, conditioned 10 s, placed in two increments and sealed with low-viscosity resin (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Large cavities:** a high-strength GIC base as a thermal barrier with ion exchange; calcium hydroxide sub-lining unnecessary (Mount et al, 2016); if the floor is very near the pulp, a thin high-fluoride auto-cure sub-lining may be added (Mount et al, 2016), whereas the Atlas says further sub-lining under GIC is not justified (Mount, 1994).
- **Undercuts left by caries removal** are filled with GIC rather than cut away (Mount et al, 2016).

### 8B. Special uses
- **Luting.** Vital tooth: do not condition dentine with polyacrylic acid because of hydraulic pressure; use a mineralising solution or tannic acid if needed and vent the crown. Non-vital tooth: condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 s (Mount, 1994). Post cementation: condition the posthole, dry with alcohol, wind the cement in with a Lentulo spiral (Mount, 1994).
- **Fissure sealant.** Pumice, condition with 10-20% polyacrylic acid for 10 s, dry field, protect with unfilled resin (Mount et al, 2016); best in partially erupted teeth and caries-active individuals (Mount et al, 2016). Resin sealants remain the first choice in many texts because of retention (Mount et al, 2016) [Likely].
- **Paediatric.** Many deciduous teeth can be restored with GIC alone (Mount et al, 2016); hypoplastic permanent molars are held with GIC while they erupt (Mount et al, 2016); stainless steel crowns on primary molars are cemented with GIC (Mount et al, 2016).
- **ART and high-viscosity GIC:** minimum-preparation restorations with hand instruments (Bhat et al, 2019).
- **Root caries and high-risk patients:** root lesions need more fluoride than enamel to remineralise (Hilton et al, 2013); for xerostomic and high-risk patients, RMGIC is an aesthetic choice for Class V (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Indirect pulp capping and transitional restoration:** GIC (or RMGIC over calcium hydroxide) seals residual caries; it is relatively insoluble and releases ions (Hilton et al, 2013; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Orthodontics:** bands cemented with GIC show better retention and less demineralisation than zinc phosphate (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Cervical resorption:** after removal of resorptive tissue the defect is restored with GIC protected by unfilled composite resin (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Elderly patients:** repair rather than replace; GIC preferred for its anti-caries value (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Therapeutic coating:** a long-lasting protective coat on susceptible surfaces where hygiene is poor (Mount et al, 2016).

### 9. Advantages, disadvantages, failure
**Advantages:** chemical adhesion; fluoride release and recharge; biocompatibility; thermal expansion close to tooth; conservative cavity; useful where isolation is difficult (Mount et al, 2016; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
**Disadvantages:** brittle, low tensile and shear strength; low wear resistance; water sensitivity; staining and loss of gloss; slow maturation; not for marginal ridge, incisal corner or heavy load (Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013). Wear is rated low by Hilton et al (2013) and good in a well-placed cement by Mount (1994).
**Failure causes:** low-powder mix or early water contact (ditching), dehydration (cracks), bulk cracks from failure to protect against dehydration or overload, contaminated surface (loss in erosion lesions) (Mount et al, 2016).
**Reported clinical performance (textbook-quoted):** Class V GIC without mechanical retention, 80% retention at 10 years (Hilton et al, 2013); Class I and Class II failure 8% and 40% at 24 months (Hilton et al, 2013); ADA review: 65% lower chance of recurrent caries around single-surface GIC than amalgam in permanent teeth, no difference in primary teeth (Hilton et al, 2013); Class V resin-modified GIC retained in 96.4% at 5 years against 51.5% for bonded composite in one study, and 94% against 67% for conventional GIC in another (Hilton et al, 2013); in retrospective reports almost half of GIC restorations were replaced for recurrent caries, which shows fluoride release does not remove caries risk (Hilton et al, 2013). These figures come from different study designs and should not be merged into one success rate [Likely].
**Special cautions:** xerostomia (Sjogren): auto-cure may disintegrate; acidic topical fluoride degrades GIC (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013); irradiated xerostomic patients lose the GIC survival advantage when topical acidic fluoride is used (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 9A. Repair and maintenance
- A failed GIC is normally replaced after removing all the old material, because the union between old and new GIC is not strong; partial removal with a composite laminate is the alternative (Mount et al, 2016). (One chapter says the opposite for sealants (Mount et al, 2016).)
- Minor marginal ditching can be reopened with a fine diamond and repaired with GIC (Mount et al, 2016).
- Beneath indirect restorations, a low-solubility luting cement is chosen because dissolution of the lute opens the margin (Mount et al, 2016). Under all-ceramic restorations, Ricketts and Bartlett advise against resin-modified GIC cores because of hygroscopic expansion and against conventional glass polyalkenoate luting agents (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011); Phillips adds that GIC's low elastic modulus gives poor support to an all-ceramic crown (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 10. Present evidence (critical appraisal)
1. **Fluoride and secondary caries.** RCT meta-analysis (64 trials): GIC has fewer secondary caries than amalgam (permanent teeth RR 0.20, primary RR 0.55) but not fewer than composite (RR 0.77, 0.92) (Ge et al, 2023). The benefit is shown against amalgam; the Atlas's "almost total prevention" (Mount, 1994) is not matched by this evidence [Likely].
2. **Longevity and ART.** Review of 14 RCTs: ART with high-viscosity GIC did not differ significantly from traditional restorations in primary teeth and single-surface permanent cavities; only 2 of 14 trials had low risk of bias (Frencken et al, 2021). Evidence overall is "conflicting" (Panetta et al, 2024). Multi-surface permanent restorations remain unproven [Likely].
3. **Sandwich technique.** Meta-analysis in cervical lesions: higher retention with sandwich at 3 years, wide CI, 4 studies from few RCTs (de Paula et al, 2019). No trial evidence found here for Class I/II [Likely].
4. **Fluoride release is not the same as clinical benefit.** Laboratory release is highest for GIC (Justen et al, 2024) but this has not been shown to be the mechanism of any clinical advantage [Likely].

5. **Conventional GIC against RMGIC.** In the same meta-analysis, secondary caries did not differ significantly between conventional GIC and RMGIC (primary teeth RR 1.12, 0.67-1.87; permanent teeth RR 1.63, 0.34-7.84) (Ge et al, 2023). Neither type is proven better for caries prevention [Likely].

### 11. FUTURE
- **Research aim:** more fracture resistance without losing ion exchange and fluoride release (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- **Antibacterial and remineralising additives:** chlorhexidine, chitosan, CPP-ACP, silver, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide; promising in vitro, not proven clinically (Mount et al, 2016; Danelon et al, 2025; Guo et al, 2024).
- **Hydroxyapatite and nano-fillers:** laboratory improvement in strength and fluoride release (Zhang et al, 2025).
- **Ion-releasing resins (alkasite, bioactive):** alkasite has composite-like laboratory properties (Justen et al, 2024) and, in short-term clinical studies, performance comparable to composite and "superior" to GIC (Laporte et al, 2026); ACTIVA matched composite and compomer in primary molars at 1-2 years (Popa et al, 2026).
- **Glass hybrid and high-viscosity systems:** equal survival to composite in moderate Class II over 5 years (94.5% against 94.4%), lower success numerically (81.9% against 90.7%) (Miletic et al, 2024); lower 5-year survival in MIH molars (59.2% against 75.5%) (Dokumacigil et al, 2026).
- **Silver diamine fluoride** (about 30%, stains black) is a separate arrest option (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); see answer P10.
- **Other directions mentioned in the DCNA literature:** amino-acid-modified, non-HEMA GICs and ultrasound-assisted hardening (Vaderhobli, 2011); bioactive orthodontic RMGIC needing further study (Zhang et al, 2017); and self-etch and self-adhesive materials that borrowed from the GIC interface (Seghi and Leyva del Rio, 2019).
- **Likely direction:** bioactivity with composite-level strength [Guessing].

### 11A. Role of GIC in modern (biological, minimal-intervention) restorative dentistry
- **Sealing the lesion rather than removing it all.** Infected dentine is removed, affected dentine may be left if the margin is fully sealed, and the lesion is expected to arrest and remineralise (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Transitional restoration.** A high-P/L GIC over affected dentine releases fluoride, strontium and phosphate ions; in a high-caries-risk mouth it may be kept as the permanent restoration and laminated later (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Dentine sealing under other materials.** GIC gives the most effective long-term seal to dentine, so it is used as the base under composite and amalgam (Mount, 1994).
- **High-risk patients.** Fluoride-releasing materials are described as essential for the high-caries-risk patient (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Moisture and technique tolerance.** Useful where isolation is difficult, though durable restorations still need moisture control, with dentine moist but not wet (Sakaguchi et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Limit of the role.** Minimal-cavity designs apply to new lesions with early dentine involvement; they are a first stage that may later be replaced by a conventional design (Mount, 1994).

### 12. Conclusion
GIC is the only restorative with a reliable chemical bond to tooth and a fluoride reservoir. Its past was a story of fixing handling (copolymers, tartaric acid, sealing); its present is a family of materials for luting, lining, sealing and low-load restoration; its future is stronger, bioactive materials that must not lose the ion exchange that defines the group (Mount et al, 2016) [Likely]. For an examiner, the three points to leave on the page are the chemistry (acid-base, three-stage set, water balance), the clinical rules that follow from it (condition, seal, delay finishing, choose by load) and the honest limit of the evidence (benefit against amalgam, not proven against composite) [Likely].


## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Wilson and Kent, 1972 in most books (1971 Sikri Ch.1; 1969 Hill); ISO name "glass polyalkenoate cement"; first product ASPA, 1976.
- Powder = fluoro-alumino-silicate glass; liquid = 40-55% polyacrylic/itaconic copolymer + tartaric acid 5-15% (Mount et al, 2016).
- Setting = dissolution, gelation (Ca/Sr, hard set 4-10 min), maturation (Al, 24 h and beyond); about 24% water (Mount et al, 2016).
- Adhesion = ion exchange; condition 10% PAA 10 s; never acid-etch; failure is cohesive (Mount et al, 2016).
- Fluoride = early burst, low steady level, rechargeable reservoir; benefit shown against amalgam, not against composite (Ge et al, 2023).
- Types I (luting, 1.5:1), II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced (3-4:1), III lining/base; only P/L separates lining from base (Mount, 1994).
- Seal immediately; finish auto-cure Type II.1 at 24 h or later, Type II.2 from 6 min, RMGIC at once (Mount, 1994).
- RMGIC has 15-25% resin; compomer and giomer are not glass ionomers (Mount et al, 2016).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling compomers or giomers "glass ionomers" (Mount et al, 2016); alkasite materials are also compared against, not counted as, GIC (Laporte et al, 2026).
2. Writing "acid-etch the tooth before GIC"; the conditioner is polyacrylic acid (10% for 10-15 s in Mount; 20% for 10-20 s in Sikri).
3. Leaving the restoration unsealed, or finishing auto-cure Type II.1 at once, then wondering why it cracked.
4. Writing "fluoride release prevents recurrent caries" without qualification; the RCT evidence shows benefit against amalgam only.
5. Quoting one date for the invention, or a precise "bond strength" figure, without noting that sources disagree on both.
