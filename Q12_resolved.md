## STUDY MODE

### S1. When a GIC restoration is the right choice [clinical decision-making]
Indications: Class V and Class III cavities, abrasion and erosion lesions, root caries, Class I in patients with high caries indices and deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020); Hilton lists provisional restorations or caries control in high-risk patients (Hilton et al, 2013). Garg and Garg (2011) add mouth breathers to the contraindications because the restoration may become opaque, brittle and fracture over time (Garg and Garg, 2011). Billings grade III root caries (dark brown, penetration over 1.0 mm, pulp spared) is restored with GIC, and pink GIC can mask gingival recession (Sikri, 2020). GIC is the material of choice for lesions not under heavy occlusal load, but is not suitable to rebuild marginal ridges or incisal corners (Mount et al, 2016). Where load is heavy, use a laminate with composite or amalgam (Mount et al, 2016).

### S2. Steps [treatment]
Isolate (rubber dam or cord), prepare conservatively, condition 10-20 seconds, mix to wet the powder only, place under a matrix and protect with resin (Sikri, 2020). Books differ on detail. Mixing: first half of the powder wet within 10 to 15 seconds, mixing complete in about 30 seconds, plastic spatula preferred (Sikri, 2020); first half within 10 seconds and finished within a further 15 seconds to a glossy wet surface (Mount et al, 2016); up to 45 seconds, a glossy surface showing unreacted polyacid and a dull one meaning the acid has reacted too much (Anusavice et al, 2013); agate spatula, plastic inappropriate (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Protection: plastic matrix for about 5 minutes, then the supplied varnish (Anusavice et al, 2013); resin enamel bond as the best protection, rather than varnish (Sikri, 2020); single-component very low viscosity resin bond (Mount et al, 2016). Finishing: initial contouring after 24 hours, as many clinicians prefer, with final polishing delayed 24 hours, and resin-modified GIC finished and polished immediately after set (Sikri, 2020); resin-modified GIC contoured and polished immediately, auto-cure GIC polished a minimum of one week later (Mount et al, 2016); a delay of 2 to 5 minutes before polishing resin-modified GIC (Hilton et al, 2013). Class III is best restored with light-cure GIC for aesthetics, using a T-shaped Mylar strip (Sikri, 2020). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed gives 10% polyacrylic acid for 20 s for Fuji IX and 10-20% conditioners for RMGI restoratives, light-curing typically 20-40 s, and says most current restorative GICs and RMGIs may be finished and polished immediately after activation, shaved with a No. 12 blade (Ritter et al, 2019); this differs from the 24-hour conventional-GIC advice of Sikri (2020) and the one-week auto-cure advice of Mount et al (2016). For Class V, Singh 2nd ed describes a tin foil matrix used mainly with glass ionomer (Singh, 2020).

### S3. Why GIC restorations fail [prognosis]
Failure comes from dissolution of immature cement (first 24 hours), long-term erosion, abrasion loss, and fracture from flexure in cervical areas, often cohesive within the cement (Sikri, 2020). An unprotected surface cracks (Sikri, 2020). Average replacement age is about 3-5 years, though not all GICs fail after five years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). In Class V, one study showed 80% retention at 10 years for restorations placed without mechanical retention, another 96.4% retention of a resin-modified GIC at 5 years, and a later study 94% for Class V RMGI against 67% for conventional GIC (Hilton et al, 2013). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed quotes a 2014 review in which glass ionomer-based materials had the lowest annual failure rate (2%) in non-carious cervical lesions (Ritter et al, 2019). Cores: Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed says reinforced glass ionomers should not be used for core build-ups (Ritter et al, 2019); Goldstein 3rd ed allows resin-modified GIC only as a partial core with adequate tooth structure (Goldstein et al, 2018); Perdigao (2016) reports all glass ionomer core crowns failed by 20,000 cycles (Badr et al, in Perdigao, 2016).

### S4. Advantages, limits and repair
Advantages are adhesion without undercuts (van Noort and Barbour, 2013), fluoride release, biocompatibility and low solubility after the full set (Sikri, 2020); limits are opacity and technique sensitivity (Sikri, 2020) and susceptibility to brittle fracture (Mount et al, 2016). Glass ionomer has a rougher surface, so polish lightly at slow speed, lubricate to prevent desiccation, and avoid ultrasonic scalers (Goldstein et al, 2018). A fractured GIC can be repaired by conditioning with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds, washing, blotting dry and packing new GIC (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
A glass ionomer restoration uses a glass polyalkenoate cement as a direct tooth-coloured filling that bonds chemically to tooth and releases fluoride (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Indications
- Class V and Class III cavities; abrasion, erosion and abfraction lesions (Sikri, 2020).
- Root caries (Billings grade III) and high-risk or xerostomic patients (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020).
- Class I in high caries-index patients; deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020); provisional, ART and interim therapeutic restorations (Hilton et al, 2013).
- As part of a sandwich restoration (Sikri, 2020).

### 3. Contraindications
Heavy occlusal load in permanent teeth, large multi-surface restorations, incisal corners and marginal ridges, cores without two walls, and where moisture control is impossible (Hilton et al, 2013; Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011; Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).

### 4. Cavity preparation
Minimal; no undercuts needed, though some undercut may be advantageous under high stress (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); outline follows the lesion; groove for wide lesions, more grooves if the lesion dictates; do not break proximal contacts (Sikri, 2020). No bevel; use a butt joint (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 5. Technique
1. Isolate (Sikri, 2020).
2. Condition 10-20 seconds and wash (Sikri, 2020).
3. Mix quickly; plastic spatula (Sikri, 2020).
4. Place under a matrix, avoiding voids (Sikri, 2020).
5. Seal with resin at once (Sikri, 2020).
6. Finish in moist conditions: conventional GIC after 24 hours, resin-modified immediately (Sikri, 2020); resin-modified immediately, auto-cure after a minimum of one week (Mount et al, 2016); resin-modified after 2 to 5 minutes (Hilton et al, 2013); most current GICs and RMGIs immediately (Ritter et al, 2019).

### 5A. Technique points by cavity class
- **Class III:** T-shaped Mylar strip; light-cure 20 seconds through the matrix, remove it, then cure further (Sikri, 2020).
- **Class V:** retraction cord for isolation; examine other teeth for further root lesions, since they rarely occur alone (Sikri, 2020).
- **Cervical Site 3, Size 2:** resin-modified or auto-cure GIC depending on light access, always conditioned with polyacrylic acid (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Advantages
Chemical adhesion, fluoride release, biocompatibility, thermal expansion near dentine, low solubility once set, minimal preparation (Sikri, 2020).

### 7. Disadvantages
Brittle (Mount et al, 2016); opaque, moisture sensitive, high early solubility (Sikri, 2020); poor wear resistance (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 8. Failures and repair
Dissolution of immature cement, erosion, abrasion, fracture from flexure; repair with polyacrylic acid conditioning and new GIC (Sikri, 2020; Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

### 8A. Maintenance
Old GIC must be protected from dehydration during prolonged isolation, especially under rubber dam (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); acidulated phosphate fluoride products can erode GIC and are avoided (Mount et al, 2016); fluoride toothpaste and gels recharge it (Mount et al, 2016).

### 9. Prognosis
Average replacement age about 3-5 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011); Class V retention 80% at 10 years in one study of restorations placed without mechanical retention, and 67% conventional GIC against 94% RMGI in a later one (Hilton et al, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Best for Class V, Class III, root caries, high-risk and deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013).
- No bevel (Hilton et al, 2013); condition 10-20 s; seal at once; finish conventional GIC at 24 h (Sikri, 2020), resin-modified at once to 5 minutes (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013).
- Failures: early dissolution, erosion, abrasion, flexural fracture (Sikri, 2020).
