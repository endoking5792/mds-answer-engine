## STUDY MODE

### S1. When a GIC restoration is the right choice [clinical decision-making]
Indications: Class V and Class III cavities, abrasion and erosion lesions, root caries, Class I in patients with high caries indices, deciduous teeth, and transitional restorations (Sikri, 2020). GIC is the material of choice for lesions not under heavy occlusal load (Mount et al, 2016). Where load is heavy, use a laminate with composite or amalgam (Mount et al, 2016).

### S2. Steps [treatment]
Isolate (rubber dam or cord), prepare conservatively, condition 10-20 seconds, mix to wet the powder only, place under a matrix, protect with resin, and finish after 24 hours (Sikri, 2020). Class III is best restored with light-cure GIC for aesthetics, using a T-shaped Mylar strip (Sikri, 2020).

### S3. Why GIC restorations fail [prognosis]
Failure comes from dissolution of immature cement (first 24 hours), long-term erosion, abrasion loss, and fracture from flexure in cervical areas, often cohesive within the cement (Sikri, 2020). An unprotected surface cracks (Sikri, 2020). Average replacement age is about 3-5 years, though not all GICs fail after five years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). In Class V, one study showed 80% retention at 10 years and another 96.4% retention of a resin-modified GIC at 5 years (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S4. Advantages, limits and repair
Advantages are adhesion without undercuts, fluoride release, biocompatibility and low solubility after the full set; limits are opacity, brittleness and technique sensitivity (Sikri, 2020). A fractured GIC can be repaired by conditioning with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds, washing, blotting dry and packing new GIC (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
A glass ionomer restoration uses a glass polyalkenoate cement as a direct tooth-coloured filling that bonds chemically to tooth and releases fluoride (Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Indications
- Class V and Class III cavities; abrasion, erosion and abfraction lesions (Sikri, 2020).
- Root caries and high-risk or xerostomic patients (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Class I in high caries-index patients; deciduous teeth; transitional, ART and interim therapeutic restorations (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013).
- As part of a sandwich restoration (Sikri, 2020).

### 3. Contraindications
Heavy occlusal load in permanent teeth, large multi-surface restorations, incisal corners, cores without two walls, and where moisture control is impossible (Hilton et al, 2013; Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011; Sikri, 2020).

### 4. Cavity preparation
Minimal; no undercuts needed; outline follows the lesion; retention grooves only for wide lesions; do not break proximal contacts (Sikri, 2020; van Noort and Barbour, 2013). No bevel; use a butt joint (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 5. Technique
1. Isolate (Sikri, 2020).
2. Condition 10-20 seconds and wash (Sikri, 2020).
3. Mix quickly; plastic spatula (Sikri, 2020).
4. Place under a matrix, avoiding voids (Sikri, 2020).
5. Seal with resin at once (Sikri, 2020).
6. Finish after 24 hours in moist conditions (Sikri, 2020).

### 5A. Technique points by cavity class
- **Class III:** T-shaped Mylar strip; light-cure 20 seconds through the matrix, remove it, then cure further (Sikri, 2020).
- **Class V:** retraction cord for isolation; examine other teeth for further root lesions, since they rarely occur alone (Sikri, 2020).
- **Cervical Site 3, Size 2:** resin-modified or auto-cure GIC depending on light access, always conditioned with polyacrylic acid (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Advantages
Chemical adhesion, fluoride release, biocompatibility, thermal expansion near dentine, low solubility once set, minimal preparation (Sikri, 2020).

### 7. Disadvantages
Brittle, opaque, moisture sensitive, high early solubility, low wear resistance (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013).

### 8. Failures and repair
Dissolution of immature cement, erosion, abrasion, fracture from flexure; repair with polyacrylic acid conditioning and new GIC (Sikri, 2020; Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

### 8A. Maintenance
Old GIC must be protected from dehydration during prolonged isolation, especially under rubber dam (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); acidulated phosphate fluoride products can erode GIC and are avoided (Mount et al, 2016); fluoride toothpaste and gels recharge it (Mount et al, 2016).

### 9. Prognosis
Average replacement age about 3-5 years; Class V retention of 80% at 10 years in one study (Banerjee and Watson, 2011; Hilton et al, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Best for Class V, Class III, root caries, high-risk and deciduous teeth (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013).
- No bevel; condition 10-20 s; seal at once; finish at 24 h (Hilton et al, 2013; Sikri, 2020).
- Failures: early dissolution, erosion, abrasion, flexural fracture (Sikri, 2020).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Bevelling margins.
2. Finishing at once or leaving the cement unsealed.
3. Recommending GIC for heavy occlusal load.
