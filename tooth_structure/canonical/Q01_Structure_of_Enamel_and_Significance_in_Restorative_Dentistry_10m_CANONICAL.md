## Explanation

**Origin and thickness.** Enamel is formed by ameloblasts (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Thickness: Sturdevant's gives about 2 mm at incisal ridges of incisors and 2.5-3 mm at molar cusps (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Summitt's gives about 2.5 mm at cusp tips and 2.0 mm at incisal edges, thinning to the CEJ (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

**Composition — the two books use different bases.** Sturdevant's: hydroxyapatite 90-92% by volume, water 4-12% by volume (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's: 96% hydroxyapatite by weight and more than 86% by volume (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Both agree on water (4-12% by volume); the mineral-by-volume figures differ (90-92% vs >86%) and are shown as given, not reconciled. Carbonated apatite is the most acid-susceptible part of the crystal (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

**The rod — two descriptions.** Sturdevant's describes millions of rods (prisms) with a rounded head about 5 µm wide lying between the tails (5 µm long) of two adjacent prisms — the head-and-tail (keyhole) model; about 5 million rods in a mandibular incisor to 12 million in a maxillary molar; rod diameter about 4 µm at the DEJ and 8 µm at the surface; crystallites about 160 nm long and 20-40 nm wide (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's states that the keyhole was the traditional "basic repeating structural unit of enamel", but the current interpretation is cylindric rods embedded in continuous interrod enamel, the two differing only in crystal orientation; an organic rod sheath surrounds three-fourths of each rod; crystals are about 30 × 60 nm in cross-section (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. The crystal figures are different measurements (length × width vs cross-section) and should be quoted with their source.

**Structural features (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].** Gnarled enamel (intertwined rods near cusps and cervical areas) resists fracture and does not yield readily to hand instruments; Hunter-Schreger bands are the optical effect of changing rod direction; tufts are hypomineralized projections from the DEJ; lamellae are leaf-like organic faults from surface to DEJ, weak areas that admit bacteria; enamel spindles are odontoblast processes crossing the DEJ with thickened ends; striae of Retzius are growth rings whose surface endings form perikymata; Nasmyth's membrane is the last product of the ameloblast, so enamel cannot repair itself; prismless enamel is a structureless outer layer about 30 µm thick. The DEJ is scalloped and about 2 µm wide.

**Properties.** Hardest tissue of the body; high elastic modulus, high compressive and low tensile strength (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Knoop hardness 343 vs 68 for dentin; rod sheaths form natural cleavage lines; enamel is semipermeable (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

**Restorative significance.** Unsupported rods fracture, so all rods should be supported by dentin (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. A wall perpendicular to the surface exposes rod sides and suits amalgam; etching removes about 10 µm, gives resin tags up to 20 µm; 30,000-40,000 rods per mm²; bond strength to crystal ends is twice that to sides, hence a 45° bevel for composite except occlusally; plane the margin with hand or low-speed instruments (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Dehydration whitens enamel within minutes, so take the shade before isolation; fluoride lowers acid solubility; pits and fissures trap food and bacteria (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Physiological wear is 15-29 µm a year; in about 10% of teeth enamel and cementum fail to meet, leaving dentin exposed (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Enamel is the acellular, highly mineralized ectodermal covering of the crown, formed by ameloblasts; it cannot repair itself (Ritter et al, 2019).

**1. Thickness:** about 2 mm at incisal edges, 2.5-3 mm at molar cusps, tapering to the CEJ (Ritter et al, 2019; Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

**2. Composition**
| | Sturdevant's | Summitt's |
|---|---|---|
| Hydroxyapatite | 90-92% by volume | 96% by weight, >86% by volume |
| Water | 4-12% by volume | 4-12% by volume |
| Organic | 1-2% by volume | small volume |

**3. Rod (prism):** 5-12 million rods per tooth; 4 µm at DEJ, 8 µm at surface; head-and-tail (keyhole) pattern (Ritter et al, 2019). Current view: cylindrical rods in continuous interrod enamel, differing only in crystal orientation, rod sheath around three-fourths of each rod (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

**4. Features:** gnarled enamel; Hunter-Schreger bands; tufts; lamellae; spindles; striae of Retzius and perikymata; prismless surface layer (30 µm); Nasmyth's membrane (Ritter et al, 2019).

**5. Properties:** hardest tissue (KHN 343 vs 68 for dentin); brittle — high modulus, low tensile strength; semipermeable (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

**6. DEJ and CEJ:** DEJ scalloped, about 2 µm wide, resists crack propagation. At the CEJ, about 10% of teeth show a gap exposing dentin — cervical sensitivity (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

**7. Significance in restorative dentistry**
- Remove unsupported enamel; every rod must rest on dentin.
- Gnarled enamel resists hand-instrument cleavage.
- Amalgam: 90° walls parallel to rods. Composite: 45° bevel to expose rod ends (bond twice as strong), except occlusally.
- Etching removes 10 µm and forms 20 µm resin tags.
- Plane margins with hand or slow-speed instruments.
- Lamellae and fissures are caries pathways; seal fissures.
- Select shade before isolation (dehydration whitens enamel).
- Fluoride lowers acid solubility (Ritter et al, 2019; Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

**Conclusion.** Rod direction and dentin support decide cavity wall design, bevelling and bonding.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Hydroxyapatite: 96% by weight (Summitt's); 90-92% by volume (Sturdevant's) — quote the basis.
- Rod 4 µm (DEJ) to 8 µm (surface); 5-12 million rods per tooth.
- Etch: 10 µm removed, 20 µm tags; bond to rod ends twice that to sides.
- Bevel composite margins except occlusal; never leave unsupported enamel.
- CEJ gap in about 10% of teeth.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
1. Keyhole rod pattern: heads 5 µm wide between tails, heads occlusal, tails cervical.
2. Cavosurface margins: 90° butt joint (amalgam, rod sides exposed) vs 45° bevel (composite, rod ends exposed).
3. Longitudinal section: striae of Retzius ending as perikymata; tufts and lamellae at the DEJ.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Giving "96%" without saying "by weight".
- Calling the keyhole the current model without noting the cylinder-in-interrod view.
- Bevelling occlusal margins of composites.
- Forgetting that enamel cannot repair itself (ameloblasts are lost).

## MNEMONIC
**"Good Hunters Tie Lamb Strips"** — Gnarled, Hunter-Schreger, Tufts, Lamellae, Spindles (plus Striae of Retzius).
