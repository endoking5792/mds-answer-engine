## 1. The pulp-dentin organ: why it reacts as one

### Explanation

Pulp and dentin form one organ; "normal form and function of one cannot be maintained without the other" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Both derive from the dental papilla (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], hence the term pulpodentin complex (D'Souza and Qin, 2012) [SRC]. Dentinal fluid is about 22% of dentin volume, and pulpal circulation keeps an outward tissue pressure of about 24 mm Hg (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; fluid movement through tubules is the basis of the hydrodynamic theory (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Tubules increase from 15,000-20,000/mm² at the DEJ to 45,000-65,000/mm² near the pulp and widen from 0.5-0.9 to 2-3 µm (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Marzouk reminds that every mm² of cut dentin contains 30,000-45,000 tubules (Marzouk et al, 1985) [SRC]. Hilton and Summitt: no material protects the pulp better than dentin; it buffers acids and insulates against heat; RDT is the single most important protective factor (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Dentin and pulp are one organ — the pulp-dentin (P-D) organ. Both arise from the dental papilla, the odontoblast lives with its cell body in the pulp and its process in the dentin, and dentinal fluid is continuous with pulpal tissue fluid; "normal form and function of one cannot be maintained without the other" (Sikri, 2020). Every operative step that touches dentin therefore touches the pulp.

**Why cut dentin transmits injury to the pulp**
1. **Tubules:** each mm² of cut dentin opens 30,000-45,000 tubules (Marzouk et al, 1985); density rises from 15,000-20,000/mm² at the DEJ to 45,000-65,000/mm² near the pulp, and diameter from 0.5-0.9 µm to 2-3 µm (Ritter et al, 2019). Deep dentin is therefore far more permeable.
2. **Dentinal fluid:** about 22% of dentin volume, under an outward pulpal pressure of about 24 mm Hg (Sikri, 2020). Movement of this fluid causes pain (hydrodynamic theory) and carries irritants inward by diffusion (Ritter et al, 2019).
3. **Odontoblasts:** cutting, heat and drying injure the processes and cell bodies directly.
4. **Rigid encasement:** the pulp cannot swell; inflammation raises pressure on nerves.

**Dentin as the protective barrier:** dentin buffers acids, insulates against heat, and dilutes toxins — 0.5 mm reduces toxicity by 75% and 1 mm by 90% (Hilton and Summitt, 2013; Sikri, 2020). No liner or base protects the pulp as well as dentin itself, so conserving tooth structure is the first rule of pulp protection (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).

**Permeability gradient:** dentin directly under the DEJ is only about 1% permeable, rising to 7.6% halfway to the pulp and 22% at the pulp surface (Sikri, 2020). A preparation that doubles in depth therefore exposes the pulp to many times more diffusion.

**Defence mechanisms of the organ:** blocking of tubules (sclerosis, smear layer, intratubular deposits) and formation of new dentin at the pulp-dentin junction are the two main defensive reactions of dentin (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013); inflammation and immune cells are the pulp's own defence. Mast cells appear only in inflamed, not normal, pulp (Sikri, 2020).

## 2. Types of pulp-dentin reaction and their grading

### Explanation

Marzouk classifies the reaction as (I) healthy reparative — sclerotic dentin and/or calcific barriers followed by normal secondary dentin; (II) unhealthy reparative — odontoblasts poisoned or aspirated, a dead tract forms and secondary dentin stops, then irregular tertiary "irritation dentin" from undifferentiated mesenchymal cells and fibroblasts seals the dead tracts; tertiary dentin "ages the pulp", reducing its defensive capacity; (III) destructive — loss of odontoblasts and the outer protective layer of the pulp, inflammation, abscess or necrosis (Marzouk et al, 1985) [SRC]. Sikri grades histological reactions as slight (more cells in the cell-free zone), moderate and severe (marked infiltration including abscess), and by the I0-I3 inflammation scale (I0 no inflammation and no disturbance of the odontoblastic layer; I3 central pulp involved) and the D-1 to D3 dentin-formation scale (D3 considerable secondary dentin); pulp and dentin usage tests place materials on dentin 1.0 mm from the pulp (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Clinical-biological classification (Marzouk et al, 1985)**
| Reaction | Events | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| I. Healthy reparative | Odontoblasts stimulated → sclerotic dentin, calcific barrier, then normal secondary dentin | Pulp undisturbed |
| II. Unhealthy reparative | Odontoblasts poisoned or aspirated → dead tract, secondary dentin stops → irregular tertiary "irritation dentin" from mesenchymal cells seals the tract | Mild, reversible pulpal change; tertiary dentin "ages the pulp" |
| III. Destructive | Loss of odontoblasts and peripheral layers → inflammation → abscess → necrosis | Irreversible; pulp or tooth removal |

**B. Histological grading (Sikri, 2020)**
- *Slight* — more cells in the cell-free zone, a few inflammatory cells, more capillaries.
- *Moderate* — mononuclear cells and neutrophils in the odontoblast-predentin zone, displaced odontoblast nuclei, occasional haemorrhage.
- *Severe* — marked infiltration, abscess; odontoblast layer destroyed, no predentin.
- Inflammation scale I0 (no inflammation, odontoblast layer intact) → I3 (central pulp involved); dentin formation scale D-1 (odontoblasts destroyed, no dentin) → D3 (considerable new dentin, >90 µm).

**Time course of the histological response (Sikri, 2020)**
1. *Cellular displacement* — odontoblasts and leukocytes move into tubules in the first few days and may persist over 30 days.
2. *Infiltration of inflammatory cells* into the odontoblast layer, zone of Weil and cell-rich zone, graded 0-3 (rarely 4).
3. *Resolution* — acute cells are replaced by chronic cells, which disappear as reparative dentin forms; usually 1-6 days, sometimes 20 days.
4. *Special features* — abscess-like collections of leukocytes between odontoblasts and predentin, usually resolving; foci of necrosis (loss of cellular detail, collapsed vessels) after cutting plus a toxic material; regeneration of odontoblasts; reparative dentin (seldom before 30 days); eosinophilic staining from plasma transudate escaping mildly injured capillaries.

**C. Biocompatibility testing:** the "pulp and dentin usage test" places a material on dentin 1.0 mm from the pulp and records inflammation, reparative dentin and trapped bacteria (Sikri, 2020) — the basis of the data quoted below.

## 3. Factors that modify the response

### Explanation

RDT: little reaction at 2 mm or more; the greatest impact at 0.25-0.30 mm or less (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Responses to the same irritant vary between persons and between teeth (Sikri, 2020; Marzouk et al, 1985) [SRC]. The final picture is cumulative — decay, then cavity preparation, then materials (Marzouk et al, 1985) [SRC]. The smear layer reduces permeability; acid conditioning increases it by removing the smear layer and widening orifices (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Sclerotic dentin is very impermeable (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Older pulps are less able to resolve a lesion (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Factors modifying the pulp-dentin response**
1. **Remaining dentin thickness — the single most important factor** (Hilton and Summitt, 2013). Little reaction at RDT ≥2 mm; maximum injury at ≤0.25-0.30 mm. (Thresholds in other books: Q05.)
2. **Dentin permeability:** depth (more and wider tubules near the pulp), tubule patency, intratubular deposits, sclerosis (very low permeability) and tertiary dentin (Pashley and Tay, 2012; Sikri, 2020).
3. **Smear layer:** reduces permeability; acid conditioning removes it and widens tubule orifices (Sikri, 2020).
   - Intratubular calcifications, collagen and proteins reduce permeability; large, high-molecular-weight solutes penetrate less than small, water-soluble ones — so small acid molecules (e.g. phosphoric acid) reach the pulp quickly (Sikri, 2020).
4. **Pre-existing state of the pulp:** the reaction is cumulative — caries, then preparation, then restorative material (Marzouk et al, 1985). An already irritated pulp tolerates less.
5. **Age:** older pulps are less able to resolve a lesion and resist infection (Sikri, 2020), though their sclerotic dentin protects them.
6. **Individual variation:** the same irritant gives different responses in different people and even in different teeth of the same person (Sikri, 2020; Marzouk et al, 1985).
7. **Extent and duration of the procedure** (section 5): deep, wide preparations such as full crowns open far more tubules than a small occlusal cavity (Haug, Kim and Heyeraas, 2019).
8. **Site — coronal vs root dentin:** coronal dentin is much more permeable than root dentin (Ritter et al, 2019); root dentin is 10-20 times less permeable than the same thickness of coronal dentin, which may explain why scaling and root planing that remove cementum rarely provoke pulpal reactions (Haug, Kim and Heyeraas, 2019). Cervical and axial walls of a preparation therefore deserve as much care as the pulpal floor.

## 4. Baseline: the pulp's response to caries

### Explanation

Even enamel lesions less than one-fourth of the way to the DEJ cause a slight pulpal reaction (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk: the more acute the decay, the more destructive the response (Marzouk et al, 1985) [SRC]. Significant adverse pulpal responses result more from bacteria or their toxins than from materials; outward fluid flow does not prevent bacteria reaching the pulp (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Reactionary dentin is associated with small, slowly progressing lesions (Smith, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Caries — the first irritant**
- The pulp reacts early: even enamel lesions less than one-fourth of the way to the DEJ cause a slight reaction (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
- Slowly progressing (chronic) lesions → reactionary dentin and sclerosis; acute lesions → odontoblast death, reparative dentin or inflammation (Smith, 2012; Marzouk et al, 1985).
- Bacteria and their toxins, not materials, cause most significant pulpal damage; outward fluid flow does not stop them (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
- Once bacteria or their toxins actually reach the pulp, severe inflammation or necrosis frequently follows (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
- Caries itself induces reparative dentin and reactive sclerosis, increasing the protection of the remaining dentin (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
- Clinical meaning: the pulp under a deep cavity is already irritated before the bur touches it; operative procedures add to that burden.

## 5. Response to tooth preparation (instrumentation)

### Explanation

Marzouk lists eleven irritating agents (Marzouk et al, 1985) [SRC]: cutting itself; pressure, which aspirates odontoblasts, nuclei or nerve endings into tubules, especially at decreased effective depth; type of instrument — sharp hand instruments are most biologically acceptable, burs are acceptable over effective depths of 2 mm and more with coolant, carbide more cool-cutting than steel, abrasive stones confined to enamel and superficial dentin; depth (most detrimental); heat (second most detrimental) — an 11 °F rise causes destruction even in a normal pulp; 3000-30,000 rpm the most deleterious range; pressure no more than 4 oz at high speed (12 oz at low speed); larger contact area more heat; heat can coagulate protoplasm and char dentin; desiccation increases permeability; plain water the most effective coolant; vibration causes a "rebound response" and microcracks; a crown preparation causes more response than a Class I. Hilton and Summitt (2013) [SRC]: reaction depends on friction and desiccation; high-speed preparation with light force (1-3 oz), new burs and coolant rarely led to endodontic treatment; water spray at the bur-tooth contact is the key; frictional heat causes burn lesions and abscesses; dry cutting injury increases with the dry area; lost tubular fluid may be replaced by harmful chemicals; cutting enamel raises temperature more than dentin; pressure affects temperature more than speed, which is why low speed has been shown to be more traumatic; diamonds heat more than carbides; 3-22% of crowned teeth need endodontic treatment. Air abrasion is no more traumatic than rotary cutting and lasers produce minimal pulpal response if water-cooled (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC], whereas Sikri states that heat from lasers is more detrimental (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Electrosurgery touching a metallic restoration causes severe pulpal reaction, worse with contact longer than 0.4 seconds (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Crown preparation reduces pulpal blood flow, and deep dry cutting causes vasoconstriction (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Irritating agents of tooth preparation (Marzouk et al, 1985; Hilton and Summitt, 2013)**

**1. The act of cutting dentin** — every mm² severs the processes of 30,000-45,000 tubules; injury spreads to millions of odontoblasts.

**2. Instrument pressure** — drives odontoblasts, their nuclei and nerve endings into the tubules ("aspiration"), and can push bacteria from an infected floor towards the pulp; worst when effective depth is small (Marzouk et al, 1985). Recommended force: Marzouk ≤4 oz at high speed and ≤12 oz at low speed; Hilton and Summitt 1-3 oz with high speed.

**3. Type of instrument**
- Sharp hand instruments: energy is spent in cutting, not crushing — most biologically acceptable.
- Burs: acceptable over 2 mm effective depth with coolant; carbide burs cut cooler than steel (Marzouk et al, 1985) and than diamonds (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
- Abrasive stones/diamonds: more heat and crushing — confine to enamel and the superficial 1 mm of dentin.

**4. Depth of the cavity** — the most detrimental factor; reaction increases as RDT falls (Marzouk et al, 1985; Hilton and Summitt, 2013).

**5. Heat — the second most detrimental factor**
- A pulpal rise of 11 °F (about 6 °C) causes destruction even in a normal pulp (Marzouk et al, 1985).
- Heat depends on speed (3000-30,000 rpm most harmful, especially with abrasives), pressure (Hilton and Summitt: pressure raises temperature more than speed, which is why low-speed cutting has been shown to be more traumatic than high speed), contact area, and the tissue cut — enamel generates more heat than dentin.
- Severe heat coagulates protoplasm, chars dentin and enamel (later sequestrated), and produces burn lesions and abscesses in the pulp (Marzouk et al, 1985; Hilton and Summitt, 2013). Burning of dentin is visible histologically when excessive pressure is used without cooling (Sikri, 2020).
- In deep penetrations without coolant, such as pin holes, speed must not exceed 3000 rpm (Marzouk et al, 1985).

**6. Desiccation** — drying draws odontoblast nuclei into tubules (osmotic disturbance), increases permeability, and lost tubular fluid is replaced by chemicals from materials; injury increases with the area dried (Marzouk et al, 1985; Hilton and Summitt, 2013). Use air only to remove debris, never to dehydrate dentin.

**7. Coolant** — water spray at the point of bur-tooth contact is the key to controlling both friction and desiccation; plain water is the most effective coolant, and air alone can do more harm than good (Marzouk et al, 1985; Hilton and Summitt, 2013). At high speed the turbulence around the bur deflects water, so a jet with enough velocity to penetrate it is needed; diamonds and cross-cut burs need water at several levels (Marzouk et al, 1985). Increasing spray volume significantly reduces pulpal temperature rise; in one retrospective study high-speed preparation with light force, new burs and coolant rarely led to endodontic treatment (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).

**8. Vibration** — eccentric instruments produce a "rebound response" (odontoblast disruption on the opposite side of the chamber, oedema, fibrosis, reduced predentin) and microcracks in enamel and dentin (Marzouk et al, 1985).

**9. Extent and duration** — a crown preparation causes more response than a Class I; 3-22% of teeth with full crowns eventually need endodontic treatment (Hilton and Summitt, 2013; Marzouk et al, 1985). Gentle grinding of outer dentin increases pulpal blood flow, but grinding in the inner half decreases it; after crown preparation flow falls because the remaining dentin may be less than 1 mm, and deep cutting without coolant causes vasoconstriction (Sikri, 2020). Knowing where each stroke starts and ends shortens the procedure and limits injury (Marzouk et al, 1985).

**10. Newer cutting methods**
- Air abrasion: no more traumatic than rotary instruments (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
- Lasers (CO₂, Er:YAG, Nd:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG): minimal pulpal response provided water coolant is used (Hilton and Summitt, 2013); Sikri, however, states that laser heat is more detrimental (Sikri, 2020). The two books disagree; both agree heat is the danger.

**11. Electrosurgery** — harmless on intact enamel, but contact with a metallic restoration causes severe pulpal damage, worse beyond 0.4 s of contact (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).

**12. Mechanical pulp exposure** — the first sign is oozing of clear pulpal fluid through microscopic cracks in the thin dentin floor, not bleeding; when injury is limited to the peripheral pulp layers no haemorrhage occurs although the pulp is actually exposed; bleeding appears only when the pulp proper is contused (Marzouk et al, 1985). A "dry" deep floor is therefore no proof that the pulp is unexposed.

**13. Individual and cumulative factors** — the same procedure produces different responses in different teeth; the final reaction sums caries + preparation + material (Marzouk et al, 1985).

**Keys to minimal injury (Hilton and Summitt, 2013):** adequate air-water coolant, light pressure, sharp cutting instruments, preservation of tooth structure.

## 6. Changes in dentin structure caused by operative procedures

### Explanation

Displacement of odontoblasts and leukocytes into the tubules appears in the first days (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; inadequate cooling displaces odontoblast nuclei, and Sikri states the older term "aspiration" should be "displacement" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Dead tracts follow injury caused by caries, attrition, erosion or cavity preparation (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Reactionary dentin is secreted by surviving odontoblasts, limited to tubules communicating with the stimulus; peak at RDT 0.25-0.50 mm; materials ranked calcium hydroxide > composite > RMGI > ZOE (Smith, 2012) [SRC]. Reparative dentin is formed by new odontoblast-like cells (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; it seldom develops earlier than 30 days, and the greater the initial response, the greater its incidence (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; slowly destroyed odontoblasts leave fibrodentin or interface dentin, which reduces permeability (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Reactive sclerosis follows mild irritation (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Signalling molecules for repair are released from the dentin matrix itself (Smith, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Changes in dentin and the odontoblast layer after operative procedures**
1. **Smear layer** on every cut surface — a layer of microcrystalline debris that controls and reduces permeability; acid removes it (Sikri, 2020). (Sikri prints its thickness as "1.0-5.0 mm"; the unit should read µm — a misprint [Likely].)
2. **Odontoblast displacement** ("aspiration") — nuclei and whole cells move into tubules within days, from pressure, heat or drying; Sikri prefers the term displacement (Sikri, 2020).
3. **Dead tracts** — tubules emptied by degeneration of processes after cavity preparation (Sikri, 2020); they are sealed later by tertiary dentin.
4. **Reactive sclerosis** — mild irritation fills tubules with mineral (Ritter et al, 2019).
5. **Reactionary dentin** — surviving odontoblasts beneath the communicating tubules increase secretion; maximal at RDT 0.25-0.50 mm (Smith, 2012).
6. **Reparative dentin** — after odontoblast death, new odontoblast-like cells form irregular dentin; seldom before 30 days; more after a greater initial injury (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019).
7. **Fibrodentin / interface dentin** — an irregular, often atubular layer between physiological secondary and tertiary dentin; tubules of primary dentin do not cross it, so it makes the affected dentin almost impermeable and protects the pulp (Sikri, 2020).
   - After resolution the odontoblast layer may remain atrophic or degenerated, or be replaced by regenerated odontoblasts (Sikri, 2020) — the visible record of past operative injury.
8. **Mechanism of repair** — cutting, etching and materials release TGF-β and other growth factors fossilized in dentin matrix, signalling odontoblasts (Smith, 2012). Dentin is not inert.
9. **Iatrogenic damage** — charred or crazed dentin from heat or vibration, later sequestrated (Marzouk et al, 1985).

## 7. Response to restorative materials and procedures

### Explanation

Bacterial leakage along the cavity-restoration gap is more toxic than the material itself (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; material reactions are mild and transitory, and favourable responses occur under many materials when bacteria are excluded; the pulp tolerates low pH if bacteria and resin components are kept out (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC]. Etching for 10-15 s has minimal pulpal effect (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; EDTA most effectively releases growth factors (Smith, 2012) [SRC]. ZOE: bland, even therapeutic on dentin; calcium in tubules chelates eugenol; on pulp tissue it causes chronic inflammation and necrosis; dose dependent (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; ZOE appeared to have no effect on reactionary dentinogenesis (Smith, 2012) [SRC]. Zinc phosphate: irritating from low pH and rapid penetration of phosphoric acid; neutral only at 48 h; luting mixes start at pH 2.0-3.3; hydraulic seating forces push acid into tubules; at 1.5 mm RDT only a moderate reaction (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Composite: monomer leaches even after curing, managed by RDT (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. GIC and its modified forms are biocompatible (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; RMGI bonds predictably, seals and is pulp-compatible (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Amalgam transmits thermal energy (Marzouk et al, 1985) [SRC]; excessive condensation at limited effective depth disrupts the odontoblasts (Marzouk et al, 1985) [SRC]. Calcium hydroxide stimulates reparative dentin and has largely been replaced by MTA and Biodentine; tricalcium silicate pulp therapy succeeds in over 90% (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Thermal sensitivity: pain from raised intrapulpal pressure in inflammation; postoperative sensitivity is overestimated and almost always disappears within 30 days; the thermal-shock theory calls for insulation (composite needs none; base ≤0.75 mm, since thicker bases weaken amalgam); the hydrodynamic theory — cold contracts fluid in the restoration gap — calls for sealing tubules, which has shifted practice from insulation to sealing (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**General principle:** pulpal damage under restorations comes mainly from bacteria leaking along the restoration-tooth gap, not from the chemistry of the material (Sikri, 2020; Hilton and Summitt, 2013). When bacteria are excluded, favourable pulpal responses are found under amalgam, composite, zinc phosphate, GIC and acrylic resin, and the pulp tolerates low pH from etchants (Hilton and Summitt, 2013). Material reactions are mild and transitory.

**Material-by-material response**
| Material | Pulpal effect | Reason / precaution |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc oxide-eugenol | Bland, sedative on dentin; chronic inflammation and necrosis on exposed pulp; dose dependent (Sikri, 2020) | Eugenol chelated by tubular calcium; inhibits prostaglandins and nerve activity at low dose; inhibits resin polymerization — no reactionary dentin stimulation (Smith, 2012) |
| Zinc phosphate | Moderate to severe reaction in deep cavities in the first days, resolving in 5-8 weeks; worse as luting agent (pH 2.0-3.3) (Sikri, 2020) | Low pH, small phosphoric acid molecules, hydraulic seating forces; pH neutral only at 48 h; use a sealer or liner first |
| Acid etchants | Minimal when applied 10-15 s (Sikri, 2020) | Increase permeability; release dentin growth factors (EDTA most) (Smith, 2012) |
| Composite resin | Mild; monomer leaches even after curing (Sikri, 2020) | Bacterial leakage from shrinkage gaps is the real threat; protect deep dentin |
| GIC / RMGI | Biocompatible (Sikri, 2020); RMGI seals and bonds predictably (Ritter et al, 2019) | Ideal liner/base under amalgam and composite |
| Amalgam | Transmits heat; condensation pressure in shallow effective depth disrupts odontoblasts (Marzouk et al, 1985) | Varnish/sealer; base only where RDT is small |
| Calcium hydroxide | Stimulates reparative dentin (Ritter et al, 2019); most effective stimulant of reactionary dentin (Smith, 2012) | Weak, soluble; cover with RMGI |
| MTA / Biodentine | Bioactive; pulp therapy success >90% (Ritter et al, 2019) | Replacing calcium hydroxide for capping |

**Further detail on two classic materials (Sikri, 2020)**
- *Eugenol* is dose dependent: at low concentration it inhibits prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis and blocks intradental nerve activity reversibly, like a local anaesthetic (the basis of ZOE's sedative effect); at high concentration it kills cells and blocks nerves irreversibly. In contact with pulp tissue it causes chronic inflammation and necrosis. Eugenol also inhibits polymerization of bonding agents and composite, so ZOE is not used under resin (Ritter et al, 2019).
- *Zinc phosphate* used as a base over 1.0 mm of dentin lifts the odontoblast layer with countable inflammatory cells; over 1.5 mm the reaction is only moderate; inflammation resolves by 5-8 weeks. Its toxicity alone does not explain pulpal damage — bacterial leakage again matters.
- *Resin adhesives* are tolerated by the pulp only if their components are prevented from traversing the tubules into the pulp (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).

**Vital bleaching:** carbamide peroxide produces only a slight pulpal reaction — less distinct cell-free zone, a few leukocytes, irregular odontoblast layer (Sikri, 2020).

**Procedural steps of restoration**
- **Cementation:** hydraulic pressure during seating forces acid and fluid into tubules (Sikri, 2020).
- **Condensation and finishing of amalgam:** pressure and frictional heat transmitted to the pulp (Marzouk et al, 1985).
- **Polymerization shrinkage** of composites can open gaps for bacteria.

**Postoperative sensitivity — two theories (Hilton and Summitt, 2013)**
1. *Thermal shock* — temperature transmitted through metal: insulate with a base. Composite has such low thermal diffusivity that it needs no insulating base; under amalgam, 0.50-0.75 mm of base suffices and more weakens the amalgam.
2. *Hydrodynamic* (accepted) — cold contracts fluid in the gap under a restoration, suddenly increasing tubular flow: the remedy is to **seal tubules**, not insulate. Practice has shifted from insulation to sealing.
- Most postoperative sensitivity is mild and disappears within 30 days; the problem is overestimated.
- Supporting evidence: SEM shows significantly more open tubule orifices in hypersensitive dentin (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
- Under amalgam a base must also be stiff: the lower its modulus of elasticity, the lower the fracture resistance of the overlying amalgam (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).

## 8. Protecting the pulp-dentin organ during operative procedures

### Explanation

Pulp protection is matched to RDT: ≥2 mm no protection beyond sealer or bonding agent; 0.5-2 mm RMGI base; <0.5 mm calcium hydroxide 0.5-1 mm then RMGI (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Burs are acceptable over 2 mm effective depth with coolant (Marzouk et al, 1985) [SRC]; crowns cut without coolant injure even at 2 mm RDT (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Conservation of tooth structure matters more than replacement with liners (Hilton and Summitt, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Biological considerations during cavity preparation — protective protocol**

*During preparation*
1. Diagnose the pulp first; remember the cumulative burden of caries.
2. Conserve dentin — RDT is the best pulp protector; avoid unnecessary extension (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
3. High speed with light, intermittent pressure; sharp carbide burs for dentin; diamonds and stones mainly in enamel.
4. Copious water spray directed at the bur-tooth contact; never dry dentin with air (Marzouk et al, 1985; Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
5. Concentric, well-balanced instruments to avoid vibration.
6. Hand instruments near the pulp; slow speed (below 3000 rpm in deep dentin without coolant, e.g. pin holes) with minimal pressure (Marzouk et al, 1985).
7. Keep the field clean and moist — rubber dam to exclude saliva and bacteria.
8. Avoid electrosurgery contact with metallic restorations.
9. Minimize time and extent; crown preparations need special care with coolant (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

*During restoration — Sturdevant's RDT protocol (Ritter et al, 2019)*
| RDT | Protection |
|---|---|
| ≥2 mm (shallow) | Sealer or bonding agent only |
| 0.5-2 mm (moderately deep) | Light-cured RMGI base at the deep site |
| <0.5 mm (very deep) | Calcium hydroxide (or calcium silicate) 0.5-1 mm, then RMGI |

*Liner and base materials*
- Varnish or sealer on all walls when effective depth is 2 mm or more (Marzouk et al, 1985); today a dentin sealer or bonding agent (Ritter et al, 2019).
- RMGI as a thin liner (about 0.5 mm) in deep preparations — bonds, seals, pulp-compatible (Ritter et al, 2019).
- Calcium hydroxide only at the deepest point, near or at exposure, covered by RMGI; calcium silicates (MTA, Biodentine) increasingly replace it (Ritter et al, 2019).
- No ZOE under composite (eugenol inhibits polymerization) (Ritter et al, 2019).

*After restoration*
- Seal margins against bacterial leakage — the single greatest threat.
- Bases thin (≤0.75 mm) under amalgam; avoid thick weak bases (Hilton and Summitt, 2013).
- Review sensitivity at 30 days before intervening.

**Conclusion.** The pulp-dentin organ reacts to every operative step as one unit, through its tubules, fluid and odontoblasts. Its response — healthy repair, unhealthy repair or destruction — depends above all on the remaining dentin thickness, on heat, pressure and desiccation during cutting, and on bacterial leakage after restoration. Modern pulp protection therefore rests on three principles: conserve dentin, cut cool and gentle, and seal the dentin rather than merely insulate it.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

- **Claim:** restorative materials are toxic to the pulp. **Textbook:** older view (zinc phosphate acid, composite monomer). **Challenge:** Hilton and Summitt (2013) and Sikri (2020) both state that bacterial leakage, not material chemistry, causes significant pulpal damage, and favourable responses occur under many materials when bacteria are excluded. **Strength:** concordant across two independent textbooks summarising animal and human histology. **Conclusion:** seal first; material toxicity is secondary, though zinc phosphate luting still warrants caution [Certain].
- **Claim:** high-speed cutting harms the pulp more than low speed. **Textbook:** Marzouk (1985) names 3000-30,000 rpm as most deleterious and links heat to rpm. **Refinement:** Hilton and Summitt (2013) show pressure raises temperature more than speed, and low-speed cutting has been shown to be more traumatic. **Conclusion:** high speed with light pressure and water is the least injurious; "speed" alone is the wrong variable [Likely].
- **Claim:** lasers are safe for the pulp. **Support:** minimal pulpal response with water coolant (Hilton and Summitt, 2013). **Challenge:** Sikri (2020) states laser heat is more detrimental. **Conclusion:** safety depends on coolant and parameters; both sources agree heat is the risk [Likely].
- **Claim:** bases are needed to prevent thermal sensitivity. **Challenge:** postoperative sensitivity is overestimated and resolves within 30 days; the hydrodynamic theory makes sealing, not insulation, the remedy (Hilton and Summitt, 2013). **Conclusion:** seal tubules; use thin bases only under metal in deep cavities [Certain].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- P-D organ: one tissue; 30,000-45,000 tubules cut per mm²; fluid 22% of dentin volume; pulpal pressure ~24 mm Hg.
- Marzouk's three reactions: healthy reparative, unhealthy reparative, destructive.
- RDT is the single most important factor; worst at ≤0.25-0.30 mm.
- Heat: 11 °F (≈6 °C) rise destructive; pressure > speed; diamonds > carbides; enamel > dentin.
- Bacteria, not materials, cause most pulpal damage.
- Postoperative sensitivity: hydrodynamic theory → seal, don't just insulate; base ≤0.75 mm.
