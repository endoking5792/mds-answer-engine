## 1. Introduction and composition

### Explanation

Dentin forms the largest portion of the tooth (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. By weight it is 70% mineral, 20% organic and 10% water (Pashley and Tay, 2012; Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; the organic part is about 90% type I collagen and 10% non-collagenous proteins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Because dentin is dense (2.05-2.30 g/cm³), weight percentages are much higher than volume percentages (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC] — the reason textbooks seem to disagree when one quotes weight and another volume. Its crystallites are small, 20-100 nm long and about 3 nm wide, like bone (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Dentin is the mineralized, tubular connective tissue that forms the bulk of the tooth, covered by enamel and cementum and enclosing the pulp; with the pulp it forms the pulp-dentin complex (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Composition (by weight):** 70% inorganic (carbonate-rich, calcium-deficient hydroxyapatite; crystals 20-100 × 3 nm), 20% organic (90% type I collagen, 10% non-collagenous proteins), 10% water (Pashley and Tay, 2012; Ritter et al, 2019). Weight values exceed volume values because dentin is dense.

## 2. Microscopic structure and ultrastructure

### Explanation

Odontoblast processes (Tomes' fibres) extend 100-200 µm into the tubules, which is why dentin is regarded as living (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Predentin is the unmineralized layer next to the odontoblasts (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Tubules run an S-curve in the crown, straighter in cusps and root, and branch near the DEJ; they increase from 15,000-20,000/mm² at the DEJ to 45,000-65,000/mm² at the pulp, and widen from 0.5-0.9 µm to 2-3 µm (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Pashley and Tay give 15,000/mm² as the minimum in peripheral coronal dentin (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Peritubular dentin lines each tubule and is much more mineralized than intertubular dentin (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]: about 95% mineral by volume against about 30%, and almost five times harder (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Mantle dentin, about 150 µm wide, is the first dentin laid down; mantle and interglobular dentin, about 200 µm from the DEJ, are less mineralized and less stiff (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Dentinal tubules**
- Contain odontoblast processes (Tomes' fibres, extending 100-200 µm) and dentinal fluid (Ritter et al, 2019).
- S-shaped course in the crown, straight in cusps and root; branch near the DEJ.
- Density: 15,000-20,000/mm² at DEJ → 45,000-65,000/mm² at pulp; diameter 0.5-0.9 µm → 2-3 µm (Ritter et al, 2019).

**2. Peritubular vs intertubular dentin**
| | Peritubular | Intertubular |
|---|---|---|
| Site | Lines tubule wall | Between tubules, bulk |
| Mineral | ~95% by volume, collagen-poor | ~30% by volume, collagen matrix |
| Hardness | ~5 × intertubular | Lower |
(Pashley and Tay, 2012)

**3. Other zones:** predentin (unmineralized, next to odontoblasts); mantle dentin (outer 150 µm, first formed by newly differentiated odontoblasts); interglobular dentin (hypomineralized, about 200 µm from the DEJ); circumpulpal dentin (bulk) (Pashley and Tay, 2012; Ritter et al, 2019). Mantle and interglobular zones are less mineralized and less stiff than the inner core, so the outer dentin is a resilient cushion under enamel (Pashley and Tay, 2012).

**4. Ultrastructural significance:** because tubules converge towards the pulp, deep dentin has far more tubule lumen and fluid (about 22% water by volume against 1% at the DEJ), while superficial dentin is mainly intertubular collagen matrix — the substrate for hybrid-layer bonding changes with depth (Pashley and Tay, 2012; Ritter et al, 2019).

## 3. Types of dentin

### Explanation

There are three types of dentin: primary, secondary and tertiary (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Secondary dentin continues at a reduced rate without obvious stimuli, and in multirooted teeth is thicker on the roof and floor than the walls of the chamber (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Tertiary dentin is reactionary or reparative (see Q06).

#### ✎ Exam core

**Types:** primary (before root completion; mantle + circumpulpal); secondary (physiological, slower, after root completion; thicker on roof and floor of chamber); tertiary — reactionary (surviving odontoblasts, tubular) and reparative (new odontoblast-like cells, irregular) (Pashley and Tay, 2012; Ritter et al, 2019).

## 4. Properties and permeability

### Explanation

Dentin hardness averages one-fifth that of enamel (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; microhardness drops from 350 kg/mm² in enamel to 50 kg/mm² just beneath the DEJ (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Modulus about 18 GPa; ultimate tensile strength about 98 MPa against 10 MPa for enamel — dentin's flexibility supports brittle enamel (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Water rises from about 1% by volume near the DEJ to 22% near the pulp, part of the difficulty in bonding to deep dentin (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Dentin is both barrier and permeable structure, depending on thickness and age; measured permeability is less than 3% of the theoretical value because tubules contain intratubular material (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Coronal dentin is much more permeable than root dentin (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Fluid movement in tubules is the basis of the hydrodynamic theory of sensitivity (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Properties:** hardness about one-fifth of enamel; microhardness falls from 350 kg/mm² in enamel to 50 kg/mm² just beneath the DEJ (Pashley and Tay, 2012); modulus ~18 GPa; tensile strength ~98 MPa vs ~10 MPa for enamel — resilient, supports enamel (Ritter et al, 2019). Water 1% (DEJ) to 22% (pulp) by volume, which makes deep dentin difficult to bond (Pashley and Tay, 2012).

**Permeability:** depends on RDT and tubule diameter; deep and coronal dentin most permeable, root dentin much less (Ritter et al, 2019); actual permeability <3% of theoretical because tubules contain processes, collagen and mineral (Pashley and Tay, 2012). Dentin is both a barrier and a permeable structure depending on thickness and age (Pashley and Tay, 2012). Fluid movement in tubules on cutting, drying or cold explains pain by the hydrodynamic theory (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 5. Changes with age

### Explanation

Dentin becomes harder with age because mineral content increases (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; the peritubular lining gradually widens (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Physiologic sclerosis (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] begins in the third decade in the apical root and advances coronally (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Older dentin is darker (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Continued secondary dentin narrows the pulp space (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Age changes**
1. Continued secondary dentin → smaller pulp chamber, recessed pulp horns (Ritter et al, 2019).
2. Peritubular dentin widens → physiologic sclerosis, starting apically in the third decade and moving coronally (Pashley and Tay, 2012; Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
3. Harder, less permeable, less sensitive; darker colour (Ritter et al, 2019).
4. Clinical: reduced sensitivity to cutting and to hypersensitivity stimuli, poorer bonding to sclerotic dentin, smaller and more apical pulp chamber as an access target, and darker teeth that affect shade selection.

## 6. Changes due to pathology

### Explanation

Under mild irritation (slow caries, attrition) peritubular dentin fills the tubules from the DEJ pulpally — reactive sclerosis — producing harder, denser, less sensitive dentin, sometimes radiopaque (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Intraluminal deposits make sclerotic dentin almost impermeable, slowing caries and protecting the pulp (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Tubule blockage and tertiary dentin are the main defences (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Dead tracts are areas of degenerated processes after caries, attrition, erosion or cavity preparation, black in transmitted and white in reflected light (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Carious dentin was divided by Arnold et al into six zones and by Marshall et al into caries-infected and caries-affected dentin (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Summitt's calls the soft, wet outer zone "infected dentin", histologically the zone of destruction, and notes sealed lesions may arrest (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pathological changes**
1. **Reactive (transparent) sclerosis** — slow caries, attrition, abrasion: tubules filled with mineral, almost impermeable, slows caries (Ritter et al, 2019; Pashley and Tay, 2012).
2. **Dead tracts** — degenerated processes after caries, attrition, erosion or cavity cutting; black in transmitted light (Sikri, 2020).
3. **Tertiary dentin** — reactionary or reparative beneath the injury.
4. **Caries:** infected dentin (soft, bacteria, collagen destroyed — remove) over affected dentin (demineralized, collagen intact — can remineralize) (Sikri, 2020; Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
5. **Operative injury:** cut tubules open to the pulp, smear layer, odontoblast aspiration and heat damage; tubule blockage and tertiary dentin are the dentin's two defences (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) (Q04).
6. **Arrest:** even infected lesions may arrest when a bonded restoration seals and entombs the bacteria (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

**Clinical significance:** remove infected, preserve affected dentin; deep dentin needs protection and is harder to bond; sclerotic dentin needs roughening before bonding; dentin distinguished from enamel by colour, dull surface and explorer yield (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Conclusion.** Dentin is a living, permeable tissue whose tubules record age and injury; its structure decides bonding, protection and caries removal.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

- **Claim:** dentin water is "10%". **Textbook:** 70/20/10 by weight (Pashley and Tay, 2012; Ritter et al, 2019). **Refinement:** Pashley and Tay show water varies 20-fold, 1% by volume at the DEJ to 22% near the pulp, and that the textbook figure is an average. **Strength:** direct calculation from tubule density data. **Conclusion:** quote composition as an average and expect deep dentin to behave wetter in bonding [Certain].
- **Claim:** sclerotic dentin is protective. **Support:** almost impermeable, slows caries (Pashley and Tay, 2012). **Challenge:** the same mineral occlusion lowers bond strength [RECALL]. **Conclusion:** protective biologically, a harder bonding substrate [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- 70/20/10 by weight; collagen 90% type I.
- Tubules 15-20k/mm² (DEJ) → 45-65k/mm² (pulp); 0.5-0.9 → 2-3 µm.
- Peritubular ~95% mineral by volume, 5 × harder; intertubular ~30%.
- Mantle dentin 150 µm; interglobular ~200 µm from DEJ.
- Sclerosis starts apically in the third decade.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
1. Longitudinal section: enamel, mantle, interglobular, circumpulpal dentin, predentin, odontoblasts, S-shaped tubules.
2. Cross-section of tubule: lumen with process, peritubular collar, intertubular matrix.
3. Carious dentin: infected over affected, sclerotic zone, reparative dentin.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Mixing weight and volume percentages.
- Saying peritubular dentin forms around the pulp (it lines tubules).
- Describing dead tracts as sclerotic (they are empty, not filled).
