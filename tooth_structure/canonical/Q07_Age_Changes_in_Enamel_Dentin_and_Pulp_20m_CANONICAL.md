## 1. Introduction

### Explanation

Aging changes are gradual and physiological, and must be separated from changes caused by disease or injury, which they resemble (sclerosis, calcification, tertiary dentin). Enamel cannot repair itself because the ameloblasts are lost (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], so its age changes are wear and ion exchange; dentin and pulp change actively because the odontoblasts stay alive.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** With age the tooth changes in every tissue: enamel by wear and ion exchange (it has no cells to repair it), dentin and pulp by continued odontoblast activity and regressive change (Ritter et al, 2019; Fristad and Berggreen, 2021). These changes must be distinguished from pathological ones, which look similar.

## 2. Age changes in enamel

### Explanation

Enamel permeability falls with age through changes in the enamel matrix — "enamel maturation" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; acid solubility, pore volume, water content and permeability are reduced, although a basic permeability remains, and hue intensifies (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Yellowing of older teeth is attributed to thinning or increased translucency of enamel, trace-element accumulation and perhaps sclerosis of underlying dentin, and can be bleached (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. The dimpled surface wears smooth (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; physiological wear is 15-29 µm a year; craze lines come from occlusal loading and age-related loss of resiliency (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Age changes in enamel**
1. **Attrition/wear:** physiological contact wear of 15-29 µm a year; cusps flatten into facets, perikymata and surface dimples wear smooth (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019).
2. **Enamel maturation:** ion uptake from saliva (fluoride, trace elements) and matrix changes reduce permeability, pore volume, water content and acid solubility — older enamel is more caries-resistant, though a basic permeability remains (Ritter et al, 2019; Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
3. **Colour:** hue intensifies; teeth yellow because enamel thins and becomes more translucent, trace elements accumulate and the dentin beneath scleroses (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
4. **Craze lines:** from occlusal loading and loss of resiliency with age (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
5. **No repair:** lost enamel is never replaced (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 3. Age changes in dentin

### Explanation

Dentin becomes harder with age because mineral content rises (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; peritubular dentin widens (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Cohen's lists the main changes as more peritubular dentin, dentinal sclerosis and more dead tracts; sclerosis narrows tubules and lowers permeability, and makes dentin almost impermeable to bacterial invasion (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Tubular sclerosis begins in the third decade in the apical root and advances coronally (Pashley and Tay, 2012) [SRC]. Older dentin is darker (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Secondary dentin continues throughout life, reducing the pulp chamber and canals (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC], thicker on the roof and floor of the chamber than on the walls (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Age changes in dentin**
1. **Secondary dentin** forms throughout life, thickest on the roof and floor of the pulp chamber (Ritter et al, 2019; Fristad and Berggreen, 2021).
2. **Peritubular dentin widens → physiological sclerosis:** begins apically in the third decade and moves coronally; tubules narrow and close (Pashley and Tay, 2012; Fristad and Berggreen, 2021).
3. **Dead tracts increase** (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021).
4. **Permeability falls**; dentin becomes almost impermeable to bacteria (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021).
5. **Hardness rises** with mineral content; colour darkens (Ritter et al, 2019).
6. **Dead tracts** are tubules with degenerated odontoblast processes; they appear black in transmitted and white in reflected light, and the surviving pulp cells try to seal them off with reparative dentin (Sikri, 2020).
7. **Physiological vs reactive change:** age sclerosis is generalized and begins apically; reactive sclerosis is localized beneath caries, attrition or a restoration (Ritter et al, 2019).

## 4. Age changes in the pulp

### Explanation

Secondary dentin reduces the pulp chamber and canals, while the cementodentinal junction width stays the same; cementum apposition increases the distance from the apical constriction to the radiographic apex (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Regressive changes: fewer cells and more and thicker collagen fibres, especially radicular; thick fibres act as foci for calcification; odontoblasts become fewer and smaller and may disappear, particularly over the furcation; nerves and vessels decrease; tissue becomes more resistant to proteolytic enzymes, hyaluronidase and sialidase (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Pulp calcifications occur in at least 50% of teeth — concentric stones coronally, diffuse calcification radicularly; two histological types (laminated smooth, unlaminated rough); it is not clear whether they are pathological or natural; they may hinder root canal treatment (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Laminated stones grow by adding collagen fibrils; unlaminated stones mineralize preformed collagen bundles; the mineral is carbonated hydroxyapatite; size ranges from particles beside arterioles to masses filling the chamber (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]. Dead tracts look black in transmitted light (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The pulp cavity usually decreases with age, visible on radiographs (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Age changes in the pulp (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021)**
| Feature | Change with age |
|---|---|
| Size | Chamber and canals narrow (secondary dentin); pulp horns recede |
| Cells | Decreased cellularity; odontoblasts fewer and smaller, absent over furcations |
| Fibres | More and thicker collagen, especially radicular (fibrosis) |
| Vessels and nerves | Progressive reduction |
| Ground substance | Altered collagen and proteoglycans; more resistant to enzymes |
| Calcification | In ≥50% of teeth: concentric stones coronally, diffuse radicularly; laminated or unlaminated |
| Apex | Cementum apposition increases constriction-to-radiographic-apex distance |

**Pulp stones (denticles):** two histological types — round or ovoid, smooth and concentrically laminated (grow by adding collagen fibrils to the surface), and irregular, unlaminated, rough (mineralization of preformed, often hyalinized collagen bundles). Their mineral is carbonated hydroxyapatite. They range from microscopic particles beside arterioles to masses filling the chamber. Whether they are pathological or a natural phenomenon is unclear (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021).

**Net effect:** the aged pulp is more fibrous, less cellular and less vascular, with a smaller volume inside thicker, less permeable dentin.

## 5. Implications

### Explanation

These changes alter diagnosis, operative care and endodontics. Calcifications may hinder root canal treatment (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]; the electric pulp test is unreliable in young teeth (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021) [SRC]; radiographs show the size of the pulp cavity (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The remaining implications follow directly from the changes above [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Implications**
*Diagnosis*
- Reduced nerves and receded pulp mean weaker, slower response to sensibility tests; older patients feel less during cavity preparation and caries may progress with fewer symptoms.
- Radiographs are essential to judge the size of the pulp cavity (Ritter et al, 2019).

*Restorative*
- Sclerotic, hypermineralized dentin bonds less predictably — roughen the surface and use enamel margins where possible.
- Less permeable dentin protects the pulp and gives less postoperative sensitivity.
- Yellowed teeth: bleaching first; shade selection must allow for darker dentin (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
- Worn, crazed enamel: check occlusion and cracks before large restorations.

*Endodontic*
- Narrow chambers and calcified canals make access and negotiation difficult; pulp stones may block orifices (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021).
- Pulp floor odontoblasts disappear over furcations, and the root apex changes with cementum apposition — working length must account for the increased constriction-to-apex distance (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021).
- Reduced vascularity and cellularity lower the healing capacity, so vital pulp therapy is less predictable in old teeth [Likely].

**Conclusion.** Aging makes the tooth harder, less permeable and less sensitive, but smaller in pulp and slower to heal; treatment planning must allow for both.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

- **Claim:** pulp calcifications are an age change. **Textbook:** they occur in at least 50% of teeth and thick old collagen fibres act as foci (Fristad and Berggreen, 2021). **Challenge:** the same chapter states there is no clear evidence whether calcification is pathological or natural, and laminated stones occur in newly erupted teeth. **Conclusion:** present them as age-associated, not purely age-caused [Certain].
- **Claim:** older dentin is "better". **Support:** harder and almost impermeable to bacteria (Ritter et al, 2019; Fristad and Berggreen, 2021). **Challenge:** the same mineral occlusion makes adhesion less predictable [RECALL]. **Conclusion:** protective biologically, difficult as a bonding substrate [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Enamel: wear 15-29 µm/year; maturation lowers permeability and solubility; yellowing.
- Dentin: secondary dentin, peritubular widening, sclerosis from apex in third decade, dead tracts.
- Pulp: fewer cells, more collagen, fewer nerves and vessels, calcification in ≥50%.
- Implications: sensibility testing, bonding, access, working length.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
1. Young vs old molar: large chamber and high horns vs narrowed chamber, recessed horns, pulp stone.
2. Root apex: cementum apposition increasing the distance from constriction to radiographic apex.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling all sclerosis "age change" (reactive sclerosis follows irritation).
- Saying enamel becomes thicker with age.
- Stating pulp stones are always pathological.

## MNEMONIC
**"Small, Stiff, Stony, Silent"** — small pulp, stiff (sclerotic) dentin, stones, silent (fewer nerves).
