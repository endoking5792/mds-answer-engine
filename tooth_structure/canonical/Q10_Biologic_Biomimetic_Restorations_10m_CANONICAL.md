## Explanation

**Concept.** Natural tooth structure withstands cyclic masticatory loading because it can flex slightly without fracture or separation of enamel from dentin; ideal restorative materials would mimic that durability, but all current materials fall short; polycrystalline materials (amalgam, glass-ceramic) can hardly flex without fracturing (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Dentin's modulus is about 18 GPa, and the DEJ is a 2 µm interwoven junction that stops cracks (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. A "biologic" or biomimetic restoration therefore aims to reproduce this enamel-DEJ-dentin unit, preserve living tissue and support the pulp's own repair [Likely].

**Principles from the sources.** Preserve natural tooth structure and rely on long-term adhesion rather than mechanical interlocks; affected dentin need not all be removed from the axial wall (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]; caries-affected dentin can remineralize, infected dentin cannot (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Replace dentin with dentinlike and enamel with enamel-like materials; composite kits supply dentinlike materials that imitate dentin's optical properties (Vargas et al, 2013) [SRC]. Dietschi's natural layering concept uses only two basic layers, dentin and enamel, with enamel types for young, adult and old teeth (Paravina, 2013) [SRC]. Translucency is needed to mimic enamel (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; replacements should contain fluorescing agents (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Bonding to the ends of enamel rods is twice as strong as to their sides (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

**Bioactive dentin replacement and stable bonds.** Biodentine simulates dentin's physical properties and induces odontoblast differentiation, but lacks long-term evaluation (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; RMGI bonds and seals predictably and is pulp-compatible; MTA and Biodentine have largely replaced calcium hydroxide (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Biomimetic remineralization uses phosphoprotein analogues (polyacrylic, polyvinylphosphonic acids) to induce intrafibrillar mineral in hybrid layers (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; hydroxyapatite produces a biomimetic coating (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Chlorhexidine preserves aged hybrid layers (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; cross-linking agents protect collagen (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** A biologic (biomimetic) restoration repairs a tooth so that it behaves like natural tooth: it preserves living tissue, bonds to it, and replaces enamel and dentin with materials that imitate their structure, mechanics, optics and biology. (No standard textbook definition; synthesis from Ritter et al, 2019 and Mount et al, 2016.)

**Rationale.** Natural teeth survive chewing because enamel and dentin flex slightly without separating at the DEJ. Ideal materials would mimic this durability, but all current materials fall short, and rigid polycrystalline materials such as amalgam and glass-ceramic fracture when they flex (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Principles**
1. **Biological conservation:** retain all possible tooth structure; adhesion replaces mechanical retention; leave caries-affected dentin, remove only infected dentin (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
2. **Pulp-dentin protection:** seal tubules; bioactive liners — Biodentine (dentin-like, induces odontoblast differentiation), MTA, RMGI (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019).
3. **Structural mimicry:** replace dentin with dentin-like, resilient material and enamel with enamel-like material; bond enamel at rod ends (bevel) (Vargas et al, 2013; Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
4. **Optical mimicry:** Dietschi's natural layering — dentin layer plus enamel layer chosen for young, adult or old enamel; translucency, opalescence, fluorescence (Paravina, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
5. **Durable bond:** chlorhexidine or cross-linkers protect hybrid-layer collagen (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
6. **Biomimetic remineralization:** phosphoprotein analogues guide intrafibrillar mineral; nano-hydroxyapatite coatings (Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).

**Clinical examples:** layered composite over a Biodentine or RMGI dentin replacement; bonded ceramic onlays instead of full crowns; sealed indirect pulp capping with selective caries removal.

**Conventional vs biologic approach**
| Feature | Conventional | Biologic / biomimetic |
|---|---|---|
| Retention | Mechanical undercuts, pins | Adhesion to enamel and dentin |
| Caries removal | All discoloured dentin | Infected dentin only; affected dentin kept |
| Dentin replacement | Rigid base or bulk metal | Dentin-like, bioactive layer |
| Pulp | Insulated | Sealed and stimulated to repair |
| Coverage | Full crown | Partial, bonded restorations |
(Mount et al, 2016; Ritter et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020)

**Limitation:** long-term evidence for bioactive dentin substitutes is not documented (Sikri, 2020), and no current material fully reproduces natural tooth (Ritter et al, 2019).

**Conclusion.** A biologic restoration saves, seals, stimulates and simulates — it treats the tooth as a living pulp-dentin organ, not a cavity to be filled.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Replace like with like: dentin-like for dentin, enamel-like for enamel.
- Preserve tissue, bond, seal, stimulate repair.
- Biodentine = dentin substitute; long-term data lacking.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
1. Layered restoration: bioactive liner → dentin-like layer → enamel-like layer, with bevelled enamel margin.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing only about esthetics; the biological (pulp-preserving) half carries marks.
- Presenting biomimetic materials as proven long-term.
