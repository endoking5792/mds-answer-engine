## 1. Remineralization in enamel

### Explanation

Enamel is semipermeable, so demineralization, remineralization and fluoride uptake work in three dimensions, not only at the surface (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Fluoride converts hydroxyapatite into more stable, less acid-soluble fluorohydroxyapatite or fluoroapatite; repeated cycles with plaque control and fluoride yield more caries-resistant enamel; newer systems add calcium and phosphate, for example amorphous calcium phosphate complexes (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Fluoride lowers acid solubility and enhances remineralization (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Nano-hydroxyapatite is regarded as highly biocompatible and bioactive (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]. Of 182 white spot lesions followed from age 8 to 15, 9% cavitated and 51% appeared sound; sealing an initial lesion with resin arrests it (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Remineralization is the gain of calcium and phosphate into a partly demineralized tissue from saliva or applied agents. In enamel it rebuilds crystals on a surviving mineral framework; in dentin it must remineralize an organic collagen matrix — the root of every difference between them.

**Enamel remineralization**
1. **Substrate:** ~96% mineral; residual crystals in the subsurface lesion act as nuclei; there is no organic scaffold to rebuild.
2. **Mechanism:** ions diffuse in three dimensions through the semipermeable enamel; fluoride forms fluorohydroxyapatite/fluoroapatite, which is less soluble than the original carbonated apatite (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
3. **Result:** the remineralized lesion is more caries-resistant than sound enamel (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
4. **Agents:** fluoride (varnish, toothpaste, water), CPP-ACP and amorphous calcium phosphate, nano-hydroxyapatite (Mount et al, 2016), resin infiltration and sealing.
5. **Clinical evidence:** most white spot lesions do not cavitate — 51% appeared sound and 9% cavitated after 7 years (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
6. **Chemistry:** acid attacks the carbonated apatite first; fluoride-rich crystals that re-form are less soluble, so a remineralized lesion may be harder to demineralize again than the original enamel (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

## 2. Remineralization in dentin

### Explanation

Dentin demineralizes at a higher pH — 6.2-6.7 against about 5.5 for enamel — so root caries progresses about twice as fast as coronal caries (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Demineralized root dentin exposes its collagen matrix, which keeps its structure while hydrated and can be remineralized by salivary calcium, phosphate and fluoride; even advanced root lesions can re-harden (Mount et al, 2016) [SRC]. Natural dentin mineralization depends on non-collagenous nucleators, chiefly dentin phosphoprotein; polyacrylic, polycarboxylic and polyvinylphosphonic acids used as analogues have induced intrafibrillar mineralization within hybrid layers (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Caries-infected dentin cannot be remineralized; caries-affected dentin can (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; RDT of 0.25-0.5 mm gives maximal remineralization of demineralized dentin (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Dentin remineralization**
1. **Substrate:** ~70% mineral by weight within a type I collagen scaffold; mineral must re-enter both between (extrafibrillar) and inside (intrafibrillar) the collagen fibrils.
2. **Critical pH 6.2-6.7** (enamel 5.5): dentin demineralizes in weak acids; root caries runs about twice as fast (Ritter et al, 2019).
3. **Collagen must survive:** demineralized root dentin keeps its collagen while hydrated and can re-harden with saliva, fluoride and remineralizing solutions (Mount et al, 2016); caries-infected dentin, with destroyed collagen, cannot remineralize, but caries-affected dentin can (Sikri, 2020).
4. **Nucleation:** native dentin uses dentin phosphoprotein to nucleate mineral; biomimetic remineralization uses polyacrylic or polyvinylphosphonic acid analogues to guide intrafibrillar mineral in hybrid layers (Ritter et al, 2019).
5. **Pulpal contribution:** vital odontoblasts help — maximal remineralization of demineralized dentin occurs at RDT 0.25-0.5 mm (Sikri, 2020).
6. **Natural reprecipitation:** even before cavitation, odontoblasts form a hypermineralized zone beneath an enamel lesion; during dentin caries, dissolved mineral reprecipitates deeper to augment sclerosis of subcarious dentin (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) — a physiological remineralization that enamel cannot produce.

## 3. Comparison

### Explanation

The table brings together the points above; the "outcome" row is an inference from the sources cited [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Enamel vs dentin remineralization**
| Feature | Enamel | Dentin |
|---|---|---|
| Mineral | ~96% by weight; no collagen | ~70% by weight in collagen scaffold |
| Critical pH | ~5.5 | 6.2-6.7 |
| Template | Residual crystals | Collagen + phosphoprotein nucleators |
| Site of mineral | Between crystals | Extra- and intrafibrillar |
| Cellular help | None (acellular) | Odontoblasts, tubular fluid, tertiary dentin |
| Fluoride effect | Strong — fluorapatite, more resistant | Weaker; mainly surface re-hardening |
| Limit | Cavitation | Collagen destruction (infected dentin) |
| Clinical agents | Fluoride, CPP-ACP, nano-HA, infiltration | Fluoride, Ca/P, biomimetic analogues, calcium silicates |
(Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019; Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020)

**Similarities:** both follow the demineralization-remineralization balance; both need plaque control and reduced sugar frequency; both arrest when sealed.

## 4. Contemporary dentin replacement and repair materials

### Explanation

Where dentin is lost a restorative material acts as a dentin substitute supporting the restoration (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. GIC is called "man made dentin" and "dentin substitute" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; RMGI bonds predictably to dentin, seals well and is pulp-compatible (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Calcium hydroxide has largely been replaced by bioactive cements such as MTA and Biodentine (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; with tricalcium silicates, vital pulp therapy success exceeds 90% (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Biodentine (Septodont, 2010) is a bioactive, modified calcium silicate cement that simulates dentin, used mainly as a dentin substitute for coronal and radicular dentin, and induces secondary dentin by stimulating odontoblast differentiation; long-term evaluation is not documented (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. TheraCal LC releases more calcium and is less soluble than MTA or Dycal (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Flowable bulk-fill composites (SDR) are used as a dentin-replacement base covered by conventional composite (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Bioactive glass raises pH by ion leaching (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; NovaMin forms a hydroxy-carbonate apatite layer (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**
1. **Glass-ionomer family (chemical bond, fluoride):** conventional GIC — "man-made dentin" (Sikri, 2020); RMGI — predictable bond, excellent seal, pulp-compatible (Ritter et al, 2019); used in sandwich technique.
2. **Calcium silicate (bioactive) cements:** MTA; Biodentine — dentin-like physical properties, dentin substitute for coronal and radicular dentin, induces odontoblast differentiation, but long-term data lacking (Sikri, 2020); TheraCal LC (light-cured resin-modified calcium silicate) — high calcium release, low solubility (Sikri, 2020). These have largely replaced calcium hydroxide; pulp-capping success >90% (Ritter et al, 2019).
3. **Resin-based:** flowable bulk-fill (SDR) as dentin-replacement base, capped with conventional composite (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019); full-body bulk-fill replaces dentin and enamel together.
4. **Bioactive glass / calcium phosphate:** raise pH, release Ca and P, form hydroxy-carbonate apatite (Sikri, 2020; Ritter et al, 2019).

**Material notes**
- *GIC/RMGI:* greatest advantages are adhesion to enamel and dentin and fluoride release (Sikri, 2020); RMGI is used as a thin liner (about 0.5 mm) over dentin in deep preparations (Ritter et al, 2019).
- *Biodentine:* strong antibacterial action over deep carious dentin; also used for retrograde filling, resorption repair, apexification and pulp capping (Sikri, 2020).
- *Full-body bulk-fill composites* can replace dentin and enamel in a single increment (Ritter et al, 2019), but they are structural, not bioactive, replacements.

**Ideal requirements of a dentin replacement** (comparison with natural dentin): dentin-like modulus and strength; bond to dentin; seal against bacteria; bioactivity — release Ca/OH, stimulate tertiary dentin; low solubility; radiopacity; easy handling.

**Clinical choice**
| Situation | Material |
|---|---|
| Deep cavity, no exposure | RMGI or Biodentine base; selective caries removal |
| Pulp exposure / pulpotomy | MTA, Biodentine |
| Large posterior composite | Bulk-fill or RMGI/Biodentine dentin replacement + composite enamel layer |
| Root caries, high risk | GIC/RMGI |

**Conclusion.** Enamel remineralizes chemically on residual crystals; dentin needs an intact collagen scaffold, nucleators and a living pulp. Contemporary dentin replacements aim to be bioactive as well as structural — calcium silicates and glass ionomers lead, with long-term clinical data still limited.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

- **Claim:** Biodentine is a "dentin substitute". **Textbook:** bioactive calcium silicate simulating dentin, inducing odontoblast differentiation (Sikri, 2020). **Challenge:** the same book notes long-term evaluation is not documented. **Strength:** textbook summary of short-term studies. **Conclusion:** use it confidently for pulp protection and as a base, but present "dentin substitute" as a property claim with limited long-term evidence [Likely].
- **Claim:** dentin can be remineralized like enamel. **Support:** root dentin re-hardens with fluoride and Ca/P (Mount et al, 2016). **Refinement:** true intrafibrillar remineralization needs nucleator analogues and remains largely experimental (Ritter et al, 2019). **Conclusion:** surface re-hardening is clinical reality; functional dentin regeneration is not yet [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Critical pH: enamel 5.5, dentin 6.2-6.7.
- Enamel: residual crystals + fluoride → fluorapatite (more resistant).
- Dentin: collagen scaffold + DPP nucleation; infected dentin cannot remineralize.
- Biodentine (2010): dentin substitute, bioactive; long-term data lacking.
- SDR = flowable bulk-fill dentin replacement under composite.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
1. Enamel subsurface lesion with crystals regrowing; dentin with collagen fibrils showing extra- vs intrafibrillar mineral.
2. Layered restoration: RMGI/Biodentine dentin replacement + composite enamel layer.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Treating dentin remineralization as identical to enamel.
- Forgetting the collagen requirement (infected vs affected dentin).
- Calling calcium hydroxide a dentin replacement (it is a liner).
