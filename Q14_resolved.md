## STUDY MODE

### S1. Idea and rationale
The sandwich technique places GIC as the dentine replacement and composite as the enamel replacement, so each material is used where it is best (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount et al, 2016). Composite shrinks and its dentine bond is hard to keep, while GIC bonds to dentine by ion exchange and to composite after cut-back and etching (Mount, 1994). McCabe and Walls (2008) add that a freshly placed lining can be made receptive to composite simply by washing its surface, and that acid etching is needed only if the restoration has been in place for some time and has matured.

### S2. Two kinds
Open sandwich: GIC forms part of the axial wall (the proximal box) and is exposed to the mouth. Closed sandwich: composite covers the GIC completely (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Summitt describes the open type as another strategy for Class II and V restorations extending below the cemento-enamel junction, with the fluoride-releasing material placed along the gingival margin, cured, and covered by composite; Summitt reports success for resin-modified GIC and says conventional glass ionomers are less successful in the open sandwich technique (Hilton et al, 2013). Summitt (11_11) keeps the resin-modified GIC increment apical to the proximal contact because it wears less well than composite (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S3. Cautions
Views on the sandwich technique differ. Pickard says closed sandwich restorations are more prone to long-term failure at interfaces (Banerjee and Watson, 2011), and limits the laminate to a composite veneer over the exposed occlusal surface of large GIC or resin-modified GIC placed at least 6 months earlier, to allow full maturation (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Sikri describes the sandwich (laminate) technique without that restriction, names the GIC itself as the weak link (failure is generally cohesive, so use adequate thickness) and prefers resin-modified GIC because it attaches chemically to the composite (Sikri, 2020). Sturdevant 5th edition (Roberson et al, 2006) does not promote routine RMGI liners beneath composite, but calls a liner under the root-surface part of a restoration a valid option (Roberson et al, 2006). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia edition, adapted from the 7th edition (Ritter et al, 2019), says something different: for composite in any preparation deeper than a minimal one, a thin RMGI liner is placed in the deepest portion (Ritter et al, 2019). The same edition calls the suggested advantages of RMGI under composite "controversial" because no longitudinal clinical trials are available, says the claim that an RMGI liner reduces shrinkage stress is not supported by current clinical evidence, and says a glass ionomer base under posterior composite does not improve restoration survival at 18 years (Ritter et al, 2019). Goldstein et al (2018) attribute the sandwich technique to McLean and others and say newer glass ionomer liners are highly suited under almost any restorative material, and recommend them over deep dentine before adhesive procedures (Goldstein et al, 2018). Nakabayashi and Pashley (1998) state that glass ionomer under resin composite provides the same stress-breaking, low-modulus function as the intermediate liners that some manufacturers recommend over hybrid layers (Nakabayashi and Pashley, 1998).

Etching set GIC under composite: the books differ. Timing before the composite (Mount, 1994): auto-cure Type II.1 sealed at 4 min and left 20 min; Type II.2 or III composite at 6 min. Etching: Mount et al (2016): etch auto-cure GIC and enamel for 15 s once the cement is set; resin-modified GIC need not be etched. Bonsor and Pearson (2013): if etched within 5 minutes the matrix is completely removed within 15 s, and successful etching is possible only after the matrix has matured, that is after 24 hours. McCabe and Walls (2008): etch only if the restoration has been in place for some time. Torres (2020): etching of the GIC must not exceed 20 s (and, elsewhere in the same book, 5 s of phosphoric acid after the initial set). Sikri (2020): 15-20 s after the cement has fully set (a delay of 20 minutes is recommended; 2-5 minutes for a faster-setting cement), and above 30 s the cement degrades. Goldstein et al (2018): it is not necessary to etch glass ionomer, but some clinicians use a 5 s final etch on that portion (Goldstein et al, 2018). These are listed side by side and not reconciled.

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 8 minutes.)*

### 1. Definition
Restoration in which GIC replaces dentine and composite replaces enamel (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Types
- **Open:** GIC exposed at part of the axial wall (proximal box) (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); used where the margin lies on cementum or dentine (Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Closed:** GIC completely covered by composite (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### 3. Rationale
Composite shrinks 1-5%; the dentine bond is hard to maintain; GIC bonds to dentine, releases fluoride, and reduces the composite needed (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

### 4. Technique
1. Condition dentine 10 seconds and place the strongest radiopaque GIC (Mount et al, 2016).
2. After setting, cut back about 2 mm to expose enamel margins (Mount et al, 2016).
3. Etch enamel and auto-cure GIC 15 s once set (Mount et al, 2016); timing differs: only after about 24 h (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013), not over 20 s (Torres, 2020), 15-20 s (Sikri, 2020), optional 5 s (Goldstein et al, 2018). Resin-modified GIC: enamel only (Mount et al, 2016).
4. Apply enamel bond; place composite in increments (Mount et al, 2016).

### 4A. Indications
Subgingival Class II and Class V margins on dentine or cementum, erosive lesions, and deep proximal amalgam restorations with GIC in the gingival half (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013; Mount, 1994). Leave GIC exposed only at a subgingival margin without enamel (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5. Advantages and limits
Fluoride exposure, less shrinkage, tissue-tolerant GIC at the margin (Mount, 1994); closed type may fail at interfaces (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- GIC = dentine replacement; composite = enamel replacement (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Open (GIC exposed) against closed (fully covered) (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- Cover all tubules, at least 1 mm (Mount, 1994).
- Etching set GIC: 15 s (Mount et al, 2016); authors differ (see step 3).
