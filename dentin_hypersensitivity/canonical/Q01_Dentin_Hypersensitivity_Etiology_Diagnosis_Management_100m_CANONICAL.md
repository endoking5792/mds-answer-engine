## 1. Definition and terminology

### Explanation

**The agreed definition.** The international consensus definition (Holland et al, 1997, as quoted by Addy and Gillam) is "short sharp pain arising from exposed dentine in response to stimuli, typically thermal, evaporative, tactile, osmotic or chemical, and which cannot be ascribed to any other form of dental defect or pathology" (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. The Canadian Advisory Board modified the ending to "defect or disease" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; the word "disease" replaced the previously used word "pathology" (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Sikri gives a shorter working definition: an exaggerated response of exposed dentin to external stimuli, regardless of its location (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Al-Sabbagh's group quotes the same "exaggerated response to nonnoxious and noxious stimuli" before giving Holland's wording (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009) [SRC].

**What the definition does.** The first half describes the pain; the second half forces a diagnosis of exclusion. Addy and Gillam say it "calls out for a diagnosis of DH by exclusion" (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], and Sikri states that a definitive diagnosis is reached through exclusion of other conditions (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Al-Sabbagh's group goes further and calls DH a symptom rather than a true disease entity (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC].

**Hypersensitivity or sensitivity?** Many authors argue that exposed dentine is merely sensitive, so "dentine sensitivity" is the more accurate term. Addy and Gillam defend "hypersensitivity": sensitive lesions have many more and wider open tubules than non-sensitive dentine, so by Poiseuille's law the exposed dentine is hyper-reactive to appropriate stimuli (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. This is a semantic debate, and both terms appear in exam questions.

**Root sensitivity (RS).** The European Federation of Periodontology recommended the term RS for the same short sharp pain from exposed dentine of periodontally involved teeth or after periodontal treatment (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Sikri also uses the term for sensitivity after recession in periodontal disease and periodontal treatment (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The reasons given for keeping RS separate are that it has a much higher prevalence and that deep bacterial invasion of dentinal tubules has been reported in periodontally involved teeth (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], which has not been shown in true DH.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition (Holland et al, 1997; Canadian Advisory Board, 2003).** Dentin hypersensitivity (DH) is characterised by short, sharp pain arising from exposed dentin in response to stimuli, typically thermal, evaporative, tactile, osmotic or chemical, which cannot be ascribed to any other form of dental defect or disease (Addy and Gillam, 2024; Olley and Bartlett, 2024). Sikri defines it as an exaggerated response of exposed dentin to external stimuli (Sikri, 2020).

**Key points in the definition**
- It describes the pain and then excludes other causes, so DH is a **diagnosis of exclusion** (Addy and Gillam, 2024; Sikri, 2020).
- It is better seen as a symptom than a disease entity (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009).

**Terminology**
- "Dentine sensitivity" is preferred by some because exposed dentin is normally sensitive. "Hypersensitivity" is defended because sensitive lesions have more and wider patent tubules, making the dentin hyper-reactive (Addy and Gillam, 2024).
- **Root sensitivity** (European Federation of Periodontology): the same pain from root dentin exposed by periodontal disease or its treatment; it is more prevalent and may involve bacterial invasion of the tubules (Addy and Gillam, 2024; Sikri, 2020).

| Feature | Dentin hypersensitivity | Root sensitivity |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | Healthy periodontium, good oral hygiene | Periodontal disease or after periodontal therapy |
| Cause of exposure | Tooth wear and "healthy" recession | Attachment loss, scaling, surgery |
| Prevalence | About 15% | 60-90% of periodontal patients |
| Tubules | Open, plaque-free | May be invaded by bacteria |

## 2. Prevalence and distribution

### Explanation

**How common.** Averaging studies that used a dental examination gives about 15% of adults with DH in one or more teeth at any one time, with published extremes from 3% to over 50% (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; Sikri gives 3-67%, with 15% the most consistent figure (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. A review of the burden of DH gave a best estimate of 10%, with an average of 33% across studies (Taha and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Al-Sabbagh's group quotes 8-35% of adults (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC], and Trushkowsky up to 30% at some period of life (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC]. The spread depends on method: questionnaire surveys give around 50%, surveys with clinical examination 1-20%, and periodontal patients 60-90% (Taha and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. In the largest European study, of 3187 adults from general practice (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], 42% had DH on examination with an evaporative stimulus (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], but only 27% reported it over the previous 12 months (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Who and where.** DH is most common between 20 and 40 years (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; Sikri gives 20-50 years with a peak at 30-40 (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Taha and Gillam put the peak in the third and fourth decades (Taha and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Females are reported to be affected more often, but not all data are significant. The order of teeth affected is canines and first premolars, then incisors and second premolars, then molars (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; Sikri says canines and premolars of both arches, buccal-cervically (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Left-sided teeth are affected more than right (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], probably reflecting brushing by right-handed people [Guessing]. The buccal cervical area is by far the commonest site (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], although occlusal surfaces with attrition can also be sensitive (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. The lesions show little or no plaque (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

**Natural history.** DH is often cyclical (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], and its clinical features are transient, so patients may not have it on the day of examination (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Many sufferers do not seek treatment: only one in four used a desensitising toothpaste in one study (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC], and in a 2023 survey 21.2% and 30.1% never or rarely booked a dental appointment after an episode (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prevalence**
- About 15% of adults at any one time; range 3% to over 50% depending on method (Addy and Gillam, 2024; Sikri, 2020). Best estimate in a review: 10% (Taha and Gillam, 2024).
- Questionnaires about 50%; clinical examination 1-20%; periodontal patients 60-90% (Taha and Gillam, 2024).
- European study of 3187 adults: 42% on examination but only 27% self-reported (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

**Distribution**
- Age 20-40 years (peak 30-40); slightly more in females (Addy and Gillam, 2024; Sikri, 2020).
- Teeth: canines and first premolars > incisors and second premolars > molars; left side > right (Addy and Gillam, 2024).
- Site: buccal cervical area; lesions are usually plaque-free (Addy and Gillam, 2024).
- Course: cyclical and transient; most sufferers self-medicate and do not report it (Olley and Bartlett, 2024; Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009).

## 3. Mechanism: theories of dentin sensitivity

### Explanation

**The puzzle.** Enamel is not sensitive to drilling, but a bur crossing the DEJ causes sharp pain at once (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. Yet nerves in dentine are restricted to its inner half (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. Three hypotheses have been proposed to explain this (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

**1. Direct neural stimulation.** Nerve endings would reach the outer dentine and be stimulated directly. Microscopy has not found nerves in the sensitive outer dentine, and Sikri states that this theory is not accepted (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

**2. Odontoblast transducer (receptor) theory.** The odontoblast would act as a receptor and pass the signal to nerves. The classical objections are that odontoblasts are not excitable cells and no synapse with nerves has been demonstrated (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; the odontoblast process extends only about one-third of the way to the DEJ (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]; and destroying the odontoblast layer does not abolish DH (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. Trushkowsky concludes that odontoblasts cannot contribute directly to stimulus transmission (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC]. Section 3.4 shows how this view has changed.

**3. Hydrodynamic theory.** Gysi proposed it in 1900 (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], and Brännström's experiments in the 1960s turned it into a theory (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Stimuli applied to exposed dentine move the fluid in open tubules; the pressure change activates mechanoreceptor A-beta and A-delta fibres at the pulp-dentine border. All stimuli cause outward flow except heat, which causes inward flow (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], so cold is more troublesome than heat (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Animal experiments show the intradental nerves are more sensitive to outward than inward flow (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC], and outward flow above about 0.75 nL/s/mm² evokes nerve responses (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC].

**Tubule physics (Poiseuille).** Convective flow varies with the fourth power of the tubule radius (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. Halving the radius cuts flow to one-sixteenth (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]; doubling it raises flow 16-fold (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Sensitive teeth have tubules about twice as wide at the buccal cervical area (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], and symptom severity correlates with the number of open tubules (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. A minimum tubule diameter of 0.83 µm has been reported as necessary to elicit DH at the cervical area (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Dentine is sensitive only if the tubules are patent from the pulp to the mouth (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

**Nerve fibres.** Short, sharp, well-localised pain corresponds to A-fibre activation (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. The unmyelinated C fibres do not respond to the innocuous stimuli that evoke DH pain (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]; they produce the dull ache of pulpitis.

**3.4 The modern view: all three act together.** Markowitz and Pashley write that the textbook picture of three competing theories "has been made obsolete since all three mechanisms appear to contribute and act together" (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. A mechanically stimulated odontoblast releases ATP, which excites nearby nerves (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. In rats with odontoblasts deleted, cold applied to exposed dentine no longer evoked pain behaviour (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. Intradental nerves also respond directly to stimuli (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]: cold and hyperosmotic solutions act on receptors on the nerve endings (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. The tubules remain the conduit through which stimuli reach the nerves and odontoblasts.

**Role of the pulp.** Pulpal inflammation may make exposed dentine hypersensitive (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. After cavity preparation or periodontal treatment, nerve sprouting peaks at 1 week (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC], matching the timing of postoperative sensitivity. Bacteria do not reach the pulp through sensitive dentine: the functional tubule diameter is about one-twentieth of the apparent diameter (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC], so dentine acts like a 0.2 µm Millipore filter (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Background.** Enamel is insensitive, but dentin is exquisitely sensitive, although nerves are found only in its inner half (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024). Three hypotheses explain this (Addy and Gillam, 2024).

| Theory | Proposal | Evidence against or for |
|---|---|---|
| Direct neural stimulation | Nerve endings in outer dentin are stimulated directly | No nerves in outer dentin; not accepted (Sikri, 2020) |
| Odontoblast transducer | Odontoblast acts as receptor and passes the signal to nerves | Odontoblast not excitable, no synapse; process reaches only one-third of dentin; DH persists after odontoblast destruction (Sikri, 2020; Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) |
| Hydrodynamic (Gysi 1900; Brännström 1960s) | Stimuli move tubular fluid; the pressure change activates A-delta and A-beta mechanoreceptors at the pulp-dentin border | Widely accepted; explains all stimuli and the action of desensitisers |

**Hydrodynamic mechanism**
- Cold, air, probing, sweet and osmotic stimuli cause rapid outward flow; heat causes slow inward flow, so cold hurts more (Addy and Gillam, 2024; Sikri, 2020).
- Nerves respond more to outward flow (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024).
- Poiseuille's law: flow ∝ r⁴. Doubling tubule diameter gives a 16-fold rise in flow; halving it gives one-sixteenth (Sikri, 2020; Markowitz and Pashley, 2024).
- Sensitive dentin has more and twice-as-wide open tubules; dentin is sensitive only if tubules are patent from surface to pulp (Sikri, 2020).
- A-delta fibres give sharp, localised pain; C fibres give the dull ache of pulpitis (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024).

**Current concept.** The three mechanisms are no longer competitors; they act together (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024). Odontoblasts are mechanosensitive and signal to nerves by releasing ATP; deleting odontoblasts abolished cold-evoked pain in rats; cold also acts directly on nerve endings. Pulpal inflammation and nerve sprouting (maximum at 1 week) increase sensitivity after restorative or periodontal treatment (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024).

**How each stimulus causes pain**
- Air blast: evaporation at the tubule orifice draws fluid outward by capillary action.
- Cold: fluid contracts and moves outward rapidly.
- Probe: pressing pushes fluid inward; recoil on release pulls it outward.
- Sweet or salty (osmotic): draws fluid outward.
- Heat: fluid expands and moves slowly inward, so heat hurts less.
(Markowitz and Pashley, 2024; Sikri, 2020)

## 4. Etiology: lesion localisation and lesion initiation

### Explanation

**Two steps.** For DH to occur, dentine must first be exposed (lesion localisation) and then its tubules opened (lesion initiation) (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Lesion localisation is exposure of dentine by enamel loss or gingival recession (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; lesion initiation follows when the tubules become patent to the pulp, usually by loss of the smear layer (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Exposure alone is not enough: DH can be uncommon even where the pulp shows through a thin bridge of dentine (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], because such dentine is sclerotic.

**Lesion localisation: tooth wear.** Tooth wear is the irreversible, non-traumatic loss of dental hard tissue by erosion, attrition, abrasion and abfraction (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Erosion is a major factor in enamel loss (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. It is chemical dissolution of apatite by an acid (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC] that is not produced by the oral flora (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], usually extrinsic and dietary rather than intrinsic (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; intrinsic acid is regurgitated stomach acid (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Abrasion is mechanical wear by foreign bodies (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], and toothbrushing with toothpaste is the main abrasive cause of enamel loss (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Toothbrushes alone have no measurable effect on enamel and dentine (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; it is the combination of erosion and abrasion, with additive or synergistic effects, that matters (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Attrition is wear from tooth-to-tooth contact (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], usually from bruxism. Abfraction, proposed by Lee and Eakle (1984) and Grippo (1991) (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], attributes cervical lesions to occlusal stress; Olley and Bartlett report that the theory is unproven, with evidence against it (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], and call the term misleading (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Lesion localisation: gingival recession.** Recession exposes root dentine; it is caused by overzealous brushing, periodontal disease, periodontal treatment, thin tissues and dehiscences. Cementum is thin and easily lost, and in 5-10% of teeth cementum does not meet enamel at the CEJ, leaving dentine exposed from the start (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC].

**Lesion initiation: loss of the smear layer.** Smear layers reduce hydraulic conductance (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC] about 10-30 times (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]; a reformed smear layer prevents 86% of fluid movement (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Smear layers are very acid labile (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], whereas toothbrushes alone remove them very slowly (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Wine, citrus juices, apple juice and yoghurt opened tubules in vitro, while Coca-Cola and Ribena did not (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], because titratable acidity matters more than pH (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. DH is more likely if an acidic drink was taken within the previous hour (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Brushing force matters: at 400 g the smear layer is removed and lesions initiate (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], while gentle brushing may recreate a smear layer over weeks (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Olley and Bartlett conclude that erosion is the most important factor for both exposure and initiation (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Periodontal treatment.** DH after scaling and root planing affected up to 57% in one study (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC] and 85-95% of periodontal patients in another (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. After non-surgical therapy it is 62.5-90% at 1 day (Taha and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; after open-flap debridement it rose from 30% to 79% at 1 week (Taha and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. The delay, with a peak at 1-3 weeks (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC] or onset 7-14 days after scaling (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC], is explained by plaque acids dissolving the smear layer left by instrumentation.

**Other factors.** DH is associated with good oral hygiene and gingival recession (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], with tobacco use (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], and smokers report more pain (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC]. Recession can be iatrogenic, after indirect restorations (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Sikri lists the factors that modify sensitivity: enamel gives more protection than cementum (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], salivary calcium and phosphate obturate tubules, and an inflamed pulp increases sensitivity while a necrotic pulp reduces it (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

**Natural desensitisation.** Salivary mineral deposits and calculus on the dentine surface reduce permeability (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. Tertiary and peritubular dentine formation reduce permeability and DH (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], as does a coagulation-like process in the dentinal fluid (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Even sclerotic dentine has fewer than 50% of tubules blocked (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC]. Plaque-covered dentine has wider tubules than plaque-free dentine (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. DH is therefore a sign of an active tooth wear process (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**DH needs two steps (Addy, 2002, in Addy and Gillam, 2024; Olley and Bartlett, 2024)**
1. **Lesion localisation:** dentin becomes exposed by loss of enamel or by gingival recession with loss of cementum.
2. **Lesion initiation:** the smear layer is removed and the tubules become patent to the pulp.
Exposed dentin is not always sensitive; sclerosed tubules do not conduct (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

**A. Loss of enamel (tooth wear)**
- **Erosion:** chemical dissolution by non-bacterial acid. Extrinsic: citrus fruits and juices, carbonated and sports drinks, wine, vinegar, vitamin C tablets, swimming pools. Intrinsic: gastric acid in reflux, bulimia, vomiting. Erosion is the most important single factor for both exposure and tubule opening (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).
- **Abrasion:** mechanical wear by foreign bodies, mainly toothbrushing with abrasive toothpaste; brushing alone does little, but brushing softened (eroded) tooth surfaces removes tissue (Addy and Gillam, 2024; Sikri, 2020).
- **Attrition:** tooth-to-tooth wear, bruxism; occlusal DH is linked to wear severity (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).
- **Abfraction:** cervical lesions attributed to occlusal flexure (Lee and Eakle, 1984; Grippo, 1991); unproven and now called misleading (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

**B. Gingival recession (root exposure)**
- Overzealous or incorrect brushing, periodontal disease, scaling and root planing, periodontal surgery, thin biotype and dehiscence, frenal pull, orthodontic movement, iatrogenic crown margins.
- In 5-10% of teeth cementum does not meet enamel, so dentin is exposed at the CEJ (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009).

**C. Opening of tubules (lesion initiation)**
- Smear layer reduces hydraulic conductance 10-30 times; it is acid labile and removed by dietary acids, especially those with high titratable acidity (wine, fruit juices, yoghurt), and by heavy brushing (400 g) (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024; Olley and Bartlett, 2024).
- Brushing immediately after acid, and plaque acids after scaling, open tubules.

**D. Periodontal therapy.** DH after scaling 62.5-90% at 1 day; after flap surgery rose from 30% to 79% at 1 week; peaks at 1-3 weeks and declines over about 6-8 weeks (Taha and Gillam, 2024; Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009).

**E. Modifying factors.** Good oral hygiene, tobacco use, inflamed pulp (more pain) and recent acid intake increase DH; tertiary and peritubular dentin, sclerosis and salivary mineral deposits reduce it (Olley and Bartlett, 2024; Markowitz and Gillam, 2024; Sikri, 2020).

**F. Natural desensitisation (why DH comes and goes)**
- Smear layer re-formation from gentle brushing.
- Salivary calcium and phosphate precipitating in the tubules; calculus on the surface.
- Plasma proteins and fibrinogen carried outward in the dentinal fluid, blocking the tubules.
- Peritubular (sclerotic) dentin and tertiary dentin laid down by the pulp.
(Markowitz and Gillam, 2024; Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009)
Erosion and heavy brushing remove these barriers again, which is why DH is cyclical and is a marker of active tooth wear (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

## 5. Clinical features

### Explanation

The pain is short, sharp and well localised, provoked by cold, air, touch, sweet or acid, and stops when the stimulus is removed (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Cold is the commonest trigger (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Pulpal pain, in contrast, is usually more prolonged, dull, aching and poorly localised (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC]. Sikri notes that the sharp pain may be followed by a dull ache for variable periods, and that pulpitis may be present when the pain continues as a throbbing ache (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The dentinal fluid, which makes up about 22% of dentine volume, is an ultrafiltrate of blood (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Freshly exposed coronal dentine is more sensitive than cervical dentine (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC]. On examination, the exposed dentine is at the buccal cervical area, with recession or an NCCL, usually plaque-free (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. The distress leads to changes in behaviour such as altered diet and cancelled dental appointments, and to poorer plaque control (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Symptoms**
- Short, sharp, well-localised pain lasting only as long as the stimulus (seconds).
- Triggers: cold drinks and air (most common), toothbrushing, touch, sweet or sour food; heat less often (Sikri, 2020).
- Several teeth often affected; may be cyclical; avoidance of cold and of brushing affected areas.

**Signs**
- Exposed dentin at buccal cervical areas of canines and premolars, with gingival recession, NCCLs, erosion or attrition (Addy and Gillam, 2024).
- Lesions usually clean and shiny, without plaque.
- Pain reproduced by an air blast or a probe on the exposed dentin; teeth are vital, not tender to percussion, with no caries or defective restorations.

**Contrast with pulpitis:** pulpal pain is prolonged, dull, aching, poorly localised and outlasts the stimulus (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011). If a sharp DH pain turns into a lingering throbbing ache, pulpitis may be present (Sikri, 2020).

**Impact:** the pain can change behaviour: patients avoid cold food, change their diet, brush affected areas less and miss appointments, which in turn favours plaque, caries and gingival disease (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011).

## 6. Diagnosis

### Explanation

**History and examination.** Trushkowsky's checklist asks whether the pain can be localised to one tooth, whether it responds to an air blast, whether the tooth is tender to percussion or sensitive on biting or release, how long the pain lasts after the stimulus, and whether radiographs show caries or periapical disease (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC]. Diet (frequency and timing of acids), brushing habits, reflux and vomiting, bruxism, recent scaling or bleaching must be recorded.

**Stimuli and measurement (Sikri's Ch.29).** Holland's guidelines recommended at least two different stimuli in clinical trials (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].
- Tactile: an explorer passed across the CEJ, graded on a 0-3 scale (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], with 3 being severe pain lasting more than 10 seconds (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. A force-sensitive electronic probe (Yeaple type) raises force until pain; a tooth with no pain at 70 g is scored non-sensitive (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].
- Thermal: air or cold water; heat testing starts at 37.5°C and rises in 1°C steps (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].
- Evaporative: air indexing directs air at the CEJ at about 45° for 0.5-1.0 s (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], with graded pressures from hole No. 1 at 2-3 psi (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; the least pressure that causes pain is the threshold patient response (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].
- Osmotic: a cotton pellet with sucrose solution on the root for 10 s (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].
- Electrical: pulp testers detect "pre-pain" (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], though Addy and Gillam regard electrical stimuli as more relevant to pulp testing than to DH.
- Patient-reported: the visual analogue scale (VAS) is used to assess and monitor DH (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC]; the Schiff 0-3 cold air scale is the usual air-blast score in trials [RECALL]. Olley and Bartlett add the CHI score for DH severity (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC] and the BEWE for tooth wear (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Differential diagnosis.** Sikri lists cracked tooth syndrome, nanoleakage from incorrectly placed adhesives (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], fractured restorations and pins, pulpal response to caries or restorations, medicaments, restorations in traumatic occlusion, palatogingival groove, chipped teeth and vital bleaching. Cracked tooth gives sharp pain on biting and on release (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; reversible pulpitis gives pain that disappears when the stimulus is removed (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Al-Sabbagh adds improperly insulated metallic restorations (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. Markowitz and Pashley show a molar whose sensitivity failed to respond to oxalate until a leaking amalgam was replaced: with caries, defective restorations or fractures, DH is unlikely to resolve with topical desensitisers (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. Bleaching sensitivity (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC] and MIH (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC] may involve different mechanisms and are excluded by the strict definition. Without a definite diagnosis, the clinician cannot successfully treat the condition (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

**Canadian Advisory Board algorithm.** This board developed an algorithm for diagnosis and treatment (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]: screen, take a history of pain and stimuli, probe for dietary and gastric acids, examine to exclude other causes, confirm DH, educate to remove risk factors, then treat non-invasively before invasively; if pain persists, review the diagnosis to exclude periodontal, neuropathic and referred pain (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. History.** Character, duration and triggers of pain; teeth involved; diet (acidic food and drink, frequency, timing relative to brushing); brushing technique, force and brush type; reflux, vomiting, eating disorders; bruxism; recent scaling, periodontal surgery, restorations or bleaching (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011).

**2. Clinical examination.** Recession, NCCLs, erosion, attrition; plaque and periodontal status; caries, defective restorations, cracks; occlusion; percussion and bite tests; vitality tests; radiographs where needed.

**3. Reproduce and grade the pain (at least two stimuli; Addy and Gillam, 2024)**

| Stimulus | Method | Scoring |
|---|---|---|
| Tactile | Explorer across CEJ; Yeaple force-sensitive probe | 0-3 scale; threshold in grams (non-sensitive if no pain at 70 g) |
| Evaporative | Air blast 0.5-1 s at 45°, 0.5 cm from the CEJ; air indexing at graded pressures | Schiff 0-3; threshold pressure |
| Thermal | Cold water; heat from 37.5°C in 1°C steps | Threshold temperature |
| Osmotic | Sucrose-soaked pellet for 10 s | Pain 1 / no pain 0 |
| Subjective | Visual analogue scale | 0-10 cm |

(Sikri, 2020; Gillam and Ramseier, 2024)

**4. Exclude (differential diagnosis)**

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Caries | Visible lesion; pain to sweet |
| Cracked tooth | Pain on biting and on release; bite test, transillumination |
| Reversible pulpitis | Pain longer than the stimulus; deep restoration or caries |
| Irreversible pulpitis | Lingering, spontaneous, throbbing pain |
| Apical periodontitis | Dull pain on biting; tender to percussion |
| Postoperative sensitivity | Recent restoration: leakage, poor bonding, high occlusion |
| Fractured restoration, palatogingival groove, chipped tooth | Visible on examination |
| Bleaching sensitivity | During or after whitening; generalised |

(Sikri, 2020; Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009)

**5. Confirm DH and identify the etiological factors** before treatment; treatment without a correct diagnosis fails (Mosquim et al, 2024). If pain persists after treatment, review the diagnosis for neuropathic, referred or periodontal pain (Canadian Advisory Board algorithm, in Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009).

## 7. Principles of management

### Explanation

**Logical versus biological.** For decades management was purely treatment: cover the exposed dentine or block the tubules. Addy and Gillam call this "clearly logical but hardly biological" (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC] because it ignores the cause. Markowitz and Gillam agree that controlling the etiological factors is the first step (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Busy clinicians tend to prescribe a toothpaste without finding the cause (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC].

**UK Expert Forum.** New guidelines were published from a UK Expert Forum on DH (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC]. They link management to three patient groups: gingival recession from mechanical trauma, tooth wear lesions, and periodontal disease or treatment (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC]. For each group the steps are clinical evaluation (for example, study casts and photographs to monitor over time) (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC], patient education, corrective clinical outcomes, and home-use recommendations. Patient compliance with oral hygiene and diet was paramount for success (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC].

**Extent and severity.** Severe pain in one or two teeth is treated in the office (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC]; in-office treatments suit a localised severe problem (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], whereas OTC products suit mild to moderate or generalised DH (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Conservative measures come before invasive ones (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]; Trushkowsky advises the least invasive technique that gives long-term relief (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC], and Al-Sabbagh's group recommends reversible interventions unlikely to cause harm (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. Endodontic treatment or extraction is not considered a valid option except in the rare case with irreversible pulpitis (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

**The ideal desensitiser.** Sikri lists immediate and lasting relief, easy application, patient tolerance, no pulp injury, no staining and low cost (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Mosquim adds fast action and sustained efficacy (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. No product meets all these requirements (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

**Classification of desensitising agents.** The most popular classification is by mechanism of action (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]: treatments either reduce dentine permeability or target the intradental nerves (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. They can also be classified by mode of delivery, in-office or home use (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Some agents work both ways, such as potassium oxalate (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principles**
1. Diagnose DH by exclusion and identify the cause.
2. Remove or control etiological and predisposing factors first; treatment alone is "logical but hardly biological" (Addy and Gillam, 2024).
3. Treat according to extent and severity: generalised mild-moderate DH with home-use (OTC) products; localised severe DH in one or two teeth with in-office agents (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024; Mosquim et al, 2024).
4. Non-invasive and reversible before invasive (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009; Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011).
5. Review and monitor (pain scores, photographs, casts); reinforce prevention.

**Ideal requirements of a desensitising agent:** immediate and long-lasting relief, easy to apply, well tolerated, non-irritant to the pulp, no staining, inexpensive, resistant to acid and brushing (Sikri, 2020; Mosquim et al, 2024). No agent meets all of these (Hill and Gillam, 2024).

**Classification by mechanism (Mosquim et al, 2024, adapted from Miglani et al, 2010)**

| Mechanism | Agents |
|---|---|
| Nerve desensitisation | Potassium nitrate, chloride, citrate, oxalate; low-power lasers |
| Tubule occlusion: protein precipitation | Glutaraldehyde/HEMA, silver nitrate, zinc chloride, strontium chloride |
| Tubule occlusion: mineral deposition | Fluorides (NaF, SnF₂, amine), oxalates, calcium phosphates, CPP-ACP, arginine-calcium carbonate, bioactive glass (CSPS, FCSPS), nano-hydroxyapatite, calcium hydroxide, high-power lasers |
| Covering the dentin | Varnishes, adhesives and sealants, GIC and RMGIC, composite restorations, root coverage surgery |

**By mode of delivery:** at-home (OTC dentifrices, mouthrinses, gels) and in-office (professionally applied) (Addy and Gillam, 2024).

**UK Expert Forum: management by patient group (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024)**
- Gingival recession: measure, correct brushing, reduce acids, desensitising toothpaste, silicone gingival veneer or root coverage, correction of subgingival margins.
- Tooth wear: identify cause, record with BEWE, casts and photographs, dietary advice, medical referral for reflux or vomiting, high-fluoride toothpaste, composite build-up or crowns if needed.
- Periodontal treatment: initial, corrective and maintenance phases with desensitising pastes and fluoride varnish at each, plaque control and short-term chlorhexidine.

## 8. Home-use (OTC) treatment

### Explanation

**Potassium salts (nerve desensitisation).** Potassium nitrate was introduced empirically (Hodosh, 1974) (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; the potassium ion is the active part (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. Potassium salts have little effect on dentine permeability in vitro (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; they depolarise the nerve (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Raising extracellular K⁺ to 10 mmol/l inhibits nerve responses (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC] because the action potential channels become inactivated and closed (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Relief takes about 2 weeks of twice-daily brushing (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; Sikri says 4-8 weeks (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. 5% potassium nitrate is the most extensively evaluated desensitising toothpaste (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], and the FDA accepted 5% KNO₃ with fluoride in 1992 (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. The doubts about efficacy are covered in the evidence synthesis.

**Strontium salts.** 10% strontium chloride was formulated for sensitive teeth in the early 1960s (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; strontium acetate is more compatible with fluoride (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Strontium may both occlude tubules and reduce nerve excitability (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], but the occlusion may come from the silica abrasive rather than strontium (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Sikri says strontium salts have minimal effect (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Bae's meta-analysis did not support strontium toothpaste (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], and it appears effective only for tactile stimuli (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

**Fluorides.** NaF precipitates CaF₂ globules that occlude tubules (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], but the crystals are small and lost rapidly (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. 0.454% stannous fluoride toothpaste has been reported more effective than MFP (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]; it forms a relatively acid-resistant stannous deposit (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], and SnF₂ with sodium hexametaphosphate gave 71% greater improvement than NaF in one trial (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009) [SRC].

**Arginine and calcium carbonate (Pro-Argin).** Typical toothpastes contain 8% arginine (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], an amino acid positively charged and found in saliva (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. It forms a calcium-phosphate deposit that occludes tubules, with relief lasting at least 28 days after one application (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. It works better against air than tactile stimuli (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], and the deposit is soluble in dietary acid (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

**Bioactive glass: calcium sodium phosphosilicate (CSPS, NovaMin).** The active ingredient is calcium sodium phosphosilicate (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], developed by Larry Hench in the late 1960s as Bioglass 45S5 (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC] and designed as a bone substitute (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. It releases calcium and phosphate and forms hydroxycarbonate apatite in tubules; toothpastes must be anhydrous (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC] and contain 2.5-7.5% glass (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. The deposit is water and acid resistant (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. A network meta-analysis found CSPS most beneficial for all three stimuli with high to moderate certainty (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Fluoride-containing glass (BioMin F) forms fluorapatite (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], which is more acid resistant than hydroxyapatite (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], and 5% BioMin F outperformed arginine toothpaste in one trial (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

**Nano-hydroxyapatite and CPP-ACP.** Carbonated nano-hydroxyapatite seals tubules (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; particles below 5 µm are needed to enter them (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. CPP-ACP was developed for remineralisation, and a systematic review found insufficient data to support efficacy for DH (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

**Mouthrinses and use.** 3% potassium nitrate with NaF mouthrinse reduced DH more than NaF alone (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Patients should use little water to avoid diluting the agent (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], and the best results come from twice-daily brushing on a regular schedule (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]; the paste can also be applied in a soft tray (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC]. OTC products take 2-4 weeks (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], and the patient should see a clinician if symptoms last longer (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indication:** mild to moderate, generalised DH; first-line therapy along with control of causes (Mosquim et al, 2024; Markowitz and Gillam, 2024).

**Desensitising dentifrices**

| Agent | Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 5% potassium nitrate (also chloride, citrate) | K⁺ diffuses to the pulp, depolarises and inactivates intradental nerves | Most studied; relief in 2-4 weeks; little effect on permeability; efficacy evidence weak to moderate (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) |
| 10% strontium chloride / 8% strontium acetate | Occludes tubules (largely silica deposit), some nerve effect | Oldest (1960s); weakest evidence; effective mainly to tactile stimuli (Mosquim et al, 2024) |
| Stannous fluoride 0.454% | Acid-resistant tin-containing deposit in tubules | Also anti-erosive (Addy and Gillam, 2024) |
| Sodium fluoride, MFP, amine fluoride | CaF₂ precipitate | Small crystals, lost quickly (Sikri, 2020) |
| 8% arginine + calcium carbonate (Pro-Argin) | Arginine-calcium-phosphate plug occludes tubules | Rapid relief; better to air; deposit acid-soluble (Mosquim et al, 2024) |
| Calcium sodium phosphosilicate (NovaMin, 5-7.5%) | Releases Ca and PO₄; hydroxycarbonate apatite in tubules | Anhydrous paste; best network meta-analysis result for all stimuli (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) |
| Fluoro calcium phosphosilicate (BioMin F) | Forms acid-resistant fluorapatite | Newer; promising (Mosquim et al, 2024) |
| Nano-hydroxyapatite | Biomimetic apatite coating, particles <5 µm enter tubules | Hill and Gillam, 2024 |
| CPP-ACP (Tooth Mousse) | Ca and PO₄ delivery | Insufficient evidence for DH (Mosquim et al, 2024) |

**How potassium works (proposed)**
1. K⁺ from the paste diffuses inward along open tubules, against outward dentinal flow.
2. Extracellular K⁺ around the intradental nerve endings rises above about 10 mmol/l.
3. The nerve membrane is depolarised; the sodium channels become inactivated.
4. The nerve no longer fires to stimuli; relief builds up over about 2 weeks of twice-daily use (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024).

**Other home measures:** potassium nitrate or oxalate mouthrinses; fluoride gels (0.4% SnF₂); 5000 ppm fluoride toothpaste in tooth wear; paste in a soft tray for 10-30 minutes.

**Instructions:** brush twice daily with a soft brush and light force; use the paste on a regular schedule; spit, do not rinse; can rub a little paste on sensitive areas; return if not better in 2-4 weeks (Sikri, 2020; Mosquim et al, 2024).

**Advantages:** cheap, easy, generalised effect. **Disadvantages:** slow onset (2-8 weeks), depends on compliance, needs continuous use (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009).

## 9. In-office (professional) treatment

### Explanation

**Approaches.** Sikri lists four approaches for in-office treatment: a smear layer formed by burnishing, precipitates within tubules, plastic resin impregnation, and bonding agents (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. The teeth are cleaned, isolated and dried; caustic agents must not touch the mucosa.

**Burnishing and varnishes.** Burnishing with a wooden stick forms a partial smear layer that reduces fluid movement by 50-80% (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Cavity varnishes reduce dentine permeability by 20-50% (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]; a fluoride varnish lasts usually 3 months (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. Mosquim gives 3-6 months of relief (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], and a single fluoride varnish reduced DH for at least 24 weeks (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC].

**Corticosteroids.** Steroids are reported to obliterate tubules completely (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], but there is insufficient evidence to support or refute their use (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC].

**Precipitating agents.** Silver nitrate precipitates the protein of odontoblast processes (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Zinc chloride-potassium ferrocyanide uses 40% zinc chloride followed by 20% potassium ferrocyanide (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Calcium hydroxide may block tubules or promote peritubular dentine (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; it gave immediate relief in over 90% of teeth in one study (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. 2% sodium fluoride and 10% stannous fluoride are used (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

**Oxalates.** Oxalate forms a calcium oxalate precipitate that occludes the tubules (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. The classic formulations are 6% ferric, 30% dipotassium and 3% monohydrogen monopotassium oxalate (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Potassium oxalate shows reliable, long-lasting effect (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], but a systematic review found no benefit except for 3% monohydrogen monopotassium oxalate (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], and Al-Sabbagh found the evidence insufficient (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC].

**Glutaraldehyde-HEMA (Gluma).** 5% glutaraldehyde with 35% HEMA (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]: glutaraldehyde reacts with serum albumin in the dentinal fluid (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC], coagulating proteins within the tubules (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. It was effective for up to 9 months (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. HEMA may cause allergy in patients and staff (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

**Adhesives and restorations.** For a bonding agent, the dentine is etched for 5 seconds, dried and the adhesive applied and cured (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Self-etch adhesives with 10-MDP bond chemically to calcium (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]; durability is reported as 3-12 months (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Scotchbond relieved 89% of teeth for up to 18 months in one report (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. Glass-ionomer has been used successfully (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] and reduces root sensitivity immediately and in the long term (Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011) [SRC]; RMGICs are highly recommended where there is indirect mechanical impact (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], and resin composite resists brushing better (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

**Iontophoresis.** The negative electrode is placed on the dentine and the positive on the patient's face or arm (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], isolating with cotton rolls rather than rubber dam (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], with 0.5 mA current (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. With fluoride it may act by reparative dentine, nerve paraesthesia or deeper fluoride penetration (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

**Lasers.** Lasers act by nerve desensitisation, dentine sealing and anti-inflammatory effects (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Pulp temperature rise is generally below 1.5°C (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], below the 5.0°C that harms the pulp (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Reported effectiveness ranged from 5% to 100% (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC], one controlled trial found Nd:YAG no better than placebo at 16 weeks (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC], and Mosquim's group does not consider lasers a clinically recommended protocol (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Details are in Q6.

**Root coverage surgery.** Root coverage reduces the frequency and intensity of DH (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], although in 2009 no study had been designed to test grafting as a DH treatment (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. Recession is classified by Miller, Cairo, and now the 2018 classification of recession defects and gingival phenotype (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

**Last resort.** Pulpal extirpation appears in the UK Forum table (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC] but is not a valid routine option (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indication:** localised severe DH in one or a few teeth, or failure of home care (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024). Clean, isolate, dry; protect the mucosa.

**1. Agents that occlude tubules**
- **Burnishing** exposed root with a wooden point or orangewood stick: partial smear layer, 50-80% fall in fluid flow (Sikri, 2020).
- **Fluoride varnish** (5% NaF, Duraphat): CaF₂ barrier; relief 3-6 months; single application effective for 24 weeks (Mosquim et al, 2024; Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011). 2% NaF, 10% SnF₂, sodium silicofluoride solutions.
- **Oxalates:** 30% dipotassium, 3% monohydrogen monopotassium, 6% ferric oxalate; calcium oxalate crystals inside tubules; potassium also acts on nerves (Mosquim et al, 2024).
- **Calcium compounds:** calcium hydroxide paste, calcium phosphate, Teethmate (tetracalcium + dicalcium phosphate), CPP-ACP, arginine prophylaxis paste, NovaMin prophylaxis paste (Sikri, 2020; Mosquim et al, 2024).
- **Protein precipitants:** silver nitrate, zinc chloride-potassium ferrocyanide, strontium chloride (Sikri, 2020).
- **Glutaraldehyde 5% + HEMA 35% (Gluma):** precipitates albumin and plugs tubules to about 200 µm; relief up to 9 months; HEMA allergy (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009; Mosquim et al, 2024).
- **Corticosteroids:** historical; insufficient evidence (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009).

**2. Sealing and covering**
- **Varnishes and resin sealants** (copal, Seal and Protect).
- **Dentin bonding agents:** etch 5 s (or self-etch with 10-MDP), dry, apply, cure; relief 3-12 months (Sikri, 2020; Mosquim et al, 2024).
- **GIC or RMGIC liners and varnish (Vanish XT), composite restorations:** for NCCLs with substance loss.

**3. Iontophoresis** with 2% NaF: negative electrode on dentin, positive on face or arm, 0.5 mA, cotton-roll isolation (Sikri, 2020).

**4. Lasers:** low-power GaAlAs (nerve effect) and high-power Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, Er,Cr:YSGG, CO₂ (tubule sealing); pulp temperature rise below 1.5°C; results variable, placebo effect large (Sikri, 2020; Mosquim et al, 2024).

**5. Periodontal plastic surgery:** coronally advanced flap, connective tissue graft, for Miller class I-II (RT1) recession with DH and aesthetic need; reduces DH (Mosquim et al, 2024). Silicone gingival veneer where surgery is not possible.

**6. Pulpectomy or extraction:** last resort only, for intractable pain (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024; Addy and Gillam, 2024).

**Typical in-office technique (fluoride varnish or oxalate)**
1. Record baseline pain (air blast and probe; VAS).
2. Remove plaque and calculus gently; avoid over-instrumentation.
3. Isolate with cotton rolls; dry with warm air.
4. Apply the agent to the exposed dentin only (an in-office potassium oxalate gel is left for 3 minutes and removed without rinsing; varnish as a thin film); keep caustic agents off the gingiva (Mosquim et al, 2024).
5. Avoid eating and brushing for the time recommended by the manufacturer.
6. Re-test; review at 2-4 weeks and repeat if needed.

**In-office versus home use:** in-office agents act immediately and target specific teeth, but cost more and need repeat visits; home products are cheap and treat many teeth but take 2-4 weeks and depend on compliance (Mosquim et al, 2024).

**Management algorithm**
Diagnosis by exclusion → identify and remove causes (diet, brushing, reflux, bruxism, perio) → desensitising toothpaste 2-4 weeks → review → if localised and persisting, in-office occluding agent, varnish or adhesive → review → restoration of NCCL or root coverage → review diagnosis if still painful → pulpectomy only as last resort (Canadian Advisory Board, in Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009).

## 10. Prevention

### Explanation

Sikri's suggestions for patients are to avoid recession through good oral hygiene, hard-bristled brushes (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], brushing immediately after acidic food or drink, excessive force, and excessive flossing or misuse of interdental aids (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Brushing should wait at least half an hour after an acidic drink (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Al-Sabbagh's group says two to three hours (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. For professionals: avoid over-instrumenting roots during scaling (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], over-polishing exposed roots, violating the biologic width with crown margins (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], and burning the gingiva during in-office whitening (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Primary prevention at population level remains a utopian goal (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Motivational interviewing is effective for many health behaviours (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC], but no published study has tested it in DH (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Patient measures (Sikri, 2020)**
- Soft brush, light force, non-abrasive low-RDA paste; no horizontal scrubbing.
- Do not brush immediately after acidic food or drink; wait at least 30 minutes (some authors say 2-3 hours) (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009).
- Reduce frequency of acidic drinks; use a straw; finish meals with milk or cheese.
- Avoid excessive flossing, toothpicks and large amounts of toothpaste.
- Treat reflux and eating disorders (medical referral).

**Professional measures (Sikri, 2020)**
- Avoid over-instrumentation of roots during scaling and root planing; avoid over-polishing exposed roots.
- Respect the biologic width with crown margins.
- Protect the gingiva during in-office bleaching.
- Apply desensitising paste or fluoride varnish after periodontal therapy, and warn the patient that sensitivity usually peaks at 1-3 weeks and settles within about 6-8 weeks.
- Monitor tooth wear with BEWE, photographs and study casts, so that DH is treated as a sign of an active wear process (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

**Behaviour change:** motivational interviewing (open questions, affirmations, reflective listening, summarising) to change diet and brushing (Gillam and Ramseier, 2024).

## 11. Conclusion

### Explanation

DH is a common, often self-managed condition that reflects an active tooth wear or recession process (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Treatment works by occluding tubules or desensitising nerves, but mineral occlusion is not permanent (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], whereas polymeric occlusion is more acid-resistant (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Products that only cover the surface are likely to be removed by acid or wear; those that occlude deep within tubules or stimulate tertiary dentine should last longer (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Control of the cause decides long-term success.

#### ✎ Exam core

DH is a diagnosis of exclusion, explained mainly by the hydrodynamic mechanism, and is a clinical sign of active tooth wear or recession (Olley and Bartlett, 2024). Management must be preventive and biological: identify and remove the causes, then desensitise with home-use dentifrices for generalised DH and in-office occluding or sealing agents for localised severe DH, moving to restorations or root coverage only when needed. Because tubule occlusion is reversible by acid and brushing, every treatment needs continued prevention and review (Mosquim et al, 2024).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1. Potassium nitrate toothpaste works by depolarising the pulpal nerves.**
- Textbook: Sikri and most textbooks state that potassium depolarises the nerves after 4-8 weeks (Sikri, 2020); KNO₃ was accepted by the FDA in 1992 (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009).
- Evidence: 10 mmol/l extracellular K⁺ inactivates nerves in animal preparations (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024). But when potassium solutions were applied to human dentine cavities without pressure, the effect was modest and short-lived, because outward dentinal flow opposes inward diffusion; little of the 500 mmol/l in a toothpaste reaches the inner dentine (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024). A Cochrane review found no clear evidence for potassium toothpastes (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024), Addy and West concluded "unproven, but not ineffective" (Addy and Gillam, 2024), whereas later meta-analyses (Bae 2015, Hu 2018, Martins 2020) were more favourable (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024).
- Appraisal: the mechanism rests on animal and in-vitro data; the clinical trials are heterogeneous, mostly industry-sponsored and affected by placebo (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024). The benefit may come partly from abrasives or fluoride in the paste (Mosquim et al, 2024).
- Conclusion: potassium toothpaste is a reasonable first-line product, but the classic "nerve depolarisation" explanation is not proven in human teeth. [Likely]

**Claim 2. The hydrodynamic theory is the mechanism of DH and the odontoblast plays no part.**
- Textbook: Sikri and Trushkowsky dismiss the odontoblast theory (Sikri, 2020; Trushkowsky and Oquendo, 2011).
- Evidence: odontoblasts express mechanosensitive piezo channels and release ATP onto nerves; deleting odontoblasts abolished cold-evoked pain in rats; cold also acts directly on nerve endings (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024).
- Appraisal: these are animal and cell-culture studies; human confirmation is lacking. They refine, rather than contradict, the hydrodynamic theory: fluid flow still drives both the odontoblast and the nerve, and sealing tubules still abolished pain (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024).
- Conclusion: write the hydrodynamic theory as the accepted mechanism, and add that odontoblasts and direct nerve activation act with it. [Likely]

**Claim 3. Desensitising agents have proven efficacy.**
- Evidence: placebo groups improve by about 40% (Addy and Gillam, 2024), with estimates of 20-60% of symptom relief due to placebo (Mosquim et al, 2024); water alone reduced DH by 5-27% in a Gluma trial (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009). Results are reported as statistical, not clinical, significance (Addy and Gillam, 2024). In 2009 Al-Sabbagh's group found the level of evidence for any intervention weak (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009).
- Appraisal: since then, network meta-analysis has ranked CSPS, SnF₂, potassium with SnF₂ or hydroxyapatite, and arginine as beneficial with moderate to high certainty (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024). In-office occluding agents rest mostly on small trials and in-vitro permeability data (Mosquim et al, 2024).
- Conclusion: tubule-occluding toothpastes have the best evidence for home use; in-office agents give faster relief but weaker long-term evidence. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Holland 1997 definition: short sharp pain from exposed dentin to thermal, evaporative, tactile, osmotic or chemical stimuli, not ascribable to any other defect or disease; a diagnosis of exclusion.
2. Prevalence about 15%; age 20-40; canines and premolars; buccal cervical; plaque-free.
3. Hydrodynamic theory (Gysi 1900, Brännström); flow ∝ r⁴; cold causes outward flow and hurts most; A-delta fibres.
4. Two steps: lesion localisation (enamel loss or recession) and lesion initiation (smear layer loss); erosion is the key factor.
5. At least two stimuli to diagnose and grade: air blast (Schiff) and tactile (Yeaple), plus VAS.
6. Treat causes first; home dentifrice for generalised mild DH, in-office agents for localised severe DH.
7. Agents act by nerve desensitisation (K⁺) or tubule occlusion (fluoride, oxalate, arginine, bioactive glass, glutaraldehyde, adhesives, lasers).
8. Placebo effect 20-60%: interpret trials with care.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Leaving out "cannot be ascribed to any other defect or disease": the definition requires exclusion.
2. Starting with desensitising agents before diagnosing and removing the cause.
3. Writing that potassium works by occluding tubules; it acts on nerves (with doubtful penetration), while strontium, fluoride and oxalate occlude.
4. Treating DH by root canal treatment as a routine step.
5. Ignoring the placebo effect when quoting trial results.

**"DIET-POT-SEAL"** for the management order: **D**iagnose by exclusion, **I**dentify causes, **E**ducate (diet, brushing), **T**oothpaste; then **POT**assium for nerves; then in-office **SEAL** (Surface occluders, Etch-and-bond, Adhesive restorations, Laser or graft).
