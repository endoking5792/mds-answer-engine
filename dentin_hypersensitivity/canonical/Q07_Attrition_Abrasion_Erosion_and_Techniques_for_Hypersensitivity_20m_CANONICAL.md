## 1. Tooth wear: definitions

### Explanation

Tooth wear is the irreversible, non-traumatic loss of dental hard tissues by erosion, attrition, abrasion and abfraction (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Physiological wear is slow and allows the pulp to lay down reparative or secondary dentine (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; pathological wear is fast or occurs in young patients.

**Attrition** is the physical wear of dental hard tissues from tooth-to-tooth contact on occlusal or incisal surfaces (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], typically from bruxism; the severity of occlusal DH follows the severity of wear (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Abrasion** is a physical process caused by mechanical wear of dental tissues by foreign bodies (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Toothbrushing with toothpaste is the main abrasive cause of enamel loss (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], although toothbrushes alone have no measurable effect (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Brushing force of 400 g initiates DH lesions in vitro (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], and toothpaste dentine abrasivity is limited by ISO to an RDA of 250 (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Erosion** is the chemical dissolution of the apatite crystals by an acid (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC] that is not produced by the oral flora (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Extrinsic dietary acids are commoner than intrinsic gastric acid (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; intrinsic acid is regurgitated hydrochloric acid from vomiting or reflux (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Titratable acidity matters more than pH (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC], and DH is more likely within an hour of an acidic drink (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Abfraction** was suggested by Lee and Eakle (1984) and named by Grippo (1991) (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]; the theory is not proven and there is evidence against it (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Interaction.** In tribology terms, attrition, abrasion and erosion are two-body, three-body and chemico-physical wear (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Erosion and abrasion act additively or synergistically (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], and erosion is the most important factor in DH (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. DH is therefore a clinical sign of an active tooth wear process (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC]. Wear is recorded with the BEWE (Olley and Bartlett, 2024) [SRC].

**Not from these sources.** The typical sites and appearances in the exam table (shiny facets, wedge-shaped notches, cupped and rounded erosion lesions, palatal erosion from gastric acid), the occlusal splint for bruxism and the advice to use straws are standard clinical descriptions not stated in the chapters read [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Tooth wear:** the irreversible, non-carious, non-traumatic loss of dental hard tissue by erosion, attrition, abrasion and (possibly) abfraction (Olley and Bartlett, 2024). Physiological wear is slow and allows secondary and tertiary dentin to protect the pulp; pathological wear is fast and causes DH.

| | Attrition | Abrasion | Erosion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition | Physical wear of hard tissue from tooth-to-tooth contact | Physical wear by foreign bodies (objects other than teeth) | Chemical dissolution of hard tissue by acids not of bacterial origin |
| Tribology | Two-body wear | Three-body wear | Chemico-physical wear |
| Causes | Bruxism, clenching, coarse diet, loss of posterior support | Toothbrush and abrasive toothpaste, horizontal scrubbing, pipe, hairpins, nail biting | Extrinsic: citrus fruits and juices, carbonated and sports drinks, wine, vinegar, vitamin C and aspirin tablets, swimming pools, industrial acid vapours. Intrinsic: gastric acid in reflux, bulimia, vomiting |
| Site | Occlusal and incisal, matching facets on opposing teeth | Buccal cervical, canines and premolars, left side in right-handers | Palatal of upper anteriors (intrinsic), labial and occlusal (extrinsic) |
| Appearance | Flat, shiny facets; sharp margins | Sharp, V- or wedge-shaped notch, hard and polished | Smooth, rounded, glazed "cupped" lesions; restorations stand proud |

(Olley and Bartlett, 2024; Addy and Gillam, 2024)

**Abfraction:** wedge-shaped cervical lesion attributed to tooth flexure under occlusal load (Lee and Eakle, 1984; Grippo, 1991); now considered unproven, the lesions being mainly erosion plus abrasion (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

**Diagnosis and monitoring of wear:** history of diet, reflux, brushing and bruxism; BEWE screening score; study casts and photographs repeated over time (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

**Relation to DH:** erosion and abrasion act together (brushing softened tooth), exposing dentin (lesion localisation) and removing the smear layer (lesion initiation). Erosion is the most important factor, and DH is a sign of active tooth wear (Olley and Bartlett, 2024).

## 2. Techniques in managing hypersensitivity

### Explanation

DH is short sharp pain from exposed dentine that cannot be ascribed to other dental conditions (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; dentine must be exposed and its tubules opened (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Treating only the symptom is logical but hardly biological (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Treatments either reduce dentine permeability or target the nerves (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], the classification by mechanism being the most popular (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. In-office treatments suit localised severe DH (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]; OTC products suit generalised mild to moderate DH (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC] and take 2-4 weeks (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Conservative treatment precedes invasive (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC].

**Preventive.** Wait at least half an hour after an acidic drink before brushing (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; Al-Sabbagh's group says two to three hours (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009) [SRC].

**Home use.** Potassium nitrate was introduced empirically (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC] and depolarises the nerves (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; stannous fluoride (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], arginine with relief lasting 28 days (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] and calcium sodium phosphosilicate (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] occlude tubules.

**In-office.** Burnishing cuts fluid flow by 50-80% (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]; fluoride varnish relieves for 3-6 months (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]; oxalate forms calcium oxalate in the tubules (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; Gluma is 5% glutaraldehyde with 35% HEMA (Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]; for adhesives the dentine is etched for 5 seconds (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], with durability of 3-12 months (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Iontophoresis places the negative electrode on the dentine (Sikri, 2020) [SRC] at 0.5 mA (Sikri, 2020) [SRC]. Lasers act on nerves, seal dentine and reduce inflammation (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. RMGICs are highly recommended for lesions under indirect mechanical impact (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], with composite more resistant to brushing (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Root coverage reduces DH (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Endodontics is not a valid routine option (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principles:** confirm DH by exclusion; identify and control the tooth-wear cause; treat generalised mild DH at home and localised severe DH in the office; reversible before irreversible (Mosquim et al, 2024).

**A. Preventive and causal (for each wear type)**
- Erosion: dietary counselling (less frequent acids, straws, no swishing), no brushing for at least 30 minutes after acid, treat reflux and eating disorders (Sikri, 2020).
- Abrasion: soft brush, light force, low-abrasive paste, correct technique.
- Attrition: occlusal splint, manage bruxism.

**B. Home-use techniques (nerve desensitisation or tubule occlusion)**
- 5% potassium nitrate, chloride or citrate dentifrice: K⁺ depolarises intradental nerves.
- Strontium acetate, stannous fluoride, 8% arginine-calcium carbonate, calcium sodium phosphosilicate (NovaMin), fluoro calcium phosphosilicate (BioMin F), nano-hydroxyapatite dentifrices: occlude tubules.
- Fluoride or potassium oxalate mouthrinses; 0.4% SnF₂ gel; 5000 ppm fluoride paste in tooth wear.
- Relief in 2-4 weeks with twice-daily use.

**C. In-office techniques**
1. Burnishing exposed dentin (smear layer; 50-80% fall in flow).
2. Fluoride: 5% NaF varnish, 2% NaF, SnF₂, sodium silicofluoride; with iontophoresis (negative electrode on dentin, 0.5 mA).
3. Precipitants: oxalates (30% dipotassium, 3% monopotassium-monohydrogen, 6% ferric), calcium hydroxide, calcium phosphate, silver nitrate, zinc chloride-potassium ferrocyanide, strontium chloride.
4. Protein coagulants: 5% glutaraldehyde + 35% HEMA (Gluma).
5. Sealants and adhesives: etch 5 s or self-etch, apply and cure; relief 3-12 months.
6. Lasers: low-power diode (nerve effect, tertiary dentin) and Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, CO₂ (tubule sealing).
7. Restorations: GIC or RMGIC (attrition-free cervical sites), composite (abrasion-prone sites) for NCCLs with tissue loss.
8. Periodontal plastic surgery: root coverage for recession.
9. Pulpectomy: last resort only.

(Sikri, 2020; Mosquim et al, 2024; Al-Sabbagh, Brown and Thomas, 2009)

**D. Choosing the technique by wear pattern**

| Situation | Preferred approach |
|---|---|
| Generalised mild DH, erosion or abrasion | Causal control + desensitising dentifrice |
| One or two very sensitive teeth, little tissue loss | Varnish, oxalate, Gluma or adhesive sealant |
| Deep NCCL, aesthetics or plaque trap | GIC, RMGIC or composite restoration |
| Recession with DH, adequate interdental tissue | Root coverage surgery |
| Occlusal DH with attrition | Splint, fluoride varnish, restoration of worn dentin |

**E. Review:** re-test at 2-4 weeks with air blast and probe; repeat or escalate; reinforce diet and brushing advice at every visit, since occlusion is lost again if erosion continues (Mosquim et al, 2024).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed)

Professionally applied tubule-sealing materials were considerably more effective than potassium dentifrices in a practice-based study (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024), yet most in-office agents are supported by small trials, and placebo explains 20-60% of reported relief (Mosquim et al, 2024). Mineral occlusion is not permanent under acid challenge (Mosquim et al, 2024), so in a patient with active erosion any technique fails unless the acid source is controlled. The tooth-wear diagnosis is therefore part of the treatment, not a preliminary. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Attrition: tooth-to-tooth; abrasion: foreign bodies; erosion: non-bacterial acid.
2. Abfraction is unproven; most NCCLs are erosion plus abrasion.
3. Erosion is the key factor in DH; do not brush straight after acid.
4. Techniques: causal control, home dentifrices, in-office occluders and sealants, restorations, root coverage.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Defining erosion as acid from bacteria; that is caries.
