## 1. The two strategies

### Explanation

Following the hydrodynamic theory, treatments should either reduce dentine permeability or target the activation or conduction of the intradental nerves (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; the mechanism-based classification is the most popular (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC], and some agents do both (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. In Sikri's table potassium nitrate is the agent that lowers nerve sensitivity, the rest occlude (Sikri, 2020) [SRC].

**Nerve desensitisation.** KNO₃ was introduced empirically (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; the potassium ion is the active portion (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. It has little effect on permeability in vitro (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], even at 30% (Al-Sabbagh, Harrison and Thomas, 2009) [SRC]. NaNO₃ and LiNO₃ lack its effect (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Raising K⁺ to 10 mmol/l inhibits nerves (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], whose channels become inactivated (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; 8-16 mM around axons sustains depolarisation (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Without inward pressure, diffusion to the nerves is limited (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], and little of the 500 mmol/l in toothpaste reaches the inner dentine (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Relief takes about 2 weeks (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Partial occlusion may enhance potassium diffusion (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], and Addy suggests a slowly forming depot at the pulp-dentine border (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Strontium has a dual action (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

**Tubule occlusion.** Flow ∝ r⁴ (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC], so tubules need not be fully sealed (Markowitz and Pashley, 2024) [SRC]. Mineral occlusion is not permanent (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]; polymeric occlusion is more acid resistant (Mosquim et al, 2024) [SRC]. Stannous deposits are relatively acid resistant (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], CSPS deposits water and acid resistant (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], arginine deposits acid soluble (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC], oxalates acid labile (Sikri, 2020) [SRC], and much of strontium's occlusion may be due to silica abrasive (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Tertiary dentine formation would last longer than surface occlusion (Hill and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

**Evidence.** A Cochrane review found no clear evidence for potassium toothpastes (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; Addy and West concluded "unproven", not ineffective (Addy and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. A network meta-analysis ranked CSPS best for all stimuli (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]; Bae did not support strontium (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC]. Professionally applied sealing materials were considerably more effective than potassium dentifrices in practice-based research (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Basis.** By the hydrodynamic theory, DH can be treated by (1) stopping fluid flow (tubule occlusion) or (2) stopping the nerve from responding (nerve desensitisation) (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024).

| Feature | Nerve desensitisation | Tubule occlusion |
|---|---|---|
| Agents | Potassium nitrate, chloride, citrate (5%); low-power laser | Fluorides (NaF, SnF₂), oxalates, strontium, arginine-CaCO₃, CSPS (NovaMin), BioMin F, nano-HA, CPP-ACP, Gluma, adhesives, varnishes, high-power lasers |
| Target | Intradental A-delta fibres | Patent dentinal tubules |
| Mechanism | K⁺ raises extracellular K⁺ above about 10 mmol/l; membrane depolarised; sodium channels inactivated; nerve stops firing | Precipitate (CaF₂, calcium oxalate, apatite, tin compounds), protein coagulum or resin layer narrows tubule; flow ∝ r⁴, so partial occlusion is enough |
| Effect on permeability | Little or none | Large reduction |
| Onset | Slow: about 2-4 weeks of twice-daily use | In-office: immediate; dentifrices: days to weeks |
| Durability | Lost when use stops | Depends on acid resistance: oxalate and arginine deposits acid labile; SnF₂ and bioactive glass more resistant; resins most resistant |
| Delivery | Mainly home use | Home and in-office |
| Weakness | K⁺ must diffuse against outward dentinal flow; little reaches the pulp | Removed by erosion and brushing unless the cause is controlled |
| Evidence | Cochrane 2006: no clear evidence; later meta-analyses more favourable, especially with SnF₂ | CSPS best for all stimuli (network meta-analysis); arginine for air; strontium weak |

(Markowitz and Gillam, 2024; Mosquim et al, 2024; Addy and Gillam, 2024)

**Combination products:** potassium nitrate with stannous fluoride, potassium with hydroxyapatite, and potassium salts with occluding abrasives or polymers aim to use both routes; partial occlusion may also help K⁺ diffuse inward by reducing outward flow (Markowitz and Gillam, 2024).

**Agents with both actions:** potassium oxalate (crystals + K⁺), strontium salts (occlusion + divalent-cation nerve effect), potassium-releasing bioactive glass.

**Clinical choice.** Start with a dentifrice (potassium or an occluding agent) for generalised DH; use in-office occluding or sealing agents for localised severe DH. Combining the two (for example, potassium with stannous fluoride) is better supported than either alone. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Potassium works on nerves, has little effect on permeability, and needs about 2 weeks.
2. Occluding agents act through flow ∝ r⁴; durability depends on acid resistance.
3. Evidence: CSPS strongest; potassium mixed; strontium weakest.
