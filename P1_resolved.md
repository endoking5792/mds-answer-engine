## STUDY MODE

### S1. The core contrast [basic science]
Composite bonds to enamel superbly but not reliably to dentine (shrinkage and hydrolysis), whereas GIC bonds to dentine reliably and is water-based (Mount, 1994). McCabe sums up the table: composite is strong, tough, insoluble, radiopaque and quick-setting but has no inherent adhesion, shrinks and releases no fluoride; GIC has inherent adhesion, little shrinkage, fluoride release and biocompatibility but is brittle, soluble, not radiopaque, wears, is water-sensitive and slow-setting (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### S2. Which to use where [clinical decision-making]
Composite is stronger than GIC under occlusal or incisal load (Mount et al, 2016). In molars composite has little enamel for etching and suffers shrinkage, fatigue and water uptake, while GIC needs support from remaining tooth (Mount et al, 2016). Composite lacks an intrinsic defence against caries, so once a gap forms microleakage and recurrent caries spread quickly (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Measured wear for GIC was 73 micrometres in a year against under 10 for composite (Hilton et al, 2013). In cervical erosion lesions the books differ: Craig reports clinical retention of GIC considerably better than composite (Sakaguchi et al, 2019), while monomeric materials resist toothbrush abrasion and acid erosive episodes better than GIC (Peumans et al, 2020, quoted in Hill and Gillam, 2024). For a minimally invasive cavity, an adhesive material should support the tooth, promote remineralisation with possible antibacterial activity, seal residual bacteria and restore function and appearance (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S3. Evidence [prognosis]
A meta-analysis of RCTs found less secondary caries with GIC than amalgam, but no significant difference from composite (primary teeth RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.77-1.10; permanent teeth RR 0.77, 0.39-1.51) (Ge et al, 2023). The Pickard Manual gives average replacement ages of about 3-5 years for GIC and 5-8 for composite (Banerjee and Watson, 2011). Conventional composites accumulate more biofilm on their surface than amalgam or glass ionomer cement (Ionescu and Hahnel, 2021).

### S4. The combination
The sandwich technique places GIC as dentine replacement under a composite enamel replacement, and uses the strengths of both (see Q7 and Q14) (Mount et al, 2016).

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement and composite resin are the two principal tooth-coloured direct restoratives; they differ in setting chemistry, adhesion, strength and biological action (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### 2. Comparison table
| Feature | Glass ionomer cement | Composite resin |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | acid-base reaction, water-based (Mount et al, 2016) | polymerisation of resin |
| Adhesion | chemical to enamel and dentine; no acid etching, but the tooth is conditioned with polyacrylic acid (Mount, 1994; Sikri, 2020) | micromechanical; excellent to enamel, uncertain to dentine (Mount, 1994) |
| Shrinkage | about 3% by volume, developing slowly and controlled by adhesion; resin-modified GIC 3-4% (Mount et al, 2016; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) | 1-5% (Mount, 1994) |
| Fluoride | release and recharge (McCabe and Walls, 2008) | none (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Strength | weaker, lacks rigidity, brittle (Mount et al, 2016) | stronger under load (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Wear | 73 micrometres a year in one measure (Hilton et al, 2013) | under 10 (Hilton et al, 2013) |
| Aesthetics | opaque, dulls (Sikri, 2020) | highest of the direct materials (general textbook statement; no page cited) |
| Radiopacity | not radiopaque in McCabe and Walls (2008); Mount (1994) says most auto-cure cements are radiolucent and most dual-cure cements are radiopaque | radiopaque (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Moisture | water sensitive; must be protected early (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Sikri, 2020) | bonding needs a dry field (general knowledge; no page cited) |
| Biocompatibility | a mild pulp irritant, especially over dentine; not for pulp exposure (Sikri, 2020) | possible release of unreacted monomer (general knowledge; no page cited) |
| Replacement age | about 3-5 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) | about 5-8 years (Banerjee and Watson, 2011) |

### 2A. Bond strength and the interface
Conventional GIC gives macroshear bond strengths of 6-12 MPa, against 22-35 MPa for modern dentine bonding agents, but GIC fails inside the cement and wets tooth well (Sturdevant 5th ed, Roberson et al, 2006; Mount et al, 2016). Composite shrinkage pulls the material from the wall, and when the stress exceeds the bond the adhesion is lost, which compromises the seal (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 2B. Cervical lesions
- A resin-modified GIC was retained in 96.4% of restorations at 5 years against 51.5% for bonded composite; in a later study conventional GIC retention was 67%, against 94% for resin-modified GIC and 81-85% for composite (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Flowable composites give better aesthetics than GIC in non-carious cervical lesions (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- Resin-modified GIC colour stability may be doubtful over longer periods (one case showed discolouration at 6 years) (Banerjee and Watson, 2011).

### 2C. Class II restorations
Conventional GIC is not recommended for Class II in adults (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). A high-viscosity GIC with a resin coat (glass hybrid) survived as well as nano-hybrid composite over 5 years in moderate-large two-surface Class II cavities (94.5% against 94.4%); the success rate was numerically lower (81.9% against 90.7%), reported as no significant difference overall (Miletic et al, 2024, abstract only).

### 2D. Clinical handling
- **GIC:** isolate, condition (Sikri, 2020: 20% polyacrylic acid for 10-20 seconds; Mount, 1994: 10% for 10-15 seconds; Torres, 2020: 10-25% for 15-30 seconds), mix, place under a matrix and seal at once (Sikri, 2020). Finishing: Sikri (2020) says the matrix finish is best and many clinicians delay initial contouring for 24 hours; Craig (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) says finish after 24 hours; Mount (1994) contours and polishes Type II.2 cement about 6 minutes after the start of mix.
- **Technique tolerance:** GIC is easier than composite at subgingival margins, and a GIC base is preferred in Class II with subgingival extension (Sikri, 2020).
- **Marginal behaviour:** composite has no defence against caries once a gap forms; GIC bonds and releases fluoride at the margin (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Sikri, 2020).

### 3. Indications
- **GIC:** Class V and root caries, high caries risk, primary teeth, transitional restorations, ART, subgingival margins, liners and bases (Sikri, 2020; Hilton et al, 2013).
- **Composite:** anterior and posterior load-bearing restorations where aesthetics and wear resistance matter (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013).

### 4. Evidence
A meta-analysis of RCTs found less secondary caries with GIC than amalgam but no difference from composite (Ge et al, 2023).

### 5. Combination: sandwich technique
GIC as dentine replacement under composite as enamel replacement; used with margins on dentine (Mount et al, 2016).

### 5A. Where the intermediate materials sit
Powers describes a hierarchy: composites release little or no fluoride; compomers are less wear-resistant than composite but aesthetic and release fluoride; resin-modified GIC releases more fluoride than compomers but wears more than they do (Powers and Wataha, 2013). They are therefore compromises between the two ends of the table.

### 6. Conclusion
Composite is stronger, aesthetic and wear-resistant; GIC is adhesive and fluoride-releasing but water-sensitive early and needs protection. The choice depends on load, caries risk, moisture control and aesthetics, and the sandwich restoration combines both.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- GIC: chemical adhesion, fluoride, little shrinkage, brittle and wears (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Composite: strong, no fluoride, shrinks, dentine bond uncertain (Mount, 1994; McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Secondary caries: GIC better than amalgam, not better than composite (Ge et al, 2023).
- Load decides: composite for heavy load (Mount et al, 2016).
