## STUDY MODE

### S1. What it is and why it was made [basic science]
Conventional GIC sets slowly and is water sensitive early, so manufacturers added a small amount of resin to give a "command set" with light (Bhat et al, 2019; Sakaguchi et al, 2019*). Sikri describes HEMA grafted onto the polyacrylic acid; light polymerises the methacrylate groups, then the acid-base reaction continues (Sikri, 2020).

### S2. Composition and setting
The liquid holds 15-25% resin (HEMA and other methacrylates) as well as polyacid, tartaric acid and water; the powder is a glass with light initiators (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020). Setting has two reactions: acid-base and free-radical polymerisation. In the materials Mount describes, any resin not set by light auto-cures by redox, so the material sets in the dark too (Mount et al, 2016). Terms differ between books: Sikri, Bhat and Bonsor call acid-base plus light "dual-cure" and reserve the redox dark cure for "tri-cure" types, while Mount et al (2016) describe redox auto-cure as part of resin-modified GIC in general, call "auto cure plus light assisted cure" dual cure, and do not use the word tri-cure (Sikri, 2020; Bhat et al, 2019; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); Q11b gives the tri-cure definitions. More resin means less acid-base reaction, until the glass-ionomer benefits are lost and the material is only a light-cured cement (Mount, 1994).

### S3. Properties [treatment relevance]
Advantages: stronger and nearly insoluble, early water resistance, better aesthetics, long working time with snap set, immediate finishing, repairable, chemical and micromechanical bonding, less post-operative sensitivity (Sikri, 2020). Books disagree on fluoride release: slightly higher than conventional GIC, mostly in the first 10-15 days (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); unchanged from conventional GIC (Anusavice et al, 2013); lower, since glass ionomers release the most (Powers and Wataha, 2013); lower than conventional GIC (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021); the same fluoride release characteristics as conventional GI cements for luting types (Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed, Ritter et al, 2019). Disadvantages: HEMA is hydrophilic, so it takes up water over 5-7 days with swelling and stain uptake (Mount et al, 2016); the swelling is real but small and clinically minor in dual-cure restoratives, as Mount (1994) reports (about 3% by volume), and larger in early linings, and it offsets part of the resin shrinkage (Sikri, 2020); residual HEMA is 4.5-15% and can release into dentine (Mount et al, 2016); its colour can change after years (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Shrinkage is up to 1% of the resin part in Mount (1994) but 3-4%, more than composite, in Bonsor and Pearson (2013), and Sikri notes occasionally increased microleakage (Sikri, 2020). Polymerisation heat can raise temperature by up to 12 C (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013), about 10 C above ambient in a medium cavity (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

### S4. Uses and cautions
Used for Class III and V, liners, bases and bonding composite in sandwich restorations (Sikri, 2020). Light only cures to the depth of penetration, so build up in increments (Mount et al, 2016). Craig advises against a dentine bonding agent before hybrid ionomer because it reduces fluoride release (Craig, 2002), whereas Phillips says a hybrid ionomer with nonreactive filler needs a dentine bonding system, so follow the manufacturer (Anusavice et al, 2013). Avoid under all-ceramic restorations because of hygroscopic expansion (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011); Phillips links the HEMA water uptake, an expansion of up to about 8% by volume, to fracture of all-ceramic crowns (Anusavice et al, 2013). RMGIC in direct contact with the pulp is contraindicated (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); use calcium hydroxide first (Anusavice et al, 2013). HEMA is a contact allergen, so wear gloves and avoid touching the mix (Craig, 2002). Garg and Garg (2011) add that the slower, delayed acid-base reaction gives an additional period of maximum flexibility to absorb stress from adjacent shrinking composite when RMGI is used as a liner (Garg and Garg, 2011); Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed calls claims that RMGI liners reduce shrinkage stress not supported by current clinical evidence (Ritter et al, 2019). A light-cured RMGIC varnish (Vanish XT) has been used for dentine hypersensitivity, with one 6-month trial showing benefit (Mosquim et al, in Gillam, 2024).

### S5. Biocompatibility
HEMA release is linked to cytotoxicity in laboratory tests (Agha et al, 2021). In cell culture, extracts of experimental RMGICs with a different monomer system gave 89-98% viability against 4% for a commercial RMGIC liner (Vitrebond), and adding HEMA did not significantly change viability within the experimental series (Potiprapanpong et al, 2023; abstract only, in vitro, not applicable to restorative RMGICs or the in vivo pulp). A systematic review quoted in a textbook found RMGICs remineralise affected dentine like conventional GIC, with additives such as bioactive glass or CPP-ACP improving this, supporting use after selective caries removal (Ghilotti et al, 2023, as quoted in Gillam, 2024).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Resin-modified (light-cure or hybrid) glass ionomer cement is conventional GIC with a small amount of water-soluble resin, usually HEMA, added so that it sets by light as well as by acid-base reaction (Sikri, 2020; Hill, 2007). It was introduced in the 1980s (Hill, 2007), the late 1980s (Sakaguchi et al, 2019*) or the early 1990s (Powers and Wataha, 2013), depending on book.

### 2. Composition
- **Powder:** ion-leachable fluoroaluminosilicate glass plus light-curing initiators (Sikri, 2020).
- **Liquid:** polyacrylic acid (some with methacrylate side groups), HEMA and other methacrylates, 15-25% resin, tartaric acid, water (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Alternative names:** hybrid ionomer, resin ionomer, dual-cure and tri-cure GIC (Bhat et al, 2019).

### 3. Setting reaction
1. Light initiates polymerisation of the methacrylate groups, giving a quick set to the depth the light reaches.
2. Acid-base reaction continues in the dark between glass and polyacid.
3. Unreacted resin auto-cures by redox in Mount's account (Mount et al, 2016); Sikri and Bhat reserve redox cure for tri-cure types (Sikri, 2020; Bhat et al, 2019).
4. Matures over days and weeks (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3A. Variants
- **Resin-modified auto-cure:** very fine powder with a catalyst so that it sets hard without light; useful in minimal cavities where light does not penetrate (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Light-sensitive powders:** some contain a light activator, so dispense just before mixing; capsules avoid the problem (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Dual cure restoratives** show only very small water uptake (volume increase about 3%, of no clinical importance), unlike early dual-cure linings (Mount, 1994).
- **Shrinkage:** light activation gives up to 1% shrinkage of the resin part, with the acid-base reaction continuing after (Mount, 1994); Bonsor gives 3-4%, more than composite (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### 4. Properties
| Property | RMGIC |
|---|---|
| Strength | stronger than conventional; tensile 2,000-3,000 psi against 900-960 (Sikri, 2020) |
| Solubility | 0.08% against 0.3-0.5% (Sikri, 2020) |
| Working and set | long working time, snap set with light (Sikri, 2020) |
| Adhesion | chemical plus micromechanical; excellent bond to composite as a base (Sikri, 2020) |
| Handling | working 3 min 45 s and set 20 s with light, against 2 min and 4 min for GIC (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Fluoride | slightly higher than conventional (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); unchanged (Anusavice et al, 2013); lower (Powers and Wataha, 2013; Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021) |
| Water | rapid uptake over 5-7 days, swelling (Mount et al, 2016), small in restoratives (Mount, 1994) |

### 5. Advantages
Better aesthetics, early water resistance, immediate finishing, repairable, minimal post-operative sensitivity, condensable (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Disadvantages
HEMA water uptake, swelling, stain, long-term colour change; polymerisation shrinkage and heat (up to 12 C, Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); residual HEMA and possible cytotoxicity; less ion exchange as resin increases (Mount et al, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount, 1994).

### 7. Clinical uses
Class III and V restorations, liner and base under composite, sandwich technique, orthodontic bands, fissure sealant, primary teeth (Sikri, 2020).

### 8. Cautions
Use increments; bonding agent first is advised against (Craig, 2002) but needed for filled types (Anusavice et al, 2013), so follow the maker; avoid under all-ceramic restorations; no direct pulp contact (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); protect from dehydration (Mount et al, 2016; Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011; Sikri, 2020).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Conventional GIC plus 15-25% HEMA-based resin; acid-base plus light, redox backup in Mount (Mount et al, 2016), but only in tri-cure types in Sikri (Sikri, 2020).
- Advantages: early water resistance, command set, immediate finishing, better aesthetics (Sikri, 2020).
- Disadvantages: HEMA water uptake, swelling, colour change (Mount et al, 2016).
- Example: Fuji II LC (Sikri, 2020).
