## STUDY MODE

### S1. What it is and why it was made [basic science]
Conventional GIC sets slowly and is water sensitive early, so manufacturers added a small amount of resin to give a "command set" with light (Bhat et al, 2019; Sakaguchi et al, 2019*). Sikri describes HEMA grafted onto the polyacrylic acid; light polymerises the methacrylate groups, then the acid-base reaction continues (Sikri, 2020).

### S2. Composition and setting
The liquid holds 15-25% resin (HEMA and other methacrylates) as well as polyacid, tartaric acid and water; the powder is a glass with light initiators (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020). Setting has two reactions: acid-base and free-radical polymerisation. Any resin not set by light auto-cures by redox, so the material sets in the dark too (Mount et al, 2016). More resin means less acid-base reaction, until the glass-ionomer benefits are lost and the material is only a light-cured cement (Mount, 1994).

### S3. Properties [treatment relevance]
Advantages: stronger and nearly insoluble, early water resistance, better aesthetics, long working time with snap set, immediate finishing, repairable, chemical and micromechanical bonding, less post-operative sensitivity (Sikri, 2020). Fluoride release is similar to conventional GIC, slightly higher in the first weeks (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Disadvantages: HEMA is hydrophilic, so it takes up water over 5-7 days with swelling and stain uptake (Mount et al, 2016); residual HEMA is 4.5-15% and can release into dentine (Mount et al, 2016); its colour can change after years (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S4. Uses and cautions
Used for Class III and V, liners, bases and bonding composite in sandwich restorations (Sikri, 2020). Light only cures to the depth of penetration, so build up in increments (Mount et al, 2016). Do not use a dentine bonding agent before hybrid ionomer, since it reduces fluoride release (Craig, 2002). Avoid under all-ceramic restorations because of hygroscopic expansion (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011).

### S5. Biocompatibility
HEMA release is linked to cytotoxicity in laboratory tests (Agha et al, 2021); experimental RMGICs with less HEMA gave far higher cell viability than a commercial RMGIC liner in cell culture (Potiprapanpong et al, 2023).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Resin-modified (light-cure or hybrid) glass ionomer cement is conventional GIC with a small amount of water-soluble resin, usually HEMA, added so that it sets by light as well as by acid-base reaction (Sikri, 2020; Hill, 2007). It was introduced in the late 1980s or early 1990s (Sakaguchi et al, 2019*; Powers and Wataha, 2013).

### 2. Composition
- **Powder:** ion-leachable fluoroaluminosilicate glass plus light-curing initiators (Sikri, 2020).
- **Liquid:** polyacrylic acid (some with methacrylate side groups), HEMA and other methacrylates, 15-25% resin, tartaric acid, water (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Alternative names:** hybrid ionomer, resin ionomer, dual-cure and tri-cure GIC (Bhat et al, 2019).

### 3. Setting reaction
1. Light initiates polymerisation of the methacrylate groups, giving a quick set to the depth the light reaches.
2. Acid-base reaction continues in the dark between glass and polyacid.
3. Unreacted resin auto-cures by redox (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).
4. Matures over days and weeks (Mount et al, 2016).

### 3A. Variants
- **Resin-modified auto-cure:** very fine powder with a catalyst so that it sets hard without light; useful in minimal cavities where light does not penetrate (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Light-sensitive powders:** some contain a light activator, so dispense just before mixing; capsules avoid the problem (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Dual cure restoratives** show only very small water uptake (volume increase about 3%, of no clinical importance), unlike early dual-cure linings (Mount, 1994).
- **Shrinkage:** light activation gives up to 1% shrinkage of the resin part, with the acid-base reaction continuing after (Mount, 1994).

### 4. Properties
| Property | RMGIC |
|---|---|
| Strength | stronger than conventional; tensile 2,000-3,000 psi against 900-960 (Sikri, 2020) |
| Solubility | 0.08% against 0.3-0.5% (Sikri, 2020) |
| Working and set | long working time, snap set with light (Sikri, 2020) |
| Adhesion | chemical plus micromechanical; excellent bond to composite as a base (Sikri, 2020) |
| Fluoride | similar to conventional, slightly higher early (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) |
| Water | rapid uptake over 5-7 days, swelling (Mount et al, 2016) |

### 5. Advantages
Better aesthetics, early water resistance, immediate finishing, repairable, minimal post-operative sensitivity, condensable (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Disadvantages
HEMA water uptake, swelling, stain, long-term colour change; polymerisation shrinkage; residual HEMA and possible cytotoxicity; less ion exchange as resin increases (Mount et al, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2015; Mount, 1994).

### 7. Clinical uses
Class III and V restorations, liner and base under composite, sandwich technique, orthodontic bands, fissure sealant, primary teeth (Sikri, 2020).

### 8. Cautions
Use increments; do not place a bonding agent first; avoid under all-ceramic restorations; protect from dehydration (Mount et al, 2016; Craig, 2002; Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011; Sikri, 2020).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Conventional GIC plus 15-25% HEMA-based resin; acid-base plus light, redox backup (Mount et al, 2016).
- Advantages: early water resistance, command set, immediate finishing, better aesthetics (Sikri, 2020).
- Disadvantages: HEMA water uptake, swelling, colour change (Mount et al, 2016).
- Example: Fuji II LC (Sikri, 2020).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling RMGIC a composite.
2. Forgetting the acid-base reaction continues in the dark.
3. Ignoring HEMA water uptake and cytotoxicity.
