# Non-radiographic methods for working length determination.

**Marks:** 20 · Long essay

**Answer blueprint:** Definition and aim → Average tooth length → Patient response → Tactile sense → Paper point → Electronic apex locators → Other methods → Comparison → Combining with radiographs → Conclusion.

## Definition and aim

**Working length** is the distance from a coronal reference point to where canal preparation and obturation should end, ideally the **apical constriction** (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012). The older methods (average length, patient response, tactile sense, paper point) are too unreliable to be the main technique (Malkhassian et al., 2012); the **electronic apex locator (EAL)** is the exception.

## 1. Average tooth length

Teeth are typically **19-25 mm** long, roots 9-15 mm (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); this gives only general guidance (Schäfer, 2017). The constriction lies 0-4 mm from the apex, so average values are **clinically unacceptable** as an endpoint (Attin and Hoer, 2010).

## 2. Patient response

Length was once taken where the patient felt the file: a pulp remnant gave too short a length, a periapical lesion too long (Weine, 2004). It hurts, inflamed tissue reacts short of the apex, and instruments may pass beyond it unfelt (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); anaesthesia prevents it (Jain, 2016). **Obsolete.**

## 3. Tactile sense

A small file is advanced slowly after patency; resistance is taken as the constriction (Sikri, 2019). Cons: obstructions mimic it, gloves blunt feel, open apices and narrow canals defeat it (Sikri, 2019). Many apices have no constriction (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); in small cylindrical canals the file binds coronally; **preflaring improves feel** (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021). Helpful in under 60% (Malkhassian et al., 2012).

## 4. Paper point (moisture or bleeding)

Dry the canal; leave a sized paper point ten seconds; a wet or blood-stained tip means the foramen was passed (Sikri, 2019). Seepage and capillary action blur the tide-mark (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) and no scientific evidence supports routine use (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). Use as a **supplementary check** (Weine, 2004).

## 5. Electronic apex locators

**Principle:** resistance between periodontium and oral mucosa is a constant 6.5 kilo-ohms; the device signals when the file reaches the periodontal ligament (Munce et al., 2019). Modern units use impedance, highest at the constriction (Attin and Hoer, 2010).

| Generation (Sikri, 2019) | Principle | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| First: resistance | Direct-current resistance | Fluid gives false "apex"; pacemakers contraindicated |
| Third: frequency | Impedance difference at two frequencies | Works with pus and pulp |
| Sixth: adaptive | Adapts to dry or wet canal | Accuracy yet to be established |

Books number the fourth and fifth differently: modified-frequency and Bluetooth (Sikri, 2019); dry-canal database and separate capacitance-resistance units (Munce et al., 2019).

**Technique:**
1. Isolate, keep the canal slightly wet, dry the chamber, avoid metal contact (Weine, 2004; Munce et al., 2019).
2. Attach lip clip and a small stopped file (Weine, 2004).
3. Advance to the "apex" mark; some pass the foramen then withdraw to the constriction (Malkhassian et al., 2012), others pull back about 0.5 mm (Peters and Arias, 2025).
4. Record the length and confirm radiographically (Weine, 2004).

**Advantages:** no radiation, so suits gag reflex, handicapped or pregnant patients (Munce et al., 2019); structures over molar apices, perforations, resorption level (Sikri, 2019); 54% shorter time (Malkhassian et al., 2012); continuous monitoring (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

**Limitations:** metal restorations, bleeding, saliva, open apices (Malkhassian et al., 2012); false positives from short circuits, false negatives in calcified or retreated canals (Attin and Hoer, 2010); battery below 7 V (Sikri, 2019); no data on canal width, curvature or number (Weine, 2004). **Pacemakers:** avoid (Teixeira et al., 2021) versus concern unnecessary (Munce et al., 2019).

**Accuracy (books differ):** 90% (Sikri, 2019); 80-95% (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); 90.4% versus 82.1% for radiographs (Munce et al., 2019); within 0.5 mm in 100% versus 15% for radiographs (Barnett, 2017).

## 6. Other methods

Jain (2016) describes the historical **apex finder** (Negm, 1982: a barbed shaft resisting withdrawal at the apical margin) and **audiometric method** (a tone matched between sulcus and canal).

## Comparison

| Method | Merit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Average length | Quick | Variable |
| Patient response | Historic | Painful |
| Tactile | Cheap | Subjective |
| Paper point | Simple | Unproven |
| Apex locator | Objective | Not open apices |

## Combining with radiographs

1. Flare the coronal canal first (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
2. Take the EAL reading, then a confirmatory radiograph (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).
3. If the film shows the EAL length well short of the apex, accept the EAL; if over 3 mm short of the desired position, adjust and re-expose (Jain, 2016).
4. Triangulate with tactile sense and paper points (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014); accuracy rises from 84% to 96% (Malkhassian et al., 2012).

Weight differs: EAL as primary (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); never replacing radiographs (Weine, 2004).

## Conclusion

The EAL is the most reliable non-radiographic method, used with radiographs.
