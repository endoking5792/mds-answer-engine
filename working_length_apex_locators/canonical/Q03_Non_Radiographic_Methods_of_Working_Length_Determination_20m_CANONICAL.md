# Non-radiographic methods for working length determination.

**Marks:** 20 · Long essay

**Answer blueprint:** Definition and aim → Average tooth length → Patient response → Tactile sense → Paper point → Electronic apex locators → Other methods → Comparison → Combining with radiographs → Conclusion.

## Definition and aim

**Working length** is the distance from a coronal reference point to where canal preparation and obturation should end, ideally the **apical constriction** (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012). The older methods are too unreliable to be the main technique (Malkhassian et al., 2012); the **electronic apex locator (EAL)** is the most accurate (Attin and Hoer, 2010).

## 1. Average tooth length

Teeth are typically **19-25 mm** long, roots 9-15 mm (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); general guidance only (Schäfer, 2017). Means run from about 20 mm (second molars) to 26.5 mm (maxillary canines) (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014). The constriction lies 0-4 mm (mean 1 mm) from the apex, so average values as an endpoint are **clinically unacceptable** (Attin and Hoer, 2010).

## 2. Patient response

Length was once taken where the patient felt the file; a pulp remnant gave too short a length, a periapical lesion too long (Weine, 2004). It hurts, inflamed tissue reacts short of the apex, instruments may pass beyond unfelt (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); anaesthesia prevents it, and it is subjective (Jain, 2016). **Unreliable.**

## 3. Tactile sense

A small file is advanced slowly after patency; resistance is taken as the constriction (Sikri, 2019). It is the least accurate method and insufficient alone; with multiple constrictions only the first is felt (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009). Obstructions mimic it, gloves blunt feel (Sikri, 2019); many apices have no constriction (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); in small cylindrical canals the file binds coronally, and **preflaring improves feel** (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021).

## 4. Paper point (moisture or bleeding)

Dry the canal; leave a sized paper point ten seconds; a wet or blood-stained tip indicates over-extension (Sikri, 2019); the deepest dry point marks the length (Schäfer, 2017). Capillary action blurs the tide-mark (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) and no scientific evidence supports routine use (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). Use only as a **supplementary check** (Schäfer, 2017; Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

## 5. Electronic apex locators

**Principle:** resistance between periodontium and oral mucosa is a constant 6.5 kilo-ohms, which let the first unit (1962) estimate the apical foramen (Munce et al., 2019). Modern units use impedance, highest at the constriction (Attin and Hoer, 2010), and locate a point between constriction and foramen (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009).

| Generation (Sikri, 2019) | Principle | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| First | Direct-current resistance | Fluid gives false "apex"; pacemakers contraindicated |
| Third | Impedance at two frequencies | Works with pus and pulp |
| Sixth | Adaptive | Accuracy yet to be established |

**Technique:**
1. Keep the canal slightly wet, dry the chamber, avoid metal contact; attach lip clip and a small stopped file (Weine, 2004; Munce et al., 2019).
2. Advance to the "apex" mark; some pass the foramen then withdraw to the constriction (Malkhassian et al., 2012), others pull back about 0.5 mm (Peters and Arias, 2025).
3. Record the length and confirm radiographically (Weine, 2004).

**Advantages:** useful where radiographs are hard (gag reflex, handicapped or pregnant patients) (Munce et al., 2019); perforations, resorption level (Sikri, 2019); 54% less time (Malkhassian et al., 2012).

**Limitations:** metal restorations, bleeding, saliva, open apices (Malkhassian et al., 2012); false positives from short circuits, false negatives in calcified or retreated canals (Attin and Hoer, 2010); no data on canal width, curvature or number (Weine, 2004). **Pacemakers:** contraindicated (Sikri, 2019); consult the cardiologist (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009); concern unnecessary (Munce et al., 2019).

**Accuracy (books differ):** 90% (Sikri, 2019); 90.4% versus 82.1% for radiographs in one trial (Munce et al., 2019); within 0.5 mm in 100% versus 15% in one study (Barnett, 2017). Endpoints differ.

## 6. Other methods

Jain (2016) describes the historical **apex finder** (introduced 1982: a barbed shaft resisting withdrawal at the apical margin) and **audiometric method** (a tone matched at sulcus and canal).

## Comparison

| Method | Limitation |
|---|---|
| Average length | Teeth vary |
| Patient, tactile | Painful, subjective |
| Paper point | Unproven |
| Apex locator | Cost, metal, open apices |

## Combining with radiographs

1. Flare first (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); take the EAL reading, then a confirmatory radiograph (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).
2. If the film shows the EAL length well short of the apex, choosing the EAL is recommended; if over 3 mm short of the desired position, adjust and re-expose (Jain, 2016).
3. Triangulate with tactile sense and paper points (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014). Root ZX alone gave 84%, with a radiograph 96% (Malkhassian et al., 2012).

Weight differs: EAL as primary means (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); never replacing radiographs (Weine, 2004).

## Conclusion

No method gives an exact length (Hülsmann and Schäfer, 2009); use the EAL with radiographs.
