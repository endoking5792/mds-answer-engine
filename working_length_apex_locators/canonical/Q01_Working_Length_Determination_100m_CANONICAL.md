# Describe working length determination during endodontic treatment

**Marks:** 100 · Single essay

## 1. Introduction and definitions

**Working length (WL)** is the distance from a coronal reference point to the point at which canal preparation and obturation should terminate (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Gopikrishna, 2021; Barnett, 2017). Gulabivala and Ng (2014) separate the **canal length** (reference point to canal terminus) from the working length, the length to which each instrument is used; some instruments are used short of it (step-back) or beyond it (patency). Most books treat the two as one measurement, and so does this answer.

| Term | Meaning (Gopikrishna, 2021 unless stated) |
|---|---|
| Anatomical apex | Tip of the root as determined morphologically |
| Radiographic apex | Tip of the root as seen on the radiograph |
| Apical foramen (major diameter) | Main apical opening, frequently eccentric to the anatomical or radiographic apex |
| Apical constriction (minor diameter) | Narrowest part of the canal; usually 0.5 to 1.0 mm short of the centre of the foramen (Jain, 2016) |
| Cementodentinal junction (CDJ) | Where dentin and cementum unite, a histologic landmark; 0.5 to 3.0 mm from the anatomical apex (Jain, 2016) |

**Why the apex matters.** Tissue apical to the CDJ is fibrous connective tissue from the periodontal ligament, not pulp (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- The foramen coincides with the anatomical apex in less than 50% of teeth (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); Sikri (2019) states that it deviates in at least two-thirds, and Barnett (2017) puts the figure at 60 to 80%, up to 3.0 mm away. Deviation is buccal or lingual twice as often as mesial or distal (Weine, 2004), so it is invisible on a straight radiograph.
- The constriction lies on average 1 mm from the apex, with a range of 0 to 4 mm, so average values are clinically unacceptable (Attin and Hoer, 2010). In a micro-CT series the constriction-to-radiographic-apex distance averaged 0.9 mm and radiographs located it within 0.5 mm in only 32% of canals (Barnett, 2017).
- Foramen to constriction averages 0.5 mm (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); Kuttler's figures are 0.524 mm at 18 to 25 years and 0.659 mm after 55 (Weine, 2004).
- A constriction is not constant: it is absent in many teeth with apical resorption (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014), and is classed as five types (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) or four (Jain, 2016). The CDJ may lie up to 3.0 mm higher on one wall than the other (Sikri, 2019).

## 2. Importance of working length

Weine (2004) lists four effects: it fixes how far instruments and debris removal reach; it limits the depth of the root filling; it governs postoperative pain; and it largely decides success or failure.
1. **Biological:** ending at the constriction means no apical or periodontal injury and preserves accessory canals (Attin and Hoer, 2010); the constriction has the least blood supply (Sikri, 2019) and gives the smallest wound for healing (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012).
2. **Microbiological:** violating the apex can inoculate the periapical tissues with necrotic tissue, debris and bacteria (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); working short where organisms are present may sustain apical periodontitis (Peters and Arias, 2025).
3. **Prognostic:** two meta-analyses link both underfilling and overfilling with a worse prognosis (Schäfer, 2017). Each millimetre lost from the radiographic apex raised the odds of failure by about 14% and 12% in two studies, and all outcome studies found overextension harmful (Barnett, 2017).
4. **Technical:** it governs apical seat formation, sealing and control of filling material, and (reasoning) the working width chosen later.

## 3. Where should the working length end?

Sikri (2019) lists four views: short of the radiographic apex (0.5 mm, later 1.0 mm), at the radiographic apex, beyond it, and at the CDJ. Short-of-apex lengths risk teardrop preparations when the canal exits over 1.0 mm short (Sikri, 2019). Weine (2004) cites a study in which, of 40 teeth, half had files extending 1.0 mm or more through the foramen when placed at the radiographic apex. Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011) describe two incompatible philosophies, confinement to the constriction or extension to the root apex, with no evidence-based data for either. Full apical-limit theory belongs to the Root Canal Anatomy answer.

| Author | End point advised |
|---|---|
| Weine (2004) | CDJ (minor diameter, from Kuttler's study); notes that all four methods have given excellent results |
| Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang (2021) | 1 mm from the radiographic apex |
| Walton and Torabinejad (2002) | Optimal 1 to 2 mm short; 0 to 2 mm if necrotic, 0 to 3 mm if vital |
| Peters, Peters and Basrani (2021) | 0.5 to 1 mm short in necrosis, 1 to 2 mm in irreversible pulpitis; no definitive validation |
| Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011) | Vital: as near the constriction as possible; necrotic: near the canal exit or slightly short of the foramen |
| Barnett (2017) | Vital: short of the foramen; necrotic with apical periodontitis: closer to the radiographic apex |
| Jain (2016) | Necrotic without lesion: physiological foramen |

The books therefore differ, and no single figure is correct for every tooth.

## 4. Preparation before measurement

1. **Coronal pre-enlargement.** Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011) warn that a length taken before straight-line access may be 1 mm less or even shorter afterwards. Grossman recommends enlarging with orifice enlargers or Gates-Glidden drills before the WL radiograph (Gopikrishna, 2021); preflaring also improves tactile sensation (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021).
2. **Reference point.** Incisal edge in anterior teeth, cusp tips in posterior teeth; fractured or undermined cusps are ground down (Gopikrishna, 2021). Walton and Torabinejad (2002) prefer the highest incisal point, a buccal cusp, and the mesiobuccal cusp in molars. The point can alter length by at least 1 mm, so it is recorded with the length (Weine, 2004; Attin and Hoer, 2010).
3. **Stops.** Silicone stops fitted perpendicular to the file and touching the reference point (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021).
4. **Initial estimate** from an undistorted preoperative radiograph. Teeth are typically 19 to 25 mm and roots 9 to 15 mm long (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Weine (2004) uses tables of average lengths: the 95th percentile for long teeth, the average, or the 5th percentile for short teeth.

## 5. Methods of determination

Grossman's classification (Gopikrishna, 2021): **I electronic** (recommended); **II radiographic** (Ingle's technique, Best, Bregman, Bramante, Grossman, Weine, Kuttler, grid, xeroradiography, direct digital); **III non-radiographic** (tactile sense, apical periodontal sensitivity, paper point), not recommended as sole methods.

### 5.1 Non-radiographic methods

| Method | How it works | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Tactile | Resistance felt as the constriction (Sikri, 2019) | Any obstruction feels like a constriction (Sikri, 2019); in small cylindrical canals file taper exceeds canal taper and binds coronally (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); helpful in under 60% of cases (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); improved by step-down preparation (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) |
| Patient response | Pain when the file meets vital tissue (Jain, 2016) | Impossible under anaesthesia (Jain, 2016); a necrotic canal may hold vital inflamed tissue several millimetres in; pain is absent beyond the apex (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |
| Paper point | Wet or bloody tip means overextension (Sikri, 2019) | No scientific evidence for routine use (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); blood seeps by capillary action (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014); suitable only to supplement (Schäfer, 2017) |

Weine (2004) records that early WL was taken where the patient felt the instrument, giving frequent errors. Two minor methods (Jain, 2016) are Negm's apex finder (1982), a barbed plastic shaft that engages the apical margin, and the audiometric method.

### 5.2 Radiographic methods

A file is placed in the canal and a radiograph exposed. Use a **modified paralleling technique**, shown superior to bisecting-angle (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); Attin and Hoer (2010) regard bisecting-angle and panoramic films as unsuitable. The file must be fixed so it does not move before the film is read, and rubber dam is retained.

**Ingle's method** (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021):
1. Measure tooth length on the preoperative radiograph from the stable reference point to the radiographic apex.
2. Subtract at least 1 mm for the constriction lying short of the apex and for image distortion (Gopikrishna, 2021).
3. Set a stop at that length on a small K-file, insert it with the stop at the reference point, and expose a radiograph.
4. Read the tip: if it is at the apex, the length stands; if beyond, subtract the overshoot; if short, add the shortfall (Sikri, 2019).
5. Final allowance: Sikri (2019) subtracts a further 1.0 to 1.5 mm, whereas Gopikrishna (2021) ends the instrument 0.5 to 1.0 mm from the radiographic apex, retaking the film if it is over 1.0 mm out.
6. In curved canals about 1.0 mm may be lost as the canal straightens, so length is reaffirmed (Sikri, 2019).

**Diagram:** Ingle's method as a flow chart: pre-op measurement, minus 1 mm, file radiograph, three outcomes (short, on, beyond), correction, retake.

| Variant | Principle |
|---|---|
| Coolidge and Kesel | Wire radiograph, ratio of image to actual length; abandoned as angulation gave incorrect results (Sikri, 2019) |
| Best; Bergman | A 10-mm steel pin on the labial surface; a 25-mm probe with a stop penetrating 10 mm (Jain, 2016). Grossman's text prints Bregman (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Bramante | Right-angled steel probes, as Bergman or Ingle (Jain, 2016) |
| Grossman | Canal length on a diagnostic file; WL 0.5 to 1.0 mm shorter (Sikri, 2019) |
| Kuttler/Weine | Locate major or minor diameter on the pre-op film, choose file by estimated width (size 10 or 15 if narrow, 20 or 25 if average), subtract 0.5 mm (up to 35 years) or 0.67 mm (older) from the major diameter; mnemonic C-L-E-W (canal configuration, length, exit, width) (Weine, 2004) |
| Walton | Subtract 3 mm from the pre-op length (1 mm foramen offset plus 2 mm magnification); working film with a binding file of No. 25 or larger (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |

**Books differ on the film.**

| Point | Positions |
|---|---|
| Smallest file on the film | ISO 15 (Attin and Hoer, 2010; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); No. 20 (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); No. 25 or larger, avoiding 8 or 10 (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |
| Accept the file if | within 1 mm of ideal (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); 0 to 2 mm from apex (Schäfer, 2017) |
| Repeat film if | correction over 3 mm (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021; Jain, 2016) or discrepancy over 3 mm (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |

**Multi-canal teeth.** Place files in all canals and apply the buccal object (SLOB) rule: the buccal structure moves opposite to the tubehead (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). A 20 to 30 degree horizontal shift is usually enough; mesial angles suit premolars and maxillary molars and distal angles mandibular incisors and molars (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021). Two views separate a Type II configuration (Weine, 2004).

**Limitations.** A two-dimensional image of a three-dimensional root (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); the zygomatic arch overlies 20% of first and 42% of second maxillary molar apices (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); the file may already be beyond the foramen at the radiographic apex (Schäfer, 2017); interpretation is subjective and radiation is added. Radiographs gave overestimation in 51% of canals against 21% with an apex locator (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012). Their strength is anatomical information: curvature, accessory and obliterated canals (Attin and Hoer, 2010).

**Digital radiography.** Radiovisiography has a sensor, display processing unit and printer, with minimal distortion (Sikri, 2019). It needs about 23% of the dose of D-speed film and removes film processing (Jain, 2016), and on-screen point-to-point measurement can follow a curve (Jain, 2016). It is not superior for WL accuracy (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012), and enhancement does not always improve interpretation (Jain, 2016). CBCT lengths correlate highly with electronic lengths, but more studies are needed before it is adopted (Jain, 2016).

### 5.3 Electronic apex locators (EALs)

**History.** Custer proposed electronic measurement in 1918 (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); Grossman's, Ingle's and Jain's chapters print this as Cluster (Gopikrishna, 2021). Resistance between periodontal ligament and oral mucosa is constant at 6.5 kilo-ohms (Sikri, 2019), shown by Suzuki in dogs and exploited by Sunada in 1962 (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012).

**Principle.** A lip clip and a file holder complete a circuit through the patient (Gopikrishna, 2021). Modern devices pass two frequencies and compare impedances, which differ maximally at the apex (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021). Electrode impedance is highest at the constriction and lowest at the foramen, where the tip leaves the insulating dentin (Attin and Hoer, 2010). The reading is therefore the constriction or foramen, not the radiographic apex.

**Generations.** Authors number them differently:

| Gen. | Sikri (2019) and Jain (2016) | Munce et al. (2019) | Gopikrishna (2021) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Resistance, direct current; needs a dry canal; painful; contraindicated with pacemakers (Sikri) | Resistance; pain from high current | Resistance (root canal meter) |
| 2 | Impedance; transparent dentin; calibration needed (Jain) | Single high-frequency impedance; calibration at the pocket | Impedance (Sono Explorer); calibrate to the sulcus |
| 3 | Frequency type; Root ZX, Endex; works with pus and pulp tissue (Sikri) | Multiple frequency; Root ZX self-calibrates by ratio (0.4 and 8 kHz) | Multiple frequency, difference at 1 and 5 kHz (Endex) |
| 4 | Modified frequency; needs dry or partly dry canals (Sikri) | One frequency at a time; needs dry or semi-dry canals | Ratio-based: Root ZX |
| 5 | Sikri: Bluetooth; copes with blood but not dry canals. Jain: not described | Capacitance and resistance measured separately; reliability varied with pulpal and periapical state | Not described |
| 6 | Adaptive; adapts to dry or wet canal (Sikri) | Adaptive, claimed | Not described |

Peters, Peters and Basrani (2021) call the ratio method the mark of the third generation, and Weine (2004) names three types only: resistance, impedance and frequency-dependent. Gopikrishna's Root ZX is thus fourth generation where four other books place it third; a difference of numbering, not of principle.

**Technique (numbered).**
1. Access, rubber dam, remove tissue and debris; estimate WL from the pre-op film (Weine, 2004).
2. Check the battery, attach the lip clip, calibrate if the model requires (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012).
3. Canal slightly wet; sterile water or peroxide preferred to hypochlorite, which ionises (Weine, 2004). Dry the chamber with a cotton pellet (Peters and Arias, 2025).
4. Choose a snug K-file (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) and keep it off metal restorations.
5. Advance slowly to the apex reading, set the stop at the reference point, record (Weine, 2004).
6. Confirm with a radiograph (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

**Where to stop.** Gulabivala and Ng (2014) use the "zero" reading, never a point short of it. Peters and Arias (2025) pull back about 0.5 mm after the patent reading. Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias (2012) pass slightly beyond the foramen, then retreat to the constriction. Walton and Torabinejad (2002) subtract 1 to 2 mm from the reading.

**Advantages.**
- Objective, and reduces radiographs (Weine, 2004; Schäfer, 2017).
- Valuable where anatomical structures overlie apices, in the gagging or handicapped patient (Sikri, 2019).
- Detects perforations, root fractures and resorption (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Jain, 2016); shows when two canals join (Sikri, 2019).
- Allows length and patency to be monitored throughout preparation (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).
- Students gain better obturation length control (Munce et al., 2019).

**Accuracy** depends on the reference (constriction or foramen), the model and the tolerance:

| Source | Figure |
|---|---|
| Sikri (2019); Jain (2016) | Overall 90%; Root ZX 90% within 0.5 mm |
| Weine (2004) | Low 90s; errors tend to be long |
| Walton and Torabinejad (2002) | 80 to 95% for impedance types |
| Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011); Schäfer (2017) | Within 0.5 mm over 90% |
| Munce et al. (2019) | Up to 95% in vitro; 81.5 to 97% clinically; 90.4% versus 82.1% for radiographs |
| Barnett (2017) | Within 0.5 mm of the minor foramen 100% versus 15% for radiographs (482 canals) |
| Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias (2012) | Root ZX 84% alone, 96% with radiograph |

**Limitations and contraindications** (the 7-mark core):
1. **Not a replacement for radiographs.** It cannot show width, curvature or number of canals (Weine, 2004), and must be confirmed radiographically (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
2. **False positive** (short circuit): metal restorations, gingival overgrowth, parapulpal pins, fractures, lateral canals, perforations. **False negative:** calcified or blocked canals, old filling remnants, insufficient irrigation, retreatment (Attin and Hoer, 2010).
3. **Remedies:** dry the chamber, lean the file away, or insulate it with a plastic capillary tip (Munce et al., 2019); nail varnish on the upper file also works (Jain, 2016).
4. **Open or immature apices** read short because the file does not touch the walls (Jain, 2016); resorption cases are discussed in section 9.
5. **File size.** Accuracy may fall as apical diameter rises (Munce et al., 2019); irregular signals in large canals (Peters and Arias, 2025).
6. **Power and operator.** A 9 V battery below about 7 V gives errors (Sikri, 2019); results improve with experience (Weine, 2004).
7. **Cost** (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012) and the **patient's sensation** with early units (Munce et al., 2019).
8. **Pacemakers:** see the table.

**Books disagree** on several points:

| Issue | Positions |
|---|---|
| Ionic irrigants | No EAL works with hypochlorite or other ionised solution (Sikri, 2019); later devices work in tissue fluids and electrolytes (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012). Sikri's statement conflicts with the others' accounts of third-generation units and appears to describe early devices |
| Preflaring | May improve accuracy (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); does not seem to affect it (Jain, 2016) |
| File size | Unaffected (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); accuracy falls as apical diameter increases (Munce et al., 2019) |
| Pacemakers | Contraindicated (Sikri, 2019); consult physician (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); no interference shown but advisable to avoid (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); concern unnecessary, though some manufacturers still warn (Munce et al., 2019) |
| Handpiece with built-in EAL | Auto-reverses beyond WL (Gopikrishna, 2021); Weine (2004) opposes it |
| Apical resorption | No significant effect (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); unreliable when the root end is wide open (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) |

## 6. Combining methods: a recommended protocol

No method is infallible (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Jain, 2016). Gulabivala and Ng (2014) advise triangulating EAL, radiograph, tactile sense and paper point, rechecking the EAL if the radiograph shows the file beyond the root. Combined use gives greater accuracy (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) and is recommended by Gopikrishna (2021). If the radiograph shows a file well short of the apex, Jain (2016) prefers the EAL reading.
1. Estimate WL from an angulated pre-op radiograph and choose a reference point (Peters and Arias, 2025).
2. Pre-enlarge the coronal canal.
3. Read the EAL with a snug file in a moist canal.
4. Expose a parallel radiograph with the file at that length (two angles if needed).
5. Adjust by the discrepancy and retake if beyond the tolerances in section 5.2.
6. Apply the end-point policy (section 3) and record length and reference point.

## 7. Working width

**Definition.** The horizontal dimensions of the canal at the constriction and at other levels are the **working width**, important at the working length, short of it, mid-canal and coronally (Sikri, 2019). It is judged from the first file that binds at WL, but that file does not accurately reflect the apical diameter (Sikri, 2019).
- **Factors misleading the estimate** (Sikri, 2019): oval or flat canals; long canals (friction); taper mismatch; curvature; obstructions such as pulp stones. Remedy: widen the orifices and flare, and use a flexible low-taper instrument.
- **Traditional rule:** enlarge at least three sizes beyond the first binding file (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019). Grossman no longer recommends it, nor judging by white shavings, since colour does not indicate infected dentin (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Current concept:** no standard minimum; it depends on initial width, whether the canal is vital, calcified or infected, periradicular pathology and curvature (Gopikrishna, 2021). Fixed sizes, such as size 80 in a maxillary central incisor, are not acceptable (Sikri, 2019). Gulabivala and Ng (2014) call size 30 optimal, with size 20 or 25 sufficient for wide tapers.
- **Small versus large:** a small apical file conserves dentin and reduces transportation; a larger one improves irrigant penetration but increases operator errors (Gopikrishna, 2021). Bacterial reduction with and without apical enlargement was insignificantly different in the study quoted by Sikri (2019); over-preparation risks weakening the root.

Ingle's standardised technique relates to this, but the full account is in the Root Canal Anatomy answer.

## 8. Maintaining the working length

- Stops can slip or be misplaced; parallax and loss of the reference point cause errors (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).
- A curved canal shortens as the instruments take a straighter path; pre-enlargement minimises this and frequent EAL checks control it (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).
- Re-measure after build-ups change the occlusal level (Attin and Hoer, 2010); cut gutta-percha cones with a scalpel, not by nicking with forceps (Attin and Hoer, 2010).
- A patency file (size 10 or 15) passed through the foramen does not increase postoperative symptoms (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- If the patient returns tender to percussion, Weine (2004) suggests a paper point past the WL: a reddish-brown discolouration shows overinstrumentation, and a shorter WL is calculated.

## 9. Special situations

| Situation | Problem and approach |
|---|---|
| Curved canal | Length is lost on straightening, so reconfirm (Sikri, 2019); straight-line access first (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) |
| Multi-rooted teeth | SLOB radiographs; EAL helps where apices are obscured (Sikri, 2019); suspect a second canal if the file is off-centre (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021) |
| Periapical lesion with resorption | Weine: end an additional 0.5 mm short, 2 mm or more if extensive (Weine, 2004). Gopikrishna (2021) attributes 1.5 and 2 mm short of the apex to him; Walton and Torabinejad (2002) give 1, 1.5 and 2 mm for no resorption, bone only, and bone with root resorption. Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011) use the most coronal sound root radiodensity as the new apex, set 1 to 2 mm coronal |
| Open apex | EAL of little use; the paper point (size 30) needs a dry canal with moist tissue (Jain, 2016) |
| Perforation | Immediate or sudden "apex" reading (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012) |
| Retreatment | Old filling remnants give false negatives (Attin and Hoer, 2010) |
| Primary teeth | EALs accurate enough (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012) |
| Maxillary sinus | File may enter the sinus and mislead; confirm electronically twice (Jain, 2016) |

## 10. Verification

Take a master cone radiograph, digital if possible, after the electronic length (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012; Munce et al., 2019). Record WL and reference point for each canal (Attin and Hoer, 2010).

## 11. Critical evaluation and conclusion

| Method | Merit | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Average length | Starting estimate | Teeth vary (Attin and Hoer, 2010) |
| Tactile, patient response, paper point | Cheap | Subjective, unreliable |
| Radiograph (Ingle's) | Shows anatomy; widely used | Two-dimensional; operator-dependent |
| Digital/CBCT | Less dose, measurement tools | Not proven superior |
| EAL | Objective; accurate; fewer films | Not 100%; cannot show anatomy |
| Combination | Highest accuracy (Jain, 2016) | Needs both skills |

Working length is the distance from a stable coronal reference to a biologically chosen apical end point. The apex cannot be seen, so every method estimates; the most reliable practice combines an apex locator with a parallel radiograph and a preflared canal, then controls and records the length.
