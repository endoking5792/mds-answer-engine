# Describe working length determination during endodontic treatment

**Marks:** 100 · Single essay

## 1. Introduction and definitions

**Working length (WL)** is the distance from a coronal reference point to the point at which canal preparation and obturation should terminate (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Gopikrishna, 2021; Barnett, 2017). Gulabivala and Ng (2014) separate the **canal length** (reference point to canal terminus) from the working length, the length to which each instrument is used; some instruments stop short of it (step-back) or go beyond it (patency). Most books treat them as one measurement, as does this answer.

| Term | Meaning (Gopikrishna, 2021 unless stated) |
|---|---|
| Anatomical apex | Tip of the root as determined morphologically |
| Radiographic apex | Tip of the root as determined radiographically |
| Apical foramen (major diameter) | Main apical opening, frequently eccentric to the anatomical or radiographic apex |
| Apical constriction (minor diameter) | Narrowest part of the canal, usually 0.5 to 1.0 mm short of the centre of the foramen (Jain, 2016) |
| Cementodentinal junction (CDJ) | Where dentin and cementum unite, a histologic landmark, 0.5 to 3.0 mm from the anatomical apex (Jain, 2016) |

**Why the apex makes this difficult.** Tissue apical to the CDJ is fibrous connective tissue from the periodontal ligament, not pulp (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- The foramen coincides with the anatomical apex in less than 50% of teeth (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); it deviates from the tip in at least two-thirds (Sikri, 2019) or in 60 to 80%, up to 3.0 mm away (Barnett, 2017). Deviation is buccal or lingual twice as often as mesial or distal, and a straight view shows only mesial or distal deviation (Weine, 2004).
- The constriction lies on average 1 mm from the apex, range 0 to 4 mm, so average values are clinically unacceptable (Attin and Hoer, 2010). In micro-CT the constriction to radiographic apex distance averaged 0.9 mm, and radiographs may locate it accurately in only 32% of canals (Barnett, 2017).
- Foramen to constriction averages 0.5 mm (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); Kuttler found 0.524 mm at 18 to 25 years and 0.659 mm after 55 (Weine, 2004).
- Not every tooth has a constriction: apical periodontitis with resorption removes it (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014). It is classed in five types (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) or four (Jain, 2016).

## 2. Importance of working length

Weine (2004) lists four effects: it fixes how far instruments and debris removal reach; it limits the depth of the root filling; it governs postoperative pain; and it plays a major part in success or failure.
1. **Biological:** ending at the constriction avoids apical and periodontal injury and keeps accessory canals (Attin and Hoer, 2010); the constriction has the least blood supply (Sikri, 2019) and gives the smallest wound for healing (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012).
2. **Microbiological:** violating the apex can inoculate periapical tissue with necrotic tissue, debris and bacteria (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); working short where organisms are present may sustain apical periodontitis (Peters and Arias, 2025).
3. **Prognostic:** two meta-analyses link both underfilling and overfilling with a worse prognosis (Schäfer, 2017). Each millimetre lost from the radiographic apex raised the odds of failure by about 14% and 12% in two studies, and all outcome studies found overextension harmful (Barnett, 2017).
4. **Technical:** the constriction allows an apical dentinal matrix for condensing filling (Sikri, 2019).

## 3. Where should the working length end?

Sikri (2019) lists four views: short of the radiographic apex (0.5 mm, later 1.0 mm), at it, beyond it, and at the CDJ. A short length risks teardrop preparations when the canal exits over 1.0 mm short (Sikri, 2019); with files at the radiographic apex in 40 extracted teeth, half passed 1.0 mm or more through the foramen (Weine, 2004). Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011) describe two incompatible philosophies, confinement to the constriction or extension to the root apex, with no evidence-based data for either. Apical-limit theory is in the Root Canal Anatomy answer.

| Author | End point advised |
|---|---|
| Weine (2004) | CDJ (Kuttler's minor diameter); all four methods he lists have given excellent results |
| Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang (2021) | 1 mm from the radiographic apex |
| Walton and Torabinejad (2002) | Optimal 1 to 2 mm short; 0 to 2 mm if necrotic, 0 to 3 mm if vital |
| Peters, Peters and Basrani (2021) | 0.5 to 1 mm short in necrosis, 1 to 2 mm in irreversible pulpitis; no definitive validation |
| Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011) | Vital: as close to the constriction as possible; necrotic: close to the canal exit or slightly short of the foramen |
| Barnett (2017) | Vital: short of the foramen; necrotic with apical periodontitis: nearer the radiographic apex |

## 4. Preparation before measurement

1. **Coronal pre-enlargement.** A length taken before straight-line access may be 1 mm less or shorter afterwards (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). Grossman enlarges with orifice enlargers or Gates-Glidden drills before the WL film (Gopikrishna, 2021); preflaring also improves tactile sensation (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021).
2. **Reference point.** Incisal edge in anterior teeth, cusp tips in posterior teeth; fractured or undermined cusps are ground down (Gopikrishna, 2021). Walton and Torabinejad (2002) prefer the highest incisal point, a buccal cusp, and the mesiobuccal cusp in molars. The choice can change the length by at least 1 mm, so record it with the length (Weine, 2004; Attin and Hoer, 2010).
3. **Stops.** Silicone stops, perpendicular to the file and touching the reference point (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021).
4. **Initial estimate** from an undistorted pre-operative film. Teeth are typically 19 to 25 mm and roots 9 to 15 mm long (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021). Weine (2004) uses tables of average lengths: the 95th percentile for long teeth, the average, or the 5th percentile for short ones.

## 5. Methods of determination

Grossman's classification (Gopikrishna, 2021): **I electronic** (recommended); **II radiographic** (Ingle's technique, Best, Bregman, Bramante, Grossman, Weine, Kuttler, X-ray grid, xeroradiography, direct digital); **III non-radiographic** (tactile sense, apical periodontal sensitivity, paper point), not recommended.

### 5.1 Non-radiographic methods

| Method | Principle | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Tactile | Resistance felt at the constriction (Sikri, 2019) | Any obstruction feels like a constriction (Sikri, 2019); in small cylindrical canals file taper exceeds canal taper and binds coronally (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); useful in under 60% of cases (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); better after step-down preparation (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) |
| Patient response | Patient reports sensitivity as the file passes the foramen (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012) | Impossible under anaesthesia (Jain, 2016); a necrotic canal may hold vital inflamed tissue several millimetres in, and pain is absent beyond the apex (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |
| Paper point | A wet or bloody tip means overextension (Sikri, 2019) | No scientific evidence for routine use (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); blood seeps by capillary action (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014); only a supplement (Schäfer, 2017) |

### 5.2 Radiographic methods

A radiograph is exposed with a file in the canal. A modified paralleling technique is superior to bisecting-angle (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021), and Attin and Hoer (2010) consider bisecting-angle and panoramic films unsuitable.

**Ingle's method** (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021):
1. Measure tooth length on the pre-operative film from the stable reference point to the radiographic apex.
2. Keep the estimate 1 mm short, compensating for image distortion and for the minor diameter lying short of the anatomical apex (Gopikrishna, 2021).
3. Set a stop at that length on a small file, insert it to the reference point and expose a film.
4. If the tip is beyond the apex, subtract the overshoot; if short, add the shortfall (Sikri, 2019); retake (Gopikrishna, 2021).
5. Allowance: Sikri (2019) subtracts a further 1.0 to 1.5 mm; Gopikrishna (2021) ends the tip 0.5 to 1.0 mm from the radiographic apex.
6. In curved canals about 1.0 mm may be lost as the canal straightens, so reaffirm the length (Sikri, 2019).

**Diagram:** Ingle's method as a flow chart: measure, minus 1 mm, file film, three outcomes, correct, retake.

| Variant | Principle |
|---|---|
| Coolidge and Kesel | Wire radiograph; abandoned because angulation gave incorrect results (Sikri, 2019) |
| Best; Bergman; Bramante | A 10-mm pin on the labial surface; a 25-mm probe whose stop penetrates 10 mm; right-angled steel probes (Jain, 2016). Grossman's text prints Bregman (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Grossman | WL 0.5 to 1.0 mm shorter than measured canal length (Sikri, 2019) |
| Kuttler/Weine | Locate major or minor diameter on the pre-op film; choose file by width (size 10 or 15 if narrow, 20 or 25 if average); subtract 0.5 mm (to age 35) or 0.67 mm (older) from the major diameter; mnemonic C-L-E-W: configuration, length, exit, width (Weine, 2004) |
| Walton | Subtract 3 mm from the pre-op length (1 mm foramen offset plus 2 mm magnification); film with a binding file of No. 25 or larger (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |

**Books differ on the film.**

| Point | Positions |
|---|---|
| Smallest file | ISO 15 (Attin and Hoer, 2010; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); No. 20 (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); No. 25 or larger, not 8 or 10 (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) |
| Accept the file if | within 1 mm of ideal (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); 0 to 2 mm from the apex (Schäfer, 2017) |
| Repeat the film if | adjustment of 2 mm or more (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Schäfer, 2017); correction over 3 mm (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021; Jain, 2016) |

**Multi-canal teeth.** Place files in all canals and use the buccal object (SLOB) rule: the buccal structure moves opposite to the tubehead, and a 20 to 30 degree shift is usually enough (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011). Mesial angles suit premolars and maxillary molars, distal angles mandibular incisors and molars (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021). Two views identify a Type II configuration (Weine, 2004).

**Limitations.** The image is two-dimensional (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); the zygomatic arch overlies 20% of first and 42% of second maxillary molar apices (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); a file at the radiographic apex may already be beyond the foramen (Schäfer, 2017); interpretation is subjective (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012) and radiation is added (Jain, 2016). Radiographs overestimated length in 51% of canals against 21% with an apex locator (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012). They do show curvature, accessory and obliterated canals (Attin and Hoer, 2010).

**Digital radiography.** Radiovisiography has a sensor, display processing unit and printer, with minimal distortion (Sikri, 2019). It needs about 23% of the dose of D-speed film, removes film processing and measures along a curve on screen (Jain, 2016). It is not superior for WL accuracy (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012). CBCT lengths correlate highly with electronic lengths, but more studies are needed before adopting it (Jain, 2016).

### 5.3 Electronic apex locators (EALs)

**History.** Custer used electric current in 1918 (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); Grossman, Ingle and Jain print the name as Cluster (Gopikrishna, 2021; Munce et al., 2019; Jain, 2016). Suzuki found resistance between periodontal ligament and oral mucosa constant at 6.5 kilo-ohms, and Sunada introduced it clinically in 1962 (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012).

**Principle.** A lip hook and file holder carry current through the patient, comparing periodontal and mucosal impedance (Gopikrishna, 2021). Modern devices pass two frequencies whose impedances differ maximally at the apex (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); electrode impedance is highest at the constriction and lowest at the foramen, where the tip leaves the insulating dentin (Attin and Hoer, 2010). The device measures to the foramen, not the radiographic apex (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012).

**Generations.** Authors number them differently:

| Gen. | Sikri (2019) | Jain (2016); Munce et al. (2019) | Gopikrishna (2021) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Resistance; dry canal; tingling; pacemaker contraindicated | Resistance; unreliable; pain from current | Resistance |
| 2 | Impedance; coated probes | Single-frequency impedance; calibration | Impedance (Sono Explorer); sulcus calibration |
| 3 | Frequency type (Root ZX, Endex); works with pus and pulp | Multiple frequencies; Root ZX ratio method | Multiple frequency (Endex, 1 and 5 kHz) |
| 4 | Modified frequency; relatively dry canal | One frequency at a time; dry canals | Ratio type: Root ZX |
| 5 | Bluetooth type; copes with blood, not dry canals | Capacitance and resistance separately; difficult in dry canals | Not described |
| 6 | Adaptive; dry or wet canal | Adaptive, moisture-adjusting | Not described |

Peters, Peters and Basrani (2021) also place the ratio method in the third generation, and Weine (2004) names only three types. Gopikrishna's fourth generation is thus the Root ZX that five other books place third: numbering, not principle.

**Technique.**
1. Access, rubber dam, remove pulp tissue and debris; estimate WL from the pre-op film (Weine, 2004).
2. Check the battery, calibrate if required, and place the lip clip (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012).
3. Canal slightly wet, with water or peroxide rather than ionising hypochlorite (Weine, 2004); dry the chamber with cotton (Peters and Arias, 2025).
4. Use a snug K-file (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) kept off metal restorations (Munce et al., 2019).
5. Advance slowly to the apex reading, set the stop at the reference point and record (Weine, 2004).
6. Confirm radiographically (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).

**Where to stop** differs: the "zero" reading, never short of it (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014); pull back about 0.5 mm (Peters and Arias, 2025); pass slightly beyond the foramen then retreat to the constriction (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); subtract 1 to 2 mm (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Advantages.**
- Objective (Weine, 2004), with fewer radiographs (Schäfer, 2017).
- Helps when structures overlie apices and in patients who gag or are handicapped (Sikri, 2019).
- Detects perforations (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002), fractures and resorption (Jain, 2016); shows if two canals join (Sikri, 2019).
- Lets length and patency be monitored throughout preparation (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014); dental students gained better obturation length control (Munce et al., 2019).

**Accuracy** varies with reference (constriction or foramen), model and tolerance:

| Source | Figure |
|---|---|
| Sikri (2019); Jain (2016) | Overall 90%; Root ZX 90% within 0.5 mm. Over 90% within 0.5 mm (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Schäfer, 2017) |
| Weine (2004); Walton and Torabinejad (2002) | Low 90s, errors tending long; 80 to 95% for impedance types |
| Munce et al. (2019) | Up to 95% in vitro; 81.5 to 97% clinically; 90.4% versus 82.1% for radiographs |
| Barnett (2017) | Within 0.5 mm of the minor foramen 100% versus 15% for radiographs (482 canals) |
| Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias (2012) | Root ZX 84% alone, 96% with a radiograph |

**Limitations and contraindications.**
1. **Not a replacement for radiographs:** it shows neither width, curvature nor number of canals (Weine, 2004), and needs radiographic confirmation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
2. **False positive** (short circuit): metal restorations, gingival overgrowth, parapulpal pins, fractures, lateral canals, perforation. **False negative:** calcified or blocked canals, old filling remnants, poor irrigation, retreatment (Attin and Hoer, 2010).
3. **Remedies:** dry the chamber, lean the file away or insulate it with a plastic capillary tip (Munce et al., 2019); nail varnish on the upper file also insulates (Jain, 2016).
4. **Open or immature apices** read short because the file does not touch the walls (Jain, 2016).
5. **File size:** accuracy may fall as apical diameter rises (Munce et al., 2019); signals are irregular in large canals (Peters and Arias, 2025).
6. **Power and operator:** a 9 V battery below about 7 V gives errors (Sikri, 2019); results improve with experience (Weine, 2004); cost (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012).
7. **Pacemakers:** see the table.

| Issue | Positions |
|---|---|
| Ionic irrigants | No EAL works with hypochlorite or other ionised solution (Sikri, 2019); later devices work in tissue fluids and electrolytes (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); Sikri's statement appears to describe early devices (reasoning) |
| Preflaring | May improve accuracy (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); no apparent effect (Jain, 2016) |
| File size | Unaffected (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); accuracy falls as the apical diameter rises (Munce et al., 2019) |
| Pacemakers | Contraindicated (Sikri, 2019); consult the physician (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); no interference shown but avoidance advisable (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021); concern unnecessary though some manufacturers still warn (Munce et al., 2019) |
| Handpiece with EAL | Auto-reverses beyond WL (Gopikrishna, 2021); Weine (2004) opposes it |
| Apical resorption | No significant effect (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021); unreliable if the root end is wide open (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) |

## 6. Combining methods: recommended protocol

No method is infallible (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Jain, 2016). Gulabivala and Ng (2014) triangulate EAL, radiograph, tactile sense and paper point. Combined use gives greater accuracy (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021) and is Grossman's recommendation (Gopikrishna, 2021); if the film shows the file well short, Jain (2016) prefers the EAL reading.
1. Estimate WL from an angulated pre-op film and choose a reference point (Peters and Arias, 2025).
2. Pre-enlarge the coronal canal, then read the EAL with a snug file in a moist canal.
3. Expose a parallel film with the file at that length, a second angle if needed.
4. Correct and retake beyond the tolerances in section 5.2.
5. Apply the end-point policy (section 3); record length and reference point.

## 7. Working width

The horizontal dimensions of the canal at the constriction and at other levels are the **working width**, important at the working length, short of it, mid-canal and coronally (Sikri, 2019). It is estimated from the first file that binds at WL, which does not accurately reflect the apical diameter (Sikri, 2019).
- **Misleading factors** (Sikri, 2019): oval or flat canals, long canals (friction), taper mismatch, curvature and obstructions such as pulp stones. Widen the orifices and flare, and use a flexible, low-taper instrument.
- **Traditional rule:** enlarge three sizes beyond the first binding file (Sikri, 2019). Grossman no longer recommends it or judging by white shavings, since colour does not indicate infected dentin (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Current concept:** no standard minimum; it depends on initial width, vital, calcified or infected state, periradicular pathology and curvature (Gopikrishna, 2021). Fixed sizes, such as 80 in a maxillary central incisor, are not acceptable (Sikri, 2019). Gulabivala and Ng (2014) call size 30 optimal, with 20 or 25 sufficient for wide tapers.
- **Small versus large:** a small master apical file conserves dentin and reduces transportation (Gopikrishna, 2021). Sikri (2019) notes that larger preparation aids bacterial elimination, that over-preparation weakens the root, and that bacterial reduction with or without apical enlargement was insignificantly different.

## 8. Maintaining and verifying the working length

- Slipping stops, parallax and loss of the reference point cause errors; a curved canal shortens as instruments take a straighter path, which pre-enlargement and frequent EAL checks control (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014).
- Re-measure after build-ups change the occlusal level, and cut gutta-percha cones with a scalpel, not by nicking with forceps (Attin and Hoer, 2010).
- A patency file (size 10 or 15) through the foramen does not increase postoperative symptoms (Peters, Peters and Basrani, 2021).
- Take a master cone radiograph, digital if possible, after the electronic length (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012; Munce et al., 2019).
- If the tooth is tender to percussion, a paper point past the WL showing reddish-brown discolouration confirms overinstrumentation, and a shorter WL is calculated (Weine, 2004).

## 9. Special situations

| Situation | Problem and approach |
|---|---|
| Multi-rooted teeth | SLOB films; suspect a second canal if the file is off-centre (Teixeira, Williamson and Shabahang, 2021) |
| Lesion with resorption | End 0.5 mm further short, 2 mm or more if extensive (Weine, 2004); Gopikrishna (2021) presents his modification as 1.5 mm short for bone and 2 mm for root resorption; Walton and Torabinejad (2002) give 1, 1.5 and 2 mm for no resorption, bone only, bone and root. Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011) take the most coronal sound radiodensity as the new apex, 1 to 2 mm coronal |
| Open apex | EAL of little use; a size 30 paper point needs a dry canal and moist tissue (Jain, 2016) |
| Perforation or retreatment | An immediate "apex" reading suggests perforation (Malkhassian, Plazas and Nahmias, 2012); old filling remnants give false negatives (Attin and Hoer, 2010) |

## 10. Critical evaluation and conclusion

Average lengths only give a start (Attin and Hoer, 2010); tactile, patient-response and paper-point methods are cheap but subjective. The radiograph shows anatomy yet is two-dimensional and operator-dependent; digital imaging lowers dose without proven accuracy gain. The EAL is objective and needs fewer films but is not 100% accurate and shows no anatomy; the combination gives the highest accuracy (Jain, 2016). The apex cannot be seen, so every method estimates: the most reliable practice combines an apex locator with a parallel radiograph in a pre-enlarged canal, then controls and records the length.
