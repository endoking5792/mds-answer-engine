## STUDY MODE

### S1. The one-line difference [basic science]
Resin-modified GIC is conventional GIC whose liquid contains 15-25% resin component (HEMA and other methacrylates), so it sets by acid-base reaction plus polymerisation, rather than by acid-base reaction alone (Mount et al, 2016). The aim, introduced in the late 1980s, was a longer working time with a quick set and immediate finishing (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Craig describes two routes: methacrylate groups hung on the polyacid (the first commercial product), or an unmodified polyacid with hydrophilic monomers such as HEMA, which gives lower water, more monomer and a high thermal expansion (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### S2. Numbers from one manufacturer pair
Van Noort compares one maker's conventional and resin-modified products: working time 2 minutes against 3 minutes 45 seconds; setting time 4 minutes against 20 seconds (the text of the same page says it sets hard after just 30 seconds of light); compressive strength 202 against 242 MPa; diametral tensile strength 16 against 37 MPa; shear bond to bovine enamel 4.6 against 11.3 MPa and to bovine dentine 4.3 against 8.2 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013). Craig ranks the two qualitatively: compressive and flexural strength medium against low-medium, wear resistance medium against low, fluoride release and recharge medium-high against high, and aesthetics good against acceptable, for resin-modified against conventional (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### S3. What stays the same and what is lost
Both bond to tooth by ion exchange; the resin-modified type bonds slightly differently, with fewer free carboxyl groups and polymerisation shrinkage (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Bonsor and Pearson say the bond strengths quoted are similar to or slightly better than conventional GIC, and that mechanical properties fall as polyHEMA absorbs water, so the cement is strongest just after placement (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Resin-modified GIC may be etched without harm and bonds to composite (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). The cost is HEMA: water uptake with swelling, possible cytotoxicity and less ion exchange as resin content rises (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013). Polymerisation shrinkage is 3-4%, greater than in resin composites (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); Phillips adds more microleakage than conventional GIC and expansion of up to about 8% by volume (Anusavice et al, 2013), while Sikri says microleakage is occasionally higher than with chemically cured cements (Sikri, 2020). On a bonding agent the books differ: Craig (2002) calls it contraindicated before a hybrid ionomer because it reduces fluoride release, while Phillips says a dentine bonding system is needed for hybrid ionomers with nonreactive filler (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 1. Introduction
Conventional GIC sets by acid-base reaction alone; resin-modified GIC adds a water-soluble resin (HEMA) so it also sets by light, giving a command set and early water resistance (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Comparison table
| Feature | Conventional GIC | Resin-modified GIC |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | acid-base only (Sikri, 2020) | acid-base plus light polymerisation, redox backup (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Working time | 2 minutes (one pair) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 3 minutes 45 seconds (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Setting time | 4 minutes (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 20 seconds in the table, 30 seconds of light in the text (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Early water resistance | poor; must be sealed at once (Sikri, 2020) | good once light-cured (Sikri, 2020) |
| Compressive strength | 202 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 242 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Tensile strength | 16 MPa (diametral) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 37 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Bond to enamel and dentine | 4.6 and 4.3 MPa (shear, bovine) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 11.3 and 8.2 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Solubility | 0.3-0.5% (Sikri, 2020) | 0.08% (Sikri, 2020) |
| Finishing | after 24 hours (Sikri, 2020; Sakaguchi et al, 2019) | immediately after set (Sikri, 2020); Sikri also gives within 10 minutes and, for cervical lesions, after one day and if possible one week |
| Aesthetics | opaque, dulls (Sikri, 2020) | better (Sikri, 2020) |
| Fluoride | high release, rechargeable (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) | slightly higher overall (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); unchanged (Anusavice et al, 2013); medium-high against high (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) |
| Etching to bond composite | auto-cure etched 15 seconds (Mount et al, 2016) | no etching needed (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Water uptake | low once set | takes up water over 5-7 days, swelling (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Biocompatibility | mild | HEMA concern; more cytotoxic in direct cell contact (Hilton et al, 2013); not for direct pulp capping (Anusavice et al, 2013) |

### 3. Advantages of resin-modified over conventional
Command set, early water resistance, better aesthetics, higher initial strength, immediate finishing, bond to composite as a base, repairability (Sikri, 2020; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Bond strength is similar or slightly better, although about double in the van Noort pair (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### 4. Disadvantages of resin-modified
HEMA-related water uptake and swelling, strength that falls as water is taken up, discolouration after years, polymerisation shrinkage of 3-4%, possible cytotoxicity near the pulp, less ion exchange with more resin (Mount et al, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2011; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Mount, 1994).

### 5. Choice
Mount et al (2016) say resin can overwhelm the ion exchange, and that the resin-free auto-cure materials remain the preferred versions under most circumstances. Sikri (2020) says Class III cavities are best restored with light-cure GIC and that traditional type II and light-cure GIC can both serve as a sandwich base. For luting, Craig calls resin-modified cements the most popular class (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) and Banerjee and Watson (2015) note their popularity for indirect restorations and posts; see P3.

### 6. Conclusion
Resin modification trades some ion-exchange purity for handling, aesthetics and early strength; neither type replaces the other.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Resin-modified = conventional whose liquid contains 15-25% resin (HEMA and other methacrylates); light cure plus acid-base (Mount et al, 2016).
- RMGIC: longer working time, 20-30 second light set, higher initial strength, immediate finishing (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Sikri, 2020).
- Cost: HEMA water uptake, cytotoxicity, less ion exchange (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013; Mount, 1994).
