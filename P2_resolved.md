## STUDY MODE

### S1. The one-line difference [basic science]
Resin-modified GIC is conventional GIC with 15-25% resin added, so it sets by acid-base reaction plus light-activated polymerisation, rather than by acid-base reaction alone (Mount et al, 2016). The aim was a longer working time with a quick true set and immediate finishing (Sakaguchi et al, 2019*).

### S2. Numbers from one manufacturer pair
Van Noort compares one maker's conventional and resin-modified products: working time 2 minutes against 3 minutes 45 seconds; setting time 4 minutes against 20 seconds; compressive strength 202 against 242 MPa; diametral tensile strength 16 against 37 MPa; shear bond to bovine enamel 4.6 against 11.3 MPa and to bovine dentine 4.3 against 8.2 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### S3. What stays the same and what is lost
Both bond to tooth by ion exchange; the resin-modified type bonds slightly differently, with fewer free carboxyl groups and polymerisation shrinkage (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). Resin-modified GIC may be etched without harm and bonds to composite (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013). The cost is HEMA: water uptake with swelling, possible cytotoxicity and less ion exchange as resin content rises (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994; Hilton et al, 2013).

### 1. Introduction
Conventional GIC sets by acid-base reaction alone; resin-modified GIC adds a water-soluble resin (HEMA) so it also sets by light, giving a command set and early water resistance (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Comparison table
| Feature | Conventional GIC | Resin-modified GIC |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | acid-base only (Sikri, 2020) | acid-base plus light polymerisation, redox backup (Mount et al, 2016) |
| Working time | 2 minutes (one pair) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 3 minutes 45 seconds (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Setting time | 4 minutes (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 20 seconds with light (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Early water resistance | poor; must be sealed at once (Sikri, 2020) | good once light-cured (Sikri, 2020) |
| Compressive strength | 202 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 242 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Tensile strength | 16 MPa (diametral) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 37 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Bond to enamel and dentine | 4.6 and 4.3 MPa (shear, bovine) (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) | 11.3 and 8.2 MPa (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |

| Finishing | after 24 hours (Sikri, 2020) | immediately after set (Sikri, 2020) |
| Aesthetics | opaque, dulls (Sikri, 2020) | better (Sikri, 2020) |

### 3. Advantages of resin-modified over conventional
Command set, early water resistance, better aesthetics, higher strength and bond, immediate finishing, bond to composite as a base, repairability (Sikri, 2020; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### 4. Disadvantages of resin-modified
HEMA-related water uptake and swelling, discolouration after years, polymerisation shrinkage, possible cytotoxicity near the pulp, less ion exchange with more resin (Mount et al, 2016; Banerjee and Watson, 2011; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Mount, 1994).

### 5. Choice
Conventional GIC where ion exchange, fluoride and simplicity matter (luting, transitional, ART); resin-modified GIC where aesthetics, early water resistance and finishing matter (Class III and V, sandwich base) (Sikri, 2020). Mount notes that resin can overwhelm ion exchange, so resin-free auto-cure materials remain preferred where the biological aim is dominant (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Conclusion
Resin modification trades some ion-exchange purity for handling, aesthetics and early strength; neither type replaces the other.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Resin-modified = conventional plus 15-25% HEMA resin; light cure plus acid-base (Mount et al, 2016).
- RMGIC: longer working time, 20-second set, higher strength, immediate finishing (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Sikri, 2020).
- Cost: HEMA water uptake, cytotoxicity, less ion exchange (Mount et al, 2016; Hilton et al, 2013; Mount, 1994).
