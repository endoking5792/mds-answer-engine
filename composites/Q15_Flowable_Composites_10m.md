## STUDY MODE

### 1. Composition and properties
Flowable composites contain dimethacrylate resin with inorganic filler of particle size 0.04 and 0.2-3.0μm, at a filler loading of only 42-53% (Craig's) or 42-62% by volume depending on source — genuinely LOWER than packable/hybrid composites (60-70%) [SRC, condensed from Q1]. This lower filler content directly causes two defining properties: a LOW modulus of elasticity (useful specifically at cervical/abfraction margins, where flexure accommodation matters) and, as the trade-off, HIGHER polymerization shrinkage (2.4-2.9% range cited in one source) and LOWER wear resistance than microhybrid/hybrid composites [SRC, condensed from Q1]. Low viscosity allows syringe dispensing for easy handling.

### 2. Indications
Cervical lesions (abfraction/erosion), pediatric restorations, pit-and-fissure sealant use, small low-stress-bearing restorations, and as a liner beneath a hybrid/packable composite in a layered (incremental) technique. **Important correction (2026-10-15 textbook audit, STURD)**: the "stress-absorbing layer" rationale for this liner use is explicitly NOT strongly supported — Sturdevant's states directly that "those claims are not supported by current clinical evidence" [composites/_AUDIT_2026-10-15.md A.23]. The liner use itself (adaptation/wetting of the cavity floor) remains a valid, commonly practised technique; only the specific mechanistic claim that it measurably relieves shrinkage stress should be presented as unproven rather than established. **Exact filler-loading/particle-size figures differ by source**: Powers & Wataha give 42-53vol%, particle size 0.4-3.0µm; Craig's gives a wider 42-62vol% range [composites/_AUDIT_2026-10-15.md B]. A named recent advance: "Vertise Flow" (van Noort) is a self-etching flowable composite that combines the etch/prime/bond step with the flowable material itself.

### 3. Clinical relevance and limitation
Flowable composite's practical handling advantage (syringe delivery, adaptation into undercuts/fine fissures) is genuine, but its lower wear resistance and higher shrinkage make it unsuitable as a PRIMARY posterior occlusal-bulk material — it is a supplementary/adjunctive material, not a general-purpose substitute for hybrid/packable composite [SRC, condensed from Q1].

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 min)**

### Answer
Flowable composites contain the same basic dimethacrylate resin matrix as other composites, but with inorganic filler loading deliberately kept lower, at roughly 42-53% by volume against 60-70% for packable or hybrid composites, using filler particle sizes of 0.04 and 0.2-3.0μm. This lower filler content directly produces the two properties that define this category clinically: a low modulus of elasticity, which is genuinely useful at cervical or abfraction margins where accommodating ongoing tooth flexure matters, and, as the necessary trade-off, higher polymerization shrinkage and lower wear resistance than microhybrid or hybrid composites. The resulting low viscosity allows the material to be delivered directly from a syringe, giving excellent adaptation into fine fissures, undercuts and hard-to-access sites that a more viscous composite would not flow into as readily.

These properties directly determine flowable composite's real clinical indications: cervical lesions, particularly abfraction and erosion defects where the low modulus accommodates ongoing tooth flexure; pediatric restorations, where ease of placement and reduced isolation demands matter; pit-and-fissure sealant use; other small, low-stress-bearing restorations generally; and, as a thin liner placed beneath a stronger hybrid or packable composite in a layered restoration, improving adaptation at the cavity floor before the higher-modulus occlusal composite is placed over it. The specific claim that this liner measurably relieves shrinkage stress ("stress-absorbing layer") should be stated with its honest evidentiary status, not as settled fact — Sturdevant's textbook states explicitly that this claim "is not supported by current clinical evidence," even though the liner's adaptation benefit is a genuinely practised, valid technique. The exact filler loading and particle size also differ meaningfully by source: Powers & Wataha give 42-53 volume percent at a 0.4-3.0µm particle size, while Craig's gives a wider 42-62 volume percent range — cite the source rather than one fixed figure. A genuinely recent advance worth naming is Vertise Flow, a self-etching flowable composite that combines the etch-prime-bond step with the flowable material itself, reducing the clinical steps needed for small, low-stress restorations.

The clinically important limitation to state explicitly is that flowable composite's real handling advantages — syringe delivery and superior adaptation into inaccessible geometry — do not extend to its mechanical performance: its lower wear resistance and higher shrinkage make it unsuitable as the primary bulk material for a posterior occlusal restoration under functional load. Flowable composite should therefore be understood as a supplementary, adjunctive material for specific low-stress or stress-relief roles, not as a general-purpose substitute for hybrid or packable composite in stress-bearing restorations.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Lower filler (42-53% vs. 60-70% for hybrid) → low modulus (good for cervical flexure) + higher shrinkage/lower wear (trade-off).
- Indications: cervical/abfraction, pediatric, sealants, small low-stress restorations, AND as a stress-absorbing liner beneath hybrid/packable composite.
- Limitation: NOT suitable as a primary posterior occlusal-bulk material despite excellent handling — a supplementary, not general-purpose, material.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Describing flowable composite only by its handling advantage (syringe delivery) without the mechanical trade-off (shrinkage/wear).
2. Omitting the stress-absorbing-liner use beneath hybrid composite as a distinct clinical application from standalone restoration.
3. Recommending flowable composite as a general posterior occlusal material — it is specifically NOT indicated for that stress-bearing role.

## MNEMONIC
**"Low-Low-High"**: LOW filler → LOW modulus (good for flexure) → HIGH shrinkage/low wear (the trade-off).
