## STUDY MODE

### 1. Why composite is now a mainstream posterior option
Sturdevant's states directly that composite "now enjoys universal clinical application," listing explicit indications spanning Class I-VI restorations, foundations/core build-ups, sealants and preventive resin restorations, esthetic enhancement, luting cement for indirect restorations, temporary restorations, and periodontal splinting [SRC, Sturdevant's p.506]. The ADA's own quoted position is narrower and more specific for posterior use particularly: composite is appropriate for sealants, preventive resins, initial Class I/II lesions with modified conservative preparations, moderate-sized Class I/II restorations, Class V restorations, esthetically important areas, and patients with metal allergy/sensitivity — but the ADA explicitly does NOT support composite in teeth under heavy occlusal stress, in sites that genuinely cannot be isolated, or in patients sensitive to composite itself; used as indicated, the ADA states composite's "expected lifetime... can be comparable to that of amalgam in Class I, Class II, and Class V restorations" [SRC, Sturdevant's p.506].

### 2. Three practical case-selection factors (Sturdevant's framework)
Three factors govern the posterior amalgam-versus-composite decision in practice, mirroring the equivalent amalgam-side framing: ISOLATION (bonding requires a genuinely contamination-free field; where isolation is truly impossible, a non-bonded amalgam may be preferable, since minor fluid contamination is less damaging to it); OCCLUSION (modern composite's wear resistance is not substantially different from amalgam's, but for heavy bruxism or restorations carrying all occlusal contacts, amalgam is still usually preferred); and OPERATOR ABILITY/COMMITMENT (composite's tooth PREPARATION is genuinely easier than amalgam's — no mechanical-retention undercuts needed — but isolation, the etch/prime/bond sequence, insertion, and finishing/polishing are all MORE demanding; composite trades preparation simplicity for placement-technique difficulty, exactly the reverse of amalgam's own trade-off) [SRC, Sturdevant's p.506].

### 3. Composition and filler classification relevant to posterior use (condensed from Q1/Q2)
Composite's four-component architecture (organic matrix, filler, silane coupling agent, initiator-accelerator system) and filler-size classification (macrofill → microfill → hybrid/microhybrid → nanofill → bulk-fill → flowable) were built in depth in this topic's Q1 and Q2; for posterior use specifically, the clinically load-bearing categories are multipurpose hybrid, nanocomposite, bulk-filled, and laboratory (fibre-reinforced) composite, per Craig's own class-by-class indication table — Class I/II restorations are specifically matched to multipurpose, nanocomposite, bulk-filled, laboratory, or special posterior-rated microfilled/compomer products; Class VI (MOD) restorations specifically to bulk-filled or nanocomposite [SRC, Craig's Table 9.1]. Formal ADA posterior-acceptance criteria (≤10% colour/marginal-discolouration change, ≤5% marginal-integrity/caries/failure, ≤50μm wear at 6-18 months) govern which products are actually validated for cuspal-replacement use [SRC, Craig's Table 9.5].

### 4. Cavity design — the cavo-surface-angle trade-off specific to posterior composite
A genuinely posterior-specific cavity-design consideration, distinct from composite's general placement principles: enamel etch-pattern quality depends on prism orientation relative to the cut surface — a 90° cavo-surface angle (the well-supported, mechanically favourable margin) exposes prisms PARALLEL to their long axis, the WORSE etching orientation, while a 120° angle exposes a combination pattern giving a BETTER bond, at the cost of being harder to finish accurately [SRC, McCabe's p.216-217]. For Class II restorations specifically, where a matrix band is used, this trade-off resolves in favour of the 90° angle DESPITE its worse etch pattern: unfilled resin tends to pool in the V-shaped gap between the matrix and an obtusely finished (120°) margin, producing a weak, wear-prone all-resin marginal zone — a matrix-compatibility problem judged more clinically important than the etch-pattern difference [SRC, McCabe's p.217]. A further, directly practical finding: a dentine bonding agent used as the enamel-bonding intermediary (rather than the classic unfilled-resin intermediary) often achieves a GREATER bond strength to etched enamel, because modern composites themselves contain too little free resin to wet the enamel surface adequately without an intermediary layer [SRC, McCabe's p.217].

### 5. The single most important technique difference from amalgam: no condensation force
Matrix selection and placement is described directly as one of the single most important steps in posterior composite restoration, for a precise structural reason: posterior composite placement is almost totally dependent on the matrix's own contour and position to establish a correct proximal contact, because — unlike amalgam — composite cannot be actively condensed against the matrix to help shape that contact [SRC, Sturdevant's p.588]. This single fact is why matrix technique receives outsized emphasis specifically for composite: EARLY WEDGING (placed and progressively re-tightened DURING tooth preparation itself, not only at restoration time) achieves the tooth separation needed to compensate for matrix-band thickness, and the matrix must be verified in absolute contact with the adjacent tooth before any composite is placed, since there is no later condensation step available to correct a poorly adapted matrix [SRC, Sturdevant's p.588].

### 6. Matrix systems — a comparative summary
Several matrix systems exist with genuinely different trade-offs for posterior composite specifically [SRC, Sturdevant's p.588-591]: ultrathin metal matrix bands (e.g. HO bands in a Tofflemire retainer) contour well and resist condensation-type force even without active condensation, suiting general Class II use; the standard two-surface Tofflemire retainer requires wedging to compensate for TWO band thicknesses simultaneously since it wraps both proximal surfaces; a customised, compound-supported metal matrix for a single proximal box only needs to compensate for ONE band thickness, making contact easier to achieve, at the cost of requiring green-stick compound stabilisation; clear polyester matrix is recommended only for very small Class II preparations, since it is thicker than metal (needing firmer wedging) and contours poorly with viscous composite; and sectional matrix with a separator ("bitine") ring simplifies proximal-contact establishment and is increasingly popular, though careless use on wide faciolingual preparations can deform the band via the ring's prongs, producing a poor contour. Practical technique points worth citing directly: metal bands should be burnished on a paper pad before placement for proper contour, exactly as with an amalgam matrix; a gaping lingual margin on a Tofflemire band (the "tinner's joint" problem) can be cinched tight above the tooth's height of contour and folded aside with a large condenser to save later finishing time; and for restorations needing both proximal surfaces in one visit, WEDGE ALTERNATION — completing one proximal surface fully before beginning the other — avoids the added complexity of managing two unstable matrix/wedge systems and two developing contacts simultaneously [SRC, Sturdevant's p.588-591; Modern Operative Dentistry p.587].

### 7. Insertion, incremental placement, and anatomy
Composite is inserted and cured in increments of 2mm or less, each cured before the next is placed, until the preparation is slightly overfilled [SRC, Sturdevant's p.591]. Light-cured composite should not be dispensed until immediately ready for use, since ambient operatory light itself can begin premature polymerisation [SRC, Sturdevant's p.591]. For Class I restorations specifically, a STRATIFIED LAYERING technique is used for esthetic replication: an opaque dentin-shade increment first replicates lost dentin anatomy (which itself follows the external tooth contour), followed by a more translucent enamel-shade increment replicating lost enamel — critically, dentin-shade increments must NEVER be allowed to reach the cavosurface margin, leaving room for the final enamel-shade layer to cover it [SRC, Modern Operative Dentistry p.586-588]. Where unsupported enamel is present, those areas are filled and cured FIRST, with initial light directed THROUGH the remaining tooth structure to reduce early shrinkage stress, before completing cure conventionally from the occlusal surface [SRC, Modern Operative Dentistry p.587]. Occlusal anatomy is preferably carved one cusp at a time using the remaining tooth structure as a shaping guide [SRC, Modern Operative Dentistry p.588]. For an initial Class I carious lesion specifically, the Preventive Resin Restoration (PRR) technique is the method of choice: only the actually demineralised fissure segments are opened and restored, adjacent sound segments are simply sealed over, no occlusal bevel is ever placed, and — distinctively — the ENTIRE fissure system, both restored and merely sealed portions, is re-etched as a single unit and covered with one continuous surface-sealing resin layer at the finishing step [SRC, Summitt's p.589-590].

Incremental technique's benefits are broader than shrinkage-stress reduction alone: enhanced marginal adaptation, decreased gap formation, decreased marginal leakage, decreased cuspal deformation, increased cusp fracture resistance, improved bond strength to cavity walls, AND decreased postoperative sensitivity — a single technique change addressing seven distinct failure modes simultaneously [SRC, Summitt's p.605], directly relevant to the ADA's own "thorough history of adverse sensitivity" posterior-acceptance criterion already noted above.

### 7b. Finishing and polishing of posterior composite
If matrix and wedge placement were correct, the proximal surface should already copy the matrix strip's own smoothness and typically needs NO further polishing — checked directly by passing dental floss gingivo-occlusally across the surface rather than relying on visual inspection alone [SRC, Sturdevant's p.626]. Gingival overhangs, where present, are removed with the coarse-grit side of a narrow abrasive strip (kept narrow specifically to avoid inadvertently grinding away the proximal contact itself) or a serrated strip saw; marginal ridges and embrasures are finished with small abrasive discs, flame-shaped burs, or a contoured carbide carver/scalpel blade [SRC, Sturdevant's p.626-627]. The polishing sequence itself uses graduated abrasive rubber points or cups, coarse grit first until surface roughness is homogeneous, followed by thorough washing/drying before the fine-grit step; felt points with aluminium-oxide or diamond paste, silicon-carbide bristled brushes, and abrasive rubber spiral wheels are further options depending on surface geometry [SRC, Sturdevant's p.627]. A genuinely counter-intuitive, evidence-based finding worth stating directly: delaying finishing and polishing to a LATER appointment, rather than completing it immediately at the restorative visit (the common practice, often simply because both dentist and patient are tired by that point), has been shown to IMPROVE the restoration's eventual wear resistance — because composite has not yet reached its maximum degree of conversion and therefore its maximum mechanical properties immediately after curing, continuing to "dark-cure" for up to 24 hours, so grinding or polishing too early subjects an incompletely matured surface to rotary-instrument stress it is not yet mechanically ready to bear [SRC, Sturdevant's p.628]. Rotary finishing itself is not without cost even when correctly timed: it creates genuine 25-50µm surface microcracks, which is why rotary-instrument use should be minimised overall and enamel margins specifically should not be touched unnecessarily [SRC, Sturdevant's p.626].

### 8. Advantages and disadvantages specific to posterior use
A direct advantage/disadvantage summary, reproduced from Sturdevant's own framing [SRC, Sturdevant's p.507]: ADVANTAGES — esthetic; genuinely conservative of tooth structure (less extension needed, uniform depth and mechanical retention features usually unnecessary); simpler tooth PREPARATION; insulative (low thermal conductivity, generally no base required); near-universal applicability; bonded (good retention, low microleakage, minimal interfacial staining, and genuine reinforcement of remaining tooth structure); and repairable. DISADVANTAGES — a real gap-formation risk, especially at root-surface margins, where polymerization shrinkage force can exceed early dentin bond strength and open a V-shaped contraction gap; and more difficult, time-consuming and costly PLACEMENT (multi-step bonding, harder insertion, harder proximal-contact/contour establishment, harder finishing/polishing) than the simpler preparation step would suggest.

### 9. Clinical performance evidence (condensed from Q1)
The amalgam-versus-composite annual-failure-rate evidence base, built in full in Q1's Study Mode, is condensed here rather than repeated: older trials (1990s-2000s) generally favoured amalgam on AFR metrics, but more recent, longer-duration trials have substantially narrowed or, in a 12-year large-Class-II trial, even reversed this gap in composite's favour overall — with high-caries-risk patients remaining a genuine exception where amalgam retains an advantage for 3-surface restorations specifically [SRC, condensed from Q1]. A separate, directly relevant dataset for the DIRECT-versus-INDIRECT composite decision (Manhart et al., 2004) found composite inlays sharing amalgam's own 1.9% AFR, direct composite performing somewhat worse (3.6%), and indirect restorations generally showing a significantly lower AFR than direct restorations across the posterior dentition (P=0.0031) [SRC, Module 3 SubModule 6.1].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, 25m-equivalent treatment — 1 claim, per the 75m scaling expectation of 2-4 claims condensed proportionally)

### Claim: "Composite's posterior wear resistance is now essentially equivalent to amalgam's across all restoration sizes and caries-risk levels."
This claim requires qualification rather than blanket acceptance. The EVIDENCE genuinely supports near-equivalence in several specific contexts: modern nanofilled composite has been shown clinically (3- and 5-year studies) to wear at a rate similar to natural enamel, and a 12-year large-Class-II trial found composite's overall AFR significantly BETTER than high-copper amalgam's. The LIMITATION is that this same 12-year trial found the opposite result specifically in high-caries-risk patients with 3-surface restorations, where amalgam retained a real advantage — meaning the "equivalence" claim is genuinely conditional on caries-risk stratification and restoration size, not a uniform finding across every posterior clinical scenario `[Likely]`. The correct, examiner-rewarded framing is therefore that composite's posterior performance is now case-dependent-equivalent rather than universally equivalent — a materially more precise and defensible claim than a flat "composite now equals amalgam."

### Introduction
Composite resin has evolved from a strictly anterior, esthetic-only material into what Sturdevant's describes as a restorative material of "universal clinical application," now including routine posterior, stress-bearing use. This essay describes composite specifically AS a posterior restorative material: its formal indications and contraindications per the ADA's own position statement, the practical case-selection factors that govern the amalgam-versus-composite decision, the genuinely posterior-specific cavity-design and matrix-technique considerations that distinguish composite placement from amalgam placement, and the clinical-performance evidence that should inform that decision.

### Indications, contraindications, and the ADA position
The ADA's quoted position statement specifies composite as appropriate for sealants and preventive resin restorations, initial Class I/II lesions treated with modified, conservative preparations, moderate-sized Class I/II restorations, Class V restorations, esthetically important areas, and patients with a metal allergy or sensitivity; it explicitly does NOT support composite use in teeth subject to heavy occlusal stress, in sites where adequate isolation genuinely cannot be achieved, or in patients sensitive to composite itself. Used within these indications, the ADA states that composite's expected lifetime can be comparable to amalgam's in Class I, Class II and Class V restorations specifically. Three practical factors govern this decision at the chairside. ISOLATION is the first and most fundamental: adhesive bonding depends on a genuinely contamination-free field, so where isolation cannot realistically be achieved, a non-bonded amalgam restoration — tolerant of minor fluid contamination in a way bonded composite is not — may remain the better choice. OCCLUSION is the second: modern composite's wear resistance is not substantially different from amalgam's in most clinical situations, but for patients with heavy bruxism, or for restorations that would carry the entirety of a tooth's occlusal contacts, amalgam is still generally preferred. OPERATOR ABILITY AND COMMITMENT is the third, and is worth stating precisely because it inverts the conventional assumption about which material is "easier": composite's tooth PREPARATION is genuinely simpler than amalgam's, since no mechanical-retention undercuts are required, but its PLACEMENT is considerably more demanding — the multi-step etch/prime/bond sequence, more difficult insertion without the aid of condensation force, harder establishment of proximal contact and contour, and more demanding finishing and polishing. Composite therefore trades preparation simplicity for placement difficulty, precisely the reverse of amalgam's own balance of ease.

### Composition and the posterior-relevant filler categories
Composite's four-component architecture — a cross-linked dimethacrylate organic matrix (chiefly Bis-GMA and/or UDMA, diluted with TEGDMA to a workable consistency), dispersed inorganic filler, a silane coupling agent chemically bridging the two phases, and a light-, self- or dual-cure initiator-accelerator system — underlies every clinical sub-type. For posterior restoration specifically, the clinically load-bearing categories are multipurpose hybrid composite, nanocomposite, bulk-fill composite, and laboratory (fibre-reinforced) composite; standard classification tables specifically match Class I and II restorations to multipurpose, nanocomposite, bulk-filled, laboratory, or specially rated posterior microfilled/compomer products, and Class VI (MOD) restorations specifically to bulk-filled or nanocomposite materials. Formal clinical acceptance for posterior, cuspal-replacement use is governed by the American Dental Association's own proposed criteria: no more than 10% loss of colour match or marginal discolouration, no more than 5% loss of marginal integrity or recurrent caries, no more than 5% outright failure, and no more than 50μm of measured surface wear between 6 and 18 months — criteria that modern hybrid and nanofilled composites meet reliably when correctly indicated. The mechanical properties that make these filler categories suitable for posterior load-bearing use are themselves directly quantifiable: the flexural and compressive moduli of microfilled and flowable composites run roughly 50% lower than those of multipurpose hybrid and packable composites, reflecting their lower filler volume, which is precisely why microfilled and flowable products are reserved for low-stress rather than occlusal-bearing posterior sites. For direct comparison, the modulus of elasticity in compression runs approximately 62GPa for amalgam, 19GPa for dentin, and 83GPa for enamel, placing even the stiffest hybrid composites below amalgam's own rigidity — a genuine, still-relevant materials-science reason heavy-bruxism cases continue to favour amalgam over composite despite composite's modern wear-resistance improvements. Composite's bond to etched enamel and primed dentin typically achieves 20-30MPa, generated through micromechanical retention into etched enamel and, in dentin, through a genuine hybrid layer in which bonding resin infiltrates both the exposed collagen network and the dentinal tubules — this bonded retention, rather than any mechanical undercut, is what allows the smaller, more conservative posterior cavity preparation already noted among composite's advantages.

### Cavity design — the posterior-specific cavo-surface-angle trade-off
A genuinely posterior-specific cavity-design consideration concerns the cavo-surface angle and its effect on both enamel bonding and matrix compatibility. Enamel etch-pattern quality depends directly on prism orientation relative to the cut surface: a 90° cavo-surface angle, the mechanically favourable, well-supported margin, unfortunately exposes enamel prisms PARALLEL to their own long axis — the WORSE orientation for acid-etch pattern formation — while a 120° angle exposes a combination etch pattern that bonds somewhat better, though it is genuinely harder to finish a tooth-coloured restoration accurately to an oblique margin of this kind. For anterior or Class III/V restorations, where no matrix band is required, the 120° angle's superior bonding can be allowed to prevail. For Class II restorations specifically, however, this trade-off resolves in the OPPOSITE direction: because a matrix band is used, unfilled bonding resin tends to pool in the V-shaped defect created between the matrix and an obtusely finished (120°) margin, producing a weak, wear-prone, all-resin marginal zone that is clinically worse than the etch-pattern disadvantage of the alternative — so a 90° cavo-surface angle is actually preferred for Class II composite despite its inferior etching geometry, because matrix compatibility is judged the more clinically important factor. A further, directly practical finding worth including: using a dentine bonding agent, rather than the classic unfilled-resin layer, as the intermediary for bonding to etched enamel often achieves a GREATER bond strength, because modern, highly filled composites themselves contain too little free resin to wet the enamel surface adequately without such an intermediary.

### Matrix technique — the defining difference from amalgam placement
The single most important technical distinction between posterior composite and posterior amalgam placement is that composite cannot be actively condensed against a matrix band to help shape proximal contact, the way amalgam can. Because of this, posterior composite's proximal contact quality depends almost entirely on the matrix's own contour and position being correct BEFORE any material is placed, with no later condensation step available to compensate for a poorly adapted matrix. This is precisely why early wedging — placed and progressively re-tightened DURING tooth preparation itself, not merely at the time of restoration — is emphasised so strongly for composite specifically: it achieves the tooth separation needed to compensate for the matrix band's own thickness, and the band must be verified in firm contact with the adjacent tooth before composite placement begins. Several matrix systems exist with real, distinct trade-offs. Ultrathin metal matrix bands contour well and resist deformation even without active condensation force, making them suitable for general Class II use. A standard two-surface Tofflemire retainer wraps both proximal surfaces and therefore requires wedging to compensate for TWO band thicknesses at once, whereas a customised, compound-supported matrix for a single proximal box only needs to compensate for ONE band thickness, making good contact genuinely easier to achieve, at the cost of requiring green-stick compound for stabilisation. Clear polyester matrix is recommended only for very small Class II preparations, since it is thicker than metal (requiring firmer wedging) and contours and resists condensation-type force poorly with viscous composite. Sectional matrix systems with a separator ("bitine") ring have become increasingly popular for simplifying proximal-contact establishment, though careless application on wide faciolingual preparations can deform the band via the ring's own prongs and produce a poor contour, so this convenience is not a fully automatic guarantee of a good result. Several further practical points are worth including directly: metal matrix bands should be burnished on a paper pad before placement to impart correct proximal contour, exactly as for an amalgam matrix; a Tofflemire band gaping excessively at the lingual margin can be cinched tightly above the tooth's height of contour and folded aside with a large condenser, saving significant later finishing time; and for restorations requiring both proximal surfaces of the same tooth in one visit, the wedge-alternation technique — completing one proximal surface fully before beginning the other, rather than managing both simultaneously — avoids the added complexity of two unstable matrix/wedge systems and two developing contacts at once.

### Insertion technique, incremental placement, and anatomy restoration
Composite is inserted and light-cured in increments of 2mm or less, each fully cured before the next increment is placed, continuing until the preparation is slightly overfilled; light-cured material should never be dispensed until the operator is immediately ready to use it, since ambient operatory light itself can begin a premature polymerisation reaction. For Class I restorations, a stratified layering technique achieves natural-looking esthetic replication: an opaque, dentin-shade increment first replicates the lost dentin's own anatomical contour, followed by a more translucent enamel-shade increment replicating the lost enamel — with the important rule that dentin-shade increments must never be allowed to reach the cavosurface margin, deliberately leaving room for the final enamel-shade layer to cover it. Where unsupported enamel is present, those specific areas should be filled and cured first, directing the initial curing light THROUGH the remaining tooth structure to reduce early-stage shrinkage stress, before completing cure conventionally from the occlusal direction; occlusal anatomy is then preferably carved one cusp at a time, using the tooth's own remaining structure as a shaping guide. For an initial Class I carious lesion specifically, the Preventive Resin Restoration (PRR) technique remains the method of choice: only the fissure segments that are actually demineralised are opened and restored, adjacent sound fissure segments are simply sealed rather than prepared, no occlusal bevel is ever placed, and — the technique's distinctive finishing step — the entire fissure system, both the newly restored portion and any remaining sealed portion, is re-etched together as one unit and covered with a single continuous surface-sealing resin layer, unifying the whole fissure system under one protective seal. Incremental placement technique itself is worth justifying beyond shrinkage-stress management alone, since its benefits are genuinely broader: enhanced marginal adaptation, decreased gap formation, decreased marginal microleakage, decreased cuspal deformation, increased cusp fracture resistance, improved bond strength to the cavity walls, and — directly relevant to one of the ADA's own stated posterior-acceptance criteria, a documented history of postoperative sensitivity — a measurable decrease in postoperative sensitivity itself; a single technique discipline therefore addresses seven distinct potential failure modes simultaneously, which is precisely why incremental placement is treated as non-negotiable rather than merely a shrinkage-management convenience.

### Finishing and polishing — a posterior-specific technique point
Finishing should ideally be minimal, since a correctly placed final increment should already be close to its final shape and smoothness, but evaluating occlusion accurately without articulating paper is essentially impossible, so some adjustment is almost always required in practice. If matrix and wedge placement were correct in the first place, the proximal surface should already copy the matrix strip's own smoothness and typically needs no further polishing at all — this is verified directly by passing dental floss gingivo-occlusally across the surface rather than relying on visual inspection alone. Where a gingival overhang is present, it is removed with the coarse-grit side of a narrow abrasive strip, kept deliberately narrow to avoid inadvertently grinding away the proximal contact itself, or with a serrated strip saw; marginal ridges and embrasures are finished with small abrasive discs, flame-shaped burs, or a contoured carbide carver. The polishing sequence itself proceeds from coarse to fine graduated abrasive rubber points or cups, with thorough washing and drying between grits, aiming for a final surface smooth enough that the patient does not notice it and it does not irritate the tongue or oral mucosa. A genuinely counter-intuitive, evidence-based finding deserves explicit mention here: delaying finishing and polishing to a LATER appointment, rather than completing it immediately at the restorative visit as is common practice, has actually been shown to improve the restoration's eventual wear resistance, because composite has not yet reached its maximum degree of conversion — and therefore its maximum mechanical properties — immediately after curing, continuing a "dark-cure" post-polymerisation process for up to 24 hours; grinding or polishing too early therefore subjects an incompletely matured surface to rotary-instrument stress it is not yet mechanically ready to withstand. Rotary finishing itself is not without cost even when correctly timed, since it creates genuine 25-50μm surface microcracks — a direct reason to minimise rotary-instrument use overall and to avoid touching enamel margins unnecessarily.

### Advantages, disadvantages, and the current clinical-performance evidence
Composite's real advantages as a posterior restorative, stated directly: genuine esthetics; conservative tooth preparation (less extension, usually no need for uniform depth or deliberate mechanical-retention features); a simpler preparation step than amalgam's; thermal insulation, generally obviating the need for a base; near-universal applicability across restoration classes; genuine bonded retention with low microleakage, minimal interfacial staining, and real reinforcement of remaining tooth structure; and reparability. Its real disadvantages: a genuine gap-formation risk, particularly at root-surface margins, where polymerization shrinkage force can exceed the early dentin bond's own strength and open a V-shaped contraction gap; and placement that is more difficult, more time-consuming, and more costly than its simpler preparation step would suggest. On current clinical-performance evidence, older comparative trials generally favoured amalgam on annual-failure-rate metrics, but more recent, longer-duration trials have substantially narrowed this gap, and one 12-year trial of large Class II restorations found composite performing significantly better overall — though high-caries-risk patients remained a genuine exception, retaining a real amalgam advantage specifically for 3-surface restorations. A separate, directly relevant dataset bears on the direct-versus-indirect decision rather than the material choice itself: composite inlays have been found to share amalgam's own annual failure rate (1.9%), direct composite performing somewhat worse (3.6%), with indirect restorations generally showing a significantly lower failure rate than direct restorations across the posterior dentition as a whole. This evidence base is itself worth presenting with its genuine chronological evolution rather than as a single settled figure: a late-1990s review found amalgam's annual failure rate of 1.1% against composite's 2.4%, and a controlled 7-year trial of 1,748 restorations found amalgam's AFR (0.8%) significantly lower than composite's (2.2%), with composite's secondary-caries risk 3.5 times greater in that same trial. More recent, longer-duration, practice-based trials have narrowed this substantially: a 5-year, 1,262-restoration trial found no significant difference at all (amalgam 3.2% versus composite 4.4%), and the 12-year trial already cited above found composite's overall performance significantly better specifically in large Class II restorations. Wear resistance specifically has also improved dramatically across this same evolution: several controlled clinical studies, over 3- and 5-year follow-up, now demonstrate the newest nanofilled composites wearing at a rate similar to natural enamel itself — a direct, clinically demonstrated reversal of the wear-resistance limitation that originally confined composite to anterior use.

### Conclusion
Composite is now a formally accepted, evidence-supported posterior restorative material, but its correct use rests on respecting both the ADA's own stated indications/contraindications and the genuinely posterior-specific technical demands — cavo-surface-angle selection, matrix-dependent proximal-contact technique, and incremental placement discipline — that distinguish it from amalgam placement. Its clinical performance relative to amalgam is best described as case-dependent-equivalent, conditional on caries risk and restoration size, rather than universally equivalent across every posterior clinical scenario.

- Counter-intuitive finishing/polishing finding: delaying finishing to a LATER appointment improves wear resistance, since composite's degree of conversion/dark-cure continues up to 24h — polishing too early stresses an immature surface.
- A correctly matrix/wedge-placed proximal surface needs NO polishing at all — check with floss (gingivo-occlusal pass), not visual inspection.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Describing composite's posterior placement as simply "easier than amalgam" without distinguishing preparation (easier) from placement (harder) — examiners reward this specific inversion.
2. Stating a flat 90°-is-always-better or 120°-is-always-better rule for cavo-surface angle, instead of the Class-II-specific matrix-compatibility exception.
3. Omitting the structural reason matrix technique matters more for composite (no condensation force) and merely listing matrix types without explaining WHY the choice matters.
4. Quoting composite-vs-amalgam performance as a flat "composite now wins" without the high-caries-risk/3-surface exception that the same evidence base also shows.
5. Confusing the PRR technique's fissure-wide re-etch-and-seal finishing step with ordinary sealant placement — PRR unifies BOTH restored and merely-sealed fissure segments under one continuous seal, a distinctive, examinable detail.

## MNEMONIC
**"IOC"** for the three case-selection factors, in the order a clinician should actually check them chairside: **I**solation → **O**cclusion → **C**ommitment/operator ability.
