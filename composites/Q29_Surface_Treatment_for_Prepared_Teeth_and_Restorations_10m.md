## STUDY MODE

### 1. Surface treatment of a PREPARED TOOTH
For enamel, acid etching (phosphoric acid) creates micro-porosities for micromechanical resin retention — the standard, foundational surface treatment, already covered in depth elsewhere in this topic's adhesion content. For dentin, the smear layer is removed/modified and the collagen scaffold is demineralized to a controlled depth (not over-etched — see Q20's nanoleakage mechanism), then infiltrated by primer/adhesive monomers to form the hybrid layer [SRC, condensed from Q20].

### 1a. Marzouk's five-type bevel/margin classification for cavity design (TEXTBOOK AUDIT 2026-10-15, MARZ)
Beyond simple etching, the cavity MARGIN itself can be prepared in five distinct, named ways, each with a specific indication — a genuinely more detailed scheme than the usual single "bevel" concept taught generically: **(1) partial bevel** — a limited bevel on accessible margins; **(2) long bevel** — a wider bevel extending further into enamel, used where greater retentive surface area and a gradual shade transition are wanted; **(3) hollow-ground bevel** — a concave bevel specifically for gingival walls and inaccessible areas, used in direct-loading (occlusal stress-bearing) zones where a simple flat bevel is mechanically inadequate; **(4) scalloped margin** — an irregular, wavy margin outline that increases retentive surface area, but is CONTRAINDICATED at gingival or otherwise inaccessible margins, since it is harder to finish and clean there; **(5) skirting** — used for wide, shallow defects, relying on enamel retention alone rather than a bevel at all. Selecting the correct margin-design type by location and access is itself an examinable surface-treatment decision, distinct from simply "to bevel or not to bevel" [MARZ].

### 1b. Enamel etch protocol — a genuine, unresolved cross-source conflict (TEXTBOOK AUDIT 2026-10-15, A.13)
Exact etch concentration and time figures differ meaningfully across sources and should be presented attributed, not resolved to one "correct" protocol: **Mount's** gives 37% phosphoric acid for 15 seconds in one part of its own chapter, but elsewhere in the SAME chapter gives 10% orthophosphoric acid for 10 seconds applied to enamel and dentin together — Mount's own text is internally inconsistent, worth noting explicitly rather than silently picking one of its two figures. **Marzouk's** gives 35-50% phosphoric acid for 60 seconds, applied by light dabbing (not rubbing), rinsed for 1 minute, with up to 3 reapplications permitted if needed. **Pickard's** gives 37% for 20 seconds on enamel specifically, but only 10-15 seconds on dentine in three-step (etch-and-rinse) systems. **Sturdevant's/Alani&Patel** give the now-standard 4th-generation total-etch figure: 35-37% phosphoric acid, enamel 30 seconds / dentin 15 seconds. All four are legitimate textbook protocols; an exam answer should name the source alongside whichever figure it states [MOUNT/MARZ/PICK/STURD/AP].

### 1c. Etched enamel must never contact saliva (TEXTBOOK AUDIT 2026-10-15, C, MARZ)
A specific, clinically important caution: once enamel has been etched, it must NEVER be allowed to contact saliva before bonding resin is applied — an instant salivary glycoprotein biofilm forms on the freshly etched, highly reactive surface and blocks further adhesion, effectively undoing the etch. If salivary contamination does occur, RE-ETCHING is possible after decontamination (re-isolating and repeating the etch step), rather than requiring the preparation to be abandoned outright [MARZ].

### 2. Surface treatment of an existing composite RESTORATION — repair indication
Repair of a fractured composite restoration is indicated when the fracture is not severe enough to justify full replacement, or when other factors favour conservation — tooth-structure preservation, cost, time, or (for a fixed prosthesis) avoiding replacement of multiple connected units [SRC, Module 3 §3.1.3, p.241].

### 3. The core problem — aged composite bonds poorly to fresh composite
Aging of a composite restoration in the oral environment severely decreases its ability to bond to freshly placed composite, so an adhesive system is specifically recommended to mediate this bond rather than relying on the aged surface alone [SRC, p.241]. A complementary mechanism (McCabe's): aged composite absorbs water and swells; a water-imbibed surface is harder to bond to, and ongoing stress is set up at the old/new interface because the NEW composite also continues absorbing water and swelling after placement — an ongoing dimensional mismatch, not just a one-time surface-chemistry problem [SRC, p.197].

### 4. The clinical repair protocol
Roughen the aged composite surface with an INTRAORAL SANDBLASTER or a diamond bur; apply PHOSPHORIC ACID for surface cleaning; THEN apply the bonding adhesive before placing new composite [SRC, p.241]. Thorough cleaning of the resin surface to remove cutting debris helps maximise attachment strength [SRC, p.197].

### 5. Silicoating — a technique beyond simple mechanical roughening
An intraoral sandblaster can be used either to mechanically roughen the aged composite surface, OR to SILICOAT it using a tribomechanical approach — depositing a thin silica layer directly via the abrasive process itself, which can then be SILANIZED to create a genuinely bondable surface, distinct from simple mechanical roughening alone [SRC, p.197].

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 min)**

### Answer
Surface treatment serves two genuinely distinct clinical purposes: preparing a tooth surface for initial bonding, and preparing an existing restoration's surface for repair. For a prepared tooth, enamel receives phosphoric acid etching, creating micro-porosities for micromechanical resin retention, while dentin undergoes smear-layer removal and a controlled depth of collagen demineralisation, subsequently infiltrated by primer and adhesive monomers to form the hybrid layer — over-etching this dentin surface beyond the depth resin can actually impregnate is itself a real cause of bond weakness and nanoleakage, making etch-depth control as important as etching itself.

Surface treatment of the cavity MARGIN itself goes further than a single generic "bevel," with Marzouk's text describing five distinct, named margin-design types, each matched to a specific clinical situation: a partial bevel for straightforward accessible margins; a long bevel where greater retentive area and a gradual shade transition are wanted; a hollow-ground, concave bevel specifically for gingival walls and inaccessible, direct-loading zones that a flat bevel cannot mechanically support; a scalloped margin that increases retentive surface area but is contraindicated at gingival or inaccessible sites, since it is harder to finish and keep clean there; and skirting, used for wide shallow defects that rely on enamel retention alone without a bevel at all. Choosing the correct margin-design type by location and access is itself an examinable surface-treatment decision, not merely a bevel-or-no-bevel question.

Exact enamel-etch protocol figures are themselves a genuine, unresolved conflict across standard textbooks, and should be stated with attribution rather than collapsed into a single "correct" protocol. Mount's gives 37% phosphoric acid for 15 seconds in one part of its chapter, but 10% orthophosphoric acid for only 10 seconds applied to enamel and dentin together elsewhere in the same chapter — an internal inconsistency worth noting rather than silently resolving. Marzouk's gives 35-50% phosphoric acid for a full 60 seconds, applied by light dabbing rather than rubbing, with up to three reapplications permitted. Pickard's gives 37% for 20 seconds on enamel but only 10-15 seconds on dentine in three-step systems, while Sturdevant's and Alani & Patel give the now-standard fourth-generation total-etch figure of 35-37% phosphoric acid, 30 seconds on enamel and 15 seconds on dentin. All four protocols are legitimate textbook sources for the same underlying etching principle. Finally, once enamel has been etched it must never be allowed to contact saliva before bonding resin is applied, since an instant salivary glycoprotein biofilm forms on the freshly etched surface and blocks further adhesion; if contamination does occur, re-etching after proper decontamination and re-isolation is possible rather than requiring the preparation to be abandoned.

Surface treatment of an existing composite RESTORATION arises specifically in the context of repair rather than full replacement, indicated when a fracture is not severe enough to justify complete removal, or where tooth-structure preservation, cost, time, or — for a fixed prosthesis — avoiding replacement of multiple connected units favours a conservative approach. The core problem this surface treatment must overcome is that aged composite bonds poorly to freshly placed composite: ageing in the oral environment severely reduces the aged surface's own ability to bond, which is why a dedicated adhesive system is specifically recommended to mediate the repair bond rather than relying on simple mechanical contact. A complementary mechanism explains why this problem persists even after surface roughening: aged composite absorbs water and swells over time, and because the newly placed composite continues absorbing water and swelling after the repair itself, an ongoing dimensional mismatch is set up at the old-new interface — a continuing, not merely a one-time, surface-chemistry problem.

The clinical repair protocol follows directly from this mechanism: the aged composite surface is first roughened, using either an intraoral sandblaster or a diamond bur, then treated with phosphoric acid for surface cleaning, before the bonding adhesive is applied and new composite placed over it — thorough cleaning to remove cutting debris is itself important for maximising eventual attachment strength. A more sophisticated option beyond simple mechanical roughening is SILICOATING: the same intraoral sandblaster can instead be used in a tribomechanical mode to deposit a thin silica layer directly onto the aged composite surface via the abrasive process itself, which is then silanized to create a genuinely chemically bondable surface — a materially different, more reliable surface treatment than roughening alone.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Two distinct contexts: PREPARED TOOTH (etch/hybrid-layer formation) vs. EXISTING RESTORATION (repair surface treatment).
- Marzouk's 5 margin-design types: partial bevel, long bevel, hollow-ground bevel (gingival/direct-loading zones), scalloped margin (NOT at gingival/inaccessible margins), skirting (wide shallow defects, enamel retention only).
- Enamel-etch protocol is a genuine cross-source conflict — state by source: MOUNT 37%/15s (or, inconsistently, 10%/10s enamel+dentin); MARZ 35-50%/60s dabbed; PICK 37%/20s enamel, 10-15s dentin; STURD/AP 35-37%, 30s enamel/15s dentin.
- Etched enamel must never contact saliva before bonding — instant glycoprotein biofilm blocks adhesion; re-etch after decontamination if contaminated.
- Core repair problem: aged composite bonds poorly to fresh composite — both a surface-chemistry AND an ongoing water-swelling/dimensional-mismatch problem.
- Repair protocol: roughen (sandblast/diamond bur) → phosphoric acid clean → bonding adhesive → new composite.
- Silicoating (tribomechanical silica deposition + silanization) is a genuinely more sophisticated option than mechanical roughening alone.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Treating "surface treatment" as only the tooth-preparation (etching) context, omitting the restoration-repair context entirely.
2. Describing composite repair failure purely as a surface-chemistry problem, missing the ongoing water-imbibition/dimensional-mismatch mechanism.
3. Confusing silicoating (tribomechanical silica deposition + silane) with simple mechanical roughening — these are genuinely different techniques.

## MNEMONIC
**"RASP"** for the repair protocol sequence: **R**oughen → **A**cid (phosphoric, cleaning) → **S**ilicoat (optional, more advanced) → **P**lace new composite with adhesive.
