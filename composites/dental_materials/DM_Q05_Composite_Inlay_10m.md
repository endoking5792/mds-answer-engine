## STUDY MODE

### 1. Fabrication workflow
The inlay is built up in layers on a die/replica of the prepared tooth, light-curing each increment, then coated with an air-barrier gel to block the oxygen-inhibited surface layer before final processing in a dedicated unit that applies additional heat, light and, in some systems, pressure (e.g. Tescera ATL, an oxygen-free heat-pressure-light system) [LIT].

### 2. Materials-science rationale
Post-curing measurably raises degree of monomer conversion — one comparative study found a 7-12% increase in methacrylate conversion between light-cured-only and post-cured material — which correlates with improved physical/mechanical properties and wear resistance versus a purely chairside-cured direct composite of the same base chemistry [LIT]. Because the inlay cures largely outside the mouth, polymerization shrinkage is confined almost entirely to the thin cement/luting layer rather than distributed through the full restoration, improving marginal stress relaxation [LIT].

### 3. Honest limitations (contested, must state explicitly)
Differences in mechanical properties between first- and later-generation indirect systems have been found minimal and not clinically significant in some reviews, and early in-vitro wear-resistance gains did NOT consistently reproduce in long-term in-vivo follow-up — early superior strength of heat-treated composite was found to be short-lived in some studies, with no long-term difference versus direct/direct-heat-treated composite [LIT]. The laboratory step also increases cost roughly 2.5-3× over a direct restoration [LIT].

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 min)**

### Answer
A composite inlay is an indirect, laboratory-processed resin-composite restoration built up in layers on a die or replica of the prepared tooth, with each increment light-cured individually, then coated with an air-barrier gel to eliminate the oxygen-inhibited surface layer before a final processing stage in a dedicated curing unit that adds further heat, light and, in some systems such as the Tescera ATL, pressure within an oxygen-free environment. This additional post-curing step measurably raises the degree of monomer-to-polymer conversion — comparative studies report increases on the order of 7-12% in methacrylate conversion versus light-curing alone — which in turn is linked to improved physical and mechanical properties and wear resistance relative to an equivalent chairside direct composite restoration cured only by intraoral light exposure. Because the bulk of the restoration polymerizes outside the mouth before cementation, polymerization shrinkage stress is confined almost entirely to the thin resin-cement luting layer rather than distributed through the entire restoration volume, which theoretically improves marginal adaptation and stress relaxation compared with a bulk-cured direct composite of equivalent size. Indirect fabrication also makes it considerably easier to control proximal contact, contour and anatomic form on a laboratory model than chairside under isolation. However, these advantages must be stated with their honest limitations: several reviews have found the mechanical-property differences between successive generations of indirect composite systems minimal and not clinically significant, and while early in-vitro studies showed substantial wear-resistance gains and excellent margin integrity for heat/pressure-processed indirect composite, subsequent long-term in-vivo follow-up has repeatedly found little to no improvement in actual clinical occlusal wear, with the early superior strength of heat-treated composite proving short-lived rather than durable. The laboratory step also raises overall restorative cost roughly 2.5 to 3 times compared with a direct restoration. Composite inlays therefore remain a materially genuine but clinically modest advance over well-placed direct composite — justified mainly by contour/contact control and shrinkage confinement to the cement layer, rather than by a dramatic or durable wear-resistance advantage.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Workflow: layered build-up on a die → oxygen-barrier gel → post-cure with added heat/light/pressure (e.g. Tescera ATL, oxygen-free).
- Post-curing raises monomer conversion ~7-12% → better physical/mechanical properties than light-cure-only direct composite.
- Shrinkage is confined to the thin cement layer, not the whole restoration — the main real advantage.
- CONTESTED/honest limitation: long-term in-vivo wear gains often did NOT reproduce despite strong in-vitro results — a genuine, examinable caveat.
- Cost roughly 2.5-3× a direct restoration.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Presenting composite inlay advantages as uniformly proven without the long-term in-vivo caveat.
2. Omitting the oxygen-inhibition-layer-blocking gel step from the fabrication workflow.
3. Confusing shrinkage CONFINEMENT (to the cement layer) with shrinkage ELIMINATION — shrinkage still occurs, just relocated.

## MNEMONIC
**"PACT"**: **P**ost-cure (↑conversion) → **A**ir-barrier gel (no O2-inhibited layer) → **C**ontour/contact control (lab advantage) → **T**rade-off (cost ↑, long-term wear gain NOT confirmed).
