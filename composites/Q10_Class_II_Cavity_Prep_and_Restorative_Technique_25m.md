## STUDY MODE

### 1. Class II cavity nomenclature
A proximo-occlusal (MO) preparation defines the full set of walls, line angles, and point angles for this class; a disto-occlusal (DO) preparation has identical geometry with mesial/distal terminology swapped; an MOD preparation has neither a distinct mesial nor distal wall, since both proximal surfaces are involved [SRC, Sturdevant's p.296].

### 2. Retention form — the defining Class II design decision, and how it genuinely differs by material
Retention form is THE defining design decision for Class II, and differs fundamentally by restorative material [SRC, Sturdevant's p.306-307]: AMALGAM relies on facial/lingual walls of the occlusal and proximal portions CONVERGING toward the occlusal surface, with occlusal convergence itself locking the restoration in (a 90° cavosurface angle at the marginal ridge is desirable precisely because of this convergence). CAST METAL inlay/onlay instead uses opposing walls DIVERGING outward by only 2-5° per wall from the line of draw, giving near-parallel walls that provide frictional resistance/mechanical locking of the luting agent. COMPOSITE RESIN, by contrast, uses enamel margins BEVELLED OR FLARED (an angle greater than 90°) specifically to expose more enamel surface for etching, with retention coming from a micromechanical bond to etched/primed enamel and dentin rather than from wall geometry at all — groove retention may still be added on a root-surface margin where additional, GIC-like adjunct retention is wanted [SRC, Sturdevant's p.306-307]. An occlusal dovetail (simulating a Class I preparation on the surface opposite the proximal involvement) may be added to any of these three designs specifically to resist occlusal tipping forces on a single-proximal-surface (MO or DO, not MOD) cavity [SRC, Sturdevant's p.307]. **2026-10-15 textbook audit (STURD)**: Sturdevant's states explicitly that NO mechanical retention form (grooves, coves, slots) is routinely needed for Class I/II composite — pure adhesive retention is the default — EXCEPT for very large foundations or cusp-capping restorations, where such features are added. This directly corrects any framing that composite Class II design routinely includes amalgam-style retentive features [composites/_AUDIT_2026-10-15.md A.22].

### 3. A genuine design tension worth stating explicitly: bevel theory vs. Class II clinical practice
Cavity-design theory states composite margins should be bevelled/flared (>90°) to maximise etched enamel surface area for bonding [SRC, Sturdevant's p.306-307] — yet, as already established in this topic's Q3/Q5 (McCabe's cavo-surface-angle analysis), Class II restorations specifically are an exception to this general rule: because a matrix band is used, an obtusely finished (120°-type) margin allows unfilled bonding resin to POOL in the V-shaped gap between matrix and margin, producing a weak, wear-prone all-resin marginal zone — so a 90° cavo-surface angle is actually preferred for Class II composite DESPITE its inferior etch-pattern geometry, because matrix compatibility is judged more clinically important [SRC, condensed from Q3, McCabe's p.217]. This is a genuine, examinable nuance: the general composite-bevel principle and the Class-II-specific practice point in opposite directions, and a complete answer must state both rather than only the general rule.

### 4. Matrix placement — the single most important technique step (condensed from Q3)
Matrix selection and placement is one of the single most important steps in posterior composite restoration, because — unlike amalgam — composite cannot be actively condensed against the matrix to shape proximal contact; contact quality depends almost entirely on the matrix's own contour and position BEFORE placement [SRC, condensed from Q3, Sturdevant's p.588]. EARLY WEDGING (placed and re-tightened DURING preparation itself) compensates for matrix-band thickness. Matrix options carry real trade-offs: ultrathin metal bands (general use) vs. standard Tofflemire (2 band thicknesses to wedge for) vs. compound-supported matrix (1 band thickness, easier contact) vs. clear polyester (small preps only) vs. sectional/bitine ring (convenient but can deform on wide preps) [SRC, condensed from Q3]. **2026-10-15 textbook audit (PICK/STURD)**: named sectional-matrix systems add real clinical detail here — Siqveland, Automatrix, Omni-matrix, and branded sectional kits (Palodent, Composi-Tight, Quickmat) each paired with a separator ring (V-Ring or BiTine ring, whose curved tines actively separate teeth and seat the matrix cervically); prewedging BEFORE band placement is the standard rationale restated across both sources; composite warmers (e.g. Calset, heating to ~68°C) are a named adjunct that lowers viscosity for better adaptation into the matrix before cure [composites/_AUDIT_2026-10-15.md B].

### 5. Incremental placement and C-factor management (condensed from Q6)
A bulk-filled Class I box has the highest common C-factor (5.0); a simple Class II box has C-factor 2.0 — intermediate risk, managed by oblique increments connecting a maximum of two walls at a time [SRC, condensed from Q6]. Each increment should not exceed 2mm; the gingival increment specifically should be kept to ≤1mm for adequate cure through this hardest-to-seal increment [SRC, condensed from Q6, Summitt's p.605]. Horizontal layering produces significantly higher stress than oblique layering, and bulk placement performs measurably worse than incremental technique in both in-vitro and in-vivo studies [SRC, condensed from Q6].

### 6. Insertion, curing and contact verification
Composite is inserted and cured in increments until the preparation is slightly overfilled; light-cured material should never be dispensed until immediately ready for use, since ambient light can begin premature polymerisation [SRC, condensed from Q3, Sturdevant's p.591]. For same-visit restoration of both proximal surfaces, WEDGE ALTERNATION — completing one surface fully before starting the other — is recommended over managing two unstable matrix/wedge systems simultaneously [SRC, condensed from Q3].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, 1 claim, 3-5 sentences per 25m scaling)
**Claim**: "Composite cavity margins should always be bevelled to maximise bond strength, including in Class II preparations." General cavity-design principle supports bevelling composite margins to expose more enamel surface for etching, and this is genuinely correct for Class III/IV/V preparations without a matrix. For Class II specifically, however, the evidence shows the OPPOSITE is preferred: an obtuse, bevelled margin against a matrix allows unfilled resin to pool into a weak, wear-prone all-resin zone, so a 90° cavo-surface angle is actually favoured there despite its inferior etch-pattern geometry [SRC, condensed from Q3/Q5]. The defensible conclusion is that bevelling is a class-dependent, not universal, principle — the matrix-compatibility problem outweighs the etch-pattern advantage specifically where a matrix is used `[Certain]`.

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~40 min)**

### Introduction
Class II composite restoration involves two genuinely distinct technical domains that must both be executed correctly: cavity PREPARATION design, which for composite differs fundamentally from the amalgam and cast-metal design philosophies it replaced, and restorative PLACEMENT technique, governed by the structural fact that composite cannot be condensed against a matrix the way amalgam can. This essay addresses both in turn, including a genuine design tension between general composite cavity-design theory and Class-II-specific clinical practice.

### Cavity preparation and nomenclature
A proximo-occlusal (MO) preparation defines the complete set of walls, line angles and point angles for Class II; a disto-occlusal (DO) preparation shares identical geometry with mesial/distal terminology reversed; an MOD preparation, involving both proximal surfaces, has neither a distinct mesial nor distal wall. Retention form is the single defining design decision for this class, and it differs fundamentally depending on the restorative material chosen. Amalgam relies on the facial and lingual walls of the occlusal and proximal portions converging toward the occlusal surface, with this convergence itself mechanically locking the restoration in place, which is why a 90° cavosurface angle at the marginal ridge is specifically desirable for amalgam. Cast metal inlays and onlays instead use opposing vertical walls diverging outward by only 2-5° per wall from the line of draw, producing near-parallel walls that frictionally resist displacement of the luting cement. Composite resin departs from both of these mechanically retentive philosophies entirely: enamel margins are bevelled or flared to an angle GREATER than 90°, specifically to expose more enamel surface area for acid etching, with actual retention coming from a micromechanical bond to etched enamel and primed dentin rather than from cavity wall geometry at all. An occlusal dovetail, replicating a Class I preparation on the surface opposite the proximal involvement, can be added to any of these three designs specifically to resist occlusal tipping forces, but only applies to single-proximal-surface MO or DO cavities, not a full MOD preparation.

### A genuine design tension: general bevel theory versus Class II practice
A point worth stating explicitly, since it is frequently tested and frequently answered incompletely, is that the general composite cavity-design principle above — bevel margins to maximise etched enamel surface — does NOT hold without qualification for Class II specifically. Because a matrix band is used in virtually every Class II composite restoration, an obtusely bevelled (effectively 120°-type) margin allows unfilled bonding resin to pool in the V-shaped gap formed between the matrix and that oblique margin, producing a weak, wear-prone, all-resin marginal zone precisely at the proximal contact. For this reason, a 90° cavosurface angle — despite giving a genuinely worse enamel-etch pattern than a bevelled margin — is actually preferred for Class II composite restoration, because the matrix-compatibility problem is judged clinically more significant than the etch-pattern disadvantage. A complete, examiner-rewarded answer must state both the general bevel principle and this specific Class II exception, rather than presenting only one as if it were universal.

### Matrix placement — the defining technique difference from amalgam
The single most important technical distinction between composite and amalgam placement for Class II is that composite cannot be actively condensed against a matrix band to help shape the proximal contact. Proximal contact quality therefore depends almost entirely on the matrix's own contour and position being correct before any material is placed, with no later condensation step available to compensate for an inadequately adapted matrix. Early wedging, placed and progressively re-tightened DURING tooth preparation rather than only at the time of restoration, achieves the tooth separation needed to compensate for the matrix band's own thickness. Several matrix systems carry genuinely different trade-offs: ultrathin metal bands suit general use; a standard Tofflemire retainer wraps both proximal surfaces and must therefore be wedged to compensate for two band thicknesses; a customised, compound-supported matrix for a single proximal box only needs to compensate for one band thickness, making good contact easier to achieve; clear polyester matrix is recommended only for very small preparations, since it contours and resists condensation-type force poorly with viscous composite; and sectional matrix with a separator ring simplifies contact establishment but can deform on wide faciolingual preparations if applied carelessly.

### Incremental placement and shrinkage-stress management
Cavity configuration directly determines shrinkage risk: a simple Class II proximal box carries an intermediate C-factor of 2.0, materially lower than a bulk-filled Class I box's 5.0 but still high enough to require deliberate management through oblique incremental placement connecting no more than two cavity walls per increment. Each increment should not exceed 2mm, and the gingival increment specifically should be kept even thinner, at around 1mm, to ensure adequate cure through this hardest-to-seal portion of the restoration. Horizontal layering produces significantly more stress than oblique layering because it bonds a larger wall area simultaneously, and both in-vitro and in-vivo evidence confirm that bulk placement in a single large increment performs measurably worse than proper incremental technique.

### Insertion, curing, and contact verification
Composite is inserted and light-cured in increments until the preparation is slightly overfilled, and light-cured material should never be dispensed until the operator is immediately ready to use it, since ambient operatory light can itself begin premature polymerisation. Where both proximal surfaces of the same tooth require restoration in one visit, the wedge-alternation technique — completing one proximal surface fully before beginning the other — is recommended over attempting to manage two unstable matrix and wedge systems, and two developing contacts, simultaneously.

### Conclusion
Successful Class II direct composite restoration rests on correctly executing two genuinely distinct technical domains: a cavity-design philosophy fundamentally different from amalgam's mechanical retention, including the specific, often-overlooked exception that a 90° rather than bevelled margin is preferred where a matrix is used, and a placement technique built around the structural fact that composite's proximal contact depends on matrix precision rather than condensation force, combined with disciplined incremental, low-C-factor placement to manage polymerization shrinkage stress.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Describing composite cavity design purely in terms of bevelling, without stating the Class-II-specific exception (90° preferred due to matrix compatibility).
2. Applying amalgam's convergent-wall retention logic to a composite Class II preparation — composite's retention is adhesive, not geometric.
3. Omitting matrix technique entirely and treating Class II composite placement as identical to amalgam condensation.
4. Using a C-factor-blind description of incremental technique instead of citing Class II's actual intermediate C-factor (2.0) and the oblique/≤2-wall rule.
5. Forgetting the occlusal-dovetail qualifier that it applies only to MO/DO, not MOD, preparations.

**"DRMI"** for the four technique domains, in logical sequence: **D**esign (retention form/90° exception) → **R**etention via bonding, not geometry → **M**atrix precision (no condensation) → **I**ncremental/C-factor placement.
