## STUDY MODE

### 1. Curing chemistry (condensed from Q1)
Light-cured composites polymerise "on demand" via blue light (~465nm peak) absorbed by the photosensitiser camphorquinone (0.1-1.0%), with an organic amine accelerating free-radical generation [SRC, condensed from Q1].

### 2. Generations of curing units `[RECALL][Certain]`
QUARTZ-TUNGSTEN-HALOGEN (QTH) lamps were the long-standing conventional standard, using a tungsten filament and a broad-spectrum filter to isolate the blue wavelength range, but generate significant heat and lose intensity as the bulb/filter ages. PLASMA ARC CURING (PAC) units use an electrical arc between two electrodes to produce very high intensity light, enabling much shorter exposure times, but at greater cost and heat generation, with a narrower effective spectral range. ARGON LASER curing units produce a single, highly specific wavelength closely matched to camphorquinone's absorption peak, giving efficient cure, but are costly and less widely adopted. LIGHT-EMITTING DIODE (LED) units are now the dominant modern standard: they generate a narrow-band blue output closely matched to camphorquinone's absorption, produce much less heat than QTH, have a far longer effective bulb life, and newer multi-wave LED units add a second wavelength to also activate alternative photoinitiators used in some low-shrink/bulk-fill formulations.

### 3. Depth of cure and intensity falloff (condensed from Q3/Q6)
Curing-light output diminishes sharply with distance — 50% of light output is lost at just 3-6mm from the tip — so the light tip should be positioned as close as possible to the resin [SRC, condensed from Q3]. A standard composite achieves adequate cure to roughly 2-2.5mm depth with a 20-second exposure; bulk-fill materials, engineered with greater translucency, extend this to 4-5mm [SRC, condensed from Q1/Q6].

### 4. Margin-dependent effectiveness of light-directing technique (condensed from Q3/Q6)
Light-reflecting wedges direct cure through a clear matrix to a gingival increment, but effectiveness is genuinely margin-dependent — helping on ENAMEL margins, but failing to reduce microleakage on DENTIN margins — and light transmission through such wedges varies 66-95%, requiring roughly 50% longer exposure to compensate [SRC, condensed from Q6].

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 min)**

### Answer
Light-cured composites polymerise on demand through blue light, with a peak wavelength near 465nm, absorbed specifically by the photosensitiser camphorquinone, present at 0.1-1.0% concentration, with an organic amine accelerating the resulting free-radical generation. Several generations of curing device deliver this wavelength. Quartz-tungsten-halogen lamps were the long-standing conventional standard, using a heated tungsten filament with a spectral filter to isolate the required blue range, but they generate significant heat and gradually lose output intensity as the bulb and filter age. Plasma arc curing units instead produce very high-intensity light via an electrical arc between two electrodes, enabling substantially shorter exposure times, at the cost of greater equipment expense, more heat generation, and a narrower effective spectral range. Argon laser units produce a single wavelength closely matched to camphorquinone's own absorption peak, giving efficient, targeted cure, but remain costly and are not widely adopted. Light-emitting diode units are now the dominant modern standard, producing a narrow-band output closely matched to camphorquinone's absorption with much less heat generation than halogen technology and a far longer effective working life; newer multi-wave LED units add a second emission wavelength specifically to activate the alternative photoinitiators used in some low-shrink or bulk-fill formulations that camphorquinone alone would not efficiently cure.

Regardless of generation, curing-light output falls off sharply with distance from the tip, with roughly 50% of output lost at just 3-6mm — a direct, practical reason the light guide must be positioned as close as possible to the composite surface during every exposure. A conventional composite reaches adequate cure to a depth of only 2-2.5mm with a standard 20-second exposure, while bulk-fill materials, engineered with deliberately increased translucency, extend this reliably to 4-5mm. Light-directing technique using a clear matrix and reflecting wedge to cure a gingival increment is genuinely margin-dependent rather than universally effective: it improves marginal adaptation on enamel margins, but has failed to reduce microleakage on dentin margins in controlled comparison, and because light transmission through such wedges varies widely, from 66% to 95% of incident output, exposure time directed through a wedge should be increased by roughly 50% to compensate for this variability.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Curing chemistry: camphorquinone (0.1-1.0%) absorbs ~465nm blue light + amine accelerator → free radicals.
- Generations: QTH (heat, bulb degradation) → PAC (high intensity, costly, narrow spectrum) → argon laser (precise wavelength, costly) → LED (modern standard; multi-wave LED covers alternative photoinitiators).
- Intensity falloff: 50% of output lost at just 3-6mm from the tip — position the tip as close as possible.
- Depth of cure: ~2-2.5mm standard composite (20s exposure) vs. 4-5mm bulk-fill (greater translucency).
- Reflecting-wedge technique: helps on ENAMEL margins, fails to reduce leakage on DENTIN margins — not universal.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing curing-light generations without connecting each to its specific trade-off (heat/cost/spectrum/bulb life).
2. Omitting the sharp intensity-falloff-with-distance figure — a commonly tested, specific numeric fact.
3. Presenting the light-reflecting-wedge technique as universally effective rather than margin-dependent.
