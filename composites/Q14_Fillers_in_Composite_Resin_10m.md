## STUDY MODE

### 1. Role of filler
Fillers are the dispersed inorganic particles (ground glass, quartz, ceramic, or silica) bonded to the resin matrix via a silane coupling agent; they directly determine a composite's mechanical strength, wear resistance, thermal expansion, polymerization shrinkage, radiopacity, and esthetics [SRC, condensed from Q1, Craig's p.136].

### 2. Classification by particle size (condensed from Q1/Q9)
Macrofill (20-30μm, opaque, low wear) → Microfill (0.04μm, 32-50% filler, best polish/esthetics, lower strength) → Hybrid/Microhybrid (dual-size blend, 60-70% filler, general-purpose/posterior) → Nanofill (true, 1-100nm throughout, 72-79% filler, high polish AND strength) → Nanohybrid (large particles + added nanoparticles, NOT a true nanofill despite branding).

### 3. Clinical significance of filler content
Higher filler loading directly RAISES mechanical strength/modulus and LOWERS volumetric shrinkage, but also raises stiffness — and shrinkage STRESS correlates with stiffness more than with raw shrinkage % [SRC, condensed from Q6]. Filler content and particle size directly control surface hardness/roughness/abrasion resistance — Vickers hardness scales from ~18 (unfilled resin) to ~100 (heavily filled hybrid) [SRC, condensed from Q7]. Radiopacity requires specific high-atomic-number filler glasses (barium, strontium, zirconium) or nanomeric zirconia, since filler loading alone does not guarantee adequate radiographic visibility [SRC, condensed from Q1].

### 4. Filler-loading-by-class and crack-mechanics (new, 2026-10-15 textbook audit)
Exact filler loading differs genuinely by source and composite class — cite with attribution rather than one number: Craig's gives multipurpose/hybrid 60-70vol%, nanocomposite 72-79%, microfilled 32-50%, bulk-fill 59-80%, flowable 42-62%; Phillips' figures are close but not identical (small-particle hybrid 65-77vol%/80-90wt%); van Noort cites "as high as 74vol%" for some posterior composites; Bonsor states modern composites reach ~86wt%/70-72vol% [composites/_AUDIT_2026-10-15.md A.1]. The mechanism by which filler toughens the matrix is now sourced precisely: STRONG filler-matrix coupling (via silane) forces a propagating crack to detour AROUND each filler particle (toughening); WEAK coupling instead produces "crack blunting," where a void opens ahead of the crack tip, perpendicular to propagation, blunting it (Phillips) [composites/_AUDIT_2026-10-15.md C]. Comparator fracture-toughness figures (McCabe): conventional composite ≈1.2, unfilled acrylic ≈1.0, heavily filled hybrid ≈2.0 MN·m⁻¹·⁵ (context: mild steel ≈100).

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 min)**

### Answer
Fillers are the dispersed inorganic particles — finely ground glass, quartz, ceramic or silica — bonded to a composite's organic resin matrix via a silane coupling agent, and they are the single most clinically significant variable determining a composite's mechanical and optical behaviour. Classified by particle size, macrofill composites use large 20-30μm particles, giving opaque, low-wear-resistance restorations that are now largely historical. Microfill composites use very fine 0.04μm colloidal silica at only 32-50% filler loading, giving the best polish and esthetics but lower strength and higher shrinkage. Hybrid and microhybrid composites deliberately blend two particle-size populations, achieving a genuinely high 60-70% filler loading through efficient packing, and remain the general-purpose, posterior-capable workhorse. True nanofill composites keep every particle within the strict 1-100nm nanotechnology range throughout the matrix, reaching the highest filler loading of all (72-79%) while every particle sits below the wavelength of visible light, combining high polish with high strength simultaneously — a genuine advance, as distinct from a nanoHYBRID, which is structurally still a hybrid material with nanoparticles merely added to it and does not achieve the same optical benefit despite similar branding.

The clinical significance of filler content is direct and multi-dimensional. Higher filler loading raises mechanical strength and elastic modulus while lowering raw volumetric shrinkage, but this comes with a genuine trade-off: a stiffer, more highly filled material converts a given shrinkage strain into more stress by Hooke's law, so shrinkage stress correlates more closely with stiffness than with shrinkage percentage alone. Filler content and particle size also directly control surface hardness, roughness and abrasion resistance — Vickers hardness scales from roughly 18 for unfilled resin up to nearly 100 for a heavily filled hybrid composite, which is why finishing and polishing technique must be matched to a composite's specific filler architecture. Radiopacity depends not simply on filler quantity but on filler chemistry: composites require specific high-atomic-number glass fillers such as barium, strontium or zirconium, or nanomeric zirconia in nanofilled products, since an inadequately radiopaque filler leaves enamel-composite margins difficult to locate on a radiograph regardless of how much filler is present. Filler particle size and loading together therefore determine, in a single unifying variable, a composite's esthetics, strength, wear resistance, shrinkage behaviour and radiographic detectability — making filler selection the central engineering decision underlying every composite sub-type's clinical indication. The precise loading figures for each class are themselves genuinely source-dependent rather than fixed: Craig's gives hybrid composites 60-70 volume percent and nanocomposites 72-79 percent, while Phillips' and van Noort's figures for the same categories differ slightly, and this variation should be acknowledged rather than quoted as one settled number. The actual toughening mechanism filler provides depends on the quality of the silane coupling at the filler-matrix interface: where coupling is strong, a propagating crack must detour around each particle it meets, genuinely absorbing energy; where coupling is weak, the particle instead produces crack blunting, a void opening just ahead of the crack tip that slows but does not meaningfully toughen the material.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Filler determines strength, wear resistance, shrinkage, radiopacity and esthetics — the single most clinically significant composite variable.
- Macrofill (20-30µm, opaque) → Microfill (0.04µm, 32-50%, best polish) → Hybrid (dual-size, 60-70%) → true Nanofill (1-100nm, 72-79%, polish+strength together).
- Higher filler → higher strength/modulus but also higher stiffness → shrinkage STRESS tracks stiffness, not shrinkage % alone.
- Vickers hardness scales with filler content: ~18 (unfilled) → ~100 (heavily filled hybrid).
- Radiopacity needs specific high-Z filler chemistry (Ba/Sr/Zr or nanomeric zirconia) — not simply "more filler."

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Listing filler classification without connecting each category to its clinical significance (strength/esthetics/shrinkage trade-off).
2. Treating a nanoHYBRID as equivalent to a true nanoFILL — a real, examinable distinction.
3. Assuming more filler automatically means more radiopaque — filler CHEMISTRY, not just quantity, determines this.

## MNEMONIC
**"SWRE"** for the four clinical-significance dimensions of filler: **S**trength/modulus → **W**ear/hardness → **R**adiopacity (chemistry-dependent) → **E**sthetics/polish.
