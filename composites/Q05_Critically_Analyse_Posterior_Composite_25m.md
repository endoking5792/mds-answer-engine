## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~40 min)**

### Introduction
A critical analysis of posterior composite restorations must weigh genuine, now well-evidenced advantages against limitations that remain genuinely unresolved, rather than defaulting to either an uncritical endorsement or an outdated dismissal. Composite has evolved from a strictly anterior, esthetic-only material into a formally ADA-accepted posterior option, but that acceptance is conditional — on correct case selection, meticulous technique, and realistic appraisal of where the clinical evidence actually stands — not unconditional. This answer weighs composite's genuine strengths against its genuine, still-unresolved limitations in turn, before stating the single most decisive piece of comparative evidence available and the conditional verdict it actually supports.

### Genuine advantages
Composite's adhesive retention requires no mechanical-undercut cavity design, making posterior preparation genuinely more conservative of tooth structure than amalgam's. Bonding also gives composite a real biomechanical advantage amalgam cannot offer: direct reinforcement of remaining tooth structure rather than simple cavity-filling. Esthetics remain excellent even in posterior sites. Critically, the historic wear-resistance gap that originally excluded composite from stress-bearing posterior use has been substantially closed: modern nanofilled composites have been shown, in controlled 3- and 5-year clinical studies, to wear at a rate similar to natural enamel itself — the strongest single evidentiary anchor for describing posterior composite's evolution as a genuine, not merely marketed, success.

A further genuine, quantified advantage worth including: composite's bond to etched enamel and primed dentin typically reaches 20-30MPa through micromechanical retention and, in dentin, a true hybrid layer of infiltrated collagen and resin tags — a real biomechanical mechanism, not a marketing claim, underlying the "reinforcement" advantage already stated.

### Genuine, unresolved limitations
Four limitations deserve equally direct treatment rather than minimisation, since a merely descriptive list of strengths would not satisfy the command word. Polymerization shrinkage and its downstream consequences — marginal gap formation, secondary-caries risk, postoperative sensitivity — remain an active engineering problem; even bulk-fill composite's marginal-adaptation evidence is described in standard sources as only "comparable" to incremental technique, with a few studies showing equivocal results and most evaluation still in vitro. Technique sensitivity is real and clinically consequential: adequate isolation, correct incremental placement, and matrix-dependent proximal-contact technique all directly determine outcome in a way amalgam's more forgiving, condensable placement does not — composite cannot be packed against a matrix the way amalgam can, so proximal-contact quality depends almost entirely on correct matrix contour and position before any material is placed. A related, genuinely posterior-specific design limitation concerns the cavo-surface angle: the mechanically favourable 90° margin gives a WORSE enamel-etch pattern than a 120° margin, yet 90° is still preferred for Class II composite specifically, because an obtusely finished margin against a matrix allows unfilled resin to pool into a weak, wear-prone all-resin zone — a real, structural trade-off rather than a simple design oversight. Biocompatibility of released monomer components (Bis-GMA, TEGDMA, UDMA, all cytotoxic in vitro as bulk monomer) remains a genuinely debated question rather than a settled non-issue, and cost together with chairside time remain real, practical disadvantages relative to amalgam.

### The decisive critical-evidence point
The single most important critical qualification concerns longevity evidence itself. Older comparative trials consistently favoured amalgam on annual-failure-rate metrics, but more recent, longer-duration trials have substantially narrowed this gap, and a 12-year trial of 1,949 large Class II restorations found composite performing significantly better overall than high-copper amalgam. Crucially, however, the SAME trial found amalgam retaining a real, specific advantage in high-caries-risk patients requiring 3-surface restorations, with no material difference at all for larger 4- and 5-surface restorations. This is the decisive critical point an examiner rewards: the evidence supports case-dependent equivalence — conditional on caries risk and restoration size — not a uniform, unconditional claim that composite now universally matches or exceeds amalgam. A separate, directly relevant dataset reinforces the same case-dependent framing from the direct-versus-indirect angle: composite inlays have been found to share amalgam's own annual failure rate (1.9%), while direct composite restorations performed somewhat worse (3.6%), and indirect restorations generally showed a significantly lower failure rate than direct ones across the posterior dentition as a whole — meaning the restorative-technique decision (direct versus indirect), not only the material itself, is a genuine, independent variable in posterior composite's real-world performance.

### Conclusion
Posterior composite restorations are a genuine, evidence-supported clinical success relative to their original, materials-science-limited posterior role, but a critically honest analysis must state their limitations — shrinkage-related marginal risk, technique sensitivity, incompletely settled biocompatibility, and a caries-risk-dependent rather than universal performance advantage — as plainly as their strengths. The correct verdict is conditional success under appropriate case selection, not unconditional superiority: composite deserves recognition as a genuine materials-science and clinical achievement relative to its original, wear-limited anterior-only role, while still requiring the same case-by-case clinical judgment — caries risk, restoration size, occlusal load, isolation feasibility — that any posterior restorative decision demands, rather than being treated as a default, unconditionally correct choice in every posterior scenario.

- The single decisive critical point: a 12-year trial found composite significantly better overall EXCEPT in high-caries-risk 3-surface restorations, where amalgam still wins.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Writing a purely descriptive answer (composition/classification) instead of a weighed, critical one — "discuss critically" specifically penalises this.
2. Stating a flat "composite now equals/beats amalgam" without the high-caries-risk/3-surface exception the same evidence also shows.
3. Omitting biocompatibility or technique-sensitivity as genuine limitations, presenting composite as flaw-free.

## MNEMONIC
