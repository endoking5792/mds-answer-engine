## STUDY MODE

### 1. The shared decision framework
Sturdevant's frames the amalgam-vs-composite choice around three practical factors, since these two materials are typically what is actually being chosen between for a direct posterior restoration [SRC, p.694]: OCCLUSAL LOAD (heavy bruxism/full-occlusal-contact restorations favour amalgam); ISOLATION (bonding needs a contamination-free field — genuinely impossible isolation favours non-bonded amalgam); and OPERATOR ABILITY/COMMITMENT (composite's preparation is simpler, but its placement — etch/prime/bond, insertion, contour, finishing — is more demanding than amalgam's).

### 2. Amalgam — indications, merits and demerits
Amalgam is indicated specifically for moderate-large Class I/II restorations (especially under heavy occlusion, poor isolation, or root-surface extension), Class V restorations that are not esthetically critical/poorly isolated/entirely on the root surface, temporary caries-control restorations in badly broken-down teeth pending pulpal reassessment, and foundations/core buildups needing extra retention before a crown [SRC, p.694-695]. MERITS [SRC, p.695]: ease of use; high compressive strength; excellent wear resistance; favourable long-term clinical research; lower cost than composite. DEMERITS: non-insulating; non-esthetic; less conservative of tooth structure (more removal needed for mechanical retention); weakens remaining tooth structure (no reinforcement); more technique-sensitive IF bonded; more difficult tooth preparation; initial marginal leakage. A directly quotable editorial honesty worth citing: Sturdevant's own amalgam chapter captions a figure of five amalgam restorations with "Most practitioners would restore all of these teeth with composite except tooth No. 30" — even an amalgam-focused chapter concedes amalgam is now the exception, not the default [SRC, p.694].

### 3. Composite — merits and demerits
MERITS [SRC, p.507]: esthetic; conservative of tooth structure (less extension, uniform depth/mechanical retention usually unnecessary); simpler tooth preparation; insulative (low thermal conductivity); near-universal applicability; bonded (good retention, low microleakage, minimal interfacial staining, genuinely strengthens remaining tooth structure); repairable. DEMERITS: gap-formation risk, especially at root-surface margins where shrinkage force can exceed early dentin bond strength, opening a V-shaped contraction gap; more difficult, time-consuming and costly placement (multi-step bonding, harder insertion/contour/finishing).

### 4. The asymmetric trade-off worth stating explicitly
The two materials trade PREPARATION ease for PLACEMENT ease in opposite directions: amalgam has harder preparation (mechanical retention undercuts) but easier placement (condensable, technique-tolerant); composite has easier preparation (adhesive, no undercuts needed) but harder placement (multi-step bonding, technique-sensitive). This is the single most examiner-rewarded structural point distinguishing a genuinely comparative answer from two separate lists.

### 5. Clinical performance evidence (condensed from Q1)
Older trials favoured amalgam; a 12-year large-Class-II trial found composite significantly better overall, EXCEPT in high-caries-risk patients needing 3-surface restorations, where amalgam retained a real advantage [SRC, condensed from Q1]. A separate dataset on direct-vs-indirect technique (Manhart et al., 2004) found composite inlays sharing amalgam's own 1.9% AFR, with direct composite performing worse (3.6%) — technique, not only material, is itself a genuine variable [SRC, condensed from Q2].

### 6. Quantified materials-science comparators (new, 2026-10-15 textbook audit)
A second, independent survival dataset worth citing alongside the above: Chadwick et al. 2001 found amalgam's 7-year survival at 94.5% vs composite's 67.4% — a starker gap than the 12-year trial above, illustrating how much comparative outcome depends on study design/era; present both rather than picking one [PHIL, ledger C]. Placement-TIME data (same review): Class I amalgam ~11min vs composite ~15min; Class II amalgam ~15min vs composite ~24min — composite consistently takes longer chairside, a real practical demerit beyond technique sensitivity alone. Relative lifetime COST ratio amalgam:composite:ceramic/composite-inlay = 1:1.62:6.35 at 5 years. Hard materials-science comparators worth citing directly rather than only qualitatively: compressive modulus — amalgam 62GPa vs dentin 19GPa vs enamel 83GPa (composite's own flexural modulus by class is much lower: hybrid 8.8-13GPa, flowable only 2.6-5.6GPa); Knoop hardness — amalgam 110 kg/mm² vs composite 22-80 kg/mm² (both CR/MICH); radiopacity — amalgam >10mm aluminium-equivalent vs composite only 2-4mm Al-equivalent for hybrids/0.5-2mm for microfilled (MICH) — a genuine clinical-detection disadvantage for composite that is often omitted from "merits/demerits" answers [composites/_AUDIT_2026-10-15.md A.6/C].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS (compressed, 1-2 claims per 20m scaling)
**Claim**: "Composite's merits make it the objectively superior posterior restorative choice in routine practice." The evidence does not support an unconditional verdict: amalgam genuinely retains real merits (strength, wear resistance, lower technique sensitivity, lower cost, and a longevity advantage specifically in high-caries-risk patients), while composite's merits (esthetics, conservative prep, bonding-based reinforcement) are real but come with genuine demerits (gap-formation risk, technique sensitivity, cost/time) [SRC, condensed]. The defensible conclusion is that the "merits vs. demerits" comparison resolves case-by-case via the three-factor framework (occlusal load, isolation, operator commitment), not to a single universally superior material `[Certain]`.

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~31-36 min)**

### Introduction
Amalgam and composite remain the two materials most directly compared for posterior restoration, and Sturdevant's frames this choice around three practical factors — occlusal load, isolation, and operator ability/commitment — since these largely determine which material's merits actually matter in a given case. This essay sets out each material's real merits and demerits in turn, then states the asymmetric trade-off and evidence that should govern the final decision.

### Amalgam: merits and demerits
Amalgam's real merits are ease of use, high compressive strength, excellent wear resistance, a long and favourable clinical research record, and lower cost than composite. Its real demerits are that it is non-insulating and non-esthetic, requires less conservative tooth preparation since mechanical retention demands more tooth removal, does not reinforce remaining tooth structure the way a bonded material can, becomes more technique-sensitive specifically when bonded, and shows some initial marginal leakage. A genuinely telling detail worth including directly: even Sturdevant's own amalgam-focused chapter captions an illustrative figure of five amalgam restorations by noting that most practitioners would now restore all but one of them with composite instead — a concession, from within the amalgam literature itself, that amalgam has become the exception rather than the default for most of its own classic indications. Amalgam's own indications remain genuinely real rather than purely historical, however: moderate-to-large Class I/II restorations under heavy occlusion or poor isolation, Class V restorations where esthetics is explicitly unimportant or isolation is poor, temporary caries-control restorations in badly broken-down teeth pending pulpal reassessment, and foundations or core build-ups needing extra mechanical retention before a crown.

### Composite: merits and demerits
Composite's real merits are genuine esthetics, more conservative tooth preparation since adhesive retention needs no mechanical undercut, a simpler preparation step overall, thermal insulation generally avoiding the need for a base, near-universal applicability across restoration classes, and bonded retention that gives low microleakage, minimal interfacial staining, and real reinforcement of remaining tooth structure, together with reparability. Its real demerits are a genuine gap-formation risk, particularly at root-surface margins where polymerization shrinkage force can exceed the early dentin bond's own strength and open a V-shaped contraction gap, and placement that is considerably more difficult, time-consuming and costly than its simpler preparation step would suggest.

### The asymmetric trade-off
The most examiner-rewarded structural point in this comparison is that the two materials trade preparation ease against placement ease in opposite directions. Amalgam demands a harder, more destructive preparation but offers an easier, more technique-tolerant placement, since it can be condensed into position. Composite demands an easier, more conservative preparation but a harder, more technique-sensitive placement, since its proximal contact and marginal seal depend on multi-step bonding rather than condensation. A genuinely comparative answer should state this inversion explicitly, rather than listing each material's merits and demerits as two unrelated columns.

### Current clinical evidence
Older comparative trials generally favoured amalgam on annual-failure-rate metrics, but more recent, longer-duration trials have substantially narrowed this gap, and a 12-year trial of large Class II restorations found composite performing significantly better overall than high-copper amalgam. High-caries-risk patients remain a genuine exception, however, retaining a real amalgam advantage specifically for 3-surface restorations in that same trial, with no material difference at all for larger 4- and 5-surface restorations — meaning the evidence supports case-dependent, not universal, superiority for either material. A further, separate dataset bears on the direct-versus-indirect decision rather than the material choice alone: composite inlays have been found to share amalgam's own annual failure rate of 1.9%, while direct composite restorations performed somewhat worse at 3.6%, with indirect restorations generally showing a significantly lower failure rate than direct restorations across the posterior dentition as a whole — a reminder that technique (direct versus indirect), not only material selection, is itself a genuine variable in this comparison.

### Quantified comparators worth citing directly
Beyond qualitative merits/demerits, several hard numbers strengthen this comparison. Chadwick et al. 2001 found amalgam's 7-year survival at 94.5% against composite's 67.4% — a starker gap than the 12-year trial discussed above, a reminder that comparative survival figures vary materially by study design and era and should be presented as a range of evidence rather than one number. The same review found composite placement consistently slower chairside — roughly 15 versus 11 minutes for a Class I restoration, 24 versus 15 minutes for a Class II — translating into a relative lifetime cost ratio of amalgam:composite:ceramic-inlay of 1:1.62:6.35 at five years. Materials-science comparators worth citing by name rather than only describing qualitatively: amalgam's compressive modulus of 62GPa comfortably exceeds composite's flexural modulus across every class (hybrid 8.8-13GPa, flowable only 2.6-5.6GPa); amalgam's Knoop hardness of 110 kg/mm² exceeds composite's 22-80 kg/mm² range; and amalgam's radiopacity, at over 10mm aluminium-equivalent, is substantially higher than composite's 2-4mm (hybrid) or 0.5-2mm (microfilled) — a genuine clinical-detection disadvantage for composite, since secondary caries or a marginal gap beneath a composite restoration is correspondingly harder to identify radiographically than beneath amalgam.

### Conclusion
Neither material is unconditionally superior: amalgam's merits (strength, wear resistance, lower technique sensitivity, cost, and a real longevity edge in high-caries-risk cases) and composite's merits (esthetics, conservative preparation, bonded reinforcement) each come with genuine, material-specific demerits. The correct clinical decision rests on the shared three-factor framework — occlusal load, isolation feasibility, and operator ability/commitment — applied case by case, not on a fixed hierarchy between the two materials.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Shared decision framework: occlusal load, isolation, operator ability/commitment.
- Amalgam merits: ease of use, strength, wear resistance, long track record, lower cost. Demerits: non-esthetic, non-insulating, less conservative prep, no reinforcement, technique-sensitive if bonded.
- Composite merits: esthetic, conservative prep, bonded reinforcement, repairable. Demerits: gap-formation risk (esp. root-surface margins), harder/costlier placement.
- The asymmetric trade-off: amalgam = harder prep/easier placement; composite = easier prep/harder placement — opposite directions.
- Evidence: composite significantly better overall in a 12-year large-Class-II trial, EXCEPT high-caries-risk 3-surface restorations (amalgam advantage retained there).
