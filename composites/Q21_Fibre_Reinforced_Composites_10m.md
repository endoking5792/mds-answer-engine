## STUDY MODE

### 1. Applications
Glass or carbon fibre reinforcement of composite resin has been developed in two areas: posts for managing root-filled teeth, and composite structures of sufficient toughness to make bridges [SRC, McCabe's p.213].

### 2. What determines the degree of reinforcement
Reinforcement depends on the quantity of fibres, their orientation, the quality of bond between fibres and resin, and fibre length. Fibre LENGTH is only relevant when fibres are short — there is a critical length for each fibre type/size below which it acts more as a conventional filler particle than a toughness-imparting fibre; once fibres exceed this critical length, reinforcement depends on fibre loading and orientation [SRC, McCabe's p.213].

### 3. The Krenchel factor — quantifying orientation efficiency
The efficiency of reinforcement for a given fibre loading is described by the KRENCHEL FACTOR. Fibres oriented in ONE direction give a Krenchel factor of 1 — maximum reinforcement for a given loading, but the resulting material is ANISOTROPIC (markedly different properties by test orientation). Fibres arranged PERPENDICULAR to each other reduce efficiency by 50% in any one direction (Krenchel factor 0.5), giving an ORTHOTROPIC material (similar properties in the two fibre-plane directions, markedly different if tested in other planes). RANDOM fibre orientation gives an ISOTROPIC material (similar properties in all directions) but at only 20% reinforcement efficiency (Krenchel factor 0.2) [SRC, McCabe's p.213]. Orientation relative to the actual strain direction the restoration will experience further influences real-world reinforcement efficiency beyond the Krenchel factor alone.

### 4. Fibre-resin bonding
The ability of the fibre reinforcement to combine with the resin composite is itself vital to its effectiveness — a poor fibre-resin bond undermines even correctly oriented, adequately loaded fibre reinforcement [SRC, McCabe's p.213].

### 5. Named commercial FRC products and clinical outcome data (TEXTBOOK AUDIT 2026-10-15, VN Table 2.2.5)
Van Noort's Table 2.2.5 names specific commercial fibre-reinforced composite products by fibre type/form: **Fibre-Kor**, **Stick**, **Vectris**, and **Ribbond**, among others. Clinical outcome data differ by application: FRC used for **splints** performs well, with good outcomes reported to **4+ years**; FRC used in **removable-denture** applications shows outcomes described as **"more variable"** — a genuine, examinable distinction, since FRC's clinical reliability is NOT uniform across all its applications [VN].

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 min)**

### Answer
Fibre-reinforced composite uses glass or carbon fibres embedded within the resin matrix to increase toughness beyond what filler particles alone can provide, with two established clinical applications: posts for restoring root-filled teeth, and composite structures strong enough to serve as bridges. The degree of reinforcement actually achieved depends on four factors — the quantity of fibres present, their orientation, the quality of the bond between fibre and resin, and fibre length. Fibre length matters specifically when fibres are short: each fibre type and size has a critical length below which it behaves essentially as a conventional filler particle rather than contributing genuine toughness; only once fibres exceed this critical length does reinforcement depend on loading and orientation in the way normally described.

Orientation efficiency is quantified precisely by the Krenchel factor. Fibres aligned in a single direction achieve a Krenchel factor of 1, the maximum possible reinforcement for a given fibre loading, but the resulting material is anisotropic, meaning its properties differ markedly depending on the direction tested — a real limitation for a restoration loaded from multiple directions in function. Fibres arranged perpendicular to each other reduce reinforcement efficiency by half in any single direction, giving a Krenchel factor of 0.5 and an orthotropic material, with similar properties in the two fibre-plane directions but markedly different properties outside those planes. A fully random fibre orientation instead produces an isotropic material, with similar properties in every direction, but at a substantially lower reinforcement efficiency of only 20%, a Krenchel factor of 0.2. The correct orientation choice therefore trades maximum strength in one direction against more uniform, direction-independent strength, and should be matched to the actual strain pattern the restoration will experience in service, not chosen by efficiency alone. Finally, even correctly loaded and oriented fibres only deliver their theoretical reinforcement if the bond between fibre and resin is itself of adequate quality — a poor fibre-resin interface undermines the mechanical benefit regardless of how the fibres are arranged. Named commercial products illustrate these principles in clinical use — Fibre-Kor, Stick, Vectris, and Ribbond among them — with outcome data that differ meaningfully by application: FRC splints perform well to 4 or more years, while FRC used in removable-denture applications shows more variable outcomes, underlining that fibre reinforcement's clinical reliability is not uniform across every indication.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Two clinical applications: posts (root-filled teeth) and fibre-reinforced bridges.
- Reinforcement depends on fibre quantity, orientation, fibre-resin bond quality, AND fibre length (relevant only below a critical length).
- Krenchel factor: uniaxial = 1 (max strength, anisotropic); biaxial/perpendicular = 0.5 (orthotropic); random = 0.2 (isotropic, lowest efficiency).
- Orientation choice trades maximum directional strength against uniform, direction-independent strength — match to actual strain direction.
- Named products (VN): Fibre-Kor, Stick, Vectris, Ribbond. Outcomes: splints good to 4+ years; removable-denture FRC "more variable."

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Stating random fibre orientation as simply "better" without noting its lower (0.2) reinforcement efficiency trade-off for isotropy.
2. Omitting the critical-fibre-length concept — short fibres below this length behave as ordinary filler, not reinforcement.
3. Treating fibre-resin bond quality as secondary to loading/orientation rather than an equally necessary condition.

## MNEMONIC
**"1-0.5-0.2"**: Krenchel factor for uniaxial / biaxial-perpendicular / random fibre orientation.
