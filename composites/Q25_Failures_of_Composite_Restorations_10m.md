## STUDY MODE

### 1. Marginal failure — the dominant failure mode for composite
Unlike amalgam (which self-seals via corrosion products over time), COMPOSITE RESIN has NO comparable self-sealing ability — loss of marginal adhesion demands immediate attention, since plaque forced into an open margin under masticatory hydraulic pressure leads to rapid caries progression. Gingival-margin failure is particularly common because long-term adhesion to DENTIN (unlike enamel) remains genuinely difficult to achieve reliably [SRC, Mount's, condensed from Q1].

### 2. Bulk failure — a distinct, less common mode
For composite, bulk failure more often shows up AT THE MARGINS rather than truly in bulk (reflecting composite's greater flexibility compared with brittle materials), and is typically traced to UNDER-CURING (insufficient light exposure time or excessive light-tip distance) or CONTAMINATION between increments [SRC, Mount's p.294-296].

### 3. Staining as an early warning sign
Composite shows generally excellent SHORT-term colour match, but is genuinely vulnerable to staining specifically at any open or leaking margin — marginal staining is therefore used clinically as an early WARNING SIGN of incipient microleakage failure, not merely a cosmetic nuisance [SRC, Mount's p.291, 296-297].

### 4. Repair vs. replacement decision
Because composite has no self-sealing safety margin (unlike amalgam), marginal failure can progress to caries dangerously fast; in most cases this requires extensive REPLACEMENT rather than a limited reseal, with resealing reserved specifically for relatively NEW restorations kept under careful subsequent observation [SRC, Mount's p.291]. A specific repair-technique detail: when re-restoring after a lost composite restoration (e.g. in an erosion lesion), freshening the surface by removing up to 100μm of tooth structure is necessary, since residual resin tags remain embedded in the surface and interfere with re-establishing fresh adhesion [SRC, Mount's p.296-297].

### 4a. Composite repair — bond strength (genuine cross-source conflict) and the correct protocol (TEXTBOOK AUDIT 2026-10-15, A.15/A.16)
When repair rather than full replacement IS appropriate (a relatively new, localized failure in an otherwise sound restoration), repair bond strength is lower than original strength, but the exact figure genuinely conflicts across sources and should be stated attributed rather than merged: **Powers & Wataha and Michigan both give composite-to-composite repair bond strength as 60-80% of original cohesive strength**, even after proper surface treatment (50µm alumina abrasion + silane + isolation); **Phillips (citing Söderholm 1986) gives a notably more pessimistic figure — repaired composite strength "less than half" of original** — even with an ideal silane/acid-etch protocol. Both figures come from legitimate textbook sources; present both rather than picking one as definitive. **A genuine, commonly confused correction: hydrofluoric acid (HF) should NOT be used on composite resin for repair, unlike its correct use on ceramic.** HF degrades/etches the silica filler of composite itself, leaving a weak, porous matrix behind — this is a materially different substrate response from ceramic, where HF is the correct etchant. The correct composite repair protocol is: roughen the surface (diamond bur or sandblast) → acid-etch clean with PHOSPHORIC acid, not HF → optional silane applied to the old exposed filler → adhesive → new composite [PW/MICH/PHIL/CR].

### 5. Quantified failure-rate data (condensed from Q1)
Older trials found composite's secondary-caries risk up to 3.5× greater than amalgam's in the same trial; more recent trials have narrowed or reversed the overall AFR gap, though high-caries-risk patients retain real elevated risk for 3-surface restorations specifically [SRC, condensed from Q1].

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~16 min)**

### Answer
The dominant failure mode for composite restorations is MARGINAL rather than bulk failure. Unlike amalgam, which develops a self-sealing margin over time through its own corrosion products, composite resin has no comparable self-sealing ability — once marginal adhesion is lost, the resulting gap allows plaque to be forced in under masticatory hydraulic pressure, leading to rapid caries progression that demands prompt attention rather than watchful waiting. Gingival-margin failure is particularly common because reliable long-term adhesion to dentin, unlike enamel, remains genuinely difficult to achieve, making the gingival margin of a Class II restoration the single most common real-world point of composite failure.

Bulk failure, by contrast, is a distinct and less common mode. When it occurs in composite specifically, it more often manifests at the margins rather than truly within the bulk of the material, reflecting composite's comparatively greater flexibility relative to brittle restorative materials, and is typically traced to a specific, identifiable technique error — either under-curing, from insufficient light exposure time or an excessive light-tip-to-surface distance, or contamination occurring between successive increments during placement.

Staining provides a genuinely useful clinical early-warning function rather than being a purely cosmetic concern. Composite restorations generally show excellent short-term colour match, but are specifically vulnerable to staining at any open or leaking margin — meaning visible marginal staining should be read clinically as an early sign of incipient microleakage failure, prompting investigation, not dismissed as simple surface discoloration.

The repair-versus-replacement decision for a failing composite restoration follows directly from its lack of a self-sealing safety margin: because marginal failure can progress to caries dangerously quickly, most failing composite restorations require extensive replacement rather than a limited, localised reseal, with resealing reserved specifically for relatively new restorations that can be kept under careful subsequent observation. When re-restoring a site after a previously lost composite restoration, a specific technical step is required: freshening the surface by removing up to 100μm of tooth structure, since residual resin tags remain embedded in the surface from the earlier restoration and will interfere with establishing a fresh adhesive bond if not removed first.

Where repair, rather than full replacement, genuinely is appropriate — a relatively new, localized failure in an otherwise sound restoration — the achievable bond strength is a real, examinable cross-source conflict rather than a single settled figure. Powers & Wataha and the Michigan text both report composite-to-composite repair bond strength at 60-80% of original cohesive strength, even after correct surface treatment; Phillips, citing Söderholm's 1986 work, gives a notably more pessimistic figure of less than half of original strength even with an ideal protocol. Both figures are legitimate and should be stated by source rather than merged into one number. A frequently confused point deserves explicit correction: hydrofluoric acid, correct for etching ceramic, must NOT be used on composite resin for repair, since it degrades and etches the silica filler itself, leaving a weak, porous matrix. The correct composite repair protocol instead roughens the surface mechanically, cleans it with phosphoric acid (not HF), optionally applies silane to the exposed old filler, then proceeds through adhesive and new composite.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Composite has NO self-sealing margin (unlike amalgam) — marginal failure is the dominant mode and demands prompt attention.
- Bulk failure in composite shows up AT THE MARGINS, traced to under-curing or inter-increment contamination — not true bulk fracture.
- Marginal staining = an early WARNING SIGN of microleakage failure, not purely cosmetic.
- Repair vs. replacement: composite usually needs REPLACEMENT (no safety margin); resealing only for relatively new, closely observed restorations.
- Re-restoration technique: remove up to 100µm of surface tooth structure to eliminate embedded residual resin tags before fresh bonding.
- Repair bond strength — genuine conflict, state attributed: PW/MICH give 60-80% of original; PHIL (Söderholm 1986) gives "less than half."
- HF must NOT be used on composite for repair (unlike ceramic) — degrades the silica filler; correct protocol = roughen → phosphoric-acid clean → ±silane → adhesive → new composite.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Describing composite failure as primarily a bulk-fracture problem rather than correctly identifying marginal failure as dominant.
2. Treating marginal staining as purely cosmetic rather than as a diagnostic warning sign.
3. Omitting the 100µm surface-freshening step when describing re-restoration technique after a lost composite restoration.

## MNEMONIC
**"MSR"**: **M**arginal failure dominant (no self-seal) → **S**taining = warning sign → **R**eplace (don't just reseal), freshen 100µm first.
