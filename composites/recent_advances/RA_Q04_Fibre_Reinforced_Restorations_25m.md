## STUDY MODE

### 1. Two structurally distinct fibre-reinforcement systems
LONG/continuous fibre systems (glass or carbon fibre, typically woven or unidirectional ribbon) are used for posts (root-filled teeth) and resin-bonded bridge frameworks — McCabe's classical application set. SHORT fibre-reinforced composite (SFRC) is a newer, structurally distinct system in which short, randomly or semi-aligned discontinuous fibres are pre-mixed directly into a flowable/packable composite base material, used as a bulk dentine-replacing layer under a conventional composite cap rather than as a discrete post/framework [SRC, condensed Q21/RA-Q3].

### 1b. Named commercial FRC products with outcome data (audit B, new — van Noort Table 2.2.5)
Fibre-Kor, Stick, Vectris, Ribbond — named by fibre type/form. Outcome data: FRC splints perform well to **4+ years**; removable-denture FRC outcomes are **"more variable"** [VN Table 2.2.5].

### 2. What determines reinforcement efficiency — the Krenchel factor
Reinforcement depends on fibre quantity, orientation, fibre-resin bond quality, and fibre length (relevant only below each fibre's critical length). Orientation efficiency is quantified by the Krenchel factor: uniaxial =1 (max strength, anisotropic); perpendicular/biaxial =0.5 (orthotropic); random =0.2 (isotropic, lowest efficiency) [SRC, condensed Q21].

### 3. SFRC's clinical evidence base (new)
A 2024 systematic review of 16 in-vitro studies found universal improvement in load-bearing capacity with an SFRC base; a 2018 review (70 articles, 4 clinical) found the same combined-base-plus-cap pattern, explicitly noting evidence remains overwhelmingly laboratory-based and that SFRC-as-complete-restoration lacks consensus [LIT, condensed RA-Q3].

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~40 min)**

### Answer
Fibre-reinforced restorations use glass or carbon fibre embedded within a resin matrix to add toughness beyond what particulate filler alone can provide, and the technology now spans two structurally distinct systems worth distinguishing precisely. The classical, longer-established system uses long or continuous fibres — woven or unidirectional glass or carbon fibre ribbons — for two established clinical applications: posts for restoring root-filled teeth, and resin-bonded bridge frameworks tough enough to withstand functional loading without a metal substructure. The newer system, short fibre-reinforced composite (SFRC), takes a structurally different approach, pre-mixing short, randomly or semi-aligned discontinuous fibres directly into a flowable or packable composite base material, which is then used as a bulk dentine-replacing layer beneath a conventional composite cap in large posterior cavities, rather than as a discrete post or bridge framework.

The degree of reinforcement actually achieved by either system depends on the same four underlying variables: the quantity of fibres present, their orientation, the quality of the bond between fibre and resin, and fibre length, which matters specifically because each fibre type and size has a critical length below which it behaves essentially as a conventional filler particle rather than contributing genuine toughness. Orientation efficiency for long/continuous-fibre systems is quantified precisely by the Krenchel factor. Fibres aligned uniaxially, in a single direction, achieve a Krenchel factor of 1, the maximum possible reinforcement for a given fibre loading, but the resulting material is anisotropic, with properties differing markedly by test direction — a real limitation for a restoration loaded from multiple directions during function, such as a post under lateral occlusal forces. Fibres arranged perpendicular to each other halve reinforcement efficiency in any single direction, giving a Krenchel factor of 0.5 and an orthotropic material with similar properties in the two fibre-plane directions but different properties outside those planes. Fully random fibre orientation instead gives an isotropic material, with similar properties in every direction, but at a substantially lower efficiency of only 0.2. This orientation choice is a genuine engineering trade-off between maximum directional strength and uniform, direction-independent strength, and should be matched to the restoration's actual anticipated strain pattern rather than chosen for maximum theoretical efficiency alone; even correctly loaded and oriented fibres deliver no benefit if the fibre-resin bond itself is of inadequate quality.

Short fibre-reinforced composite applies this same underlying fibre-mechanics logic in a bulk rather than discrete form, and its clinical rationale is specifically aimed at composite's weakest mechanical property — fracture toughness — in the large posterior cavities where that weakness is most consequential, since randomly distributed short fibres within the bulk material act to arrest and deflect propagating cracks before they reach the full restoration thickness. The evidence supporting this approach, while still overwhelmingly laboratory-based, is notably consistent: a 2024 systematic review of sixteen in vitro studies found that every single included study reported improved load-bearing capacity when an SFRC base was used beneath a conventional composite cap, and an earlier 2018 review of seventy articles found the same combined-base pattern, with the SFRC base improving both load-bearing capacity and the mode of eventual failure compared with plain composite alone. That 2018 review, however, found only four of its seventy included articles to be clinical reports rather than laboratory studies, and there is explicitly no scientific consensus yet on extending SFRC from a reinforcing base to a complete, stand-alone restoration — a distinction that should be stated precisely rather than allowing the strong in vitro base-reinforcement evidence to be over-generalised into an unqualified recommendation for SFRC restorations more broadly. Fibre-reinforced restorations therefore represent a mechanistically well-understood and, for long-fibre post/bridge applications, clinically well-established technology, now extended through SFRC into a genuinely promising but still laboratory-dominated bulk-reinforcement strategy for exactly the large posterior cavities where composite's fracture-toughness limitation matters most.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Two distinct systems: LONG/continuous fibre (posts, bridge frameworks — classical) vs. SHORT fibre-reinforced composite/SFRC (bulk dentine-replacing base — newer).
- Krenchel factor: uniaxial =1 (max strength, anisotropic); perpendicular/biaxial =0.5 (orthotropic); random =0.2 (isotropic, lowest efficiency).
- Critical fibre length: below it, a fibre behaves as ordinary filler, not reinforcement.
- SFRC evidence: 2024 review — 16/16 in-vitro studies show improved load-bearing capacity with an SFRC base; 2018 review — only 4/70 articles were clinical.
- NO consensus yet on SFRC as a COMPLETE restoration (vs. as a reinforcing base) — a precise, examinable distinction.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Conflating long/continuous-fibre post-and-bridge technology with short fibre-reinforced composite (SFRC) bulk bases — they are structurally and clinically distinct.
2. Treating random fibre orientation as simply "better" without its lower (0.2) Krenchel efficiency trade-off.
3. Extrapolating SFRC-as-base evidence into "SFRC restorations are proven" — the complete-restoration use case lacks consensus.
4. Omitting critical fibre length — short fibres below it act as ordinary filler, not reinforcement.

## MNEMONIC
**"1-0.5-0.2, base not whole"**: Krenchel factors for uniaxial/biaxial/random orientation, plus the key SFRC caveat — proven as a BASE, not yet as a whole restoration.
