## STUDY MODE

### 1. Build-up (layering/stratification)
Opaque dentin-shade increment first (replicating lost dentin anatomy, including mamelons), NEVER reaching the cavosurface margin; translucent enamel-shade increment placed over it to cover the margin; opaque incisal "halo" filament matched to cervical shade for natural incisal opacity [SRC, condensed Q13].

### 2. Sculpting — contour control via matrix choice

## EXAM MODE

**(Estimated writing time: ~40 min)**

### Answer
A Class IV composite restoration — replacing a fractured or missing incisal angle — is built, sculpted, finished and polished as one continuous, carefully sequenced process rather than as independent steps, since each stage directly constrains what the next stage can achieve. Build-up uses a layering, or stratification, technique that reproduces the natural tooth's own polychromatic structure: an opaque, dentin-shade increment is placed first to replicate the lost dentin's anatomy, including its natural lobed mamelon projections visible through translucent enamel in younger teeth, with the specific rule that this dentin-shade increment must never be allowed to reach the cavosurface margin. A more translucent enamel-shade increment is then placed over this to replace the lost enamel and to cover that margin, and a thin opaque "halo" filament, matched to the tooth's own cervical shade, is added along the incisal edge to reproduce the natural incisal-opacity band. Sculpting — the control of proximal and incisal contour and anatomic form during build-up — depends heavily on matrix selection. A standard polyester strip matrix serves typical defects, but its flexibility makes contour control genuinely difficult; creasing or folding the matrix specifically at the lingual line angle corrects the single most common resulting error, undercontouring at that point. For larger or more complex restorations, particularly where an existing restoration's lingual contour is worth duplicating, a custom lingual matrix is used instead: a silicone index of the existing lingual and incisal contour is taken before any tooth structure is removed, and after bonding, the first composite increment is placed against this repositioned index — a single increment that generally establishes the lingual, proximal and incisal contours of the entire final restoration together, substantially reducing the manual sculpting that would otherwise be needed afterward.

Finishing and polishing rest on quantifiable materials science rather than instrument choice alone. Surface hardness and abrasion resistance scale directly with a composite's filler content — Vickers hardness runs from roughly 18 for unfilled resin through the 30s-40s for microfilled composite to nearly 100 for a heavily filled hybrid — so the finishing-abrasive sequence must be matched to the specific composite's filler architecture rather than applied uniformly. Gloss itself is highly sensitive to small roughness changes: an increase in surface roughness from only 0.1 to 0.5 micrometres can reduce measured gloss from 90 units to just 2, meaning finishing technique has a disproportionately large effect on final appearance relative to how large the physical roughness change actually is. A direct, quantified comparison of polishing methods on hybrid composite illustrates this precisely: the untouched, matrix-cured baseline measured 0.08µm roughness and 89 gloss units; graduated medium-fine-superfine abrasive discs came closest to this baseline, at 0.12µm and 66 gloss units; fine polishing paste gave 0.27µm and 23 gloss units; and — the single most clinically important, genuinely counter-intuitive finding — an abrasive-impregnated rubber point, routinely regarded as the gentlest available instrument, produced the WORST result of every method tested, at 0.32µm and only 9.3 gloss units. The clinical implication is that perceived gentleness is not a reliable predictor of actual surface outcome, and a graduated abrasive-disc sequence should generally be preferred over rubber points where fine control of final gloss specifically matters. A further, genuinely counter-intuitive, evidence-based timing point deserves explicit mention: delaying finishing and polishing to a later appointment, rather than completing it immediately as is common practice, has been shown to improve the restoration's eventual wear resistance, because composite continues maturing toward its maximum degree of conversion — "dark-curing" — for up to 24 hours after placement, and polishing an immature surface too early subjects it to rotary-instrument stress it is not yet mechanically ready to withstand. Build-up, sculpting, finishing and polishing of a Class IV composite restoration are therefore best understood as a single sequence in which correct layering and matrix choice minimise the finishing burden that follows, and correct finishing technique and timing then preserve, rather than undo, the contour and gloss that careful build-up and sculpting already achieved.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Layering: dentin-shade (with mamelons) NEVER reaches cavosurface margin; enamel-shade layer covers it; opaque halo at incisal edge.
- Sculpting: standard matrix + lingual-line-angle crease, OR custom lingual matrix (silicone index BEFORE removal) — first increment sets lingual+proximal+incisal contour together.
- Finishing: Vickers hardness scales with filler content (18→~100) — match abrasive sequence to composite type.
- Polishing: graduated discs (0.12µm/66GU) ≈ baseline; rubber points (0.32µm/9.3GU) = WORST of all methods tested — counter-intuitive, examinable.
- Delayed finishing (later appointment) IMPROVES eventual wear resistance — dark-cure continues up to 24h.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Describing build-up/sculpting without the matrix-technique distinction (standard+crease vs. custom lingual) that actually controls contour.
2. Assuming rubber-point polishing is superior/gentler — the quantified data show the opposite.
3. Finishing immediately without mentioning the evidence-based delayed-finishing wear-resistance benefit.
4. Allowing dentin-shade layer description to reach the cavosurface margin — explicitly wrong.

## MNEMONIC
**"BSFP"**: **B**uild-up (dentin→enamel→halo) → **S**culpt (matrix choice) → **F**inish (filler-matched abrasives) → **P**olish (discs > paste > rubber point; consider delay).
