## 1. Definition, mechanism and test

### Explanation

Phillips defines flow as the relative ability of wax to deform plastically when heated slightly above body temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha call it the change in shape under an applied force (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. It results from slippage of the wax molecules over each other; above the melting point it equals viscosity, below it it measures plastic deformation (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Because inlay wax has a mixed crystalline-amorphous structure with little long-range order, it lacks rigidity and can flow under pressure even at room temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Test: a cylindrical specimen is loaded at a set temperature and the percentage reduction in length is recorded (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; McCabe gives the specimen as 10 mm diameter by 6 mm, loaded with 2 kg for 10 minutes, with temperature held to ±0.1 °C (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; Craig gives the same load as 19.6 N for 10 minutes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Flow is the plastic deformation (change in shape) of wax under a small applied force at a given temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013; Powers and Wataha, 2013). It is due to slippage of wax molecules over one another (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Test (ADA Sp. No. 4 / ISO 1561).** Cylinder 10 mm × 6 mm, load 2 kg (19.6 N) for 10 minutes at the test temperature; flow = % reduction in length (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Craig and Powers, 2002).

## 2. Why flow matters

### Explanation

At 45 °C, the insertion temperature, the wax must flow enough to enter all parts of the preparation and reproduce its detail (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Too little flow from underheating gives poor cavity detail and locks stress into the pattern; too much from overheating leaves the wax without "body", so it cannot be compressed (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. At mouth temperature a direct-pattern wax must have essentially no flow, so the pattern is not distorted on withdrawal (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; low flow at 37 °C allows carving and removal without distortion (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Flow of a pattern wax at room or mouth temperature produces permanent distortion (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. McCabe adds that inlay wax should be brittle, so it fractures rather than distorts when drawn from an undercut (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Softer, higher-flow waxes give larger castings because they expand more and resist the expanding investment less (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Significance**
- High flow just above mouth temperature (45 °C): wax fills the preparation and records margins and fine detail.
- Minimal flow at 37 °C: pattern can be carved and withdrawn without distortion (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Underheated wax: poor detail and internal stress; overheated wax: no "body", cannot be compressed (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Flow after carving = permanent distortion, so an ill-fitting casting (Powers and Wataha, 2013).

## 3. Specification requirements

### Explanation

Both books give the same numbers but with **opposite type numerals**. Phillips: Type I is the low-melting wax used in the mouth for direct patterns; Type II is the softer laboratory wax for indirect patterns (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Type I maximum flow at 37 °C is 1%, and both types must flow 70-90% at 45 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Revised ANSI/ADA Specification No. 4 (ISO 1561), as given by Craig: Type 1 is soft and used indirectly; Type 2 is hard and used for direct patterns (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. McCabe uses the Craig numbering and notes that "in the past the opposite classification was used" (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Craig gives the soft wax about 9% flow at 37 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Bhat et al agree on 1% at 37 °C and 70-90% at 45 °C (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC], but their list of desirable properties says "more than 1% at 37 °C" (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Their own next page sets 1% as the maximum, so this is plainly a slip for "not more than".

| Temperature | Direct (hard) wax: Phillips Type I / ISO Type 2 | Indirect (soft) wax: Phillips Type II / ISO Type 1 |
|---|---|---|
| 30 °C | – | max 1% |
| 37 °C | max 1% | – |
| 40 °C | max 20% | min 50% |
| 45 °C | 70-90% | 70-90% |

Sources: Table 10-1 (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Table 4.1 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]; Table 14-5 (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], whose 45 °C maximum is lost in the file.

#### ✎ Exam core

**ADA Specification No. 4 (ISO 1561) flow requirements**

| Temp. | Direct (hard) wax | Indirect (soft) wax |
|---|---|---|
| 30 °C | – | max 1% |
| 37 °C | max 1% | – |
| 40 °C | max 20% | min 50% |
| 45 °C | 70-90% | 70-90% |

**Note on numbering:** Phillips calls the direct wax Type I and the indirect wax Type II (Anusavice et al, 2013). The revised specification, followed by Craig and McCabe, reverses this: Type 1 soft (indirect), Type 2 hard (direct) (Craig and Powers, 2002; McCabe and Walls, 2008). Values are identical.

## 4. Factors affecting flow and the flow-temperature curve

### Explanation

Flow depends on wax temperature, the deforming force and the time it acts, and rises sharply as the melting point is approached (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. For a hard inlay wax at 40 °C flow is linear with time and only 2% after 10 minutes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Each wax shows a sharp transition temperature at which it loses plasticity, lower for soft wax than hard wax (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. On cooling, Phillips' Type I wax begins to harden slowly below 65 °C and is solid below about 48 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Bhat et al give about 50 °C and 40 °C (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Composition: a typical inlay wax is 60% paraffin, 25% carnauba, 10% ceresin, 5% beeswax; flow is reduced by more carnauba or a higher-melting paraffin, and even 1% resin changes it (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. 10% carnauba raises the melting range of paraffin (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Factors affecting flow**
1. **Temperature:** flow rises steeply near the melting range (Craig and Powers, 2002).
2. **Force applied:** greater load, more flow.
3. **Duration of load:** flow increases with time.
4. **Composition:** paraffin raises flow; carnauba reduces it (Craig and Powers, 2002).
5. **Type:** soft wax loses plasticity at a lower temperature than hard wax (Anusavice et al, 2013).

**Flow-temperature curve.** Flow stays near zero up to mouth temperature, then rises steeply over a few degrees to 70-90% at 45 °C; the curve for soft wax lies to the left of hard wax.

**Clinical implications**
- Heat evenly with dry heat to the working temperature, then apply sustained pressure while cooling.
- Use cold instruments and avoid warming the pattern; invest promptly.
- Choose hard (direct) wax for intraoral patterns.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- 37 °C: max 1% (direct wax); 45 °C: 70-90% (both types).
- Test: 2 kg (19.6 N), 10 min, % shortening of a cylinder.
- Type numerals differ between Phillips and the revised ISO/ADA specification; state which you use.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Quoting Type I/Type II without saying which numbering is used.
- Writing "more than 1% at 37 °C" (it is a maximum).
- Forgetting load and time when defining flow.

## MNEMONIC
**"One at body, ninety at forty-five"**: max 1% at 37 °C, up to 90% at 45 °C.
