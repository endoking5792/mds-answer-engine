## 1. Definitions and the waxes used

### Explanation

A wax pattern is "the wax form that is positive likeness of an object to be fabricated" (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]. Phillips' defines the indirect wax technique as a procedure in which the pattern is prepared on a die (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Garg and Garg say simply that it is prepared outside the oral cavity (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC], and Chandhak that it is fabricated on the cast (Chandhak, 2020) [SRC]. The direct pattern is made in the mouth on the prepared tooth.

**Wax types.** Phillips' gives the standard pairing: type I is a medium wax employed in direct techniques (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC] and type II a soft wax used in indirect techniques (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Chandhak (2020), Bhat and Nandish (2019) and Garg and Garg (2011) say the same [SRC]. Marzouk uses a different scheme: casting waxes classified into three types, A to C, by flow at 37°C, type A lowest (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. Both are written below; the ADA type I/II naming is the one examiners expect [Likely]. The flow requirements themselves belong to Q1/Q2 of this topic.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** In the lost-wax technique every cast inlay, onlay or crown begins as a wax replica of the future restoration. A wax pattern is the wax form that is the positive likeness of the object to be fabricated (Sikri). The pattern is perfected in occlusion, contact, contour, anatomy and margins, sprued, invested, burnt out and replaced by alloy (Marzouk et al).

**Definitions.**
- **Direct wax pattern:** made in the mouth, directly on the prepared tooth.
- **Indirect wax pattern:** made on a die, outside the oral cavity (Anusavice et al, 2013; Garg and Garg, 2011).

**Waxes (ADA Specification No. 4):**
- Type I, medium wax, for the direct technique: must soften at a temperature safe for the pulp and harden above mouth temperature.
- Type II, soft wax, for the indirect technique (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019; Chandhak, 2020).
- Marzouk et al classify casting waxes instead as types A, B and C by flow at 37°C, type A having the lowest flow.

Because the indirect pattern is formed at room temperature on a die, a softer wax with more flow can be used.

## 2. Direct versus indirect pattern (predicted component)

### Explanation

Craig's says the direct method can only be used for small inlay restorations (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and the indirect method for all types of restorations (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Garg and Garg allow the direct method for inlays and onlays but not crowns (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

**On fit, the books disagree.** Garg and Garg state that a direct pattern produces better fitting than the indirect one (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Phillips' argues the opposite: a direct pattern will shrink appreciably as it cools to room temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], the direct technique is rarely used because of wax's sensitivity to pressure and temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], and thermal expansion matters less in the indirect technique because the pattern does not pass from mouth to room temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's adds that the die makes wax flow less critical (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Phillips' gives the mechanism; Garg and Garg give the claim without one. Write both.

Indications for the indirect method (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]: large preparations such as onlays, full crowns and MODs; poor access and visibility; minute details such as skirts and collars [SRC]. Advantages: less chairside time, and finishing and polishing can be done on the die [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Feature | Direct pattern | Indirect pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Where made | In the mouth, on the tooth | On a die, outside the mouth |
| Wax | Type I (medium) | Type II (soft) |
| Restorations | Small inlays only (Craig and Powers); inlays and onlays (Garg and Garg) | All: inlays, onlays, crowns, bridges |
| Impression and die | Not needed | Needed; adds steps and errors |
| Thermal change | Cools from mouth (37°C) to room temperature, so shrinks | Made and kept at room temperature |
| Access, visibility | Limited; saliva, tongue, patient comfort | Unlimited; all surfaces visible |
| Proximal contact and contour | Hard to build and check | Built and checked against adjacent dies on an articulated cast |
| Margins | Finished on the tooth | Finished on the die under magnification |
| Lubricant | Saliva/dentinal fluid; thin separating medium | Die lubricant needed |
| Chair time | More | Less |
| Laboratory time | Less | More |
| Skill | High dexterity in the mouth | Technician skill; less operator stress |

**Disagreement on fit.** Garg and Garg state that the direct pattern fits better than the indirect. Phillips' argues the opposite: the direct pattern shrinks appreciably as it cools from mouth to room temperature, which is why the direct technique is rarely used, and thermal expansion matters less in the indirect technique (Anusavice et al, 2013). Craig and Powers add that the die makes wax flow less critical.

**Indications for indirect patterns (Garg and Garg, 2011):** onlays, full crowns and MOD restorations; insufficient access and visibility; preparations with skirts and collars; multiple restorations in one quadrant.

## 3. Prerequisites: impression, working cast and dies

### Explanation

Marzouk calls wax pattern fabrication still the most empirical of all the steps in cast fabrication (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC], so the die must be right first.

**Definitions and systems.** Sturdevant's defines dies as replicas of the prepared teeth (and adjacent teeth) that are individually removable (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Its Pindex system positions dowel pins automatically parallel (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and dies should be about 15 mm high occlusogingivally (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Marzouk compares two approaches. Two pours (a separate die plus a solid working cast): the pattern is transferred repeatedly, a manipulative procedure that can induce stresses in the wax (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. One pour (removable die in the working cast): less distortion to the wax pattern since it is not moved from die to die (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; a dowel pin may be incorporated in the die material mix (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC].

**Die materials.** Type IV (high strength) and type V (high strength, high expansion) stones (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; die stones expand 0.05-0.10% on setting (Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Electroformed copper or silver dies (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; epoxy resin, whose abrasion resistance, strength and detail reproduction exceed gypsum (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; divestment, a highly accurate die-and-investment technique (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Sequence before waxing (Ritter et al, 2019):** interocclusal record → provisional → final impression (retraction, elastomer) → working cast and dies → wax pattern → spruing, investing, casting.

**Impression.** Elastomeric (usually addition silicone) or reversible hydrocolloid, recording the finish line completely; disinfected and poured as recommended by the manufacturer.

**Working cast and die.** Dies are replicas of the prepared teeth that are individually removable (Ritter et al, 2019).
- *Two-pour, separate die (Marzouk et al):* die from the first pour, an articulated solid cast from the second. The prepared tooth is fully immobile while contacts and occlusion are waxed, but moving the pattern between die and cast several times induces stresses in the wax.
- *One-pour, removable die:* dowel pin set in the die, die removable from a stone base. Less distortion because the pattern is not transferred; some mobility and extra materials (Marzouk et al).
- *Pindex system:* pins drilled and set automatically parallel; dies about 15 mm high (Ritter et al, 2019).
- The cast is mounted on an articulator to give occlusal form.

**Die materials (brief).**
- Type IV and type V dental stone (Garg and Garg); setting expansion of die stone 0.05-0.10% (Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011).
- Electroformed (copper/silver-plated) dies: hard, abrasion resistant.
- Epoxy resin dies: better abrasion resistance, strength and detail than gypsum (Garg and Garg).
- Divestment: die and investment of the same material, so the pattern is invested on its die.

## 4. Preparing the die: trimming, hardening, spacer, lubricant

### Explanation

**Trimming and ditching.** Marzouk wants the margin readable: the simplest way is to leave a flat or convex surface peripheral to it (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC], followed by a groove or troughing in the die (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; he also tapers the die to a cone shape with a flat base (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC].

**Hardening.** Powers and Wataha describe gypsum hardening solutions (colloidal silica) mixed in place of water; they raise hardness only 2% with silicone impressions (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC] but much more with polyether. Bhat and Nandish note that die abrasion resistance is increased by surface hardeners or electroforming (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Painting a cyanoacrylate or resin hardener on the die surface is [RECALL].

**Die spacer: purpose.** Sikri: an agent applied to a die to provide space for the luting agent (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]. Marzouk: the main objective is to create room for the cement (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC], so hydraulic pressure during seating is minimized (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; he also reports an estimated 25% increase in retention (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC].

**Spacer thickness and extent: four books, four figures.**
- Marzouk: insoluble paints, airplane paints are very adequate (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; film of at least 25 microns (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC], which may need 4-5 applications (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC], each a different colour (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; on the axial walls and floor, short of the circumferential tie (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC].
- Craig's: 10-30 µm, hard to control (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and not on the margins (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].
- Phillips': resins are the most common spacers (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], applied in several coats to within 0.5 mm of the finish line (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].
- Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts: two to three coats (Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC] give a thickness printed as "25–30 mm", painted just short of the preparation margin (Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. "mm" is clearly a misprint for µm (25-30 mm would be thicker than the tooth) [Certain].

**Lubricant.** Marzouk: a thin film-forming oil (mineral or vegetable) (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. Garg and Garg: the lubricant should produce a very thin separator film (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Phillips': preferably a lubricant with a wetting agent (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Excess separator leads to inaccuracies in the pattern (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], so Marzouk keeps the separating-medium film to the smallest dimension (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Die trimming and ditching.** The margin must be readable. Leave a flat or convex surface just beyond the finish line, then cut a groove (ditch, trough) apical to it with a round or inverted cone bur; taper the die root to a cone with a flat base (Marzouk et al). Excess die stone gingival to the margin is removed with a large bur and a scalpel (Ritter et al). The margin is then outlined with a sharp red pencil [RECALL].

**B. Die hardening.** Gypsum hardening solutions (colloidal silica in water) are used instead of water; they increase hardness only about 2% with silicone impressions but much more with polyether (Powers and Wataha, 2013). Surface hardeners or electroforming increase abrasion resistance (Bhat et al, 2019). A thin cyanoacrylate or resin hardener painted on the stone protects the margin from the carvers [RECALL].

**C. Die spacer.** An agent applied to the die to provide space for the luting agent (Sikri).
*Purpose (Marzouk et al):* room for cement; less hydraulic pressure during seating; room for the roughness the investment adds to the casting; complete seating; about 25% more retention.
*Material:* insoluble model or airplane paints (Marzouk et al); resins are the commonest (Anusavice et al).
*Thickness and extent, as each book gives it:*

| Source | Thickness / coats | Where it stops |
|---|---|---|
| Marzouk et al | at least 25 µm; 4-5 coats, each a different colour | short of the circumferential tie (margins and bevels) |
| Craig and Powers, 2002 | 10-30 µm, hard to control | never on margins |
| Anusavice et al, 2013 | several coats | within 0.5 mm of the finish line |
| Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011 | 2-3 coats, printed "25-30 mm" (misprint for µm) | just short of the margin |

Each layer must dry before the next; remove any paint near the margin with solvent (Marzouk et al). Craig and Powers warn that a spacer must not be used to compensate for errors elsewhere in the casting process.

**D. Die lubricant (separating medium).** Applied after the spacer dries: a thin film-forming oil (Marzouk et al), preferably with a wetting agent (Anusavice et al). It must form a very thin film (Garg and Garg); excess causes inaccuracy and a poor cast surface (Craig and Powers) and stops wax wetting the die. Excess is blown away with air.

## 5. Instruments and equipment

### Explanation

Sturdevant's uses a No. 7 wax spatula, adding liquid wax by the "flow and press" method (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Craig's warms metal dies under an electric lamp or on an electric heating pad (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], since wax adapts better to a die warmed throughout to near body temperature (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Phillips' mentions dipping waxes (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], kept molten as a station for mass production of patterns (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], and prefers dry heat to a water bath (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk anneals wax in a 55°C oven (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. The PKT (P.K. Thomas) waxing instruments, Ward's/Hollenback-type carvers used on wax, and electric waxing units with thermostatically controlled tips are not described for waxing in the books read and are written as [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Heat source:** Bunsen or alcohol lamp; dry heat preferred to a water bath, which can leave water droplets in the wax (Anusavice et al, 2013). Wax can be annealed in a 55°C oven (Marzouk et al).
- **Adding instruments:** No. 7 wax spatula ("flow and press" method) (Ritter et al, 2019); PKT instruments No. 1 and 2 (adding), No. 3 (burnisher), No. 4 and 5 (carvers) [RECALL].
- **Carvers:** Hollenback, Ward's, discoid-cleoid, Le Cron [RECALL]; warm and sharp (section 8).
- **Electric waxing units:** thermostatically controlled tips that keep the wax at a constant working temperature, reducing overheating and residual stress [RECALL]. Craig and Powers warm metal dies under an electric lamp or on an electric heating pad.
- **Dipping pot:** wax kept molten for dipping copings and for mass production of patterns (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Others:** articulator, die spacer and lubricant, silk cloth or nylon stocking for polishing, sticky wax, sprue formers, magnifying loupe.

## 6. Step-by-step fabrication of the indirect pattern

### Explanation

Marzouk lists four basic methods for producing wax replicas (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]: carving a mass, incremental build-up with wax cones, triangles and drops (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC], direct intraoral patterns, and an anatomic core.

**Carving method (Marzouk).** After lubricating the die, all details are overfilled with wax (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; limits are marked, axial contours and occlusal anatomy carved and checked, proximal contour carved, components blended, margins finished and the pattern polished using silk cloth and brushes (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. Phillips' gives the same sequence: overfill, then carve to contour (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Garg and Garg: adapt the wax by flowing or by compression (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

**Sturdevant's sequence.** Step 1: lubricate the die and add liquid wax by "flow and press" to form proximal, facial and lingual surfaces (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Step 2: form proximal contour and contact, which Sturdevant's calls the advantage of indirect over direct restorations (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Step 3: complete the occlusal wax-up with a warmed No. 7 spatula (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Its figure shows a thin layer of wax poured first, then finger pressure while it cools.

**Annealing first.** Marzouk starts with stress-free or annealed wax (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Marzouk et al describe four methods: (1) carving an overfilled mass of wax; (2) incremental (additive) build-up with wax cones, triangles and drops; (3) direct intraoral pattern; (4) anatomic core. Methods 1, 2 and 4 are indirect.

**General step-by-step technique (combining Marzouk et al; Ritter et al, 2019; Anusavice et al, 2013; Garg and Garg, 2011):**

1. **Prepare the wax.** Use type II inlay wax, annealed (fully molten, or kept at 55°C for 15 minutes) so it starts stress-free (Marzouk et al).
2. **Prepare the die.** Trim, ditch and mark the margin; spacer; thin film of lubricant.
3. **Initial layer (internal adaptation).** Flow molten wax onto the internal surface of the preparation first. *Dipping technique* (crowns): dip the lubricated die into molten wax to form an even coping, then build on it [RECALL]. *Adding technique:* flow small increments from the spatula ("flow and press"), and hold finger pressure while the first layer cools so it adapts to the walls (Ritter et al, 2019). Garg and Garg allow flowing or the compression technique.
4. **Overfill.** All internal details and margins are overfilled with wax, with no voids (Marzouk et al; Anusavice et al).
5. **Proximal surfaces and contacts.** Seat the die in the working cast; add wax until the contact with the adjacent tooth is correct in position and size: about at the junction of the occlusal and middle thirds, slightly facial to centre, with correct embrasures. Avoid contacts that are too broad, too gingival, too occlusal or open (Ritter et al, 2019).
6. **Axial contours.** Facial and lingual heights of contour reproduced from the adjacent teeth; not overcontoured.
7. **Occlusal form.** Built by carving or by the additive technique (section 7), checked in centric and excursions on the articulator.
8. **Carve and blend** all components with warm, sharp instruments.
9. **Finish the margins** (section 8).
10. **Polish** with silk cloth, rubbing toward the margins (Anusavice et al).
11. **Inspect** under magnification for voids, thin areas and open margins; add wax where deficient.
12. **Sprue on the die** (section 10), remove and invest promptly (section 11).

## 7. Occlusal waxing: additive (cone) technique and anatomic core

### Explanation

Sikri defines the wax addition technique as developing a pattern through organized sequential addition of wax (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]; Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts build crown patterns with the wax additive technique (Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Marzouk's incremental sequence: cusps with their tips built in their exact location and tested (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; triangular ridges added and tested (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; marginal ridges built and tried (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; axial contour; fossae and grooves completed (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; then the pattern is marginated and polished (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. The attribution of the cone sequence to P.K. Thomas and the colour-coded powder checks are [RECALL]. The anatomic core technique fabricates a pattern in minimal time (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Additive (cusp-cone) technique.** Wax is added, not carved, so each element is placed in its correct position and checked against the opponent (Sikri; Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011). Marzouk et al give the order:
1. Cusp tips built as cones in their exact positions and checked in static and dynamic occlusion.
2. Triangular ridges of each cusp added and checked.
3. Marginal ridges built and checked.
4. Facial, lingual and proximal contours added and checked.
5. Fossae and grooves completed.
6. Pattern marginated and polished.

The cone method is commonly attributed to P.K. Thomas; occlusal contacts are checked with zinc stearate powder [RECALL]. Advantages: correct cusp position, contacts on cusp tips and marginal ridges, and less carving (so less stress).

**Carving technique:** an overfilled occlusal mass is carved to anatomy with warm, sharp instruments; quicker for small inlays but more likely to leave stresses and interferences.

**Anatomic core technique (Marzouk et al):** before preparation, the tooth is built in hard wax to the desired form and adjusted through all movements; a plaster core of it is made in a functionally generated path tray. After preparation, each die is filled with molten wax and the lubricated core pressed on. Little adjustment is needed and the pattern is made in minimal time. Indicated when occlusion is not to be changed, for small to moderate restorations, with anterior and posterior stops, and when the teeth do not guide lateral or protrusive movements.

## 8. Margins, carving, finishing and removal

### Explanation

Marzouk: always use warm to hot instruments for carving so that induced stresses are consumed (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; on hardened wax use sharp instruments, if carving is required (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha: patterns are carved with warm (37°C) instruments (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's: a cool instrument burnishing or cutting wax introduces tensile and compressive stresses (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Sturdevant's warms the spatula so marginal wax can be carved with light pressure without damaging the stone margins (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips': some clinicians apply finger pressure as the wax cools (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Marzouk passes a hot blunt explorer deeply into each increment (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC] and warns that air bubbles can be trapped during incremental build-up (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. Craig's: before spruing, the die is cleaned and relubricated, the pattern replaced and the margins finalized (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Removal.** Marzouk: first by the sprue former; second with two fingers (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; third with a staple (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. Phillips' uses a staple-like wire for MOD patterns (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Carving:** use warm instruments on soft wax and sharp instruments on hardened wax (Marzouk et al). Powers and Wataha give 37°C for the carver. A cool instrument burnishing or cutting the wax builds in tensile and compressive stresses (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Margin readaptation (most important step for fit):**
- Remelt the marginal 1-2 mm of wax with a warm instrument and let it flow onto the margin, holding light finger pressure as it cools [RECALL; finger pressure per Anusavice et al].
- Carve the margin with the instrument resting on the die surface just apical to the finish line, moving from wax toward die, so the stone is not abraded (Ritter et al, 2019; Anusavice et al).
- Bevels and flares must be fully covered; the margin must be neither short nor over-extended.
- Pass a hot blunt explorer into each increment to release stresses and close voids; inspect for trapped air (Marzouk et al).
- Before spruing, remove the pattern, clean and relubricate the die, reseat and finalize the margins (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Polishing:** silk cloth or nylon, rubbing toward the margins (Anusavice et al).

**Removal from the die (Marzouk et al):**
1. By the sprue former, along the path of withdrawal.
2. By thumb and finger on both sides (onlays, crowns).
3. By a staple of wire fixed with sticky wax to both ends of an MOD pattern, pulled evenly (also Anusavice et al).

Avoid handling with the fingers after removal, since warmth distorts it.

## 9. Inlay, onlay and crown patterns

### Explanation

Garg and Garg list onlays, full crowns, MOD preparations and preparations with skirts and collars as indications for the indirect method (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Craig's describes the Y-sprue for MOD inlay patterns (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Marzouk's two-finger removal is for patterns with two or more sides (onlays, crowns). The specific points for each pattern type below (coping by dipping, cusp coverage thickness, collar) are from recall and are flagged.

#### ✎ Exam core

| | Inlay (especially MOD) | Onlay | Full crown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main risk | Distortion of the two proximal boxes ("springing") as stresses release | Thin wax over reduced cusps; bevel/shoulder adaptation | Internal adaptation of the whole die; collar |

## 10. Sprue former: attachment, diameter, location, angle, reservoir

### Explanation

**Attach on the die.** Marzouk: attach while the pattern is on the die, to confine it (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; Phillips' agrees, provided removal is in line with the path of withdrawal (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Joining: Marzouk applies sticky wax to minimize stress release while joining a sprue former to a pattern (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; hollow metal sprues are filled with sticky wax (Marzouk et al, n.d.; Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Diameter: five wordings.** Greater than the thickest part (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; equal to or greater than the thickest part (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; approximately the same as the thickest area (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; identical with the broadest part (Chandhak, 2020) [SRC]; theoretically larger, but practically it could be slightly smaller (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Gauges: 8-18 gauge, 3 to 0.8 mm (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; 6-12 gauge, 4.1 to 2.0 mm (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Too narrow gives "suck-back" porosity (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Location.** Bulkiest part (Marzouk et al, n.d.; Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Marzouk calls the proximal surfaces ideal (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; Phillips' notes some prefer the occlusal surface (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Angle: a real disagreement.** Marzouk: directed away from thin sections or at 45° to them (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; Garg and Garg: 45° to the bulkiest portion (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; Chandhak: 45° to the proximal part (Chandhak, 2020) [SRC]; Phillips' figure: sprue at about 45° to the proximal wall (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's instead says the sprue should be directed toward the margins (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Both aim at non-turbulent flow; Craig's direction is from bulk toward thin margins, not a right-angle impact on them [Likely].

**Flare.** Flared for high-density gold, constricted for base metal (Garg and Garg, 2011; Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Length and position in ring.** End of pattern 1/8 to 1/4 inch from the open end of the ring (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; within 6 mm (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; about 6 mm (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Chandhak gives both 6-8 mm (Chandhak, 2020) [SRC] and 0.25 inch (Chandhak, 2020) [SRC].

**Reservoir.** 1-2 mm from the pattern (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; 1 mm (Chandhak, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Purpose:** a channel for wax elimination and alloy entry, and a reservoir that solidifies last to feed shrinkage.

**Types:** wax (burns out, low heat transfer), resin/plastic (rigid, burns out; needs a two-stage burnout, Anusavice et al) and metal (rigid, removed before burnout; hollow ones filled with sticky wax) (Marzouk et al; Craig and Powers).

**Principles:**
1. **Attach on the die**, so the pattern is supported while heat is applied; join with a drop of sticky wax, then do not disturb the joint (Marzouk et al; Anusavice et al).
2. **Diameter:** books differ in wording: greater than the thickest part (Marzouk et al); equal to or greater (Garg and Garg); about the same (Anusavice et al); identical (Chandhak); theoretically larger, practically slightly smaller (Craig and Powers). Range 8-18 gauge (3-0.8 mm) (Marzouk et al) or 6-12 gauge (4.1-2.0 mm) (Craig and Powers). Too narrow gives suck-back porosity.
3. **Location:** bulkiest part of the pattern, away from margins and anatomy; proximal surface ideal (Marzouk et al); some prefer occlusal or a non-functional cusp (Anusavice et al).
4. **Angle:** 45° to the proximal wall, never at 90° to a flat wall or thin section (Marzouk et al; Garg and Garg; Chandhak; Anusavice et al). Craig and Powers instead say "directed toward the margins" so that flow favours them.
5. **Joint:** smooth, flared for high-density gold, constricted for base metal (Garg and Garg; Anusavice et al).
6. **Length:** pattern end 1/8-1/4 inch from the ring end (Garg and Garg), about 6 mm (Anusavice et al; Craig and Powers), 6-8 mm (Chandhak), so gases escape and investment resists impact.
7. **Reservoir:** a ball of wax 1-2 mm from the pattern (Marzouk et al) or 1 mm (Chandhak), larger than the thickest part; essential with long or thin sprues.
8. **Number:** one for most inlays and crowns; Y-sprue for MOD inlays (Craig and Powers); multiple sprues with a runner bar for bridges.

## 11. Surface treatment and prompt investing

### Explanation

Casting waxes are hydrophobic and difficult to wet (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; surfactants are applied. Garg and Garg wash the pattern with soap and water (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]; Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts paint investment on with a brush to ensure no air bubbles attach (Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

**How soon to invest: the books differ.** Marzouk: the only reliable hedge is immediate investment (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]; refrigeration is a less reliable measure (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC] if investment is not possible within 5 minutes (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. Chandhak: as soon as possible after removal from the die (Chandhak, 2020) [SRC], and not more than 20-30 minutes (Chandhak, 2020) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha: less than 30 minutes (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], refinishing contacts and margins if storage is suspect (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Garg and Garg: immediately (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC]. Phillips': a casting fits most accurately when the pattern is invested immediately (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], yet the same chapter says the pattern should be retained on the die for several hours to avoid distortion (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. These are reconcilable only if "several hours" means on the die, not off it [Guessing]; write "invest immediately after removal from the die". Bhat and Nandish: invest immediately or store at low temperature (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Surface treatment.** Wax is hydrophobic (Marzouk et al). Clean the pattern of lubricant and debris with soap and water or a surfactant (wetting agent), without pooling in internal line angles (Garg and Garg). Paint investment onto the pattern with a brush, then fill the ring under vibration or vacuum so no air bubbles attach (Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011).

**Timing of investment:**
- Immediately: "the only reliable hedge" (Marzouk et al); also Garg and Garg; Anusavice et al.
- If not possible within 5 minutes, refrigerate, a less reliable measure (Marzouk et al); or store at low temperature (Bhat and Nandish).
- Not more than 20-30 minutes (Chandhak); under 30 minutes (Powers and Wataha), and refinish contacts and margins if storage was long.
- Phillips' contains both "invest immediately" and "retain on the die for several hours"; keep the pattern on the die if delayed, but invest as soon as it is removed.

Once the investment sets, the pattern can no longer distort (Anusavice et al).

## 12. Wax properties that govern each step, and distortion

### Explanation

**Thermal expansion.** Marzouk: the highest of all dental materials, up to 400 ppm/°C (Marzouk et al, n.d.) [SRC]. Phillips': average 350 × 10⁻⁶/K (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Bhat and Nandish: 150-400 ppm/°C (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Garg and Garg: high (Garg and Garg, 2011) [SRC].

**Elastic memory and stress relief.** Elastic memory is the tendency of solid wax to partially return to its original shape (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Bhat and Nandish call it wax memory (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. A completely stress-free pattern would appear improbable (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Causes of distortion.** Phillips': occluded air, physical deformation, release of trapped stress, excessive storage time, extreme temperature changes (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].

**Flow.** Marzouk links higher flow with better adaptation but more distortion. The flow table belongs to Q2 of this topic.

#### ✎ Exam core

| Property | Value / behaviour | Step it governs and what to do |
|---|---|---|
| Coefficient of thermal expansion | Highest of all dental materials: up to 400 ppm/°C (Marzouk et al); average 350 × 10⁻⁶/K (Anusavice et al); 150-400 ppm/°C (Bhat and Nandish) | Keep pattern at one temperature; indirect patterns avoid the mouth-to-room contraction |
| Low thermal conductivity | Heats and cools slowly | Heat uniformly; allow cooling time; soften by dry heat |
| Flow | Rises sharply with temperature; type II allowed more flow | Good adaptation when warm; low flow at room temperature resists distortion |
| Residual stress and elastic memory | Wax partly returns to its original shape when stresses relax, faster when warm (Anusavice et al; Bhat and Nandish) | Anneal wax, add in small increments, carve warm, avoid cold burnishing, invest promptly |
| Contact angle with die | Molten wax wets best | Flow wax at its most molten for internal surfaces; minimal lubricant |
| Burnout | Must volatilize with residue under 0.10% at 500°C (Anusavice et al) | Ensures clean mould |

**Causes of distortion (Anusavice et al, 2013):** air occluded in the pattern; deformation during moulding, carving or removal; release of stresses trapped during cooling; excessive storage; temperature change during storage. Garg and Garg add mishandling and unequal movement of pattern walls as the investment sets.

**Prevention:**
1. Start with annealed, uniformly softened wax (Marzouk et al).
2. Add molten wax in small increments; avoid adding cold wax to warm.
3. Carve with warm (37°C) or sharp instruments (Powers and Wataha; Marzouk et al).
4. Avoid touching or warming the pattern after it is finished.
5. Sprue on the die; remove evenly (staple for MOD).
6. Invest immediately; if not, refrigerate (Marzouk et al) and refinish margins (Powers and Wataha).

A completely stress-free pattern is improbable (Craig and Powers); the aim is the least and most even stress. For MOD distortion (proximal walls springing apart) see casting_alloys_defects Q17.

## 13. Pattern resins, CAD/CAM-milled and 3D-printed patterns

### Explanation

Powers and Wataha: resins offer higher strength and lower flow than pattern waxes (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]; light-cured resins offer more working time than self-cured (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]; all pattern waxes and resins must burn out with no residue (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Phillips': use a two-stage burnout whenever plastic patterns are involved (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts: CAD-CAM can computer-design and mill wax blocks to form patterns (Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

**Literature (abstracts only).** Lalande et al (2018) found that gold crowns cast from CAD-CAM-milled acrylate resin patterns had marginal discrepancies not significantly different from hand-waxed ones [LIT] (doi:10.1016/j.prosdent.2017.07.019). Kim et al (2018) found Co-Cr copings cast from stereolithography (3D-printed) resin patterns gave overall space 70.2 µm against 63.2 µm for lost wax, with no significant difference, while directly milled copings were worse at 130.3 µm [LIT] (doi:10.1016/j.prosdent.2018.01.012). Pekkan et al (2025) found 3D-printed polymer patterns gave the highest overall discrepancies of five techniques in their study [LIT] (doi:10.1007/s10266-025-01178-2). The two printed-pattern studies disagree; both are single in vitro studies [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Pattern resins.** Self-cured (e.g., Duralay-type acrylic) and light-cured resins [product name RECALL] offer higher strength and lower flow than wax; light-cured resins give more working time (Powers and Wataha, 2013). Uses: post-and-core patterns, copings, thin sections and margins that must not distort, and patterns that must be stored or transported. Drawbacks: polymerization shrinkage, harder to carve, and a two-stage burnout is needed because plastics soften above the melting point of inlay wax and must burn out completely (Anusavice et al, 2013; Powers and Wataha).

**CAD/CAM-milled patterns.** The die or tooth is scanned, the restoration designed on screen with a set cement space, and a wax or castable acrylate block is milled, then sprued and cast in the usual way (Chadwick, Radford and Ricketts, 2011). Marginal discrepancy of crowns cast from milled resin patterns was not significantly different from hand-waxed ones (Lalande et al, 2018, in vitro).

**3D-printed (additive) patterns.** Castable resin or wax patterns printed by stereolithography or DLP [RECALL] and cast by lost-wax. Copings cast from SLA-printed patterns fitted as well as conventional lost-wax copings (Kim et al, 2018), but another study found printed polymer patterns gave the largest discrepancies (Pekkan et al, 2025). Evidence is in vitro and conflicting.

**Advantages of digital patterns:** no die spacer variability, no wax stresses or elastic memory, reproducible, storable designs. **Limitations:** equipment cost, scan accuracy at subgingival margins, burnout residue of resins.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

1. **Claim: an indirect pattern fits better than a direct one.** Phillips' gives the mechanism (no mouth-to-room contraction, less critical flow); Garg and Garg state the reverse without one. Craig's limits the direct method to small inlays. Conclusion: the indirect pattern is the standard for all but small inlays; the claim of better direct fit is unsupported in the sources read [Likely].
2. **Claim: die spacer improves seating.** All four books agree on purpose; they disagree on thickness (10-30 µm, at least 25 µm, "25-30 mm" misprint) and on stopping point (short of the tie, never on margins, within 0.5 mm). Marzouk's 25% retention figure is reported without a study in the text read. Conclusion: stop short of the margin; 20-40 µm is a safe exam figure [Guessing for the range; Certain that it stays off the margin].
3. **Claim: invest immediately.** Every source agrees on minimizing time; the limits vary (5 min, 20-30 min, under 30 min), and Phillips' also asks for several hours on the die. Conclusion: invest as soon as the pattern leaves the die [Certain].
4. **Claim: digital patterns match conventional ones.** One milled-resin and one SLA study say yes; a 2025 study says printed polymer patterns were worst. In vitro only, abstracts only [Likely that milled patterns are equivalent; Guessing for printed ones].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Type I medium wax: direct; type II soft wax: indirect (ADA Sp. No. 4).
- Inlay wax CTE about 350-400 × 10⁻⁶/°C, the highest of any dental material.
- Die preparation order: trim and ditch → harden → spacer (short of margin) → lubricant (thin).
- Spacer: at least 25 µm, 4-5 coats (Marzouk et al); 10-30 µm (Craig and Powers); within 0.5 mm of finish line (Anusavice et al).
- Additive order: cusp tips → triangular ridges → marginal ridges → axial contours → fossae and grooves → margins.
- Carve warm or sharp; never burnish cold wax.
- Sprue on the die, at the bulkiest part, 45° to the proximal wall, diameter about that of the thickest part, reservoir 1-2 mm from the pattern, pattern about 6 mm from the ring end.
- Invest immediately; if not, refrigerate and refinish margins.
- Resin patterns: higher strength, lower flow; two-stage burnout.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
1. Prepared die: ditch below the margin, spacer layers stopping short of the finish line, lubricant film.
2. Additive (cone) waxing sequence on a molar: cones → ridges → completed occlusal table.
3. MOD pattern with staple for removal and Y-sprue.
4. Sprued pattern in the ring: crucible former, sprue at 45°, reservoir 1-2 mm from the pattern, 6 mm investment beyond, liner 3 mm short of each end.
5. Thermal expansion curve of inlay wax (expansion rising sharply above about 35°C).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing type I wax for the indirect technique (it is type II).
- Painting die spacer onto the margin.
- Leaving a thick lubricant film, which blunts margins.
- Carving with a cold instrument or burnishing hard wax.
- Removing the pattern before spruing and spruing it in the hand.
- Attaching the sprue to a thin margin or at 90° to a flat wall.
- Leaving the pattern overnight before investing.
- Copying "25-30 mm" from Advanced Operative Dentistry; it is µm.
- Forgetting the two-stage burnout for resin patterns.

## MNEMONIC
**"Dentists Should Lube, Then Add, Carve, Mark, Sprue, Invest"**: **D**ie (trim, ditch, harden), **S**pacer (short of margin), **L**ubricant (thin), **T**ype II wax, **A**dd (cones: cusps → ridges → fossae), **C**arve warm or sharp, **M**argins readapted, **S**prue on the die (bulk, 45°, reservoir), **I**nvest immediately.
