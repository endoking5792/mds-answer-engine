## 1. Introduction, history and definition

### Explanation

Wax has been used for over 2000 years: beeswax for coating, preserving, candles and sculpture (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. One of the oldest recorded dental uses was for impressions (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Modern dental waxes come from mineral, plant and insect sources, plus synthetic waxes from petroleum, which are more uniform than natural waxes (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Bhat describes waxes as thermoplastic organic materials of low molecular weight hydrocarbons with esters, alcohols and free acids (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC].

Phillips defines inlay wax as a specialised dental wax applied to dies to form direct or indirect patterns for the lost-wax technique, used to cast metals or hot-press ceramics (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. The lost-wax process: the pattern is invested, the wax is burnt out, and molten alloy fills the mould (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Because the casting can be no more accurate than its pattern, the wax decides the fit.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Waxes have been used for over 2000 years; beeswax has been used since ancient times, and one of the oldest dental uses was taking impressions (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002). Dental waxes are thermoplastic materials: low molecular weight hydrocarbons with esters, alcohols and free acids, from mineral, plant and insect sources, with synthetic waxes added for uniformity (Bhat et al, 2019).

**Definition.** Inlay wax is a specialised dental wax applied to dies (or prepared teeth) to form direct or indirect patterns for the lost-wax technique, used to cast metals or hot-press ceramics (Anusavice et al, 2013). It is also called inlay casting wax or inlay pattern wax.

**Lost-wax principle.** Wax pattern → sprued → invested in a gypsum- or phosphate-bonded investment → wax burnt out → molten alloy (or ceramic) fills the mould → casting reproduces the pattern (Craig and Powers, 2002). Hence a casting can be no more accurate than the wax pattern from which it was made; every error in the wax is transferred to the restoration.

**Uses.** Patterns for inlays, onlays, crowns, bridge units, cast post-cores and pressed ceramics; ivory-coloured inlay wax for diagnostic wax-ups shown to patients (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

## 2. Classification of dental waxes (inlay wax in context)

### Explanation

Phillips gives two schemes: clinic versus laboratory waxes (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], and the use-based three groups of pattern, processing and impression waxes (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's Table 14-4 has the same three groups (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and notes that all pattern waxes share two problems, thermal dimensional change and the tendency to warp or distort on standing (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Bhat lists classification by origin as "now not in use" (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson divide use into three areas: appliance construction, auxiliary aids and occlusal registration (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. According to use** (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002; Bhat et al, 2019)

| Group | Waxes | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Pattern waxes** | **Inlay (Type I, Type II)**, casting wax (sheets, ready shapes for RPD frameworks), baseplate wax (Types 1-3) | Form the size and contour of a restoration to be cast or processed |
| **Processing waxes** | Boxing, beading, utility, sticky, carding, block-out, white | Auxiliary aids in clinic and laboratory |
| **Impression waxes** | Corrective impression wax, bite registration wax | Record non-undercut tissues and jaw relations |

**B. According to origin** (now not used for classification; Bhat et al, 2019)
1. Natural: mineral (paraffin, microcrystalline, ceresin, montan), plant (carnauba, candelilla, ouricury, Japan wax), insect (beeswax), animal (spermaceti).
2. Synthetic: polyethylene, polyoxyethylene glycol, halogenated hydrocarbon and hydrogenated waxes.

**C. According to site of use** (Anusavice et al, 2013): clinical waxes (bite registration, disclosing, utility, Type I direct inlay wax) and laboratory waxes (boxing, baseplate, sticky, beading, hard, medium and soft Type II inlay waxes).

**Place of inlay wax.** Inlay wax is a pattern wax. Like all pattern waxes it has two inherent problems: large thermal change in dimension and a tendency to warp on standing (Craig and Powers, 2002). Casting wax is a separate pattern wax for partial denture frameworks; it has no ADA specification but a federal specification (U-W-140) (Craig and Powers, 2002).

## 3. Types of inlay wax: direct and indirect

### Explanation

The books disagree on numbering. Phillips: Type I is a medium wax for direct techniques and Type II a soft wax for indirect techniques (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Bhat repeats this under "ADA specification No. 4" (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Craig, citing the revised ANSI/ADA No. 4 (ISO 1561) (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], reverses it: Type 1 is a soft indirect wax (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC] and Type 2 a harder wax for direct patterns in the mouth (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls follow Craig's numbering (Type 1 for indirect) (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC] and warn that this caused confusion because the opposite classification was used in the past (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Both numberings give the same flow figures, so the difference is in the label, not the material. Phillips also says type II waxes come as hard, medium and soft, and that a harder or medium wax with low flow suits warmer climates (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Craig describes hard, regular (medium) and soft inlay waxes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. The direct technique is rarely used now because of the wax's sensitivity to pressure, temperature and heating-cooling rates (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; McCabe and Walls reserve it for simple cast post-cores on anterior teeth (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification of inlay wax.** Two types, distinguished by their flow. The numbering differs between textbooks:

| | Phillips (Anusavice et al, 2013); Bhat et al, 2019 | Craig and Powers, 2002 (revised ANSI/ADA No. 4, ISO 1561); McCabe and Walls, 2008 |
|---|---|---|
| Direct wax (in the mouth) | **Type I, medium** | **Type 2, hard** |
| Indirect wax (on a die) | **Type II, soft** | **Type 1, soft** |

McCabe and Walls (2008) note that the older numbering was the opposite of the current one, which causes confusion. In the exam, state both, then describe them as "direct (harder, low flow at 37 °C)" and "indirect (softer)" so the meaning is clear whichever number is used.

**Direct technique wax.** Must flow well a few degrees above mouth temperature, at a heat the pulp and patient tolerate, and harden at a temperature above mouth temperature, with almost no flow at 37 °C so the pattern can be carved and withdrawn without distortion (Anusavice et al, 2013). Now rarely used (Anusavice et al, 2013); kept mainly for simple post-core patterns on anterior teeth (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**Indirect technique wax.** Used on a stone die at room temperature, so it can soften at a lower temperature and carve easily, but must not flow at room temperature (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**Hardness grades.** Inlay waxes are also sold as hard, regular (medium) and soft (Craig and Powers, 2002); a soft or regular wax suits indirect work in cool weather, a harder low-flow wax suits warm climates (Anusavice et al, 2013).

## 4. Composition

### Explanation

Phillips says most dental waxes contain 40-60% paraffin (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; paraffin flakes on trimming and lacks gloss, so modifiers are added (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Gum dammar improves smoothness in moulding and resistance to cracking and flaking (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Carnauba, one of the hardest waxes (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], is combined with paraffin to decrease flow at mouth temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Candelilla can replace carnauba; its melting point is lower and it is softer (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Ceresin may replace part of the paraffin to modify toughness and carving (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Synthetic waxes based on esters of montan wax acids may replace part of the carnauba (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Some products contain a filler to control expansion and shrinkage (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Paraffin for type I waxes melts higher than that for type II (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].

The percentages differ by book. Bhat tabulates the composition of inlay wax "ADA specification No. 4" (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]: paraffin 60% (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC], carnauba 20% (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC], ceresin 5%, gum dammar 3%, beeswax 5% (reduces flow at mouth temperature and brittleness) (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC], synthetic resins 2% (stable flow) (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]; the listed figures add up to 95%. Craig gives an example formula of 60% paraffin, 25% carnauba, 10% ceresin and 5% beeswax (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and notes that flow can be reduced by adding carnauba or by a higher melting paraffin (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and that resins even at 1% affect flow (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson give paraffin about 60% (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]; Powers and Wataha say five or six waxes may be blended (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Colours: blue, green, yellow, red and ivory (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; deep blue, green or purple sticks about 7.5 cm long (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; ivory for wax-ups (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Inlay wax is a blend of five or six waxes and resins (Powers and Wataha, 2013). Hydrocarbon (paraffin-type) waxes form the major part.

**Composition of inlay wax** (Bhat et al, 2019, table "ADA specification No. 4"; function column from Bhat et al, 2019 and Anusavice et al, 2013)

| Ingredient | wt% | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Paraffin wax (mineral) | 60 | Main base; gives mouldability and sets the melting range. Alone it flakes on trimming and gives no gloss |
| Carnauba wax (plant) | 20 | Very hard, high melting; raises the melting range, decreases flow at mouth temperature, adds gloss |
| Ceresin (mineral, from ozokerite) | 5 | Replaces part of paraffin; modifies toughness and carving |
| Gum dammar (natural resin) | 3 | Smoothness in moulding, resistance to cracking and flaking, toughness, lustre |
| Beeswax (insect) | 5 | Reduces flow at mouth temperature and reduces brittleness |
| Synthetic resins | 2 | Stable flow properties |

**Other ingredients**
- **Candelilla wax:** partly or wholly replaces carnauba; similar qualities but lower melting point and softer (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Synthetic waxes:** a nitrogen derivative of higher fatty acids and esters of montan wax acids replace part of the carnauba; more uniform than natural waxes (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Microcrystalline wax** (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- **Fillers:** in some products, to control expansion and shrinkage; must burn out without residue (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Colourants:** blue, green, purple, red, yellow or ivory, to contrast with the die (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Other quoted formulas.** Craig and Powers (2002): 60% paraffin, 25% carnauba, 10% ceresin, 5% beeswax. Anusavice et al (2013): 40-60% paraffin in most dental waxes. Bonsor and Pearson (2013): paraffin about 60%. The books give different proportions; the principle is the same.

**Controlling flow by composition.** Flow falls with more carnauba or a higher melting paraffin; a hard wax may contain less carnauba than a regular wax yet flow less because of its higher melting paraffin; even 1% resin alters flow (Craig and Powers, 2002). The paraffin in direct-type wax melts higher than that in indirect-type wax (Anusavice et al, 2013).

**Supply.** Sticks about 7.5 cm long, pellets, cones or small jars (Craig and Powers, 2002).

## 5. Ideal requirements

### Explanation

Phillips lists the most important properties: uniform softening, colour contrast, no flaking, carvable to a thin layer without chipping, and complete burnout (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. It must harden above mouth temperature for direct use (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls want the wax brittle enough to fracture rather than distort from an undercut, revealing the undercut (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

(Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008)
1. Softens uniformly, with no graininess or hard spots.
2. Colour contrasts with the tooth and die, with enough opacity in thin layers to finish margins.
3. Does not flake or roughen when moulded after softening.
4. Carves to a very thin margin without chipping or pulling away with the instrument.
5. Direct wax: plastic just above mouth temperature, at a temperature safe for the pulp; rigid at mouth temperature.
6. Low flow (≤1%) at 37 °C (direct) or at 30 °C (indirect), so the pattern does not distort on removal.
7. High flow (70-90%) at 45 °C to reproduce cavity detail.
8. Dimensionally stable, rigid until invested; low thermal expansion is desirable but never achieved.
9. Burns out completely, leaving no residue that would spoil the casting.
10. Fractures rather than distorts when withdrawn from an undercut cavity, so the undercut is detected (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

Note: Bhat et al (2019) print "flow more than 1% at 37 °C" in their list; their own properties section and the specification give a maximum of 1%. Write "not more than 1%".

## 6. ADA Specification No. 4 (ISO) requirements

### Explanation

Phillips' Table 10-1 gives flow requirements (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]: Type I maximum 1% at 37 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], and both types 70-90% at 45 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's Table 14-5 gives the same values with the types renamed (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], but lists only the 70% minimum at 45 °C; the 90% maximum is from Phillips. The test: cylindrical specimens under a designated load (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], 19.6 N for 10 minutes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; McCabe and Walls give 10 mm × 6 mm cylinders under 2 kg (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Residue: Phillips, not more than 0.10% when vaporised at 500 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Craig, a maximum of 0.10% at 700 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; Bhat, complete vaporisation at 500 °C with no residue (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. The specification also requires instructions on softening and working temperature (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and no longer requires thermal expansion data (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. The current ISO standard number (ISO 15854) for inlay wax is [RECALL]; Powers and Wataha cite ISO 15854 only for baseplate wax (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Specification.** ANSI/ADA Specification No. 4 for dental inlay casting wax, revised in line with ISO 1561 (Craig and Powers, 2002); ISO 1561 was later replaced by ISO 15854, which covers casting and baseplate waxes [RECALL]. It covers waxes for direct and indirect techniques.

**Flow requirements** (% shortening of a cylinder; Anusavice et al, 2013, Table 10-1; Craig and Powers, 2002, Table 14-5)

| Temperature | Direct wax (Phillips Type I / Craig Type 2) | Indirect wax (Phillips Type II / Craig Type 1) |
|---|---|---|
| 30 °C | - | maximum 1% |
| 37 °C | **maximum 1%** | - |
| 40 °C | maximum 20% | minimum 50% |
| 45 °C | minimum 70%, maximum 90% | minimum 70%, maximum 90% |

**Meaning of each value**
- 37 °C ≤1% (direct): the pattern can be carved and removed at mouth temperature without distortion (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- 30 °C ≤1% (indirect): stable at room temperature on the die.
- 40 °C: indirect wax must be soft and carvable (≥50%); direct wax must not slump (≤20%).
- 45 °C 70-90%: the insertion temperature; enough plasticity to reproduce detail, but not so fluid that the wax lacks body (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Flow test.** Cylindrical specimens (10 mm diameter, 6 mm high) loaded across their flat faces (2 kg, i.e. 19.6 N) for 10 minutes at the test temperature held to ±0.1 °C; flow = percentage reduction in length (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Residue on burnout.** Not more than 0.10% of the original weight: at 500 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013) or at an ignition temperature of 700 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002). Excess residue causes incomplete casting of margins (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Other requirements** (Craig and Powers, 2002)
- Softens without flaking; does not chip or flake when trimmed to a fine margin.
- Manufacturer must state the method of softening and the working temperature.
- Thermal expansion data for the direct wax are no longer required.
- Colour contrast with the die (Anusavice et al, 2013).

## 7. Properties

### Explanation

**Flow and melting range.** Phillips defines flow as plastic deformation when heated slightly above body temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. On the cooling curve a type I wax begins to harden below 65 °C and is solid below about 48 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; Bhat gives hardening at about 50 °C and solidification at about 40 °C (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Craig: flow of the indirect (Type 1) wax is about 9% at 37 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Thermal properties.** Thermal conductivity is low (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Inlay wax expands up to 0.7% for a 20 °C rise (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC] and contracts up to 0.35% from 37 to 25 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; mean coefficient 350 × 10⁻⁶/K, range 217-512 (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; it expands more per degree than any other dental material (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], and the high coefficient makes it very sensitive to temperature change (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Other figures: 300-700 ppm/°C (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]; 350-450 × 10⁻⁶/°C (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) [SRC]. Contraction from mouth to room temperature: 0.4% (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; 0.3-0.4% (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]; 0.1-0.5% (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Craig: waxes have the largest coefficient of any restorative material (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], mineral waxes more than plant waxes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. The change of slope near 35 °C is called the glass transition temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls note that the die was itself made from an impression cooled from mouth temperature, so its contraction may approach that of a direct pattern (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC].

**Mechanical properties.** Compressive strength falls from 83 to 0.5 MPa between 23 and 40 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; inlay wax is regarded as brittle (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. A wall-to-occlusal modulus ratio of 7:1 (inlay wax to soft casting wax) evens expansion during investing (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Ductility.** Waxes with lower melting temperatures have greater ductility at a given temperature (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC].

**Elastic memory and residual stress.** Elastic memory is the tendency of solid wax to partly return to its original shape when stored warmer than it was cooled (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; the horseshoe demonstration: a stick bent and chilled opens on standing (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Bhat calls it wax memory (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. Residual stress exists in every completed pattern (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]: wax cooled under compression expands more on reheating; wax cooled under tension expands less or even contracts.

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Flow**
- Flow is the ability of wax to deform plastically when heated slightly above body temperature (Anusavice et al, 2013). It comes from molecules slipping past each other and depends on temperature, load and time; it rises steeply near the melting range (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Direct wax: high flow a few degrees above mouth temperature, essentially none at 37 °C (≤1%). Indirect wax flows about 9% at 37 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Too little flow (underheating) gives poor detail and stress in the pattern; too much (overheating) leaves the wax without body to compress (Craig and Powers, 2002). Details: Q2.

**B. Melting (softening) range**
- Waxes are mixtures, so they have a melting range, not a point (Bhat et al, 2019). On cooling, Type I wax begins to harden below 65 °C and is solid below about 48 °C (Anusavice et al, 2013); Bhat et al (2019) give about 50 °C and 40 °C.
- Paraffin melts at about 44-62 °C, carnauba at about 50-90 °C (Bhat et al, 2019).

**C. Thermal conductivity:** low; the wax must be heated long enough to soften throughout and cooled slowly, otherwise uneven contraction builds stress (Anusavice et al, 2013).

**D. Coefficient of thermal expansion**
- Inlay wax expands and contracts more per degree than any other dental material (Anusavice et al, 2013; Craig and Powers, 2002).

| Source | CTE of inlay wax | Contraction, mouth to room temperature |
|---|---|---|
| Anusavice et al, 2013 | mean 350 × 10⁻⁶/K (217-512); up to 0.7% expansion for a 20 °C rise | up to 0.35% (37 to 25 °C) |
| Craig and Powers, 2002 | largest of any restorative material | 0.4% (about 0.04% per °C) |
| Bhat et al, 2019 | 300-700 ppm/°C | 0.3-0.4% |
| Sakaguchi et al, 2019 | 350-450 × 10⁻⁶/°C | - |
| McCabe and Walls, 2008 | - | 0.1-0.5% (direct pattern) |

- Expansion rate rises sharply above about 35 °C (glass transition temperature) where wax components change form (Anusavice et al, 2013). Mineral waxes expand more than plant waxes (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Significance: a direct pattern shrinks when it leaves the mouth; the casting is then undersized, so investment expansion must compensate. The indirect pattern changes less, but the die was cast from an impression that cooled from mouth temperature (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**E. Mechanical properties**
- Low modulus, proportional limit and strength, strongly temperature-dependent: compressive strength falls from 83 to 0.5 MPa between 23 and 40 °C (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Brittle at room temperature (Craig and Powers, 2002), which is useful: it fractures rather than distorts in an undercut (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**F. Elastic memory and residual stress**
- Elastic memory: the tendency of a solid wax to partly return to its original shape when stored at a temperature higher than that at which it was cooled (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Demonstration: a softened stick bent into a horseshoe, chilled, then floated in room-temperature water opens out over hours (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Every pattern contains residual stress (Craig and Powers, 2002). Wax cooled under compression expands more than normal on reheating; wax cooled under tension expands less or may contract. Stress release with time and warmth is the main cause of distortion.

**G. Ductility**
- Ductility, like flow, increases with temperature; waxes with lower melting temperatures are more ductile at any given temperature (Craig and Powers, 2002; Bhat et al, 2019).
- A blend whose components have a wide spread of melting ranges is more ductile than one with a narrow range, because the lowest melting component softens first and plasticises the whole mass (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Highly refined waxes are brittle; microcrystalline waxes with occluded oil are soft and plastic (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**H. Residue:** ≤0.10% after burnout (section 6). Incomplete burnout leaves carbon or film on the mould walls and spoils the casting surface and margins (Anusavice et al, 2013).

## 8. Manipulation

### Explanation

Dry heat is preferred to a water bath (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]; McCabe and Walls rotate the stick above a flame until it becomes shiny (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC] and favour a thermostatically controlled annealer for inlay waxes (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Bonsor and Pearson heat the wax to 45-50 °C for at least 20 minutes before use (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) [SRC]. Craig: higher wax temperature at shaping gives less distortion (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]; use warmed instruments and die and add wax in small amounts (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha carve with warm (37 °C) instruments (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Bhat uses a cold carving instrument for direct patterns (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC] and pressure by finger or by the patient biting on the wax (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. For indirect patterns the die is lubricated with a wetting-agent lubricant (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. An MOD pattern is removed with a staple-like wire whose prongs sit over the proximal walls (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Invest immediately (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Softening**
- Dry heat is preferred; a water bath can trap water droplets that spatter on flaming, smear the surface and distort the pattern (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- Stick wax is rotated above a flame until shiny, removed, then reheated repeatedly until soft throughout, then kneaded (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Best: a thermostatically controlled wax annealer keeping wax just above its softening point (McCabe and Walls, 2008); heat at 45-50 °C for at least 20 minutes before use (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- Use the lowest temperature possible; wax oxidises and leaves gummy deposits on prolonged heating, so clean the pot and replace the wax periodically (Anusavice et al, 2013).

**B. Direct technique (direct-type wax)** (Bhat et al, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008)
1. Isolate the prepared tooth with a thin lubricant.
2. Soften the wax uniformly; knead and shape roughly to the cavity.
3. Press into the cavity and hold under pressure (finger, or the patient biting) while it cools gradually to mouth temperature.
4. Carve to anatomy and margins (Bhat et al, 2019 recommend a cold instrument).
5. Remove along the path of insertion: penetrate slightly with an explorer point; for an MOD pattern use a staple-shaped wire, prongs over the proximal boxes, pulled by floss parallel to the axial walls (Anusavice et al, 2013).
6. Handle as little as possible; invest at once. Thermal contraction 0.1-0.5% (McCabe and Walls, 2008). If there is an undercut the wax should fracture, showing where the cavity needs correction (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

**C. Indirect technique (indirect-type wax)** (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019; McCabe and Walls, 2008)
1. Impression (usually elastomeric) → stone die.
2. Lubricate the die with a lubricant containing a wetting agent; avoid excess, which prevents intimate adaptation.
3. Apply the first layer by dipping or with a thin sheet, then add molten wax in small increments with a spatula or waxing instrument (wax-additive technique).
4. Overfill, then carve to contour; do not abrade the die at the margins.
5. Polish with silk or fine cloth, rubbing toward the margins.
6. Remove carefully and invest immediately. Full technique: Q3.

**D. Rules to reduce stress during manipulation**
- The higher the wax temperature when the pattern is adapted and shaped, the less the distortion (Craig and Powers, 2002).
- Warm instruments and a warm die; add wax in small amounts (Craig and Powers, 2002); carve with warm (37 °C) instruments (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
- Hold under uniform pressure while cooling; avoid repeated remelting of large areas.

**E. Direct versus indirect (brief)**

| | Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|---|
| Where | In the mouth | On a stone die |
| Wax | Harder, ≤1% flow at 37 °C | Softer, carvable at room temperature |
| Thermal contraction | Larger (mouth → room) | Smaller, but die errors added |
| Use today | Rare: simple post-cores (McCabe and Walls, 2008) | Routine |
| Chair time [RECALL] | More | Less |

Phillips notes a direct pattern may give a looser-fitting inlay than an indirect one because of its larger thermal shrinkage (Anusavice et al, 2013).

## 9. Wax distortion: causes and prevention

### Explanation

Distortion is the most serious problem in forming and removing a pattern (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC], arising from occluded air, physical deformation during moulding, carving or removal, release of trapped stress, long storage and temperature change (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Prolonged storage distorts by stress relaxation (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. Craig: refrigerate a pattern left uninvested longer than 30 minutes (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], let it warm before investing, use a solid wax sprue or sticky-wax-filled hollow metal sprue (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], and readapt margins if it was stored (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha limit the delay to under 30 minutes (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Bhat's list starts with choosing the correct type of wax (Bhat et al, 2019) [SRC]. A soft wax pattern gives a slightly larger and rougher casting than a hard wax pattern (Anusavice et al, 2013) [SRC]. The MOD pattern is covered in casting_alloys_defects Q17.

#### ✎ Exam core

Distortion is the most serious problem in making and removing a wax pattern (Anusavice et al, 2013).

**Causes** (Anusavice et al, 2013; Bhat et al, 2019)
1. Occluded air in the pattern.
2. Physical deformation during moulding, carving or removal.
3. Release of residual stresses trapped during cooling (elastic memory):
   - wax not heated uniformly, so parts contract more than others;
   - wax not held under uniform pressure while cooling;
   - molten wax added to deficient areas, which contracts and stresses the pattern.
4. Excessive storage time (stress relaxation).
5. Temperature change during storage; higher storage temperature gives more warpage (Craig and Powers, 2002).
6. Removal from undercuts or along the wrong path.

**Prevention** (Craig and Powers, 2002; Bhat et al, 2019; Powers and Wataha, 2013)
1. Select the correct type of wax for the technique.
2. Soften uniformly (annealer); adapt at the highest practical wax temperature.
3. Warm instruments and die; add wax in small increments.
4. Cool under uniform pressure.
5. Carve without pulling at the margins; remove along the long axis.
6. **Invest immediately**: the single best method. Once the investment sets, the pattern cannot distort (Anusavice et al, 2013). Keep the delay under 30 minutes (Powers and Wataha, 2013).
7. If delay is unavoidable, store in a refrigerator and warm to room temperature before investing; readapt the margins (Craig and Powers, 2002).
8. Sprue with a solid wax sprue or a hollow metal sprue filled with sticky wax (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Effect of wax type on casting size.** Soft waxes expand more as the investment heats during setting and resist investment expansion less, so they give slightly larger, rougher castings than hard waxes (Anusavice et al, 2013). Using inlay wax for the walls and softer casting wax for the occlusal surface (modulus ratio 7:1) evens expansion (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**MOD pattern.** The MOD pattern is most prone: proximal walls tend to move inward and the gingival margins open [RECALL]. Causes, compensation and prevention: see casting_alloys_defects Q17 (Distortion of MOD wax pattern).

## 10. Newer pattern materials and conclusion

### Explanation

Light-cured modelling resins (urethane dimethacrylate-based) have lower polymerisation heat and shrinkage than acrylics, higher strength and flow resistance than wax, and burn out without residue (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha: resins offer higher strength and lower flow than pattern waxes (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC], and all pattern materials must burn out without residue (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Q3 covers these in depth.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Newer pattern materials** (details in Q3)
1. **Light-cured modelling resins** (urethane dimethacrylate pastes and liquid): lower heat of polymerisation and shrinkage than acrylics, higher strength and flow resistance than wax, good dimensional stability, burnout without residue; inlay marginal gaps smaller than with wax (Craig and Powers, 2002).
2. **Self-cured acrylic pattern resins** [RECALL for the type]: higher strength and lower flow than wax (Powers and Wataha, 2013); used for post-core patterns.
3. **Preformed plastic patterns** (pontics, bars, clasps) and CAD/CAM milled or printed wax or resin patterns [RECALL].
All must burn out with no residue (Powers and Wataha, 2013).

**Conclusion.** Inlay wax is a blend of paraffin, carnauba, ceresin, beeswax, resins and colourants, made in direct and indirect types controlled by ADA Specification No. 4 for flow (≤1% at mouth or room temperature, 70-90% at 45 °C) and residue (≤0.10%). Its high thermal expansion and elastic memory make distortion the main hazard. Uniform softening, warm working, cooling under pressure and immediate investment give an accurate pattern and a well-fitting casting.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

1. **Claim: Type I is the direct (medium) wax and Type II the indirect (soft) wax.** Textbook: Anusavice et al (2013); Bhat et al (2019). Challenge: Craig and Powers (2002), citing the revised ANSI/ADA No. 4 (ISO 1561), and McCabe and Walls (2008) number them the opposite way. Strength: both camps cite the same flow values, so this is a labelling change between editions of the standard [Likely]. Conclusion: write both and define each type by its use and flow [Certain].
2. **Claim: residue ≤0.10% at 500 °C.** Anusavice et al (2013) say 500 °C; Craig and Powers (2002) say 700 °C ignition; Bhat et al (2019) say no residue at 500 °C. The limit (0.10%) agrees; the test temperature differs. Quote both [Certain that the books differ; Guessing which the current ISO text uses].
3. **Claim: thermal contraction mouth-to-room is about 0.4%.** Ranges: 0.35% (Anusavice et al, 2013), 0.4% (Craig and Powers, 2002), 0.3-0.4% (Bhat et al, 2019), 0.1-0.5% (McCabe and Walls, 2008). CTE ranges likewise vary (217-512, 300-700, 350-450 × 10⁻⁶). The figures depend on the wax and the temperature interval; all agree it is the highest of any dental material [Certain].
4. **Claim: the indirect technique avoids thermal contraction.** Refinement: McCabe and Walls (2008) point out that the impression and die were themselves cooled from mouth temperature, so the error is moved, not removed [Likely].
5. **Claim: hard wax is better.** Refinement: a soft wax gives a slightly larger casting (helpful for compensation) but rougher (Anusavice et al, 2013); choice depends on technique and climate [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

- Inlay wax: pattern wax for the lost-wax technique; ADA Spec No. 4 (ISO 1561, later ISO 15854 [RECALL]).
- Two types: direct (harder, Phillips Type I / Craig Type 2) and indirect (softer, Phillips Type II / Craig Type 1). State both numberings.
- Composition (Bhat): paraffin 60, carnauba 20, ceresin 5, dammar 3, beeswax 5, synthetic resin 2. Craig: 60/25/10/5 paraffin/carnauba/ceresin/beeswax.
- Flow: direct ≤1% at 37 °C; indirect ≤1% at 30 °C, ≥50% at 40 °C; both 70-90% at 45 °C. Test: 19.6 N, 10 min.
- Residue ≤0.10% (500 °C Phillips; 700 °C Craig).
- CTE about 350 × 10⁻⁶/K, highest of any dental material; 0.35-0.4% contraction mouth to room.
- Elastic memory: horseshoe demonstration; residual stress in every pattern.
- Best way to avoid distortion: invest immediately (within 30 min); otherwise refrigerate.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Giving only one Type numbering as if universal; examiners using Craig or McCabe will mark the opposite as wrong.
2. Writing "flow more than 1% at 37 °C" (a misprint in one list); it is a maximum of 1%.
3. Listing composition without functions of each ingredient.
4. Forgetting residue on burnout and the flow test method when asked "ADA specifications".
5. Saying the indirect technique has no thermal error.
6. Recommending storage of patterns at room temperature overnight; invest immediately or refrigerate.
7. Confusing inlay wax with casting wax (RPD sheets, federal specification) or baseplate wax (ADA No. 24).
