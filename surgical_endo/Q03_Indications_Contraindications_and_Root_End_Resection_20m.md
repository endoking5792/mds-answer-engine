## STUDY MODE

**Indications, as the sources frame them.** The list has shrunk. Harty's states that older "cookbook" indications were largely removed by better understanding of apical periodontitis, and gives three principles: retreatment impossible or unlikely to do better; strong likelihood of failure of nonsurgical treatment with signs or symptoms (for example a calcified canal); need for biopsy (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's says surgery is indicated when retreatment is impractical or unlikely to improve the result, and names teeth with long posts, irretrievable separated instruments, non-negotiable ledges, blockages or transportation, hard cement fillings, failed retreatment, suspected vertical root fracture, and need for biopsy (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's groups them as anatomical complexity, irretrievable material, persistent symptoms, procedural accidents and exploratory surgery (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's adds that retreatment should have been done at least twice before surgery (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The RCS England guideline adds two practical indications: where the periradicular tissues must be seen because a perforation or root fracture is suspected, and where prolonged retreatment is inexpedient for patient reasons (Evans et al, 2012) [SRC]. Its listing of a post whose removal risks root fracture is the commonest clinical version of "irretrievable material" (Evans et al, 2012) [SRC].

**Where sources differ.** Cohen's also recognises surgery as a reasonable first choice even when retreatment is technically possible, if the risks or costs of retreatment are excessive, for example disassembling a recent bridge abutment, and cites one study finding microsurgery the most cost-effective option (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's and Ingle's are more restrictive (Gutmann, 2017; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. The RCS guideline notes that only two randomised trials compare the two approaches and that surgery may be better in the short term but retreatment in the long term (Evans et al, 2012) [SRC]. So the "first-line" rule is a consensus position, not settled by strong trial evidence [Likely].

**Contraindications.** Few are absolute (Evans et al, 2012; Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Grossman's lists inadequate periodontal support with uncontrollable periodontal disease, poor restorability, nearness to the inferior alveolar nerve, lingual nerve, mental foramen or sinus, serious systemic conditions and the operator's skill (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's names medical or systemic complications, indiscriminate use of surgery (unethical when nonsurgical treatment would probably succeed), anatomic factors and an unidentified cause of failure (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Proximity matters because the mesiobuccal apex of the maxillary second molar lies on average 0.83 mm from the sinus floor (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC].

**Root-end resection: why and how much.** The surgery has two aims: remove the cause, and stop recontamination (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Two principles set the extent of resection: remove the cause (including diseased tissue and, when needed, reduce a fenestrated apex) and give room for inspection and management of the root end (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. About 75% of teeth have canal aberrations in the apical 3 mm, so a 3 mm resection removes most of them (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman's gives 98% of apical ramifications and 93% of lateral canals (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's reports no consensus on the amount, and says not to remove more than 3 mm (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Anatomical limits (mental foramen, mandibular canal) may set the extent instead (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Angle.** Cohen's: enhanced magnification and illumination removed the need for a bevel in most cases, and the biologically best cut is perpendicular to the long axis (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Reasons: a perpendicular cut 3 mm from the apex is more likely to include all ramifications, a steeper angle exposes more tubules connecting the canal to the periradicular tissue, extending the cavity coronally is simpler, and stress is distributed evenly (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's recommends little or no bevel (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC].

**Microsurgical points.** After resection, inspect the cut surface under high magnification, with methylene blue, for cracks, canal aberrations, missed canals and isthmuses (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. A smooth surface helps inspection, although fibroblast attachment was not impaired by roughness in the one study cited (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Midrange magnification is used for resection, preparation and filling (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The resected surface may be conditioned; 15 to 24% EDTA for about 2 minutes is judged the most suitable agent from periodontal research, but its effect on human periradicular outcome is not established, and the MTA manufacturer advised against EDTA with MTA (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. A root-end filling is placed if the rest of the canal cannot be verified irritant-free (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim: a perpendicular 3 mm cut is better than a bevelled one.** Textbook: Cohen's and Ingle's both recommend it, on anatomical and mechanical reasoning (Johnson et al, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Retrieved evidence: the 94% against 59% pooled outcome (Q01) compares whole packages of technique, so it cannot isolate the bevel [Likely]. The reasoning is anatomical, and I did not retrieve a trial that isolates the angle. Conclusion: well-reasoned and consistent across sources, but supported by anatomy and bundled outcome data, not by an isolated comparison [Likely].

### Indications of endodontic surgery
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Nonsurgical treatment or retreatment impossible or unlikely to improve | Non-negotiable canal, ledge, blockage, separated instrument, long post, hard cement, calcified canal with symptoms |
| Failure of previous treatment | Persistent disease after retreatment (retreatment should have been done at least twice); failed surgery |
| Anatomical or iatrogenic | Severe curvature, complex anatomy, perforation, overfilling with symptoms |
| Diagnostic | Biopsy; suspected perforation or vertical root fracture |
| Others | True cyst not resolving; horizontal apical root fracture; hemisection or radisection; patient reasons |
Surgery may be chosen first when disassembling a restoration is too costly or risky (Johnson et al, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021; Evans et al, 2012).

### Contraindications
- **Patient:** serious systemic disease (blood disorders, uncontrolled diabetes, severe cardiac disease, immunocompromise); psychological factors.
- **Local:** inadequate periodontal support; poor restorability; nearness to mandibular canal, mental foramen or sinus; lack of access.
- **Operator:** inexperience.
- **Indiscriminate use** where nonsurgical treatment would probably succeed; **unidentified cause of failure** (Gopikrishna, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).

### Decision making
Nonsurgical retreatment is usually the first choice; surgery is chosen when retreatment is impractical or unlikely to improve the result. Cohen's calls for shared decision making in which the clinician sets out outcome probabilities, risks and cost and the patient's preferences are weighed. Under ideal conditions the prognoses of retreatment, surgery and an implant are about equal, so the choice depends on the case (Johnson et al, 2021). The RCS guideline says surgery may be better in the short term and retreatment in the long term, based on only two randomised trials (Evans et al, 2012).

### Pre-operative assessment before deciding
- Clinical: swelling, sinus tract, restorations and cracks, periodontal probing including any isolated deep pocket, occlusion, sensibility and percussion (Evans et al, 2012).
- Radiographs: parallel periapical, extra angled views, at least 3 mm beyond the apex; CBCT for posterior teeth (Evans et al, 2012).
- Medical: bleeding disorders, anticoagulants, systemic disease; informed consent, with the options and risks explained (Evans et al, 2012).
- Antibiotic prophylaxis for postoperative infection has not been shown to help in patients who do not need it for medical reasons (Evans et al, 2012).

### Why resect the root end?
1. To remove the disease: apical ramifications and lateral canals harbour bacteria; resection is the only definitive way to remove them (Johnson et al, 2021).
2. To gain access and see: to inspect the root end, prepare a cavity and place a filling (Johnson et al, 2021).
3. To reduce a fenestrated apex below the cortical level so bone can cover it (Johnson et al, 2021).
4. To remove foreign material or reach a palatally placed root.
5. To give a flat surface in which a cavity can be prepared and a filling placed; a perpendicular cut also makes it easier to extend the cavity coronally (Johnson et al, 2021).

### Microsurgical root-end resection
**Aim:** remove the etiologic factor and give access to inspect and seal the root end.

**Steps:**
1. After flap, osteotomy and curettage, identify the apex under low magnification.
2. **Resect 3 mm** perpendicular to the long axis, with a bur in an Impact Air 45-degree handpiece (Gopikrishna, 2021) and saline coolant. This removes about 98% of apical ramifications and 93% of lateral canals; do not remove more than 3 mm, so root length is kept for strength (Gopikrishna, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
3. **Little or no bevel.** Old technique: 45-60 degree bevel, which exposes tubules and costs buccal bone; the perpendicular cut exposes fewer tubules and distributes stress evenly (Johnson et al, 2021).
4. **Inspect** the cut surface at mid to high power with methylene blue: cracks, missed canals, isthmus (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
5. Smooth the surface to aid inspection.
6. **Prepare and fill** the root end (see Q07 and Q08). Condition with EDTA only if the filling material allows it.

**Limits to resection:** mental foramen, mandibular canal, fenestrated apex, sinus.

### Advantages of the microsurgical cut
Smaller osteotomy, fewer exposed tubules, better inspection, better preparation.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
3 mm cut; 98% and 93%; perpendicular, little or no bevel; indications shrinking, retreat first; contraindications few; indiscriminate use and unknown cause are contraindications.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
Apex with 3 mm resection line and ramifications; bevelled versus perpendicular cut with tubules.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Listing every old indication (cyst, separated file) as automatic.
- Writing a 45 degree bevel as the microsurgical technique.
- Resecting more than 3 mm.
- Omitting inspection of the cut face for isthmus.
- Forgetting that unidentified cause of failure is itself a contraindication.
