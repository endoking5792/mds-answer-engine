## STUDY MODE

**What the operation is for.** The purpose of periapical surgery is to stop irritants leaving the root canal system for the periapical tissues, by gaining access to the root end and sealing the portals of exit with a biocompatible material that permits complete regeneration of the periapical tissues (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's 14th edition gives the working objectives as three words: curettage of affected periradicular tissue that cannot be reached from the canal, resection of the apex where its ramifications cannot be eliminated nonsurgically, and inspection of the periradicular area to find why treatment failed (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The common operation is periradicular surgery, made of curettage, root-end resection, root-end preparation and root-end filling, and Harty's calls the old terms "apicectomy" and "apicoectomy" archaic and too narrow (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC].

**History in one breath.** The first recorded endodontic surgical act is replantation in the 11th century by Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi (Albucasis); G.V. Black recommended resecting the apex in 1886; Partsch described root-end resection through a semilunar incision in the 1890s; the focal-infection era (1900 to 1939) drove extraction in America while surgical techniques went on developing in Europe (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Ochsenbein and Luebke's flap dates to 1974 in Ingle's account (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's also records that this flap-type incision was described earlier by Neumann (1915), so the eponym is not the first description [Likely, from the same chapter].

**Why indications have shrunk.** This is the single most important conceptual point. Harty's notes that older texts gave "cookbook"-type indications (separated instrument, apical fracture, inadequate filling, cyst), and that better understanding of apical periodontitis removed most of them (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Outcome studies show a higher success rate with high-quality nonsurgical treatment, and most teeth referred for surgery would be better treated nonsurgically (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Ingle's says the primary modality for treatment failure should be nonsurgical retreatment whenever possible (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's agrees it is generally the preferred first approach, with surgery indicated when retreatment is impractical or unlikely to improve on the previous result (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Von Arx and White give the same first-line position and note that apical surgery can reach extracanal bacteria, which retreatment cannot (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC].

**The biological reasons surgery is still needed.** Microorganisms survive even in apparently well-treated teeth, in tubules, irregularities, deltas and isthmuses (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. *Enterococcus faecalis* is commonly isolated from failing teeth and is hard to eliminate (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Extraradicular biofilm cannot be diagnosed noninvasively but is suspected in well-treated, refractory cases (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. True cysts, completely enclosed and epithelium-lined, may not resolve after nonsurgical treatment, so excisional biopsy is the definitive route (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Nonsurgical therapy just before surgery may still be advised to reduce the intracanal microbial load (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Classifications that a viva examiner expects.** Gutmann's classification (in Grossman's): fistulative surgery (incision and drainage, cortical trephination, decompression); periradicular surgery (curettage, root-end resection, preparation, filling); corrective surgery (perforation repair, root and tooth resection, intentional replantation) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Kim's microsurgical classes A to F grade the case by lesion size, periodontal pocket, mobility and endo-perio communication; Class B (small lesion, normal probing depth, no mobility) is the ideal microsurgical candidate (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Indications and contraindications.** Grossman's lists failure of nonsurgical treatment, failure of retreatment (it should have been done at least twice), failure of previous surgery, anatomical problems, iatrogenic errors, horizontal apical fracture, exploratory surgery and biopsy, and periodontal indications for hemisection and radisection (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's reduces these to anatomical complexity, irretrievable material, persistent symptoms, procedural accidents and exploratory surgery (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's frames three cases: retreatment impossible or no better, strong likelihood of failure of nonsurgical treatment with symptoms, and need for biopsy (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Contraindications are few: inadequate periodontal support and uncontrollable periodontal disease, poor restorability, nearness to the mandibular canal, mental foramen or sinus, serious systemic disease, and the operator's lack of skill (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's has "indiscriminate use of surgery" and "unidentified cause of failure" as contraindications in their own right (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's says contraindications are "few and far between" (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC].

**Anatomy that decides technique.** The mesiobuccal apex of the maxillary second molar lies closest to the sinus floor (mean 0.83 mm) (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. An isthmus was present 100% of the time at the 4 mm level in mesiobuccal roots of maxillary first molars with two canals (Weller, in Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; this is why the resected surface must be inspected for an isthmus. Other points (mental foramen, greater palatine foramen, mandibular canal) are in Q04 and Q06.

**Pre-operative preparation.** Written informed consent, medical history, and bleeding investigations (bleeding time, clotting time, prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time) where indicated (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Chlorhexidine rinse from 1 day before surgery (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's adds periodontal examination before surgery and recommends rinsing to reduce oral microorganisms (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC].

**Technique, step by step, with the reasoning.** Local anesthesia and hemostasis come first because bleeding hides landmarks; Grossman's names 2% lidocaine with 1:50,000 epinephrine as the usual choice and gives the ADA maximum of 200 μg epinephrine per day for a healthy adult (about 10 carpules at 1:50,000) (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. *Flap*: do not place incisions over a bony defect, and keep about 5 mm between the defect and the incision line (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; vertical releasing incisions go on interdental bone, never on the radicular eminence (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Crestal bone loss of about 0.5 to 0.62 mm is reported after a full-thickness flap (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; this is why papilla-based and submarginal flaps exist (Q04). *Hemostasis*: epinephrine pellets, ferric sulfate, calcium sulfate, collagen, Gelfoam; bone wax is mechanical, and remnants cause persistent inflammation so it is not recommended where alternatives exist (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; ferric sulfate is safe only if the coagulum is removed completely (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. *Osteotomy*: irreversible bone damage threshold is between 44 and 47 °C for one minute, so use light brushing strokes with saline coolant (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; round burs no. 6 or 8 give smoother cuts and shorter healing than fissure or diamond burs (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; keep the window small, ideally 4 mm so a 3 mm ultrasonic tip moves freely (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], because small lesions heal faster (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. *Curettage*: remove the pathologic tissue for access and for biopsy; total removal is not needed for success (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; send tissue for histology, since Grossman held that anything removed from the periradicular area deserves a diagnosis (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. *Resection*: remove 3 mm, which includes about 98% of apical ramifications and 93% of lateral canals; more than 3 mm is not advised (Gopikrishna, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; with microsurgery, little or no bevel is needed (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. *Preparation*: ultrasonic tips give a Class I cavity at least 3 mm deep along the canal (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. *Filling*: MTA or another calcium-silicate cement is the recommended material (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. *Closure*: reposition and compress for 2 to 3 minutes (5 for palatal tissue), because compression leaves a thin fibrin clot (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], then interrupted sutures, removed at 4 days (Gopikrishna, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC].

**Healing.** First intention across the incision takes about 5 days if sutures hold; failure leads to second intention, 4 to 6 weeks and sometimes a year (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Periradicular repair is usually complete within a year, with progress visible at 6 months (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Healing speeds differ by tissue: free gingiva 7 days, attached gingiva 14, alveolar mucosa 28 (Selvig and Torabinejad, in Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. A radiolucent "surgical defect" can persist after full repair when the lingual or palatal plate was lost; it heals by fibrous tissue, not bone (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Andreasen and Rud describe three types of repair after root resection (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's frames the goal as regeneration rather than scar repair (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Other surgical procedures** the essay should mention briefly: incision and drainage, radisectomy and hemisection, intentional replantation, perforation repair (Gopikrishna, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC].

**Further textbook points (second audit against the remaining books, 2026-10-18).** 
*Outcome grading.* The RCS guideline and von Arx and White grade surgical outcome as successful, incomplete (often fibrous scar), uncertain or unsuccessful (Molven's four categories, the most widely used system); success needs no symptoms or signs and a periodontal ligament space that is normal or at most twice the normal width, with normal lamina dura and bone pattern and no root resorption (Evans et al, 2012; von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]. Reported healing ranges widely (37% to 91% in the guideline; 67% to 97% in studies using the microscope or endoscope), so outcomes must be defined and audited (Evans et al, 2012; von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]. Review follows suture removal, then radiographs with the same angulation yearly until healing is seen (Evans et al, 2012) [SRC]. One year is the usual assessment point, but early successes can later fail, so the tooth is re-examined at every later check-up (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC].

*Long-term and prognostic data.* Tsesis found complete healing in 91% with the modern technique against 44% with the traditional bevelled technique (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]. Pooled success of microsurgery was 90% at 2 to 4 years and 84% at 4 to 6 years, with tooth survival of 94% and 88% (von Arx and White, 2019, citing Torabinejad et al) [SRC]. In a meta-analysis of 38 studies, age and sex did not influence healing; mandibular molars did worst, and an isthmus in a molar carried a six-fold risk of failure (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]. Favourable factors in one systematic review included lesions not over 10 mm and noncystic lesions (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: modern microsurgery has transformed outcome (94% against 59%).** *Textbook:* Cohen's, Ingle's and von Arx and White all quote Setzer and colleagues' pooled success of 94% for microsurgery against 59% for traditional surgery (Johnson et al, 2021; von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]. *Retrieved evidence:* the PubMed abstract confirms 12 traditional studies (925 teeth) and 9 microsurgical studies (699 teeth), 59% against 94%, a relative probability of 1.58 (Setzer et al, 2010) [LIT, abstract]. The second meta-analysis found 88% with microinstruments and loupes against 94% with a microscope or endoscope, and the difference reached significance for molars only, not premolars or anteriors (Setzer et al, 2012) [LIT, abstract]. A third found 94.4% for ultrasonic preparation with Super-EBA, IRM, MTA or silicate cements against 82.2% for shallow, bonded resin fillings (Kohli et al, 2018) [LIT, abstract]. *Strength:* these are meta-analyses of cohort and case-series data, not randomised trials; von Arx and White themselves note very few randomised trials exist (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]. A later review restricted to randomised trials and prospective studies with at least 2 years follow-up found a pooled success of 91.3% in the randomised trials but 78.4% in prospective studies (Pinto et al, 2020) [LIT, abstract]. *Interpretation:* the direction of effect is consistent, but "microsurgery" bundles together magnification, ultrasonic preparation and new materials, so no meta-analysis isolates one cause, and the old traditional studies are historical controls [Likely]. *Limitation:* none of this shows microsurgery beats nonsurgical retreatment. *Implication:* when surgery is chosen, use the modern technique; the absolute figure depends on the study design.

**Claim 2: nonsurgical retreatment should come first.** *Textbook:* Ingle's cites a systematic review where surgery was initially higher at 2 to 4 years but outcome shifted towards nonsurgical retreatment at 4 to 6 years (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's holds that high-quality nonsurgical treatment gives a higher success rate (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's says that under ideal conditions the prognoses of retreatment, surgery and an implant should be roughly equal (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC], and that microsurgery may be the most cost-effective option in one study (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. *Conclusion:* the sources agree that retreatment is first-line in general but disagree on how far the evidence separates the options. Ingle's older comparison predates much of the modern technique; I have not read the primary comparison [Guessing about how much that matters]. The choice rests on case factors, restorative cost and patient preference (shared decision making) (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Claim 3: regenerative adjuncts (bone graft, membrane) improve healing.** A 2024 meta-analysis of 8 randomised trials found significantly better outcomes with grafting with or without a membrane (OR 2.18), yet no individual graft, membrane or combination reached significance and the authors say more trials are needed before routine use (Flynn et al, 2024) [LIT, abstract]. *Implication:* not routine; reasonable in large through-and-through defects [Guessing].

**Claim 4: guided surgery.** A 2026 review found static guides and dynamic navigation more accurate than freehand in laboratory and cadaver studies, but only two clinical reports, so clinical benefit is unproven (Rewthamrongsris et al, 2026) [LIT, abstract].

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: ~150 minutes)*

### 1. Introduction
Surgical endodontics is the group of surgical procedures used to retain a tooth when root canal treatment or retreatment cannot achieve or maintain periapical health, or when a biopsy or exploration is needed. Initial nonsurgical treatment survives above 90% in large series, so surgery is a second-line procedure (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). Its purpose is to stop irritants leaving the canal system by sealing every portal of exit with a biocompatible material that allows regeneration of the periapical tissues (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).

### 2. Objectives (Grossman)
1. **Curettage** of tissue not reachable from the canal (resistant granuloma, true cyst, foreign body reaction).
2. **Resection** of the apex where ramifications cannot be cleaned nonsurgically.
3. **Inspection** of the periradicular area, to find the cause of failure (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 2A. Why nonsurgical treatment can fail (Cohen's)
- Microorganisms survive in apparently well-treated teeth: tubules, canal irregularities, deltas and isthmuses.
- *Enterococcus faecalis* is commonly isolated from failing teeth and is hard to eliminate; fungi and viruses may have a role.
- Extraradicular biofilm cannot be diagnosed noninvasively; suspect it in well-treated, refractory cases.
- True cysts and cholesterol crystals may not resolve nonsurgically.
- Vertical root fracture is a significant cause; exploratory surgery often confirms it, and extraction is usually the treatment.
- Overfilling with toxic paste (for example formaldehyde pastes) may cause chronic inflammation; minor overextension alone is rarely a sole indication.
- Host factors: diabetes and immunosuppression may delay healing (Johnson et al, 2021).

### 3. Brief history
- 11th century: replantation, Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi (Albucasis).
- 1886: Black resected the apex.
- 1890s: Partsch, semilunar incision.
- 1900-1939: focal infection era; extraction in America, surgery continued in Europe.
- 1974: Ochsenbein and Luebke flap.
- Later decades: microsurgery with magnification, ultrasonic tips and MTA (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). The decade is not stated in the cited chapter [Guessing].

### 4. Classification
**Gutmann (Grossman's):**
| Group | Procedures |
|---|---|
| Fistulative | Incision and drainage, cortical trephination, decompression |
| Periradicular | Curettage, root-end resection, root-end preparation, root-end filling |
| Corrective | Perforation repair (mechanical, resorptive), root and tooth resection, intentional replantation |

**Kim's microsurgical classes:** A no lesion, symptoms only; B small lesion, normal pocket, no mobility (ideal); C large lesion, no pocket; D as C with deep pocket; E large lesion with endo-perio communication to the apex; F apical lesion with complete buccal plate loss, no mobility (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 5. Indications
- **Failure of nonsurgical treatment** when retreatment is impossible or impractical (post, crown, bridge, calcified canal).
- **Failure of retreatment**: nonsurgical treatment should have been rendered at least twice (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Failure of previous surgery** (resurgery).
- **Anatomical problems**: non-negotiable canal, severe curvature, complex anatomy, calcification.
- **Iatrogenic errors**: ledge, blockage, separated instrument, perforation, overfill with symptoms.
- **Irretrievable material**: posts, cemented cores, fragments.
- **Persistent symptoms** after good treatment.
- **Horizontal apical root fracture** with a necrotic apical segment.
- **Exploratory surgery and biopsy**: suspected fracture; radiolucency not of endodontic origin.
- **True cyst** that will not resolve (Johnson et al, 2021).
- **Periodontal indications**: hemisection, radisection.
Surgery may be the first choice when retreatment risks or costs are excessive, such as taking down a new bridge (Johnson et al, 2021).

### 6. Contraindications
- Inadequate periodontal support or uncontrollable periodontal disease.
- Poor restorability.
- Nearness to the mandibular canal, mental foramen, or sinus.
- Serious systemic disease: blood disorders, uncontrolled diabetes, severe cardiac disease, immunocompromise.
- **Indiscriminate use**: unethical when a nonsurgical approach would probably succeed.
- **Unidentified cause of failure**.
- Operator inexperience (Gopikrishna, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).

### 6A. Decision making: surgery, retreatment or implant
Shared decision making is preferred: the clinician sets out the probabilities, risks and costs and the patient's preferences are weighed. Cohen's states that the prognoses of retreatment, surgery and implant placement are, under ideal conditions, roughly equal, and that treatment thresholds differ widely between clinicians (Johnson et al, 2021). Nonsurgical retreatment before surgery may reduce the microbial load (Johnson et al, 2021). Surgery is the first choice when retreatment is impractical, for example a long post or a recently placed bridge (Johnson et al, 2021; Evans et al, 2012).

### 7. Pre-operative evaluation
- Medical history; investigations (bleeding time, clotting time, prothrombin time, thrombin time, partial thromboplastin time) when there is a bleeding concern; anticoagulant status.
- Radiographs from more than one angle; CBCT where proximity to anatomical structures or lesion extent is uncertain.
- Periodontal assessment; restorability.
- Informed consent: procedure, success chance, alternatives, risks (pain, swelling, bruising, paraesthesia).
- Chlorhexidine rinse from the day before; ibuprofen 400-800 mg before surgery and for 48 hours; antibiotics are generally avoided except for medically compromised patients, with amoxicillin or clindamycin if indicated (Gopikrishna, 2021). The RCS guideline finds no benefit from prophylactic antibiotics to prevent postoperative infection in patients who do not need them for medical reasons (Evans et al, 2012; Gutmann, 2017).

### 7A. Surgical anatomy that decides the plan
- **Maxillary sinus:** the mesiobuccal apex of the maxillary second molar is closest to the floor (mean 0.83 mm) and the buccal apex of the first premolar furthest (mean 7.05 mm). Oroantral communication occurred in 41 of 314 apical procedures (13%) in one clinical study with no difference in healing, and in 50% of 200 maxillary first molars in another, where only two developed sinusitis (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
- **Greater palatine foramen:** usually between the second and third molars, about 1 cm from the palatal gingival margin; severing the palatal artery is life-threatening (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
- **Mandibular canal:** runs an S-shaped course; the apices of the second molar and second premolar are closest to it (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
- **Mental foramen:** variable; in a series of 1,000 radiographs, 70% lay between the apices of the two premolars; accessory mental foramina were present in 7% on CBCT (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
- **Isthmus:** present 100% of the time at the 4 mm level in two-canal mesiobuccal roots of maxillary first molars (Weller), 76% in a clinical series at 3-4 mm resection, and 85% in the mesial root of mandibular first molars on micro-CT (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
- **Bone over the apex:** the buccal cortical plate is thinnest over the maxillary canine and the buccal root of the first premolar (about 1.6 mm) and thickest over the distal root of the mandibular second molar (8.51 mm) (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).

### 7B. Medically complex patients (Cohen's)
- ASA 1 patients need no modification; ASA 2-3 may need medical consultation; ASA 4-5 are not treated in a dental office (Johnson et al, 2021).
- Vasoconstrictor is essential for hemostasis; patients who cannot tolerate it may not be good candidates. For cardiovascular disease, 0.036-0.054 mg epinephrine (two to three cartridges of 1:100,000) is safe for most except severe disease, recent infarction, uncontrolled failure (Johnson et al, 2021).
- Transient bacteremia is certain with periradicular surgery, so give endocarditis prophylaxis only to the highest-risk group per AHA (Johnson et al, 2021).
- Warfarin: consult the physician; INR on the day of surgery; do not stop routinely. Low-dose aspirin need not be stopped, but oozing can hamper visibility (Johnson et al, 2021).
- Bisphosphonates: stopping the drug does not help; nonsurgical retreatment first, then conservative surgery with primary closure and chlorhexidine (Johnson et al, 2021).

### 8. Armamentarium: the microsurgical triad
Magnification, illumination and instruments. Operating microscope in low (x2.5-8), mid (x8-14) and high (x14-30) range; micromirrors, mini-blades, Molt curettes, KP retractors, ultrasonic retrotips, microsuction, microplugger, MTA carrier (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 9. Operative procedure
**9.1 Anesthesia and hemostasis.** 2% lidocaine with 1:50,000 epinephrine, buccal and palatal/lingual infiltrations over the apices; the epinephrine limit is 200 μg/day for a healthy adult, about 10 carpules (Gopikrishna, 2021). Injecting after the incision is ineffective because local vasodilators override the vasoconstrictor (Gopikrishna, 2021). Local hemostatic agents are adjuncts (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019):
| Agent | Key points |
|---|---|
| Epinephrine pellet (Racellet no. 3) | About 0.55 mg per pellet; systemic effect minimal in the studies cited; count the pellets and curette out cotton fibres |
| Ferric sulfate | pH 0.8-1.6, necrotizing; safe only if the coagulum is curetted out and the crypt irrigated |
| Calcium sulfate | Resorbable physical barrier |
| Collagen | Osseous regeneration uneventful |
| Gelfoam | Resorbed fully; defects healed in a rat study |
| Surgicel | Retarded healing, most intense inflammatory reaction when left in |
| Bone wax | About 88% beeswax and 12% isopropyl palmitate; mechanical only; remnants cause persistent inflammation, sinus tract and delayed healing, so avoid |

**9.2 Flap.** Rules: incise to bone in one stroke; vertical incisions on interdental bone, not over a root eminence; never over a bony defect (keep about 5 mm clear); keep tissue moist; make it tension free (Gopikrishna, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
| Flap | Use |
|---|---|
| Triangular (one vertical) | Good access, limited apical reach |
| Rectangular (two verticals) | Long roots, anterior region |
| Sulcular full thickness | Microsurgery choice; recession risk |
| Papilla-based | Recession-free healing |
| Luebke-Ochsenbein (submarginal scalloped) | Crowned anterior teeth; needs at least 2 mm attached gingiva above the incision |
| Semilunar | Drainage only |

**9.3 Elevation and retraction.** Undermining elevation with periosteal elevator; retractor rests on cortical bone; irrigate flap with saline.

**9.4 Osteotomy.** Round bur no. 6 or 8, light brushing strokes with saline coolant; bone damage above 44-47 °C for 1 minute; incremental drilling lowers temperature; diamond burs cut least well and delay healing; window about 4 mm; locate the apex with a probe, radiopaque marker or CBCT; methylene blue helps separate root from bone. High-speed handpieces have caused surgical emphysema of the face (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). Small defects heal more completely: bone regenerated fully in 5-8 mm defects but with fibrous herniation in 9-12 mm defects (Boyne, in Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).

**9.5 Curettage and biopsy.** Remove soft tissue to give access and reduce bleeding; inject a little anesthetic into the lesion to ease removal; Lucas and periodontal curettes are suitable; if the Schneiderian membrane is involved, remove the lesion carefully to avoid sinus perforation. Complete removal is not needed for success (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). Anything removed deserves a histopathologic diagnosis; if a cyst is suspected, try to remove the lesion in one piece with its lining, and have the biopsy bottle ready (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**9.6 Root-end resection.** Perpendicular to the long axis, 3 mm, which removes about 98% of ramifications and 93% of lateral canals; leave root length for strength (Gopikrishna, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). Traditional 45-60 degree bevel exposes tubules and costs buccal bone; modern technique uses little or no bevel. Inspect the cut surface under high power with methylene blue for isthmus, cracks and missed canals.

**9.7 Root-end preparation.** Ultrasonic retrotip, Class I cavity 3 mm deep along the canal axis, following the isthmus; fewer lingual perforations, cleaner walls, smaller osteotomy. One randomised trial reported 80.5% success with ultrasonics against 70.9% with burs (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). Microfractures can occur, but they have not been linked to failure (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). Advantages of ultrasonic tips over burs: smaller osteotomy (a 3 mm tip needs about 4 mm), more precise preparation, less risk of lingual perforation, easier access, cleaner and deeper cavities, easy management of the isthmus (Gopikrishna, 2021); parallel walls, less smear layer, and less operator fatigue (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). Final inspection with a micromirror should show no gutta-percha in the 3 mm depth.
Dry with microsuction and Stropko irrigator; check with micromirror.

**9.8 Root-end filling.** *Ideal properties:* seals against bacteria, non-toxic, non-carcinogenic, biocompatible, insoluble, dimensionally stable, unaffected by moisture, radiopaque, non-staining, bioinductive. No material meets all (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). *Materials:* MTA and other calcium-silicate cements (recommended), IRM, Super-EBA; amalgam is no longer recommended (corrosion, tattoo, safety) (Gopikrishna, 2021). MTA: set pH about 12.5; compressive strength about 40 MPa at setting rising to 70 MPa at 21 days; sets in 2.5-3 hours (the same chapter also gives 3 hours 45 minutes, an internal inconsistency); requires moisture to set; cannot be mixed to a clay-like consistency, so use a carrier; keep the field dry and do not irrigate it away before closure; produced new cementum over the filling in animal studies (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). Full account in Q08.

**9.9 Final check and closure.** Radiograph for fill and for retained fragments; clean under the flap; reposition; compress with moist gauze 2-3 minutes (5 for palatal); interrupted sutures (monofilament preferred; silk collects plaque) (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).

**9.10 Sutures.** Sutures re-approximate the wound edges and stabilise the flap; they assist initial attachment and should not be incorporated into healing, since an epithelial sleeve can form around a suture in as little as 3 days (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019). Interrupted sutures for vertical releases and looped interrupted sutures for the horizontal component (Gopikrishna, 2021). Reverse-cutting needles are recommended; materials include silk, gut, polyglactin, nylon; Tevdek is one-third stronger than silk (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 10. Post-operative care
Ice pack 1-2 hours with breaks; no lifting of the lip; chlorhexidine rinse; warm saline from day 2; analgesic (ibuprofen) as needed; antibiotics not routine; sutures out at 4 days (96 hours) (Gopikrishna, 2021). Emergency contact for 24 hours.

### 11. Healing
- Soft tissue: first intention about 5 days; second intention 4-6 weeks (Gopikrishna, 2021). Free gingiva 7 days, attached 14, alveolar mucosa 28 (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
- Hard tissue: clot, then granulation, then calcification; repair usually within a year, visible at 6 months (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Soft tissue (Cohen's):** clot; neutrophils from 6 hours, peaking at 24-48 hours; macrophages peak on days 3-4; fibroblasts arrive from day 3 and peak around day 7; epithelium migrates 0.5-1 mm/day and seals the primary wound in 21-28 hours; the epithelial barrier forms by 36-42 hours; maturation begins at 5-7 days. Compression with iced gauze thins the fibrin clot (Johnson et al, 2021).
- **Bone:** new bone about day 6; 3-4 weeks, the crypt is 75-80% filled; defect filled with bone by 16 weeks, with remodeling for months (Johnson et al, 2021).
- **Cementum:** begins 10-12 days after resection and covers the root end in about 28 days; PDL fibres reorient about 8 weeks (Johnson et al, 2021).
- NSAIDs may block the prostaglandins needed for bone healing, but their effect on healing after periradicular surgery is not established (Johnson et al, 2021).
- Three types of repair (Andreasen and Rud): complete with PDL; scar tissue adjacent to PDL with mild inflammation; scar with moderate inflammation.
- "Surgical defect": persistent lucency with fibrous repair when a cortical plate was lost.

### 12. Complications: causes, prevention and management (Tsesis et al, 2025)
| Complication | Cause | Prevention or management |
|---|---|---|
| Gingival recession, scar | Wrong repositioning, poor flap circulation, flap contraction; thin biotype carries higher risk | Tension-free flap, preserve root-associated tissue, papilla-based or submarginal flap in the esthetic zone |
| Flap necrosis or tearing | Insufficient blood supply, crushing, short releases | Parallel verticals, flap reflected as one unit, short operating time |
| Wound dehiscence | Tissue failure or improper suturing within the first week | Re-close or leave to heal; sutures passive, holding for at least 3 days |
| Bone necrosis | Heat above the threshold, desiccation | Light brushing strokes, copious irrigation |
| Large osteotomy | Excessive access | Aim for 4-5 mm with microsurgery; large defects may heal by fibrous tissue |
| Missed canal | Inadequate resection, poor view | Resect fully; inspect the cut face under magnification |
| Ultrasonic tip fracture, dentinal cracks | Heavy power | Light touch, moderate power, irrigation |
| Hemorrhage, hematoma | Vessel injury; bleeding disorder | Compression, gauze, ligation, topical agents; hematoma lasts up to 2 weeks; do the hemostasis-dependent work first, because bleeding can rebound after the anesthetic wears off |
| Nerve injury | Compression, stretch, cutting, overheating, puncture; commonest at the mandibular second molar | CBCT planning; record sensation; early management |
| Sinus perforation | Roots near the floor; perforations in 9.6-50% | Healing is not harmed if no foreign material or root tip enters the sinus |
| Pain, swelling, infection | Tissue damage, hemorrhage, edema | Swelling peaks on days 1-2; pre-emptive analgesia; no consensus on antibiotics (a dexamethasone regimen left 76% pain-free at day 1 in one study) |
| Periodontal lesion present | Poor prognostic factor | Endodontic success under 80% against over 90% without a lesion |
Excessive resection can reduce crown to root ratio, but resected teeth were not prosthodontically compromised unless periodontal bone loss was also present.

### 13. Other surgical procedures
- **Incision and drainage:** fluctuant swelling, evacuates exudate.
- **Radisectomy:** removal of a root of a multirooted tooth; **hemisection:** sectioning the crown with removal of one root; endodontic treatment first and coronal seal set before cutting; contraindicated where bone loss involves more than one root or roots are fused (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Intentional replantation:** extraction, extraoral root-end work, immediate replacement; useful when access is poor (lower second and third molars) or the apex lies near the mental nerve; keep extraoral time short and the PDL wet (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Perforation repair** with MTA.

### 14. Recent advances
- Operating microscope and endoscope; micro-instruments.
- Ultrasonic root-end preparation.
- MTA and other calcium-silicate materials.
- CBCT planning.
- Papilla-preserving flaps.
- Guided surgery (static guides, dynamic navigation): accurate in laboratory studies; clinical evidence limited (Rewthamrongsris et al, 2026).
- Bone graft and membrane adjuncts: some benefit, not yet routine (Flynn et al, 2024).

### 15. Outcome
- Modern microsurgery: pooled success 94% against 59% for traditional surgery (Setzer et al, 2010; von Arx and White, 2019). Another meta-analysis found 89% at 1 year for modern technique with no difference between microscope and endoscope but both better than loupes (von Arx and White, 2019).
- Randomised trials alone: 91.3%; prospective studies: 78.4% (Pinto et al, 2020).
- Survival: teeth treated by microsurgery are still lost at a low rate over time (von Arx and White, 2019); 5-year success was about 8% lower than at 1 year in one study (Johnson et al, 2021).
- Patients report less pain but more functional difficulty after the modern technique (von Arx and White, 2019).
- Cohen's quotes 5-10 year implant success of 90-97% (93% survival at 5 years in one meta-analysis) and states that careful microsurgery has exceeded 90% in many studies; 85% at 10 years in one study (Johnson et al, 2021).
- Prognostic factors: smoking, tooth type and location, dentinal defects, interproximal bone level and root-end material (Pinto et al, 2020).

### 15A. Intentional replantation: the key variables
Atraumatic extraction and minimal extraoral time are the key prognostic variables; wash the root with HBSS during the extraoral work, resect and retrofill with ultrasonic tips and MTA, replant, compress the plates and splint semirigidly (Gopikrishna, 2021).

### 16. Conclusion
Surgery is second-line but indispensable for cases beyond nonsurgical reach, and it should be chosen only after the cause of failure is known. With microscope, ultrasonic preparation and calcium-silicate filling, success is high and predictable in well-selected cases (Johnson et al, 2021).

- **Outcome grading:** successful, incomplete (scar), uncertain, unsuccessful; success = no symptoms, PDL space normal or at most twice normal, normal lamina dura; yearly radiographs until healed (Evans et al, 2012; von Arx and White, 2019).
- **Incision and drainage:** only when fluctuant; vertical incision, rubber-dam drain out in 2 to 3 days; surgery after healing (Torabinejad (Ch.5), 2019).
- **Sinus entered:** iodine gauze barrier, full-thickness closure, no nose blowing (Tataryn, 2019); proximity to the sinus is not itself a contraindication.
- **Antibiotics** only for systemic involvement (Reiser, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Objectives: curettage, resection, inspection. 3 mm resection removes 98% ramifications and 93% lateral canals. Osteotomy about 4 mm. Ultrasonic tip cavity 3 mm deep.
- Microsurgery 94% against traditional 59% (Setzer). Kim classes A-F. Gutmann classification.
- Retreatment is first-line; surgery for the cases it cannot reach. MTA is the material of choice; amalgam is out. Sutures out at 4 days.
