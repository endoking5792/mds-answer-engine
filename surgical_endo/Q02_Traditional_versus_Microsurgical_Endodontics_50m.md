## STUDY MODE

**Definition.** Grossman's: microsurgery is a surgical procedure on exceptionally small and complex anatomical structures with the dental operating microscope (DOM), letting the surgeon judge the pathologic cause more precisely and remove the lesion with minimal tissue damage; the term "microdentistry" was coined by Apotheker in 1980 (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Its working framework is the microsurgical triad: magnification, illumination and instruments (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Rubinstein and Fayad list the stages as flap design, reflection, retraction, osteotomy, curettage, biopsy, hemostasis, apical resection, evaluation of the resected apex, preparation, drying, selecting, mixing, placing, condensing and finishing the filling, and flap closure (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC].

**Why traditional surgery failed.** Floratos and Kim (Penn group) trace it to the technique: a large osteotomy, a steep bevel (45 to 60 degrees), no inspection of the cut face, isthmuses neither seen nor treated, a bur-prepared cavity that rarely lay inside the canal, and amalgam (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]. A steep bevel exposes more dentinal tubules; Gilheany and colleagues found leakage increased with the slope of the bevel and that the minimum retrograde filling depth was 1 mm at 0 degrees, 2.1 mm at 30 degrees and 2.5 mm at 45 degrees (Niemczyk, 2010; Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC]. Floratos and Kim state there is no biological justification for a steep bevel (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC].

**Comparison table (Kim and Kratchman, reproduced in Floratos and Kim)** (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]: osteotomy 8 to 10 mm against 3 to 4 mm; bevel 45 to 65 against 0 to 10 degrees; inspection of the cut face none against always; isthmus impossible against always; root-end preparation seldom inside the canal against always inside; instrument bur against ultrasonic tip; filling material amalgam against MTA; sutures 4-0 silk against 5-0 or 6-0 monofilament; suture removal at 7 days against 2 to 3 days; healing success at 1 year 40 to 90% against 85 to 97%.

**The microscope and ergonomics.** Before 1999 only 52% of surveyed endodontists used the DOM, against 97% of those aged 35 or less in a later survey (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]; yet a survey found almost 77% claimed difficulty with access and visualisation (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. Patient position matters less than the position of the root apex; the microscope's visual axis must be parallel to the root's long axis at the resection level, or the resection copies the skew (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. Low power (x2.5 to x8) is for orientation, mid (x8 to x14) for curettage, resection, preparation and filling, and high (x14 to x30) for fine detail and documentation (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's adds a steep learning curve that demands regular use (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC].

**Anesthesia and flap.** Niemczyk's protocol: a regional block with a long-acting agent such as bupivacaine, then a slow infiltration of lidocaine 1:50,000 epinephrine over the flap, avoiding skeletal muscle injection, which causes beta-receptor vasodilation and bleeding (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. Microscalpels and mini periosteal elevators allow atraumatic papilla handling; vertical incisions are made 1.5 to 2 times longer than in conventional surgery so the flap lies out of the light path (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC]. The flap choices are in Q04.

**Osteotomy.** The osteotomy is about 3 to 4 mm, enough for a 3 mm ultrasonic tip to vibrate freely; smaller osteotomies heal faster, with lesions under 5 mm taking an average of 6.4 months, 6 to 10 mm lesions 7.25 months and larger lesions 11 months (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]. It should be "as small as possible but as large as practical" (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. An Impact Air handpiece vents air through the back, reducing the risk of emphysema (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC]. Piezosurgery gives protection of soft tissues, a clear field and less blood loss, at the cost of equipment and longer surgical time, and is useful near nerves and the sinus (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC].

**Resection.** 3 mm perpendicular to the long axis, "like slicing a piece of bread", not a chainsaw cut (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. 3 mm eliminates 93% of lateral canals and 98% of other ramifications (Niemczyk, 2010; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The 3 mm level is also where the root is thickest in the apical 6 mm, giving a cushion for ultrasonic energy (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC]. Check completeness by staining with methylene blue; the PDL should appear as an uninterrupted circular line, and incomplete resection is a common reason for failure (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC].

**Inspection: the stage traditional surgery lacked.** At x14 to x25 with a micromirror at 45 degrees on a dried, stained surface, the surgeon finds gaps in the filling (the commonest finding), isthmuses, cracks, extra canals, accessory canals (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]. Niemczyk describes the "bow-tie effect", faint lines at transitional dentin line angles that are not fractures (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. Isthmuses are present in premolars and molars in about 80 to 90% of cases at 3 mm (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]; microorganisms were found in the isthmus in 10 of 11 root tips that had one, which is why resection without cleaning the isthmus often fails (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]. Rubinstein and Fayad add that retreatment cannot clean isthmuses and that the microorganisms reside in isthmuses, fins, debris, deltas and filling voids (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC].

**Ultrasonic root-end preparation.** Introduced by Carr in the early 1990s (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. A piezoelectric crystal vibrates at 28,000 to 40,000 cycles per second; most tips are 0.25 mm wide and about 3 mm long (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC]. Technique: align the tip with the root's long axis under low power, then use midrange magnification, and use short interrupted strokes with a very light touch, because ultrasonics cut by vibration, not pressure (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]. A high-pitched sound means the tip is binding or off-axis (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. Ingle's reports a randomised trial where success was 80.5% with ultrasonics against 70.9% with burs (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. Ultrasonics increase microfractures in vitro, but none of the studies showed clinical significance, and diamond tips did not significantly raise cracking (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC]; Ingle's agrees that no correlation with success has been shown (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. There is also an Er:YAG laser option which showed lower leakage than ultrasonic in vitro (Karlovic et al, 2005) [LIT, abstract; in vitro only].

**Hemostasis.** Niemczyk's grouping: ferric sulfate (remnants must be removed, avoid near nerve, sinus, nasal floor); buffered 20% aluminium chloride gel (Hemodette); gauze products (ActCel, HemCon); physiologic clotting agents (Avitene, expensive and hard to place; CollaPlug or CollaTape) (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. Patients should stop daily 81 mg aspirin or fish oil for 7 days (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC], although Cohen's says low-dose aspirin need not be stopped (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]; the sources disagree and the physician should be consulted.

**Root-end filling.** MTA is the material of choice (Floratos and Kim, 2017; Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC], and it needs a dry cavity because blood or moisture can wash it away before setting (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. Bioceramic root repair materials appear equally reliable and probably easier to use (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]; in a dog model, a bioceramic gave a better tissue response next to the root end than MTA (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]. A prospective randomised trial found no significant difference between Super-EBA and MTA, so technique may matter more than material (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019) [SRC]. A bonded restoration (Geristore) is used where a post prevents a normal cavity (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. Details in Q08.

**Closure.** Compress for about 5 minutes, ice for 24 hours, remove sutures at 1 to 3 days (Niemczyk, 2010) [SRC]. Grossman's gives 96 hours as the maximum for non-absorbable sutures (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; silk is not recommended (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC].

**Further textbook points (second audit against the remaining books, 2026-10-18).** 
*Optics and team.* Loupes are limited to about X3.5 to X4.5 in practice, whereas the operating microscope gives variable power, coaxial shadow-free light and Galilean optics (parallel beams, so the eyes rest); it improves the view but not physical access to the site (Rubinstein (Ch.7), 2019) [SRC]. Two assistants are used: one seated with an articulating binocular, the other standing on the surgeon's dominant side passing instruments; operating position is set by six factors (Rubinstein (Ch.7), 2019) [SRC]. Endoscopes give a two-dimensional image that fails in blood, and 3D heads-up systems are being tried; whether they could replace the microscope is unknown (Rubinstein (Ch.7), 2019) [SRC]. History: the microscope was first reported in apical surgery in 1984 by Reuben and Apotheker; the term "microdentistry" was coined by Apotheker in 1980 (so "Apothkar" above is a misspelling) (Rubinstein (Ch.7), 2019) [SRC].

> **SOURCE CONFLICT, microscope bands.** *Rubinstein* (Torabinejad Ch.7): X2.5 to X8 orientation, X10 to X16 operating, X20 to X30 detail; above X30 is of little use. Grossman's (above): x2.5 to 8, x8 to 14, x14 to 30. Both are kept.

*Papilla-base flap detail.* The papilla-base incision needs 7-0 sutures, at least two per papilla, and magnification of at least X4.3, whereas simple flaps need little magnification; the microscope is used to best advantage for osteotomy, resection, preparation, filling and documentation (Rubinstein (Ch.7), 2019) [SRC]. This differs from the 5-0 or 6-0 monofilament quoted for closure above.

*Why traditional surgery failed and outcomes.* West found an unfilled or underfilled portal of exit in every failed specimen examined, and amalgam-sealed surgery fell to 57.7% at 10 years (Rubinstein (Ch.7), 2019) [SRC]. Tsesis found complete healing in 91% (modern) against 44% (traditional) (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: microsurgery succeeds far more often than traditional surgery.** *Textbook:* Cohen's and von Arx and White give 94% against 59% (Johnson et al, 2021; von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]; Floratos and Kim give 96.8% at 1 year and 91.5% at 5 to 7 years (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]. *Retrieved evidence:* Setzer and colleagues' meta-analysis confirms 12 traditional studies (925 teeth) at 59% and 9 microsurgical studies (699 teeth) at 94%, relative probability 1.58 (Setzer et al, 2010) [LIT, abstract]. In their second analysis, microinstruments with loupes only reached 88% against 94% with a microscope or endoscope; the difference was significant for molars but not premolars or anteriors (Setzer et al, 2012) [LIT, abstract]. A third found ultrasonic preparation with Super-EBA, IRM, MTA or silicate cement 94.4% against 82.2% for shallow bonded resin (Kohli et al, 2018) [LIT, abstract]. *Strength:* meta-analyses of case series from different eras; von Arx and White say randomised trials are very few (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]. *Interpretation:* the improvement tracks a package, not one factor [Likely]. Higher magnification itself adds a smaller increment (88% to 94%) with benefit shown mainly in molars [Likely]. *Limitation:* no meta-analysis isolates the microscope, ultrasonic tip or MTA.

**Claim 2: the high results last.** *Textbook:* teeth are still lost at a low rate over time (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's cites 5-year success 8% lower than at 1 year and 85% at 10 years in another study (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. *Retrieved evidence:* a systematic review restricted to trials and prospective studies with at least 2 years of follow-up found a pooled success of 91.3% in the randomised trials but 78.4% in the prospective studies, with survival 79 to 100% (Pinto et al, 2020) [LIT, abstract]. *Disagreement preserved:* the Penn group's 91.5% at 5 to 7 years (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC] is higher than the 78.4% pooled prospective figure (Pinto et al, 2020) [LIT, abstract]; different case selection and success criteria probably explain some of the gap [Guessing]. *Implication:* quote ranges and the study type.

**Claim 3: case selection matters as much as technique.** Kim's classes A to C had 95.2% success against 77.5% for D to F in a prospective study (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC].

**Claim 4: guided surgery (static guide, dynamic navigation) is the next step.** A 2026 review of 33 studies, 31 of them laboratory or cadaver, found static and navigated guidance more accurate than freehand; dynamic navigation had greater resection-angle deviation than static guides; clinical evidence was minimal (Rewthamrongsris et al, 2026) [LIT, abstract].

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: ~80 minutes)*

### 1. Definition and introduction
Endodontic microsurgery is periradicular surgery performed under the operating microscope with ultrasonic root-end preparation, microinstruments and biocompatible root-end filling materials. It rests on the **microsurgical triad of magnification, illumination and instruments** (Gopikrishna, 2021). It replaced "traditional root-end surgery" because the older technique left isthmuses and ramifications untreated and sealed a beveled root end poorly (Floratos and Kim, 2017).

### 2. Objectives and rationale
1. Remove the cause: infected apical ramifications, isthmus, extraradicular infection.
2. See what failed: inspect the cut root face.
3. Seal every portal of exit with a biocompatible material.
4. Minimise tissue trauma: small osteotomy, atraumatic flap, faster healing (Floratos and Kim, 2017; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).

### 3. Traditional versus microsurgical technique
| Feature | Traditional | Microsurgery |
|---|---|---|
| Osteotomy | 8-10 mm | 3-4 mm |
| Bevel | 45-65 degrees | 0-10 degrees |
| Inspection of cut face | None | Always |
| Isthmus | Impossible to find | Always found and treated |
| Root-end preparation | Seldom inside canal | Always inside canal, 3 mm |
| Instrument | Bur | Ultrasonic tip |
| Filling | Amalgam | MTA or bioceramic |
| Sutures | 4-0 silk | 5-0 or 6-0 monofilament |
| Suture removal | 7 days | 2-3 days |
| 1-year healing | 40-90% | 85-97% |
(Floratos and Kim, 2017, after Kim and Kratchman)

### 4. Armamentarium
- **Magnification and illumination:** operating microscope (x2.5-8 orientation, x8-14 surgery, x14-30 detail), coaxial light, beam splitter for documentation; endoscope as alternative (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Examination:** micromirrors (1-5 mm; rhodium coated), CX-1 microexplorer, periodontal probe.
- **Incision and elevation:** microscalpels or 15c blade, mini Molt curettes, periosteal elevators.
- **Retraction:** KP or Rubinstein retractors resting on bone.
- **Osteotomy and resection:** Impact Air 45-degree handpiece, Lindemann bur, or piezosurgery.
- **Curettage:** Columbia 13/14, Jacquette 34/35, Lucas 85.
- **Root-end preparation:** ultrasonic unit with stainless steel, diamond or zirconium nitride tips.
- **Irrigation and drying:** Stropko irrigator, microsuction.
- **Filling:** MTA carrier (MAP system), microplugger.
- **Hemostasis and sutures** (Gopikrishna, 2021; Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019; Niemczyk, 2010).

### 5. Procedure
1. **Planning:** CBCT shows lesion extent, cortical thickness, relation to canal, foramen, sinus, which can rule surgery in or out (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019). Kim's classes A-F grade cases; Class B is ideal.
2. **Anesthesia and hemostasis:** chlorhexidine rinse before the incision (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019); regional block with bupivacaine; slow lidocaine 1:50,000 infiltration (Niemczyk, 2010).
3. **Flap:** sulcular, papilla-base or Luebke-Ochsenbein; verticals 1.5-2 times longer (Q04).
4. **Osteotomy:** 3-4 mm window; locate apex with CBCT measurement, explorer or radiopaque marker.
5. **Curettage and biopsy:** every tissue sent for histology.
6. **Resection:** 3 mm, perpendicular, using the guide-slot technique; remove 93% of lateral canals and 98% of ramifications.
7. **Inspection:** stain with methylene blue, view with micromirror at 45 degrees; look for gaps, isthmus, cracks, missed canals.
8. **Preparation:** ultrasonic Class I cavity 3 mm deep along the canal, including isthmus; light touch; check with micromirror; remove debris from the facial wall.
9. **Drying:** Stropko microtip.
10. **Filling:** MTA or bioceramic, condensed, finished.
11. **Closure:** compress about 5 minutes; fine sutures; ice for 24 hours; remove sutures at 2-3 days.

### 5A. Key steps in more detail
**Ergonomics and positioning.** The position of the root apex, not the patient, sets the setup: patient supine to slight Trendelenburg, operator at the 11-12 o'clock position, elbows close to the body, microscope axis perpendicular to the flap. For the resection the microscope visual axis must be parallel to the long axis of the root at the resection level; a skewed view produces a skewed cut. For posterior teeth the patient may need to lie on the side (rolled towels, donut headrest) so the cortical plate is parallel to the floor (Niemczyk, 2010).

**Flap and retraction.** Microscalpels (sizes 1-5, Feather blades) and Rubinstein mini-Molt elevators (2 mm and 3.5 mm ends, against 3.5 and 7 mm for a standard Molt 2/4) lift the papilla without tearing; retractors are bent at 110 degrees to keep the hand out of the light path and serrated to avoid slipping near the nasal spine, canine eminence and mental nerve (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019).

**Osteotomy.** Impact Air 45 with a Lindemann H161/H162 bone cutter (9 mm, four flutes, less clogging); light brushstrokes starting slightly coronal to the root end, then "peck and check" with a DG16 explorer; or piezosurgery near the mental nerve, vessels or sinus (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019).

**Curettage.** Columbia 13/14 then Jacquette 34/35 to remove tissue at the root surface; Lucas 85 for larger lesions. All tissue goes for histology, never a clinical guess, and foreign material is removed (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019).

**Resection.** Bur used like slicing bread: the first passes make a guide slot, so length and angle can be corrected. Avoid coarse diamonds and crosscut burs, which leave a rough surface that makes the filling hard to finish. Do not use air-driven coolant (embolism risk). Check completeness: a jagged outline or "dog-eared" dentin means the root was snapped, not cut (Niemczyk, 2010). If the root is sharply curved at 3 mm, the resection is lengthened or shortened so the 3 mm tip does not run into the curve (Niemczyk, 2010).

**Inspection.** Dry with a Stropko irrigator, stain with 1% methylene blue, view with a micromirror at 45 degrees at x14-25. Findings: gap in filling, untreated canal, isthmus (partial or complete), crack. "Radial dentin" points to a canal when the dye does not show it, so the surgeon prepares along it or resects slightly more (Floratos and Kim, 2017; Niemczyk, 2010).

**Ultrasonic preparation.** Begin with the lowest power and raise it until the cut is smooth and chatter-free (tip specificity: not every tip works in every unit). Cut a small groove on the buccal cortical plate parallel to the root's long axis as a visual guide. In a root with two canals joined by an isthmus, prepare each canal first, then the isthmus at the same angle. Diamond-coated tips are used last because they are efficient at removing gutta-percha and do not significantly crack the root end; coated tips are best when new (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019; Niemczyk, 2010). Gilheany: a 3.5 mm deep preparation, measured radiographically, allows for errors of vertical angulation (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019). Longer tips (Endo Success 3, 6, 9 mm) clean up to 9 mm when a post was placed without orthograde treatment (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019).

**Hemostasis in the crypt.** Ferric sulfate: heme-agglutination, effective, but all remnants must be removed and it should not be used near the mandibular nerve, mental foramen, sinus or nasal floor. Hemodette (buffered 20% aluminium chloride gel): similar action without the side effects, blue and easily rinsed. ActCel and HemCon: gauze that turns to gel. Avitene: effective but costly. CollaPlug or CollaTape: clotting lasts only minutes after removal (Niemczyk, 2010).

**Filling.** Dry the cavity; place MTA with a carrier (MAP syringe or a wipe-on block); direct a gentle stream of air over the surface for 20-30 seconds so it firms, then wipe away excess with a microbrush; rinse gently away from the filling (Niemczyk, 2010).

**Closure and aftercare.** Compress for about 5 minutes to express blood and begin attachment; interrupted or sling sutures; ice 10-15 minutes on, 5 off for 24 hours; sutures out at 1-3 days (Niemczyk, 2010).

### 5B. Why the bevel and depth matter (Gilheany et al)
| Bevel | Minimum retrograde filling depth for a good seal |
|---|---|
| 0 degrees | 1 mm |
| 30 degrees | 2.1 mm |
| 45 degrees | 2.5 mm |
Leakage increased with the slope of the bevel and decreased with filling depth; a 3.5 mm radiographic depth allows for angulation error. A 3 mm circumferential preparation along the long axis, including the isthmus, therefore meets the criterion for every case (Niemczyk, 2010; Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019).

### 5C. Case selection (Kim's classes) and when not to operate
- **A** no lesion, symptoms only. **B** small lesion, normal pocket, no mobility (ideal). **C** large lesion, no pocket. **D** large lesion with deep pocket. **E** large lesion with endo-perio communication to the apex. **F** apical lesion with total loss of the buccal plate, no mobility (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Classes A-C are mainly endodontic; D-F carry a periodontal component and a lower success rate (95.2% against 77.5%) (Floratos and Kim, 2017).
- CBCT can show that surgery is impractical: for example, a mandibular molar root lying against the lingual cortical plate, or an untreated canal that nonsurgical retreatment should address first (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019).

### 6. Advantages
Smaller osteotomy and faster healing; less bone loss; inspection; isthmus treatment; better seal; less patient pain; higher success (Floratos and Kim, 2017; von Arx and White, 2019). Patients report less pain but more difficulty with mouth opening, chewing and speech after the modern technique (von Arx and White, 2019).

### 7. Disadvantages and limits
Cost of equipment; steep learning curve; ergonomic difficulty (77% of surveyed endodontists reported difficulty with access and visualisation); longer surgery with piezosurgery; technique-sensitive materials; no randomised trial isolating the microscope (Niemczyk, 2010; Gutmann, 2017; von Arx and White, 2019).

### 8. Outcome
- Microsurgery 94% against traditional 59% (Setzer et al, 2010).
- Microscope or endoscope 94% against loupes 88%; difference significant for molars (Setzer et al, 2012).
- Ultrasonic and calcium-silicate or Super-EBA/IRM 94.4% against resin 82.2% (Kohli et al, 2018).
- Long-term: 91.5% at 5-7 years in one account; pooled 91.3% in randomised trials and 78.4% in prospective studies in a review (Floratos and Kim, 2017; Pinto et al, 2020).
- Kim's classes A-C 95.2%; D-F 77.5%.

### 9. Recent advances
CBCT planning; piezosurgery; bioceramic root repair materials; papilla-preserving flaps; endoscopes; static-guide and dynamic-navigation surgery (laboratory evidence only) (Rewthamrongsris et al, 2026); regenerative adjuncts (see Q01).

### 10. Conclusion
The gain comes from a whole technique, not one gadget: small osteotomy, flat cut, inspection, ultrasonic preparation of the whole canal and isthmus, and a biocompatible seal. It should be the standard whenever surgery is chosen; but surgery remains second to nonsurgical retreatment where retreatment is feasible, and the figures quoted should always be tied to the study design.

- **Microscope vs loupes:** loupes about X3.5 to X4.5; microscope variable power, coaxial light, does not improve access (Rubinstein (Ch.7), 2019).
- **Papilla-base flap** needs 7-0 sutures and at least X4.3 magnification (Rubinstein (Ch.7), 2019).
