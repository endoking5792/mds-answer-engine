## STUDY MODE

**The decision.** After failed root canal treatment the options are nonsurgical retreatment, apical microsurgery, or extraction with replacement by a bridge or implant. The usual first line is nonsurgical retreatment (von Arx and White, 2019) [SRC]; surgery is considered when retreatment is impractical or unlikely to help (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Lieblich notes that clinical studies have not shown retreatment to be more successful than surgery, and that retreatment, with post removal and further tooth loss, adds fracture risk (Lieblich, 2012) [SRC].

**What the evidence says.** Cohen's states that, in broad terms and under ideal conditions, the prognoses for nonsurgical retreatment, surgery and implant placement should be roughly equal, and that the choice rests on evidence, clinical judgment and patient preference (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. A systematic review compared endodontic microsurgery with implant-supported single crowns: the quality of studies was moderate, the two treatments had never been compared directly, outcome criteria were unclear and success could not be compared; at 4 to 6 years implants had higher survival than microsurgery-treated teeth, but both were high (Torabinejad et al, 2015) [LIT, abstract]. Lieblich points out that implants can outlast tooth-supported restorations and that severe periodontal bone loss, especially with furcation involvement, favours extraction and an implant (Lieblich, 2012) [SRC]. The old figures of 60 to 70% for surgery have risen to above 90% with ultrasonic preparation and MTA (Lieblich, 2012) [SRC]. The two ways of reading the evidence are compatible: implant survival is somewhat higher, while microsurgery survival is also high, and survival is not the same as success.

**Cost.** One study cited in Cohen's found endodontic microsurgery the most cost-effective option for persistent periapical disease, compared with retreatment, a fixed partial denture and a single implant (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. This is a single study quoted second-hand.

**Case factors.** Lieblich's list of factors for success includes a dense orthograde filling, healthy periodontium, a lesion confined to the apical third and a maxillary incisor or mesiobuccal root of a maxillary molar; factors for failure include a fracture, poor filling, leakage of crown or post, poor periodontal condition, and a mandibular incisor (Lieblich, 2012) [SRC]. If several adverse factors exist, extraction and an implant are more predictable (Lieblich, 2012) [SRC].

**Shared decision.** Comparative probabilities are difficult to project because many variables are complex and not easily quantified, and definitions of success vary (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's recommends shared decision making (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC].

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: ~16 minutes)*

### Options after failed root canal treatment
1. Nonsurgical retreatment (usual first line) (von Arx and White, 2019).
2. Apical microsurgery.
3. Extraction with an implant or fixed prosthesis.

### When surgery rather than retreatment
When retreatment would need removal of a post or a recently placed restoration, for example disassembling a bridge abutment, it may be technically possible but not economically feasible, and surgery becomes the more reasonable choice (Johnson et al, 2021; Lieblich, 2012).

### Points in favour of microsurgery
- Keeps the natural tooth and its supporting tissues.
- Success above 90% with ultrasonic preparation and MTA; the older 60 to 70% has been overtaken (Lieblich, 2012).
- No need to remove a post or crown that would risk fracture (Lieblich, 2012).
- Might be the most cost-effective option in one study (Johnson et al, 2021).
- Gives tissue for histology (Lieblich, 2012).

### Points in favour of extraction and implant
- Single implants have higher survival than microsurgery-treated teeth at 4 to 6 years (Torabinejad et al, 2015).
- Preferred when periodontal loss is severe, a fracture is present, or several adverse factors combine (Lieblich, 2012).
- Implants can outlast tooth-supported restorations (Lieblich, 2012).

### Factors for success of surgery (Lieblich, 2012)
- Dense orthograde filling, healthy periodontium, lesion confined to the apical third, maxillary incisor or mesiobuccal root of a maxillary molar.
- Postoperative bone fill, resolution of pain and loss of sinus tract.

### Factors for failure (Lieblich, 2012)
- Fracture, poor or absent orthograde filling, leaking crown or post, furcation involvement or poor periodontal condition, mandibular incisor.

### Evidence
- Direct comparisons are lacking; the two are not interchangeable endpoints (survival of an implant against healing of a tooth). Both survival rates are high (Torabinejad et al, 2015).
- Under ideal conditions the prognoses are roughly equal (Johnson et al, 2021).

### Decision
Decide by shared decision making: the evidence, tooth-specific factors (restorability, periodontal status, fracture risk), cost and the patient's wishes (Johnson et al, 2021).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
