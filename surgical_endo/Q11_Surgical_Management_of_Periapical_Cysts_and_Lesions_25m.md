## STUDY MODE

**What the lesion is.** A radicular (periapical) cyst is an inflammatory cyst lined by nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium that arises from the epithelial rests of Malassez or Serres in response to long-standing infection of a necrotic pulp, and it is surrounded by a dense fibrocollagenous wall (Said-Al-Naief, 2019) [SRC]. Reported frequency varies widely, from 7% to 54% of apical lesions in one chapter, 22% in another and 36.7% in a large series, because of sampling and histologic criteria (Sedgley et al, 2021; Said-Al-Naief, 2019) [SRC]. Studies using whole serially sectioned specimens report fewer cysts than older studies (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]. Variants are the lateral radicular cyst and the residual cyst left after extraction (Said-Al-Naief, 2019) [SRC].

**True and pocket cysts.** A pocket (bay) cyst opens into the root canal through the apical foramen; a true cyst is completely enclosed by epithelium (Sedgley et al, 2021; Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]. The textbooks differ on why this matters, and the difference runs between chapter authors, not only between books.

> **SOURCE CONFLICT, true cysts.** *Roda, Gettleman and Johnson* (Cohen's Ch.10): true cysts, being self-sustaining, probably do not heal after nonsurgical treatment and usually need enucleation. *Johnson, Fayad and Berman* (Cohen's Ch.11): a true cyst may not be expected to resolve after nonsurgical treatment, so surgery is indicated. *Landwehr* (Cohen's Ch.3): healing potential of true cysts may be limited after conventional treatment. *Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad* (Principles and Practice Ch.22): about 60% of cysts in one study were true cysts, more likely to heal only after extraction or surgery. *Lin and Huang* (Cohen's Ch.16) say both: true cysts are less likely to heal and need surgery (outcomes paragraph), but may regress like pocket cysts by apoptosis (wound-healing section), with no direct evidence either way. *Sedgley, Silva and Fouad* (Principles and Practice Ch.1): the hypothesis that true cysts need enucleation has never had direct evidence, and cysts and granulomas heal equally well once irritants are controlled. *Seltzer and Bender* (Ch.16): true cysts must be surgically removed whereas bay cysts may heal after nonsurgical treatment, and true cysts are probably under 10% of lesions (Seltzer and Bender, 2012) [SRC]. *Ørstavik* (Endodontic Radiology Ch.16): a true cyst may be resistant to conventional root canal treatment and need surgical extirpation (Ørstavik, 2012) [SRC]. *Siqueira* (Treatment of Endodontic Infections Ch.4): the claim that true cysts do not respond to root canal treatment is only speculative with no scientific evidence, no true cyst was found in a series of 24 treatment failures, and the distinction between true and bay cysts seems to be of no clinical relevance (Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. I have not resolved this; the exam answer should state the two positions and the absence of direct evidence.
>
> **SOURCE CONFLICT, biopsy.** *Gulabivala and Ng* (Ch.9): routine histopathology is accepted as the standard of care, and recovered tissue must go into 10% formalin with a full request form (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. *Lieblich* (2012) records the open controversy over whether every surgically treated periapical lesion should be biopsied (Lieblich, 2012) [SRC].

**Diagnosis.** A cyst cannot be told from a granuloma clinically or on an ordinary radiograph, although lesions of 200 mm2 or more are more likely to be cysts (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC]; CBCT may help (Said-Al-Naief, 2019) [SRC]. The tooth is nonvital, with an asymptomatic swelling at times (Said-Al-Naief, 2019) [SRC]. Look-alikes include the odontogenic keratocyst, the most common lesion that mimics periapical pathology, nasopalatine duct cyst and unicystic ameloblastoma (Said-Al-Naief, 2019; Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Lieblich reports a cystic ameloblastoma that was found in a lesion on a tooth with vital pulp (Lieblich, 2012) [SRC].

**Step 1: nonsurgical (full account in Q12).** Landwehr states that treatment of cysts and granulomas is identical: remove the stimulus by root canal treatment or extraction (Landwehr, 2021) [SRC]. Radicular cysts are best managed by root canal therapy and/or extraction with conservative excision, and root canal treatment is followed by surgery if the lesion fails to resolve (Said-Al-Naief, 2019) [SRC]. Persistence after treatment has five biologic causes: intraradicular infection, extraradicular infection, a true cyst, foreign body reaction and scar (Said-Al-Naief, 2019) [SRC]; so causes outside the canal (extraradicular infection, a true cyst, a foreign body reaction, scar) cannot be cured from the crown, and Cohen's names a true cyst as an indication for excisional biopsy and surgery (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Step 2: surgical options.** (a) *Decompression.* A very large cyst that does not heal after root canal treatment can be exposed surgically, root-end surgery done and a polyethylene tube left for several weeks so that the cyst communicates with the mouth and the cyst wall collapses; in the case shown bone had regenerated at one year (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; Ingle's lists decompression among fistulative procedures (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. The recent evidence is thin and mostly from oral surgery. A retrospective cohort of 40 patients with mandibular odontogenic cysts (10 radicular) found mean CBCT volume fell from 3764 to 1413 mm3 after decompression, about 5.2% a month, with no difference between cyst types, and called decompression an effective first-stage treatment before enucleation (Raabe et al, 2026) [LIT, abstract]. In 39 patients decompressed for a mean of 7.9 months the histologic diagnosis was unchanged in 16 of 17 radicular cysts, so the biopsy taken before decompression was reliable (Kader et al, 2024) [LIT, abstract]. In 59 mandibular cysts over 3 cm, marsupialisation and enucleation gave similar trabecular healing at 12 months on fractal analysis; large cysts healed faster after marsupialisation, cysts over 5.5 cm took longer, and the centre of the cyst healed last (Kaygisiz and Karsli, 2024) [LIT, abstract]. In endodontics there is a case report of a large periapical lesion treated by aspiration, irrigation and a Penrose drain after nonsurgical treatment failed, with root-end surgery at 3.5 years and complete three-dimensional healing at 4.5 years (Wang et al, 2024) [LIT, abstract; single case]. None of these is a randomised trial, and the series mix cyst types, so they support decompression as a reasonable first stage for large cysts without proving superiority over enucleation [Likely]. (b) *Enucleation with root-end surgery.* The standard approach. Enucleation and curettage create the working space to reach the apex (Barnes, 1984) [SRC]. Resolution of a simple cyst can be expected if the epithelial sac is broken up, most of the epithelium removed, and the apical seal stops the toxic stimulus (Barnes, 1984) [SRC]. Harty's adds that every remnant of reactive tissue need not be removed, because the periphery contains fibroblasts, vessels and bone matrix (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's makes the same point (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC].

**Technique points for a large lesion.** Place the incision at least 5 mm clear of the bony defect (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]; a large lesion often has a larger bone defect than the radiograph shows. Peel the tissue from the crypt wall with the concave surface of the curette against bone, then scrape (Johnson et al, 2021; Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. The centre of the lesion may remain sensitive despite anaesthesia and needs local infiltration (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Protect adjacent roots and their blood supply, the antrum, mental foramen and canal; sometimes tissue is left in place (Gutmann, 2017) [SRC]. Barnes advises antibiotics before enucleating a large lesion (Barnes, 1984) [SRC]. Evidence on antibiotics is, however, not settled (Tsesis et al, 2025) [SRC]. All tissue goes for histology (Johnson et al, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Lieblich notes guidelines under which submission can be omitted, and that distinguishing granuloma from cyst does not change outcome, so that distinction is not a reason to send tissue (Lieblich, 2012) [SRC].

**Grafts and membranes.** Pecora and colleagues' randomised trial of 20 lesions of at least 10 mm found that lesions covered with an e-PTFE membrane healed quicker and with better bone quality at 3 to 12 months than uncovered controls (Pecora et al, 1995) [LIT, abstract]. Defects of Kim and Kratchman class D to F, apicomarginal defects (healing reduced by about 20%) and lesions of 15 mm or more are the cases where grafting and a membrane are considered (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC]. A resorbable membrane gave no benefit when the buccal bone over the root was intact (Torabinejad et al, 2019) [SRC]. See Q09.

**Healing.** Healing of small lesions is quicker: under 5 mm took 6.4 months on average and 5 to 10 mm about 7 months (Floratos and Kim, 2017) [SRC]. A film at 3 months and 6 months, then yearly, is the usual follow-up (Lieblich, 2012) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim: a true periapical cyst needs surgery.** *Textbooks:* Cohen's and the outcomes chapter say true cysts are less likely to heal nonsurgically (Johnson et al, 2021; Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC]; the pathogenesis chapter says no direct evidence supports this (Sedgley et al, 2021) [SRC]. *Interpretation:* the idea is a plausible rationale, not a demonstrated fact, and since a true cyst cannot be diagnosed before biopsy the practical rule is the same for granuloma and cyst: control the infection first, operate if the lesion does not heal, and send the tissue [Likely]. *Limitation:* I searched PubMed for decompression and for radicular cysts but found no trial directly testing it, so the textbook disagreement remains.

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: ~40 minutes)*

### Introduction
A radicular (periapical) cyst is an inflammatory cyst that arises from the epithelial rests of Malassez in a periapical lesion of a tooth with a necrotic pulp. It is lined by nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium and has a dense fibrocollagenous wall (Said-Al-Naief, 2019). Reported frequency varies from about 7% to 54% of apical lesions, because sampling and histologic criteria differ (Sedgley et al, 2021).

### Pathogenesis
Three stages are described: dormant epithelial rests of Malassez proliferate in response to growth factors; the cystic epithelium becomes well defined; the cyst then grows by osmotic pressure and other molecular events, with inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6) and bone-metabolism factors that favour osteolysis contributing to expansion (Said-Al-Naief, 2019).

### Types
- **Pocket (bay) cyst:** the lumen opens into the root canal through the apical foramen; it has a good chance of healing after root canal treatment.
- **True cyst:** completely enclosed by epithelium with no communication with the canal; may not heal after nonsurgical treatment, so surgery or extraction may be needed (Landwehr, 2021; Setzer et al, 2021).
- Variants: lateral radicular cyst and residual cyst (Said-Al-Naief, 2019).
- Direct proof that true cysts need surgery is lacking, and cysts and granulomas heal equally well once the irritants are controlled (Sedgley et al, 2021).

### Diagnosis
- Nonvital tooth, sometimes a painless swelling or mobility; well-defined radiolucency with or without a corticated border (Said-Al-Naief, 2019).
- Lesions of 200 mm2 or more are more likely to be cysts; CBCT helps; a cyst cannot be separated from a granuloma without histology (Setzer et al, 2021; Said-Al-Naief, 2019).
- Rule out mimics: odontogenic keratocyst, nasopalatine duct cyst, unicystic ameloblastoma (Said-Al-Naief, 2019; Gutmann, 2017).

### Management
**1. Nonsurgical treatment first**
- Root canal treatment or retreatment removes the stimulus; cysts and granulomas are treated alike (Landwehr, 2021).
- Surgery follows if the lesion fails to resolve (Said-Al-Naief, 2019).

**2. Indications for surgery**
- Failure to heal after good treatment; extraradicular cause; true cyst suspected; large lesion; biopsy needed (Johnson et al, 2021; Said-Al-Naief, 2019).

**3. Surgical methods**
- *Decompression:* for a very large cyst. After surgical exposure and root-end surgery a polyethylene tube is left for several weeks so that the cavity communicates with the mouth and the wall collapses (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002). In a cohort of mandibular jaw cysts volume fell about 5.2% a month, and histology after decompression was unchanged in 16 of 17 radicular cysts; the evidence is retrospective (Raabe et al, 2026; Kader et al, 2024). *Gulabivala and Ng* (Ch.9) treat decompression and marsupialisation as the same procedure: a flanged cannula keeps the opening patent for daily irrigation by the patient, and the procedure reduces the risk of damage to adjacent vital teeth and anatomical structures (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].
- *Enucleation with apicoectomy:* the standard method.
  - Local anaesthesia; flap with the incision at least 5 mm clear of the bony defect (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019).
  - Osteotomy; peel the sac from the bony wall with a sharp curette, concave side to bone (Johnson et al, 2021).
  - Resect 3 mm of the root, prepare with ultrasonic tip and fill with MTA (see Q06 to Q08).
- Resolution is expected if the epithelial sac is broken up, most epithelium removed and the apex sealed against the root canal stimulus (Barnes, 1984). Every remnant need not be removed (Gutmann, 2017).

**4. Pre-operative planning and precautions**
- CBCT shows the true extent of the lesion and its relation to the sinus, canal and neighbouring roots (Said-Al-Naief, 2019; Evans et al, 2012).
- Test the vitality of adjacent teeth before surgery, because a large lesion can involve their roots and pulpal blood supply (Gutmann, 2017).
- Warn the patient that the operation is exploratory and may change (Lieblich, 2012).
- Protect adjacent roots and their blood supply, the maxillary sinus, mental foramen and inferior alveolar canal; tissue may be left on a structure at risk (Gutmann, 2017).
- Antibiotic cover before enucleating a large lesion is advised in older text, but evidence is unsettled (Barnes, 1984; Tsesis et al, 2025).

**5. Histology**
- Send all removed tissue for histology (Gopikrishna, 2021). Omission is allowed only under documented guidelines in clearly typical cases (Lieblich, 2012).

**6. Bone graft and membrane**
- Considered for Kim and Kratchman classes D to F, apicomarginal defects and lesions of 15 mm or more: resorbable membrane extending 2 to 3 mm beyond the crypt, with graft support (Johnson et al, 2021). A trial of lesions of 10 mm or more found faster healing under an e-PTFE membrane (Pecora et al, 1995).
- No benefit if the buccal bone is intact (Torabinejad et al, 2019).

### Post-operative care and complications
- Compress the flap for at least 3 minutes, give written instructions, review the sutures (Rubinstein et al, 2021).
- Swelling is greatest on the first and second days; hematoma, sinus communication and nerve injury (mostly second mandibular molars) are the main complications (Tsesis et al, 2025).
- Keep the root apex and filling material out of the sinus (Tsesis et al, 2025).

### Healing and follow-up
- Radiograph at 3 and 6 months, then yearly (Lieblich, 2012).
- Lesions under 5 mm heal in about 6.4 months, 5 to 10 mm in about 7 months, and larger lesions take longer (Floratos and Kim, 2017).
- Healing by scar is possible and is accepted if symptom-free (Rubinstein and Fayad, 2019).

### Prognosis
Worse with apicomarginal defects (healing reduced by about 20% or more) and lesions of 15 mm or more (Johnson et al, 2021).

### Critical point
Textbook authors disagree on whether a true cyst needs surgery (Roda et al, Johnson et al and Setzer et al say it is likely; Sedgley et al say there is no direct evidence; Lin and Huang say both). The safe approach is to treat the canal first, operate if healing fails and always examine the tissue.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
Pocket versus true cyst; 200 mm2; decompression tube; enucleation plus apicoectomy; sac integrity broken, epithelium removed, apex sealed; send tissue for histology; classes D to F need graft and membrane.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW
Pocket cyst (lumen open to the apical foramen) beside a true cyst (closed sac), and the decompression tube.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Describing only apicoectomy and omitting decompression.
- Not distinguishing true from pocket cyst.
- Forgetting biopsy and look-alike lesions.
- Not saying that nonsurgical treatment comes first.
