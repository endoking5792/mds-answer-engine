## STUDY MODE

**The rationale.** Apical periodontitis is an infectious disease whose bacteria sit mainly in the root canal and not in the periapical tissue (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Kakehashi's classic study showed that surgically exposed pulps caused necrosis and periradicular inflammation in conventional rats but not in germ-free rats; in humans, traumatized teeth with necrotic but uninfected pulps showed no periapical bone destruction, whereas infected ones did (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Newly introduced, persistent or recurrent microorganisms are therefore the primary cause of endodontic disease (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC]. If the cause is the canal, the cure is to disinfect and seal the canal, which is the whole logic of endodontic treatment and of treating periapical lesions conservatively. The converse is the basis for surgery: periradicular surgery is indicated when nonsurgical retreatment is impractical or unlikely to improve the situation (Johnson et al, 2021) [SRC].

**How healing follows.** Once irritants are eliminated, inflammatory mediators fall and the lesion is cleared by activated macrophages, which Lin and Huang call biologic debridement (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Granulation tissue forms, necrotic tissue and dead bacteria are removed, and tissue is restored mostly by regeneration and partly by repair (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Periodontal ligament cells, endosteal and periosteal osteoprogenitor cells regenerate cementum, bone and PDL; this resembles guided tissue regeneration in periodontics because both remove irritants and create a favourable microenvironment (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. What switches inflammation off is poorly understood (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Healing is covered in Q13.

**What conservative management consists of.** (1) Diagnosis, including CBCT where needed. (2) Root canal treatment or retreatment with chemomechanical disinfection; interappointment calcium hydroxide may further reduce microorganisms (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC]. (3) A filling confined to the canal: a 94% healing rate was found when the filling lay within 0 to 2 mm of the apex, falling to 68% when underinstrumented by more than 2 mm; overinstrumentation and overfilling can cause foreign body reactions and carry bacteria into the tissue (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC]. (4) A coronal seal and good restoration (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC]. (5) Review.

**What conservative treatment can achieve.** The clear majority of root-filled teeth heal without further intervention (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC]. In clinical outcome studies teeth with apical periodontitis healed in 78% of cases after proper nonsurgical treatment (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Lesions with an abscess or a sinus tract heal after nonsurgical treatment without systemic antimicrobials (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Chronic lesions with reactive bone formation (focal condensing osteitis) mostly heal and the dense bone remodels (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. Nonsurgical retreatment is the first choice for most failures; a pooled success was 77.2%, falling to 65% where apical periodontitis was present and 41% for lesions over 5 mm, though follow-up may have been too short for the large ones (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's and Harty's agree that retreatment is the first modality and that a high-quality nonsurgical treatment does better than a poor one (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019; Gutmann, 2017) [SRC].

**Cysts and conservative management.** A cyst is an inflammatory outgrowth of a granuloma (granulomas may, but do not always, develop into cysts) (Said-Al-Naief, 2019) [SRC]; it cannot be told from a granuloma radiographically or clinically (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC]. Radicular cysts are best managed by root canal treatment with or without conservative excision, and surgery follows if the lesion does not resolve (Said-Al-Naief, 2019) [SRC]. The textbooks disagree about true cysts, which are enclosed in epithelium and lack a connection to the canal.

> **SOURCE CONFLICT, true cysts.** *Roda, Gettleman and Johnson* (Cohen's Ch.10): true cysts, because they are self-sustaining, probably do not heal after nonsurgical treatment and usually need enucleation. *Setzer, Chogle and Torabinejad* (Principles and Practice Ch.22): true cysts, about 60% of cysts in one study, are more likely to heal only after extraction or surgery. *Lin and Huang* (Cohen's Ch.16) say both things: in the outcomes paragraph that true cysts are less likely to heal and need surgery, and in the wound-healing section that true cysts, like pocket cysts, may regress by apoptosis once infection is controlled, while stating that no direct evidence exists either way. *Sedgley, Silva and Fouad* (Principles and Practice Ch.1): no direct evidence has ever supported the hypothesis, and cysts and granulomas heal equally well once irritants are controlled. *Seltzer and Bender* (Ch.16): true cysts must be surgically removed whereas bay cysts may heal after nonsurgical treatment, and true cysts are probably under 10% of lesions (Seltzer and Bender, 2012) [SRC]. *Ørstavik* (Endodontic Radiology Ch.16): a true cyst may be resistant to conventional root canal treatment and need surgical extirpation (Ørstavik, 2012) [SRC]. *Siqueira* (Treatment of Endodontic Infections Ch.4): the claim that true cysts do not respond to root canal treatment is only speculative with no scientific evidence, no true cyst was found in a series of 24 treatment failures, and the distinction between true and bay cysts seems to be of no clinical relevance (Siqueira, 2022) [SRC]. I have not resolved this; see Q11.

**Large cysts.** For a very large cyst that does not heal, surgical exposure with root-end surgery and a polyethylene drainage tube for several weeks has been described (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; recent decompression literature is in Q11.

**Factors that modify healing.** Diabetes reduces the likelihood of healing; immunocompromised patients such as those with HIV responded as well as others (Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. A lesion that is unchanged or larger after 1 year is unlikely to resolve; if it is smaller at 6 months it is healing and review continues; larger lesions take longer; late healing and late failure both occur (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim: nonsurgical treatment should be the first approach even for large lesions with possible cysts.** *Textbook:* all sources place nonsurgical treatment first (Setzer et al, 2021; Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019) [SRC]. *Evidence in the textbooks:* healing of 78% of apical periodontitis cases after proper treatment (Lin and Huang, 2021), but only 41% for retreatment of lesions over 5 mm (Setzer et al, 2021) [SRC], and the texts disagree on true cysts. *Limitation:* these are pooled figures from different eras and criteria, and the cyst argument rests on theory rather than direct evidence (Sedgley et al, 2021; Lin and Huang, 2021) [SRC]. *Conclusion:* disinfect first and reassess at 6 to 12 months, then operate if the lesion is not shrinking; this is the common position of the sources and the more cautious reading of the cyst evidence [Likely].

## EXAM MODE
*(Estimated writing time: ~40 minutes)*

### Introduction
Apical periodontitis is an inflammatory reaction in the periapical tissue caused chiefly by infection of the root canal, so the rationale of endodontic treatment is to remove the infection and seal the canal. When it succeeds, the periapical lesion heals without surgery, which is conservative management (Lin and Huang, 2021; Setzer et al, 2021).

### Scientific basis
- Germ-free rats with exposed pulps showed no periapical inflammation, whereas conventional rats did (Kakehashi) (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Necrotic but uninfected pulps in traumatized human teeth gave no bone destruction; infected ones did (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Bacteria, toxins and metabolic by-products escape through the foramen and stimulate the host response; bacteria are mainly in the canal, not in the periapical tissue (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Newly introduced, persistent or recurrent microorganisms are the primary cause of endodontic disease (Setzer et al, 2021).

### Objectives of treatment
1. Eliminate or entomb microorganisms: chemomechanical cleaning, irrigation, medicament.
2. Seal the canal apically and coronally.
3. Provide an environment where the periapical tissue can heal (Setzer et al, 2021).

### How lesions heal after nonsurgical treatment
- Irritants removed; inflammatory mediators fall; macrophages clear the lesion (biologic debridement).
- Granulation tissue; regeneration, and some repair, of bone, cementum and PDL; similar to guided tissue regeneration (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Cysts must regress for the original architecture to return (Lin and Huang, 2021).

### Steps in conservative management
1. **Diagnosis:** history, tests, periapical radiographs, CBCT when needed.
2. **Root canal treatment or retreatment:** coronal access, complete cleaning and shaping, irrigation; calcium hydroxide between visits may help (Setzer et al, 2021).
3. **Obturation within the canal:** filling within 0 to 2 mm of the apex gave 94% healing, against 68% when more than 2 mm short; overfilling risks a foreign body reaction (Setzer et al, 2021).
4. **Coronal seal and restoration.**
5. **Review:** at 6 months, 1 year and later.

### Types of lesion and response
| Lesion | Expected response to conservative treatment |
|---|---|
| Abscess or sinus tract | Heals without systemic antibiotics (Lin and Huang, 2021) |
| Granuloma | Heals |
| Pocket cyst | Likely heals |
| True cyst | Disputed (see below) |
| Condensing osteitis | Heals; bone remodels (Lin and Huang, 2021) |

### Results
- 78% healing of apical periodontitis after proper treatment (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Retreatment: pooled success 77.2%, 65% with apical periodontitis, 41% for lesions over 5 mm (Setzer et al, 2021).
- Retreatment is the first choice for most failures (Setzer et al, 2021).

### True cysts: authors disagree
- Roda et al and Setzer et al: true cysts probably need surgery.
- Lin and Huang: they may regress, with no direct evidence either way.
- Sedgley et al: no direct evidence that they need surgery.
- Practical rule: treat the canal, review, operate if no healing.

### Large cyst
Surgical exposure with root-end surgery and a drainage tube left for weeks (decompression) (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

### Why conservative treatment comes first
- Ingle's states that the primary modality for treatment failure should be nonsurgical retreatment, and Harty's reports a higher success rate when the nonsurgical treatment is of high quality (Torabinejad, Sabeti and Glickman, 2019; Gutmann, 2017).
- The Royal College guidelines note that only two randomised trials exist comparing retreatment with surgery, so the choice is not settled by trials (Evans et al, 2012). Lieblich finds retreatment no more successful than surgery, but retreatment adds fracture risk when posts must be removed (Lieblich, 2012).
- Surgery debrides quickly, but it seals the infection inside the canal rather than removing it, and may carry a higher risk of late failure (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Surgery is more invasive and costly; therefore a lesion is treated conservatively unless retreatment is impractical or unlikely to help (Johnson et al, 2021).

### Causes of a periapical lesion that conservative treatment must remove
- Microbes and their toxins, chemical agents, mechanical irritation, foreign bodies and trauma are exogenous causes; cholesterol and urate crystals are endogenous (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Apical periodontitis can exist as inflammation without infection: inflammation is the reaction to irritants from the canal, infection is the presence of microbes in the periapical tissue (Lin and Huang, 2021).
- Extraradicular biofilm, for example Actinomyces, can resist treatment from the canal (Setzer et al, 2021).

### Size of lesion and cysts
Lesions of 200 mm2 or more are more likely to be cysts, but no test separates a cyst from a granuloma before biopsy (Setzer et al, 2021). Granulomas may, but do not always, develop into cysts (Said-Al-Naief, 2019).

### Evaluation of healing
- Healed, healing, nonhealed (functional).
- Lesion unchanged or larger at 1 year is unlikely to resolve; smaller at 6 months means healing continues; large lesions take longer; PAI may need 4 to 5 years (Setzer et al, 2021).

### When conservative management fails
Persistent lesion with a cause outside the canal (extraradicular infection, foreign body, true cyst, scar) or a canal that cannot be retreated: surgery (Johnson et al, 2021; Said-Al-Naief, 2019).

### Factors modifying outcome
Diabetes lowers the chance of healing; HIV patients responded as well (Lin and Huang, 2021). Lesion size, quality of the coronal seal, and the apical extent of the filling matter.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
Kakehashi; macrophage biologic debridement; filling within 0 to 2 mm gives 94%; 78% healing overall; retreatment 77.2%, 41% when over 5 mm; true cyst is disputed; review at 6 and 12 months.
