## 1. Introduction and definition

**Explanation.** The question "in how many visits?" is not a technical detail: it is a question about what root canal treatment is for. Siqueira and Rôças put the goal plainly: endodontic treatment of infected canals consists of eliminating the existing infection and preventing reinfection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC]. Single-visit treatment does both in one session (chemomechanical preparation, then immediate obturation); multiple-visit treatment inserts a period with an intracanal medicament between preparation and obturation. Sikri opens the chapter by admitting that the number of visits needed is not clearly documented, and that even the rationale for multiple visits is not established (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. *Endodontology* adds that for vital teeth, where infection is only superficial in the coronal pulp, immediate obturation after pulpectomy is generally accepted as the most appropriate treatment; the real debate concerns necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. That framing, that the debate is about the infected tooth and not the vital one, is the key to the whole answer.

#### ✎ Exam core
**Definition.** Single-visit endodontics (SVE) is the completion of root canal treatment, that is access, cleaning, shaping, disinfection and obturation (and, in retreatment, removal of the old filling), in one appointment (Baumann and Beer, 2010). Multiple-visit endodontics (MVE) completes the same steps over two or more appointments, with obturation at the last visit (Mergoni et al, 2022), usually with an interappointment intracanal medicament such as calcium hydroxide (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Why it matters.** The aim of root canal treatment is to eliminate infection from the canal system and prevent reinfection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a). The number of visits is debated because it decides whether that aim is achieved by instruments and irrigants alone (SVE) or by instruments, irrigants and time with a medicament (MVE).

**Scope of the debate.**
- Vital teeth (irreversible pulpitis): the canal is not infected; immediate obturation after pulpectomy is generally accepted (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis and retreatments: this is where the controversy lies (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a).

## 2. Historical background and the two schools of thought

**Explanation.** For most of the twentieth century multiple visits were the norm, and the reason was microbiological sampling: you obturated only when a canal culture was negative. Two Swedish studies by Sjögren and co-workers became the scientific backbone of the multiple-visit school. In 1991 they cultured single-rooted teeth after complete preparation and found bacteria remaining in more than half; after 7 days of calcium hydroxide no viable bacteria were detected, and they concluded that a dressing for at least 7 days was necessary (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. In 1997 they followed 55 teeth for 5 years: complete healing occurred in 94% of teeth that had a negative culture before filling and in only 68% of those with a positive culture (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Note what this second study does and does not show: it shows that residual bacteria at obturation matter; it does not itself compare one visit with two, because all teeth were filled in the same visit regardless of culture. *Endodontology* also notes that two failures occurred in the negative-culture group, one with bacteria later found in a lateral canal, so culture sensitivity was limited and the study should be read with caution (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

The single-visit school answers with the entombment idea: most bacteria are removed during preparation and irrigation, and the rest are "obscured or inhumed" in tubules by the filling, starved of nutrients and space, and further reduced by sealer and the zinc in gutta-percha (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Siqueira and Rôças accept that obturation denies nutrients and space, but add an important limit: above a certain bacterial threshold, and depending on where the residual infection sits, obturation cannot successfully entomb the bacteria (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC].

Practice has changed in parallel. Cohen's cites practitioner surveys: in 1977 completing vital cases in one visit was not even asked about; by the most recent survey 79% of respondents would complete vital cases in a single visit (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021) [SRC]. Weine, writing from long personal practice, makes a point of professional ethics: if one-sitting treatment is best for the operator it should be used in all applicable cases, but the choice should never be left to the patient (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Historical background.**
- Traditional practice: multiple visits, with obturation only after a negative canal culture.
- Sjögren et al (1991): bacteria remained in more than half of fully prepared canals; none were detected after a 7-day calcium hydroxide dressing; conclusion, a dressing of at least 7 days is needed (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Sjögren et al (1997): 5-year complete healing 94% with a negative culture before filling versus 68% with a positive culture (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Single-visit treatment was described almost 100 years ago; multiple visits later gained popularity, and with rotary nickel-titanium instruments single visit has regained acceptance for most cases (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Since the early 1980s single-visit treatment has become more acceptable, especially for vital pulps; in practitioner surveys the proportion completing vital cases in one visit rose to 79% (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021).

**Two schools of thought** (Baumann and Beer, 2010):
1. **Single-visit school:** preparation and irrigation remove most bacteria, and those left are entombed by a well-fitting filling placed at once; the "obscure or inhume" theory (Sikri, 2019).
2. **Multiple-visit school:** some bacteria almost always survive instrumentation, so an intracanal medicament between visits is needed to kill the residual flora before obturation.

**Traditional reasons for multiple visits, and the counter-arguments** (Sikri, 2019):
| Reason given | Counter-argument |
|---|---|
| Instrumentation and extruded debris cause postoperative pain | Better techniques, instruments and experience minimise irritation and extrusion |
| Single visit is believed to fail more often | Not true if cleaning and shaping are achieved without disturbing the periodontium |
| Lack of time in a busy practice | Multiple visits waste time on re-seating, re-isolation, removing temporaries and re-irrigating; overall time is saved in one visit |
| Interappointment dressing is needed | A dressing kills bacteria only for the time being; it does not prepare the canal |
| Fees cannot be the same for one visit | An assumption; patients will pay for time saved |

**Questions that a single-visit protocol must answer** (Sikri, 2019): Is the outcome the same? Is healing the same? Are failures and sequelae the same? Is it psychologically acceptable to the patient? Is it commercially viable?

**Limit of entombment.** Above a threshold bacterial load, and depending on where residual bacteria lie, obturation cannot entomb them (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a).

## 3. Biological basis: vital versus necrotic pulp

**Explanation.** The microbiology decides the protocol, and Harty's puts it most clearly. A tooth with irreversible pulpitis has no intraradicular infection: any bacteria are in the exposure or the coronal pulp, and the radicular pulp may be inflamed but is not infected; such teeth should be treated without delay, preferably in one visit, to minimise the risk of contaminating the canal (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017) [SRC]. A necrotic tooth with apical periodontitis is different: a mixed biofilm community lives in the main canal, isthmuses, ramifications and dentinal tubules. Gulabivala and Ng state that even rigorous disinfectant irrigation does not remove all adherent biofilm, so residual bacteria can multiply and recolonise the canal (Gulabivala and Ng, 2022) [SRC]. Sikri quotes the figure that preparation with sodium hypochlorite irrigation renders only 33% of canals bacteria-free at the first appointment (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

Calcium hydroxide is the classic medicament for the interval: it dissolves tissue, kills bacteria, detoxifies antigenic material (including lipopolysaccharide) and acts as a slow-release agent because of its low solubility (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]. Its antibacterial reliability is now itself questioned: Sathorn et al's 2007 systematic review, as cited in *Endodontology*, concluded that it has limited effectiveness in eliminating bacteria from human canals (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. This is why the debate cannot be closed by microbiology alone: if the medicament does not reliably sterilise, a second visit is not automatically better.

Cohen's case-planning chapter adds a softer, clinically important point: total elimination of bacteria may not be absolutely necessary for healing; maximal reduction, an effective filling and a timely coronal restoration may be enough, but whatever the number of visits, effective disinfection is critical (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Vital pulp (irreversible pulpitis).**
- The radicular pulp is inflamed but not infected; bacteria, if any, are coronal (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017).
- Treat without delay, preferably in a single visit, to avoid contaminating the canal between visits (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a).
- Vital pulpectomy success rate is greater than 94% (Goldberg, 2019).

**Necrotic pulp with apical periodontitis.**
- A mixed bacterial biofilm occupies the canal, isthmuses, ramifications and tubules.
- Instruments and irrigants do not eliminate all adherent biofilm; residual bacteria may recolonise (Gulabivala and Ng, 2022).
- Preparation with sodium hypochlorite irrigation leaves only about one-third of canals bacteria-free at the first visit (Sikri, 2019).

**Role of the interappointment medicament (calcium hydroxide).** Dissolves organic tissue, kills bacteria, detoxifies antigenic material and acts as a slow-release agent (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021); it inactivates endotoxin (Sikri, 2019). Limitation: its antibacterial effectiveness in human canals is limited (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide).** LPS is the most powerful endotoxin acting on periapical tissues; irrigants are largely ineffective against it, whereas calcium hydroxide inactivates it (Sikri, 2019). Xavier et al (2013) found two visits more effective than one in reducing endotoxins, though not cultivable bacteria (Sikri, 2019).

**Ecological view.** The canal flora behaves as a community. Partial elimination may kill it (good outcome) or let it reorganise around more resistant or virulent species (bad outcome, flare-up); hence maximal reduction before obturation is the aim (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a).

**Key principle.** Total sterility may not be necessary for healing; maximal bacterial reduction, a sound filling and a prompt coronal restoration matter, and effective disinfection is critical whatever the number of visits (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021).

## 4. Case selection: indications, contraindications and criteria

**Explanation.** Every source agrees that case selection decides whether one visit is safe. Siqueira and Rôças put the conditions for completing a vital case in one visit as adequate time, sufficient operator skill and favourable anatomy (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC]. Cohen's frames it as "what can be done and what should be done": most cases can technically be completed in one visit, but systemic health, anxiety, symptoms, anatomical complexity and the clinician's experience must decide whether they should be (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. A patient in severe pain, with or without swelling, is not a prime candidate; the first goal is relief of pain, with filling deferred (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. Weine adds the practical rule that one-sitting treatment is acceptable when no apical radiolucency is present and the tooth is not tender to percussion, and that anterior teeth, easier to radiograph, offer a better chance of one-visit success than molars (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

There is a real contradiction inside Sikri's chapter that an examiner may probe. The indications list "teeth having sinus tract" and "uncomplicated nonvital teeth", and the same chapter then advises that single visit treatment should be avoided in vital teeth because pulp tissue left at the apical constriction may cause later problems; yet a few lines on, it reports that most authors find vital teeth easy to treat in one visit and calls the point controversial (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's, Harty's, *Endodontology* and Siqueira and Rôças all favour one visit for vital teeth. The safe exam position is the majority one, with Sikri's caution stated as a minority view about incomplete apical pulp removal [Likely].

Grossman gives the classic decision box (favourable versus unfavourable criteria) and, like Sikri, is stricter than the trial evidence: it lists the presence of a periradicular lesion and a hot tooth as unfavourable and all nonsurgical retreatment as a contraindication, while stressing that the clinician's experience is the most important single factor (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Pooled RCTs, by contrast, show similar healing in necrotic teeth with lesions (Mergoni et al, 2022) [LIT]; the textbook lists are best read as guidance for operators still making the transition to single-visit practice, which is how Grossman frames them [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Decision-making criteria** (Gopikrishna, 2021):
| Favourable | Unfavourable |
|---|---|
| Clinically experienced operator | Relatively inexperienced operator |
| Cooperative patient | Noncooperative patient |
| Good access and mouth opening | Poor access and mouth opening |
| Normal anatomy, patent canals, easy curvatures | Extra roots/canals, calcifications, ledges, acute curves |
| No periradicular lesion | Periradicular lesion present |
| Complete pulpal anaesthesia easily achieved | Hot tooth |
| Primary treatment | Retreatment |

**Criteria in detail** (Sikri, 2019; Rosenberg and Malek, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a):
1. **Patient:** cooperative, able to keep the mouth open for a long appointment; no TMJ disorder, gagging or limited opening.
2. **Operator:** adequate clinical experience and skill; able to manage errors during the visit.
3. **Tooth:** easy access, uncomplicated anatomy (single-rooted and anterior teeth are best; Weine, 2004).
4. **Symptoms:** absence of severe pain or swelling.
5. **Pulp and periapex:** vital pulp is the strongest indication; asymptomatic necrotic teeth are acceptable for experienced operators.
6. **Time:** an appointment long enough to complete preparation, adequate irrigation and obturation.
7. **Canal condition at the end of preparation:** dry, with no exudate (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**Indications** (Sikri, 2019; Weine, 2004; Siqueira and Rôças, 2017):
- Vital teeth with irreversible pulpitis.
- Fractured teeth, especially anteriors, needing immediate post-and-core restoration for aesthetics (no radiolucency, not tender to percussion).
- Uncomplicated asymptomatic non-vital teeth, including teeth with a sinus tract.
- Physically compromised patients who cannot visit the clinic often; medically compromised patients needing antibiotic prophylaxis; patients who need sedation or an operating room (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Apprehensive but cooperative patients.

**Contraindications** (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021; Rosenberg and Malek, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010):
- Acute apical abscess, severe pain or swelling.
- Canals that cannot be dried (weeping canals, persistent exudate or pus).
- Difficult access; aberrant canal anatomy.
- Nonsurgical retreatment cases (Gopikrishna, 2021); flare-ups are commoner in symptomatic retreatments (Trope 1991; Yoldas 2004, in Sikri, 2019).
- Chronic periapical lesions of long duration (Sikri, 2019).
- Hot tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Patients with TMJ problems, limited opening or poor cooperation.
- Inexperienced operators.
- Doubt about whether all canals were located or fully prepared.

*Note:* textbook lists are stricter than the trials; pooled RCTs show similar healing in necrotic teeth with lesions (Mergoni et al, 2022).

## 5. Clinical protocol of single-visit treatment

**Explanation.** The single-visit protocol is the ordinary root canal procedure carried out without a break, but with less margin for error because there is no second visit to put things right. Weine's own routine shows what changes: a clean, complete pulp extirpation close to the apical constriction; an accurate working length verified by radiograph or apex locator, because overinstrumentation must be avoided; preparation to the maximum size consistent with the original canal shape to remove predentin and tissue tags; obturation by condensation; and, in the prosthetic case, post space prepared at the same visit (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. If overinstrumentation occurs by error, the tooth should not be completed that day; a corticosteroid-antibiotic apical medicament is placed and completion deferred (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. *Endodontology* adds the two exit conditions: if the canals cannot be dried, dress with calcium hydroxide and reappoint; if there is doubt about the completeness of preparation or the location of all canals, place a dressing and finish at a second sitting (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The 2022 Cochrane review only included trials using rubber dam and sodium hypochlorite irrigation, which it treated as the current standard of care (Mergoni et al, 2022) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Steps** (Weine, 2004; Baumann and Beer, 2010; Mergoni et al, 2022):
1. Diagnosis, case selection, preoperative radiograph and informed consent.
2. Profound anaesthesia (book 15-20 minutes for an inferior alveolar block; Rosenberg, Malek and Okamoto, 2019); rubber dam isolation (mandatory).
3. Complete caries removal; leakproof access cavity; locate all canals (magnification helps [RECALL]).
4. Clean pulp extirpation close to the apical constriction.
5. Accurate working length by apex locator and radiograph; strict confinement of instruments to the canal.
6. Cleaning and shaping to an adequate apical size and taper.
7. Copious sodium hypochlorite irrigation; EDTA rinse; supplementary step such as ultrasonic or mechanical activation of hypochlorite or a final 2% chlorhexidine rinse (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a).
8. Check the canal: dry, no exudate, all canals prepared.
9. Obturation with gutta-percha and sealer by a condensation technique (Weine, 2004).
10. Immediate coronal seal with a core build-up: without it, canals reinfected within 19 days in vitro (Gopikrishna, 2021); definitive restoration, or post-and-core where indicated, at the same or an early visit.
11. Postoperative analgesic instructions and review.

**Stop and convert to two visits if** (Weine, 2004; Baumann and Beer, 2010):
- Canals cannot be dried (pus or exudate) or bleeding persists; severe preoperative pain or acute symptoms (Goldberg, 2019).
- Overinstrumentation or overextrusion occurs.
- Doubt remains about missed canals or incomplete preparation.
- The patient tires or the appointment time runs out.

*If treated over two visits:* allow 5-7 days between instrumentation and obturation of a vital case so periradicular tissues recover (Rosenberg, Malek and Okamoto, 2019).

## 6. Advantages and disadvantages

**Explanation.** The advantages of one visit fall into three groups: biological (no interappointment leakage or contamination through a lost temporary), technical (the operator knows the canal anatomy, working length and reference points while they are fresh; no medicament residue to impair sealer flow), and practical (time, cost, patient convenience, an immediate final restoration that reduces fracture risk, and fewer patients abandoning treatment) (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The disadvantages are almost a mirror image: no access through the canal if a flare-up occurs, no opportunity to use a medicament or to reassess symptoms and missed canals, and a long appointment that tires patient and operator alike (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Sikri adds that removing the filling after a flare-up is tedious and that beginners should not attempt one visit (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| No interappointment contamination through leaking or lost temporary fillings | No easy canal access to drain or relieve a flare-up |
| Canal anatomy, shape and working length are fresh in the operator's mind; reference points are not lost | No opportunity for an intracanal medicament |
| No residual medicament to impair sealer flow or react with dentine | No chance to re-evaluate missed canals or the response of symptoms |
| Saves chair time for patient and operator; cost-effective | Long appointment: patient fatigue, TMJ strain, reduced cooperation |
| Immediate final restoration: reduced risk of fracture; immediate aesthetics in anterior teeth | Operator fatigue from long concentration |
| Patient anxiety and discomfort limited to one appointment | Removing a fresh filling after a flare-up is tedious |
| Fewer incomplete treatments (patients who do not return once pain subsides) | Less flexible scheduling; retreatment time hard to predict |
| Fewer anaesthetic injections and isolations | Not suitable for inexperienced operators |

*(Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010)*

## 6A. Flare-ups after single-visit treatment: causes, prevention and management

**Explanation.** The flare-up is the most feared complication of one-visit treatment, because the canal is already filled and cannot simply be opened and drained. Sikri lists the operative causes: overinstrumentation, pushing debris beyond the apex and any irritation of the periodontium; mild pain during treatment is normal and does not affect outcome (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Siqueira and Rôças give the microbial mechanism: partial disruption of the canal community may allow it to reorganise around more virulent species, predisposing to flare-ups (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC]. Grossman defines the flare-up as an acute exacerbation of an asymptomatic pulp or periapical pathosis after starting or continuing treatment, reports an incidence of 1.4-19%, and notes that step-back shaping extrudes more debris than crown-down (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The patient's preoperative state matters: in Trope's 1991 series there were no flare-ups in teeth without preoperative symptoms, but a higher rate in symptomatic retreatments (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Prevention is therefore mostly case selection plus strict apical control; Weine says that if overinstrumentation is discovered, the tooth should not be completed but dressed with a corticosteroid-antibiotic paste and completed a few days later (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Definition.** An acute exacerbation of an asymptomatic pulp or periapical pathosis after the initiation or continuation of root canal treatment, needing an unscheduled visit; incidence 1.4-19% (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Causes after a single visit.**
1. Mechanical: overinstrumentation, extrusion of debris, irrigant or filling material (Sikri, 2019).
2. Microbial: extrusion of infected debris; reorganisation of a partly disrupted bacterial community around more virulent species (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a).
3. Patient and tooth factors: preoperative pain or swelling, acute alveolar abscess (highest incidence), necrotic rather than vital pulp, retreatment (Gopikrishna, 2021; Trope 1991, cited in Sikri, 2019).
4. Irrigant extrusion (hypochlorite accident) (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Evidence.** Trope (1991): no flare-ups in teeth without preoperative symptoms; higher rate in symptomatic retreatments. DiRenzo et al (2002): one flare-up (1.3%), in the multiple-visit group (Sikri, 2019). Pooled RCTs show no difference in flare-up (Mergoni et al, 2022), although one meta-analysis found a higher risk after one visit on very weak evidence (Schwendicke and Göstemeyer, 2017).

**Prevention.**
- Careful case selection (asymptomatic cases, no exudate).
- Accurate working length; instruments confined to the canal (Weine, 2004).
- Crown-down rather than step-back shaping; passive irrigation (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Convert to two visits if overinstrumentation occurs: place a corticosteroid-antibiotic dressing and complete later (Weine, 2004).

**Management.** NSAIDs with acetaminophen, long-acting local anaesthetic, anxiety reduction and occlusal relief (Gopikrishna, 2021); antibiotics only when systemic signs are present (Goldberg, 2019); incision and drainage of a fluctuant swelling [RECALL]; do not leave the tooth open for drainage (Teixeira and Cheung, 2021). Removal of a fresh filling to drain the canal is tedious, which is the main practical drawback of single-visit treatment (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## 6B. Single-visit treatment in special situations

**Explanation.** Several clinical situations fall outside the routine vital-versus-necrotic split. Retreatment is the hardest: Yoldas et al found two-visit retreatment better at eliminating pain in previously symptomatic teeth, and Trope found more flare-ups in symptomatic retreatments (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; *Endodontology* adds that the time needed for retreatment is hard to predict in a single sitting (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. A recent meta-analysis of gutta-percha retreatments, however, reported that multiple-visit retreatments had a lower success rate under strict criteria (Olivieri et al, 2024) [LIT], so even here the direction of evidence is not settled. Immature teeth treated by regenerative procedures follow a deliberately multi-visit protocol: the AAE protocol usually requires two treatment visits plus follow-ups (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) [SRC]. For endo-periodontal lesions a systematic review suggested a two-visit approach, to allow an intracanal medicament, though the certainty was low (Gill et al, 2023) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core
| Situation | Recommended approach |
|---|---|
| Irreversible pulpitis | Single visit preferred (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017) |
| Fractured anterior tooth needing immediate post-crown, no radiolucency, not tender | Single visit (Weine, 2004) |
| Asymptomatic necrotic tooth, with or without sinus tract | Single visit acceptable with good case selection (Sikri, 2019; Mergoni et al, 2022) |
| Acute apical abscess, severe pain or swelling | Relieve pain first; obturate later (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) |
| Weeping canal / persistent exudate | Calcium hydroxide dressing; reappoint (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| Retreatment | Contraindicated for single visit in Grossman; two visits preferred if symptomatic (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019); evidence mixed (Olivieri et al, 2024) |
| Immature apex, regenerative endodontics | Planned two treatment visits (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021) |
| Endo-perio lesion | Two visits with intracanal medicament may be considered (Gill et al, 2023) |
| Physically or medically compromised patient who cannot attend often | Single visit where clinically feasible (Sikri, 2019; Gopikrishna, 2021) |

## 7. Evidence: postoperative pain, flare-up and healing

**Explanation.** The evidence must be separated into short-term outcomes (pain, flare-up, analgesic use) and long-term outcomes (radiographic healing, tooth survival), because they point in slightly different directions.

*Short-term.* Most individual trials found no difference in postoperative pain (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's table of trials shows the spread: Roane et al (1983) found more pain after multiple visits, Risso et al (2008) found pain in 10.5% after one visit versus 23% after multiple visits in necrotic molars, while Yoldas et al (2004) found two-visit retreatment better at eliminating pain in previously symptomatic teeth (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The 2022 Cochrane update (47 trials, 5693 teeth) found no difference in pain up to 72 hours or flare-up, but moderate-certainty evidence of more participants reporting pain within one week after single-visit treatment (RR 1.55), with the excess seen in small subgroups of vital teeth and mechanical instrumentation (Mergoni et al, 2022) [LIT]. Schwendicke and Göstemeyer's meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis found a higher flare-up risk after one visit (RR 2.13) but graded that evidence "very weak" and found that firm evidence was not reached for any outcome (Schwendicke and Göstemeyer, 2017) [LIT].

*Long-term.* Healing shows no detectable difference: Cochrane 2022 found no difference in radiological failure (RR 0.93, moderate certainty) and very low-certainty evidence on extraction (Mergoni et al, 2022) [LIT]; an overview of eight systematic reviews by Moreira et al found similar success regardless of pulpal and periapical condition (Moreira et al, 2017) [LIT]. On the other side, Siqueira and Rôças list studies in which two visits with calcium hydroxide gave a 10% to 20% higher success rate, Ricucci et al's 5-year single-operator cohort of 1369 canals in which necrotic cases did significantly better with a calcium hydroxide dressing, animal histology favouring two visits, and a CBCT-assessed human trial favouring two visits (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC]. Cohen's sums up that most RCTs found no effect of the number of visits on healing, but all lacked statistical power (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**A. Postoperative pain and flare-up.**
- Most trials: no significant difference in postoperative pain between single and multiple visits (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Sikri, 2019).
- Exceptions: Roane et al (1983), more pain after multiple visits; Risso et al (2008), pain 10.5% after one visit versus 23% after multiple visits in necrotic molars; Yoldas et al (2004), two visits better for symptomatic retreatment (Sikri, 2019).
- Cochrane 2022 (47 trials, 5693 teeth): no difference in pain up to 72 hours, flare-up or analgesic use; more patients with pain within one week after a single visit (RR 1.55, moderate certainty), seen in small subgroups of vital teeth and mechanical instrumentation; pain at the one-week time point itself did not differ (Mergoni et al, 2022).
- Schwendicke and Göstemeyer (2017): flare-up risk higher after a single visit (RR 2.13), but very weak evidence; no outcome reached firm evidence.

**B. Periapical healing.**
| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| Pekruhn (1986), 925 teeth, single visit | 95% overall success at 1 year; failures higher in retreatment and apical periodontitis |
| Field et al (2004), 223 teeth, single visit | 89.2% success; anterior better than posterior |
| Kvist et al (2004), 96 teeth with apical periodontitis | No significant difference |
| Sjögren et al (1997) | 94% healing with negative culture vs 68% positive (not a visit comparison) |
| Trope et al (1999) | Two visits without medicament worst; one visit = two visits with calcium hydroxide |
| Weiger et al (2000) | No significant difference |
| Peters and Wesselink (2002) | No significant difference (4.5-5 years) |
| Molander et al (2007); Penesis et al (2008); Paredes-Vieyra and Enriquez (2012) | No significant difference in healing |

*(Baumann and Beer, 2010; Sikri, 2019; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a; Mergoni et al, 2022)*

**C. Systematic reviews.**
- Sathorn et al (2005): no significant difference in healing for teeth with apical periodontitis (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- A systematic review found single visit slightly more effective (6.3% higher healing rate), not statistically significant (Rosenberg, Malek and Okamoto, 2019).
- Moreira et al (2017), overview of 8 systematic reviews: similar repair or success rates regardless of pulpal and periapical status; a slight trend favouring one visit in apical periodontitis.
- Mergoni et al (2022, Cochrane): no difference in radiological failure or extraction; more pain within one week after one visit.
- Schwendicke and Göstemeyer (2017): insufficient evidence to rule out important differences; multiple visits might be preferred for teeth with periapical lesions.

**D. Opposing data favouring two visits** (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a): some trials report 10-20% higher success with two visits and calcium hydroxide; Ricucci's 5-year single-operator cohort favoured two visits in necrotic teeth; animal histology and a CBCT-assessed human trial favoured two visits.

**Interpretation.** For vital teeth the evidence and biology agree: one visit. For necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis, pooled trials show equivalence but lack statistical power and are of low-to-medium quality (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a; Mergoni et al, 2022).

## 8. Current concepts and controversies

**Explanation.** Four controversies remain live.

1. *Is equivalence real?* Siqueira and Rôças judge the clinical trial evidence low to medium in quality, with small samples and biases in operator skill, randomisation, apical enlargement and outcome criteria, so the data are insufficient for definitive conclusions (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng add that only large, properly documented RCTs can resolve it, because unrecorded confounders such as operator skill and case complexity bias non-randomised studies (Gulabivala and Ng, 2022) [SRC].
2. *Is radiography sensitive enough?* CBCT detects lesions missed on periapical films; in dogs two visits were superior on CBCT but not on radiographs, and a human CBCT trial favoured two visits (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC]. Equivalence on periapical radiographs may therefore hide a difference.
3. *Does calcium hydroxide work?* If its antibacterial effect is limited (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC], a second visit offers less than its theory claims. Siqueira and Rôças respond that more potent pastes (calcium hydroxide with camphorated paramonochlorophenol or chlorhexidine) give predictable disinfection, and that the best infection-control protocols still involve two visits with medication (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC].
4. *More than two visits is worse.* Both of Siqueira and Rôças's chapters warn that treatment over more than two visits predisposes to secondary infection and reduced tooth survival (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017; Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC]. The debate is therefore one visit versus two, not one versus many.

*Contemporary direction.* Research is looking for protocols that expedite disinfection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC]; their intravisit options are ultrasonic or mechanical activation of hypochlorite or a final 2% chlorhexidine rinse (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC]. Laser-activated irrigation and photodynamic therapy are also studied, but PDT needs further modification before its clinical use can be recommended (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022b) [SRC]. *Endodontology* is candid that, in the absence of firm guidance, the decision often comes down to individual clinician preference and dogma (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**Current consensus.**
1. **Vital pulp (irreversible pulpitis):** single visit is preferred when conditions allow (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017; Baumann and Beer, 2010).
2. **Necrotic pulp, asymptomatic, with or without a lesion:** either approach gives similar radiographic healing in pooled RCTs (Mergoni et al, 2022; Moreira et al, 2017); single visit is acceptable for experienced operators with good case selection.
3. **Necrotic pulp with large lesions, symptomatic teeth, bleeding or exudate, retreatment:** two visits with an intracanal dressing are preferred (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a; Goldberg, 2019; Schwendicke and Göstemeyer, 2017).
4. **Avoid more than two visits:** risk of secondary infection and reduced survival (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a).

**Controversies.**
| Issue | Pro single visit | Pro multiple visits |
|---|---|---|
| Residual bacteria | Entombed and starved by the filling (Sikri, 2019) | Survive and recolonise; entombment fails above a threshold (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) |
| Calcium hydroxide | Limited antibacterial effect in human canals (Baumann and Beer, 2010) | Potent pastes give predictable disinfection (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) |
| Healing evidence | No difference in RCTs and systematic reviews (Mergoni et al, 2022) | Trials underpowered; cohort, animal and CBCT data favour two visits (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) |
| Pain | No difference in most trials (Baumann and Beer, 2010) | More pain in the first week after one visit (Mergoni et al, 2022) |
| Vital teeth | Generally accepted; preferred where feasible (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017) | Apical pulp tags may remain (Sikri, 2019; minority view) |
| Outcome measurement | Periapical radiographs show equivalence | CBCT may reveal differences (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) |

**Future direction.** Supplementary intravisit disinfection (ultrasonic or mechanical activation of hypochlorite, final chlorhexidine rinse) to make single-visit treatment of infected teeth predictable (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a). Large, well-powered RCTs with CBCT follow-up are needed (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021).

## 9. Conclusion

#### ✎ Exam core
Single-visit endodontics is a valid, evidence-supported option, not a shortcut. The number of visits matters less than the quality of disinfection, obturation and coronal seal (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021). Vital teeth should, where possible, be completed in one visit. For necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis, pooled trials show equal healing, but the evidence is underpowered, and two visits with calcium hydroxide remain the more cautious choice in symptomatic, exudative, complex or retreatment cases (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a; Mergoni et al, 2022). The decision rests on case selection, operator skill and the patient's condition, and should never be left to the patient alone (Weine, 2004). Whichever route is chosen, more than two visits should be avoided, a planned single visit must be converted to two whenever the canal cannot be dried or doubt arises, and the coronal seal must be placed promptly (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a; Baumann and Beer, 2010).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Single and multiple visits heal equally."**
- *Textbooks:* Sikri and *Endodontology* report no significant difference across most trials (Sikri, 2019; Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Cohen's says most RCTs found no effect but all lacked power (Ng and Gulabivala, 2021) [SRC]; Siqueira and Rôças find the data insufficient and lean to two visits for infected teeth (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC].
- *Retrieved evidence:* Cochrane 2022, 13 trials, 1505 teeth: RR 0.93 for radiological failure, moderate certainty (Mergoni et al, 2022) [LIT]; Schwendicke and Göstemeyer: trial sequential analysis showed firm evidence of benefit, harm or futility was not reached for any outcome (Schwendicke and Göstemeyer, 2017) [LIT].
- *Agreement/challenge:* The literature agrees there is no detectable difference; it does not show proven equivalence. Absence of evidence of a difference in underpowered trials is not evidence of no difference [Certain].
- *Strength:* RCTs, many at high or unclear risk of bias, assessed on periapical radiographs at 1 to 2 years [Certain]. The opposing data are a single-operator cohort, animal histology and one CBCT trial: plausible, but lower in the hierarchy or limited in number [Likely].
- *Conclusion:* Equivalence is the best current estimate for radiographic healing; a small advantage for two visits in necrotic teeth with lesions cannot be excluded [Likely].
- *Clinical implication:* One visit is defensible for well-selected necrotic cases; two visits remain the cautious default for large lesions and complex anatomy.

**Claim 2: "Single visit causes more postoperative pain."**
- *Textbooks:* No difference in pain (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Siqueira and Rôças cite a review showing slightly more swelling and more painkiller use after one visit (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022a) [SRC].
- *Retrieved evidence:* The 2016 Cochrane version found more painkiller use after one visit (RR 2.35) (Manfredi et al, 2016) [LIT]; the 2022 update, after excluding trials without rubber dam and hypochlorite, found no difference in analgesic use but more pain in the first week (RR 1.55), mainly in vital teeth (Mergoni et al, 2022) [LIT].
- *Agreement/refinement:* The textbook's "more painkillers" claim reflects the 2016 review and is refined by 2022; first-week pain trended higher in 2016 (RR 1.50, confidence interval crossing 1, not significant) and was significantly higher in 2022 (RR 1.55, moderate certainty); flare-ups did not differ [Likely].
- *Conclusion and implication:* Warn patients about short-lived pain after a single visit and prescribe analgesics; the effect is modest and does not argue against single-visit treatment of vital teeth on biological grounds [Likely].

**Claim 3: "Calcium hydroxide sterilises the canal between visits."**
- Sjögren 1991 found no detectable bacteria after 7 days (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Sathorn 2007 (cited in the same text) found limited effectiveness, and Peters and Wesselink found bacteria increasing during a 4-week dressing (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Evidence is internally inconsistent; culture methods and placement technique differ [Certain]. Conclusion: calcium hydroxide reduces but does not reliably eliminate bacteria, so the rationale for a second visit is real but weaker than the 1991 study suggested [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
1. Vital teeth: radicular pulp not infected, so complete in one visit where possible (Siqueira and Rôças, 2017).
2. Real controversy: necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis and retreatments.
3. Sjögren 1997: 94% vs 68% healing with negative vs positive culture at filling.
4. Cochrane 2022: no difference in radiological failure (RR 0.93); more patients with pain within the first week after one visit (RR 1.55).
5. Flare-up RR 2.13 after one visit (Schwendicke and Göstemeyer, 2017), very weak evidence.
6. Never more than two visits: secondary infection, reduced survival.
7. Convert to two visits if canals cannot be dried, overinstrumentation occurs or canals may be missed.
8. "What can be done and what should be done" are different decisions (Rosenberg and Malek, 2021).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Saying "single visit is contraindicated in non-vital teeth": it is debated, not contraindicated; pooled RCTs show similar healing.
2. Quoting Sjögren 1997 as a one-visit versus two-visit trial: it compared culture-negative with culture-positive teeth, all filled at one visit.
3. Writing that single visit "causes more flare-ups" as settled fact: the RR 2.13 finding is very weak evidence, and the 2022 Cochrane found no difference in flare-up.
4. Arguing for "multiple" visits without saying "two": more than two visits worsens outcome.
5. Omitting the exit criteria (wet canal, overinstrumentation, missed canals) that convert a planned single visit into two.
