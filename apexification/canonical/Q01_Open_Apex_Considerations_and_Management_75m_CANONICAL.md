# Open Apex – considerations and management.

**Marks:** 75 · Single essay

## 1. Introduction and definitions

An **open apex** is a root apex that has not formed, or has lost its constriction, so that the canal has **no apical stop**. Weine describes it as the absence of enough root development to give the canal a conical taper: the canal is wider toward the apex than at the cervix, the **"blunderbuss" canal** (Weine, 2004); the AAE Glossary defines the blunderbuss canal as an incompletely formed root whose apical diameter is greater than its coronal diameter (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). An open apex is normal in a developing root; it becomes a clinical problem when the pulp dies before the root is complete, or when a mature apex is opened by resorption (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002). "Open apex" is therefore a description, not a diagnosis: management depends on one question, **is the pulp vital?**

- **Apexogenesis** (now "vital pulp therapy", Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002): a vital pulp therapy procedure performed to encourage continued physiological development and formation of the root end (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Sikri, 2019); Grossman specifies capping or pulpotomy (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Apexification** (now "root-end closure"): a method to induce a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex in a tooth with a necrotic pulp (American Association of Endodontists, 2012); in an immature, pulpless tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Apical barrier:** blockage of the apical foramen by induced hard tissue or an artificial material (dentin chips, collagen, MTA, calcium hydroxide) (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). The MTA apical plug is called **one-step apexification** (Tonini and Garo, 2024).
- **Regenerative endodontics:** biologically based procedures designed to replace damaged dentin, root structure and cells of the pulp-dentin complex (American Association of Endodontists, 2012).

## 2. Root development and its stages

**Hertwig's epithelial root sheath (HERS)** is the template for odontoblast differentiation and root dentin; it lengthens the root and closes the apical foramen (Shabahang, 2019), in interaction with the mesenchymal cells of the apical papilla (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Root formation continues for about **3 years after eruption** (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021; Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002), whereas Kang and Bogen (2017) give **1 to 5 years**. Without a vital pulp the root cannot complete development; loss of radicular odontoblasts arrests it (Shabahang, 2019; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Because odontoblasts rarely survive necrosis (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011), **apexification produces a barrier, not a root**. Trauma between the ages of 6 and 14 years is the usual interruption (Shabahang, 2019).

**Stages of root development** (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021):

| Stage | Root | Apex |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | one-fourth formed | blunderbuss |
| 2 | half formed | blunderbuss |
| 3 | three-fourth formed | blunderbuss |
| 4 | full length | open |
| 5 | full length | partially closed |
| 6 | full length | closed |

Cvek's scale has five stages, 1-4 maturing and 5 complete (Widbiller and Galler, 2024); the two scales differ only in how the late stages are split. The earlier the stage at which the pulp dies, the shorter and thinner the root and the higher the fracture risk (Cvek, 2007).

## 3. Causes and types of open apex

No textbook gives a formal classification by cause; the grouping below collects causes named in several books. Pitt Ford and Shabahang (2002) separate the immature apex from a mature apex opened by orthodontic resorption, periradicular inflammation or healing after trauma.

| Group | Cause | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Developmental (pulp dies before the root is complete) | Trauma: crown fracture with exposure, luxation, intrusion, avulsion | Intrusion will most likely need endodontics (Shabahang, 2019); luxated immature pulps often survive (Cvek, 2007) |
| | Caries in young permanent teeth | First molars; **molar-incisor hypomineralisation** (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010) |
| | Developmental anomalies | **Dens evaginatus**: tubercle wears, pulp exposed (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) |
| Pathological (formed apex opened) | Apical inflammatory root resorption | Blunderbuss terminus, extrusion of materials (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) |
| | Internal resorption, apex wider than ISO 50-60 | Apical plug indications (Tonini and Garo, 2024) |
| | Orthodontic apical resorption | Mature apex opened (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002) |
| Iatrogenic | Over-instrumentation beyond the apex | Used experimentally to create open apices (Shabahang, 2019) |
| | Incorrect working length | Zipping or "blowing out" of the foramen (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) |
| | Root-end resection | Orthograde plug indicated (Tonini and Garo, 2024) |

**Types by canal shape:** (1) **divergent (blunderbuss)**; (2) **parallel**; (3) **slightly convergent** with a wide foramen; the shape follows the stage of root formation (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002; Tonini and Garo, 2024). The buccolingual dimension is greater than the mesiodistal and is the last to converge (Shabahang, 2019), so a canal that looks parallel on the radiograph may be divergent buccolingually.

## 4. Considerations: why the open apex is difficult

1. **Pulp diagnosis is unreliable.** Pulp tests are less reliable in children; **cold** is the most reliable test and the electric pulp test is less accurate in immature apices (Shabahang, 2019; Sikri, 2019). Thermal and electric tests assess nerve function, not circulation; after trauma blood flow may take up to **9 months** to return, and CO2 snow or dichlorodifluoromethane are the best agents in young teeth (Kahler, 2021). After luxation nerves may be damaged while the blood supply survives (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002). Laser Doppler flowmetry measures blood flow, with a dental dam to exclude gingival signal (Shabahang, 2019). Treat the pulp as vital until **at least two signs** of necrosis and infection are present (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).
2. **Radiographic interpretation.** The periapical film underestimates buccolingual width; **CBCT** shows the true anatomy (Shabahang, 2019). A lesion is hard to tell from the normal radiolucent papilla (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010): the normal zone has a **corticated margin**; compare with the contralateral tooth (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).
3. **Thin, short roots.** Dentin does not thicken without a pulp; thin cervical walls fracture after later trauma (Shabahang, 2019; Cvek, 2007). Loss of vitality in an immature tooth can be catastrophic (Kahler, 2021).
4. **Disinfection without weakening.** Debridement is chiefly **chemical**; the canals are already wide (Shabahang, 2019). Brush the walls gently with a cotton-wrapped file (Bakland, 2007; Weine, 2004); do not remove vital apical tissue (Cvek, 2007); over-instrumentation compromises success (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010). Wide tubules increase permeability (Kang and Bogen, 2017), yet residual bacteria stop root-end development (Shabahang, 2019). **Irrigant strength differs:** 0.5% NaOCl in volume, because of extrusion through the apex (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021), vs 1.5% NaOCl for 15-20 minutes (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) vs 5% NaOCl with 17% EDTA (Sikri, 2019); exposure over 20 minutes may weaken dentin (Shabahang, 2019).
5. **Working length.** Apex locators are inaccurate; use radiographic length, since extrusion may damage remaining HERS (Shabahang, 2019). End-points differ: at least **2 mm short** of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs plug 1 mm from the radiographic apex (Tonini and Garo, 2024) vs the level where bleeding is first met (Bakland, 2007).
6. **No apical stop:** softened filling material moves into and injures the periapical tissues (Kahler, 2021).
7. **Patient factors:** cooperation, many visits and drop-out with long calcium hydroxide therapy (Shabahang, 2019); growth and orthodontic advice for molars (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010).

## 5. Treatment planning

In an immature tooth with a vital pulp, every attempt must be made to maintain vitality so the apex can complete its formation (Shabahang, 2019; Cvek, 2007); vital pulp therapy should always be attempted if feasible (Kahler, 2021). With irreversible inflammation or necrosis, the choice between conventional treatment and root-end closure depends on the **level of apical development** (Shabahang, 2019). Apexification is indicated where open apices and thin walls prevent an apical stop (Kahler, 2021); regeneration suits immature necrotic teeth whose crown can be restored directly (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

| Pulp status | Root stage | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Vital, reversible (exposure) | Immature | Apexogenesis: pulp capping, partial (Cvek) pulpotomy, cervical pulpotomy |
| Heavily inflamed coronal pulp, radicular pulp bleeding normally | Immature | Cervical pulpotomy to fresh bleeding tissue |
| Necrotic | Cvek stages 1-3, apex at least 1 mm, no post needed | Regenerative endodontic procedure, or barrier |
| Necrotic | Stage 4 onward or near complete | MTA/bioceramic apical plug; calcium hydroxide apexification |
| Necrotic | Mature root opened by resorption or iatrogenically | Apical plug and obturation; surgery if this fails |

**Indications and contraindications** (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002). *Vital pulp therapy*: radicular pulp presumed healthy and a restorable crown; not for avulsed or severely luxated teeth, crown-root fractures needing a post, unfavourable horizontal root fractures or unrestorable caries. *Root-end closure*: a restorable necrotic immature tooth; not with vertical or most horizontal root fractures, **replacement resorption**, very short roots or marginal periodontal breakdown. Alternatives: apical plug, root canal treatment, periapical surgery (best avoided in children) and extraction. The one-visit plug suits children with behaviour-management problems (Kang and Bogen, 2017).

**Diagram to draw:** Decision flowchart. Immature tooth → pulp vital? Yes → apexogenesis by depth of inflammation (capping, partial, cervical pulpotomy). No → root stage: stages 1-3 → REP or barrier; stage 4 on → MTA plug or calcium hydroxide apexification; mature open apex → apical plug.

## 6. Management of the vital pulp: apexogenesis

**Rationale.** Young pulps are well vascularised; after a traumatic exposure Cvek found pulps vital for up to 7 days with inflammation limited to the surface **2 mm** (Shabahang, 2019); Kahler (2021) gives no more than 2 mm at 48 hours. Goals: keep HERS vital (crown-root ratio), let odontoblasts thicken the walls (less fracture), form a natural constriction and a dentin bridge (Sikri, 2019). After apexification the root stays short, blunt and wide (Weine, 2004).

1. **Pulp capping:** a small exposure treated within about 24 hours (Cvek, 2007); pinpoint exposures with a healthy pulp (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010).
2. **Partial (Cvek) pulpotomy:** removal of a small portion of vital coronal pulp (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). Rubber dam; high-speed diamond with copious cooling; amputate about **2 mm below the exposure**; saline until bleeding stops; avoid an extrapulpal clot, which is linked to internal resorption and calcification (Cvek, 2007). Assess under the microscope; bleeding that persists despite haemostasis means deeper inflammation (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Cap with calcium hydroxide, MTA or Biodentine (MTA may be placed on slightly bleeding pulp) and seal with a bonded restoration (Bakland, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021). Advantages: cervical dentin keeps forming and sensitivity testing remains possible (Cvek, 2007). Success **94-96%** (Kahler, 2021); 95% vs 80% for direct capping (Gopikrishna, 2021).
3. **Cervical (full) pulpotomy:** for deeper inflammation, exposures over 72 hours or carious exposures (Kahler, 2021). Remove the coronal pulp to the cervical third, leaving as much radicular pulp as possible and without twisting the stump (Gopikrishna, 2021), to fresh bleeding tissue (Cvek, 2007). Cover with **more than 4 mm** of tricalcium silicate (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), or calcium hydroxide 1-2 mm / MTA at least 2 mm under an RMGIC base (Gopikrishna, 2021). Success about **75%** (Kahler, 2021). Even if only the apical 3 or 4 mm of pulp is vital, the apex can complete (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Diagram to draw:** Partial versus cervical pulpotomy. Two incisor sections: 2 mm amputation under the exposure with capping material, base and composite; amputation to the cervical third with a thick calcium silicate layer.

**Indications differ between books.** Partial pulpotomy within 15-18 hours (Gopikrishna, 2021) or within 24 hours (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010) vs neither exposure size nor time is critical when only superficial pulp is removed (Cvek, 2007); Cvek's view rests on his clinical series. Grossman lists irreversible pulpitis as a contraindication (Gopikrishna, 2021), whereas Diogenes, Botero and Kang (2021) perform a chamber pulpotomy for a heavily inflamed coronal pulp: the later books judge the radicular pulp by its bleeding.

**Medicament.** MTA/calcium silicate is better than calcium hydroxide in healing, seal and biocompatibility (Gopikrishna, 2021) and needs no re-entry (Bakland, 2007), vs calcium hydroxide as the most predictable long-term (Sikri, 2019); MTA can discolour (Kahler, 2021). Weine (2004) used formocresol, but it is for primary teeth only (Gopikrishna, 2021); it caused internal resorption in primates and is replaced by calcium silicates (Bogen and Chandler, 2019).

**Follow-up and after closure.** Radiographs and sensitivity tests every 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021); success means no symptoms or pathosis, a hard-tissue bridge and **continued root development** (Cvek, 2007), judged against the contralateral tooth (Bogen, Dammaschke and Chandler, 2021). Apexogenesis takes one to two years (Sikri, 2019); recall for at least 4 years (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002). If the pulp dies, start apexification (Gopikrishna, 2021). Cvek (2007) regards cervical pulpotomy as temporary, followed by pulpectomy once the root is complete, vs a definitive treatment in some cases (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021); histology does not support routine pulpectomy (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).

## 7. Management of the necrotic pulp (1): calcium hydroxide apexification

**Principle.** Introduced by Frank in 1966 (Shabahang, 2019). Calcium hydroxide (**pH 12.5**) disinfects, dissolves necrotic tissue and, against vital apical tissue, is followed by a hard-tissue barrier against which the canal is filled (Cvek, 2007). Pitt Ford and Shabahang (2002), however, call its inductive role a mistaken belief: thorough debridement and a coronal seal are what matter. Either way, **disinfection is indispensable** and no true root growth occurs (Gopikrishna, 2021; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**Technique**
1. Radiograph, rubber dam, straight-line access; working length from a radiograph, at least 2 mm short of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021).
2. Light circumferential filing and 0.5% NaOCl; Hedstrom filing is not advocated because thin walls may perforate (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002), although Weine (2004) rasps with a large Hedstrom.
3. Dry; creamy calcium hydroxide by Lentulo for at least 1 week and not more than 1 month (Kahler, 2021).
4. Then a thick paste with sterile water or saline packed to the apex until the canal is as radiodense as dentin (Kahler, 2021). **Radiopacifier:** barium sulphate 9:1 (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002) vs no radiopaquer, so washout can be seen (Kahler, 2021). Camphorated parachlorophenol is not justified (Cvek, 2007).
5. Seal the access with ZOE, IRM or RMGIC (Cvek, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021).
6. Review at 4-6 weeks (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002), then every 3 months.
7. When a barrier is felt with a file (often not visible radiographically), fill to it with softened gutta-percha (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).

**Duration varies:** 6-18 months (Cvek, 2007) vs 3-18 months (Kahler, 2021) vs 3 months to 2 years (Shabahang, 2019) vs 9-18 months (Sikri, 2019); Grossman gives both 6 months to 4 years and usually 6 months to 2 years (Gopikrishna, 2021). Closure is faster when the apical opening is narrower (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).

**Dressing change:** every 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs every 6-8 weeks (Sikri, 2019) vs every 3-6 months (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010) vs changing is counterproductive (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); Shabahang (2019) finds no consensus. Kahler (2021) and Pitt Ford and Shabahang (2002) replace it only when density falls.

**The barrier** is cementum-like or osteocementum tissue at, over or within the apex (Cvek, 2007; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011), porous with a **"Swiss cheese"** consistency (Kahler, 2021), so a tight coronal seal remains essential.

**Outcome and drawbacks.** Success 79-96%; periapical healing in 92% of 589 teeth (Cvek, 2007). Drawbacks: variable time, many visits, coronal leakage and reinfection, porous barrier and **cervical root fracture** (Shabahang, 2019); no lengthening or thickening of the root.

## 8. Management of the necrotic pulp (2): MTA / bioceramic apical plug

**Rationale.** An artificial barrier is placed at once instead of waiting for a calcified one; MTA itself is the apical stop, sets in moisture and blood, and cementum forms over it (Bakland, 2007; Shabahang, 2019).

**Technique**
1. Access, radiographic length, gentle debridement with low-strength NaOCl.
2. **Calcium hydroxide pre-medication:** for 7 to 14 days because set MTA has little antibacterial effect (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017), or a week to raise periapical pH (Gopikrishna, 2021); a dog study found it unnecessary (Shabahang, 2019).
3. Remove the dressing; a totally dry canal is not needed (Bakland, 2007).
4. **Matrix:** generally not required (Shabahang, 2019) vs collagen (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), calcium sulphate pushed through the apex (Kahler, 2021) or platelet-rich fibrin (Sikri, 2019).
5. Mix MTA 3:1 to wet-sand consistency (Gopikrishna, 2021); carry it in small increments and compact with pre-fitted pluggers, the smallest loose about 0.5 mm from working length. **Compaction:** ultrasonic activation on the plugger (Shabahang, 2019) vs manual, to limit extrusion (Kang and Bogen, 2017).
6. **Plug thickness:** 3-4 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs 3-5 mm (Shabahang, 2019) vs 4 mm (Bakland, 2007) vs 4-5 mm (Kahler, 2021) vs at least 5 mm (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). The figures meet at **4 mm**. Confirm radiographically; clean the walls with moist paper points.
7. Moist pellet and temporary seal; wait at least 4-6 hours (Bakland, 2007), or verify the set at 48 hours (Gopikrishna, 2021).
8. Backfill with thermoplasticised gutta-percha, or fill the whole canal with MTA in short roots (Bakland, 2007; Sikri, 2019).

**Outcome.** MTA healing 81-100% (Tonini and Garo, 2024); treatment complete in **0.75 months vs 7 months** for calcium hydroxide, with similar success in meta-analysis (Shabahang, 2019). Coronal discoloration occurred in 22.7% of one series (Shabahang, 2019). **Barrier time** of 5-20 months (Bogen, Dammaschke and Chandler, 2021) measures the later biological barrier, not the immediate artificial stop.

**Limitations.** No root lengthening or thickening; cervical fracture remains; long setting, cost and difficult handling (Tonini and Garo, 2024). **Extrusion:** overfilling is the major limitation (Tonini and Garo, 2024) and beyond 2 mm may retard healing (Shabahang, 2019), vs overfill does not compromise the outcome (Kang and Bogen, 2017).

**Diagram to draw:** Calcium hydroxide apexification vs MTA plug. Left: canal filled with calcium hydroxide, barrier at the apex after months, then gutta-percha. Right: collagen matrix, 4 mm MTA plug, gutta-percha backfill, bonded resin below the CEJ.

## 9. Regenerative endodontic procedures (outline)

The canal is disinfected without instrumentation and bleeding is induced so that apical papilla stem cells enter on a blood clot (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Case selection: Cvek stages 1-3 (Widbiller and Galler, 2024), apex at least 1 mm (Diogenes and Smith, 2019), compliant patient, no post needed (Kim, 2016), dental dam possible and ASA below 3 (Widbiller and Galler, 2024). Visit 1: 1.5% NaOCl, calcium hydroxide or triple antibiotic paste (minocycline stains). Visit 2: 3% mepivacaine without vasoconstrictor, 17% EDTA, bleeding to the CEJ, collagen matrix, calcium silicate cap and bonded restoration (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). If it fails, apexification is still possible (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).

## 11. Obturation after apexification

Fill **to the barrier, not to the radiographic apex**, with gentle pressure, because the calcium hydroxide barrier is porous and the walls thin (Kahler, 2021; Cvek, 2007). Options: thermoplasticised gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021); a master cone with only its tip softened, then lateral condensation (Cvek, 2007); custom or inverted cones for very wide canals (Weine, 2004; Sikri, 2019); after an MTA plug, backfill once it has set (Shabahang, 2019).

## 12. Restoration and reinforcement of thin roots

- Remove gutta-percha or MTA to below the CEJ and bond composite resin into the canal (Kahler, 2021; Bakland, 2007).
- Glass ionomer over MTA, then bonded composite or a fibre post; **metal posts are contraindicated** (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Tonini and Garo, 2024).
- Reinforcement evidence is laboratory (Bakland, 2007); retaining the tooth through growth preserves the alveolus (Cvek, 2007).

## 13. Prognosis, comparison and follow-up

| | Calcium hydroxide apexification | MTA apical plug | Regenerative procedure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Healing | 79-96% | 81-100% | nearly 100% (lesion resolution) |
| Time | 6-18 months or longer | 1-2 visits (0.75 months) | 2 visits; months for root change |
| Root length or thickness | No increase | No increase | Increase in 27-55%, variable |
| Fracture risk | High (cervical) | Thin root remains (Kim, 2016) | Lower if walls thicken |

**Cervical root fracture** falls from 77% in the least developed roots to 2% in mature teeth (Cvek, 2007); about 30% in another series (Kahler, 2021).

**Criteria** (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002). Success after root-end closure: no signs or symptoms and a calcific barrier shown radiographically or by tactile probing. Failure is mainly from **bacterial contamination**: persistent signs, no barrier, continual loss of calcium hydroxide, bleeding granulation tissue short of the apex; later, bacteria trapped in the barrier or an undetected root fracture. Review at 3-6 months, then yearly for at least 4 years (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002; Sikri, 2019).

## 14. Critical appraisal of the evidence

1. **Does long-term calcium hydroxide cause cervical fracture?** It weakens dentin beyond one month (Shabahang, 2019) and younger teeth fracture more (Bakland, 2007); 23% fractured within 18 months (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021). However, Cvek (2007) relates fracture chiefly to root stage, with calcium hydroxide one of several factors, Kahler (2021) calls the evidence inconclusive and Gulabivala and Ng (2014) limit the effect to the surface. Weakening data are laboratory; clinical rates are uncontrolled series. Limiting the dressing to 1-4 weeks is reasonable, but no textbook shows clinically that plugs prevent fracture; the thin root is the main cause.
2. **Is the MTA plug more successful?** Individual series favour MTA, but meta-analyses found no significant difference in clinical or radiographic success (Shabahang, 2019; Tonini and Garo, 2024), pooling only four studies; bioceramics in one visit give similar results (Ørstavik, 2020). The correct statement is **equal healing, far shorter treatment**; choice rests on time, cost, cooperation and discoloration.
3. **How often should calcium hydroxide be changed?** Recommendations conflict (section 7) and rest on old, small studies; review at 3 months and replace only on washout.
4. **Does regeneration succeed in nearly 100%?** That figure is lesion resolution (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021); root growth occurs in only 27-55% (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017), outcome is variable (Kahler, 2021) and the tissue is repair rather than pulp (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Periapical healing and survival do not differ between apexification, plug and revitalisation (Widbiller and Galler, 2024).

## 15. Conclusion

Preserve the pulp whenever possible, because only a vital pulp completes the root. When the pulp is lost, disinfect chemically and gently, place a barrier (an MTA/bioceramic plug is as successful as calcium hydroxide and far quicker), consider regeneration in very immature roots, fill to the barrier, reinforce the cervical root with bonded resin and review for at least 4 years.
