# Open Apex – considerations and management.

**Marks:** 75 · Single essay

## 1. Introduction and definitions

An **open apex** is a root apex that has not formed, or has lost its constriction, so that the canal has **no apical stop**. The canal lacks a conical taper and is wider toward the apex than at the cervix, the **"blunderbuss" canal** (Weine, 2004), whose apical diameter exceeds the coronal (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). An open apex is normal in a developing root; it becomes a clinical problem when the pulp dies before the root is complete, or when a mature apex is opened by resorption (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002). Management depends on one question: **is the pulp vital?**

- **Apexogenesis** (now "vital pulp therapy", Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002): a vital pulp therapy procedure performed to encourage continued physiological development and formation of the root end (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Sikri, 2019).
- **Apexification** (now "root-end closure"): a method to induce a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex of an immature, pulpless tooth (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Apical barrier:** blockage of the apical foramen by induced hard tissue or an artificial material (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). The MTA apical plug is called **one-step apexification** (Tonini and Garo, 2024).
- **Regenerative endodontics:** biologically based replacement of dentin, root structure and pulp-dentin cells (American Association of Endodontists, 2012).

## 2. Root development and its stages

**Hertwig's epithelial root sheath (HERS)** is the template for odontoblast differentiation and root dentin; it lengthens the root and closes the apical foramen (Shabahang, 2019), with the apical papilla (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Root formation continues for about **3 years after eruption** (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021; Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002), whereas Kang and Bogen (2017) give **1 to 5 years**. Without a vital pulp the root cannot complete development (Shabahang, 2019); odontoblasts rarely survive necrosis (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011), so **apexification produces a barrier, not a root**.

**Stages of root development** (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021):

| Stage | Root | Apex |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | one-fourth formed | blunderbuss |
| 2 | half formed | blunderbuss |
| 3 | three-fourth formed | blunderbuss |
| 4 | full length | open |
| 5 | full length | partially closed |
| 6 | full length | closed |

Cvek's scale has five stages, 1-4 maturing and 5 complete (Widbiller and Galler, 2024). The earlier the stage at which the pulp dies, the shorter and thinner the root and the higher the fracture risk (Cvek, 2007).

## 3. Causes and types of open apex

Causes are grouped below; Pitt Ford and Shabahang (2002) separate the immature apex from a resorbed mature apex.

| Group | Cause | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Developmental (pulp dies before the root is complete) | Trauma: crown fracture with exposure, luxation, intrusion, avulsion | Intrusion most likely needs endodontics (Shabahang, 2019) |
| | Caries in young permanent teeth | First molars; **molar-incisor hypomineralisation** (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010) |
| | Developmental anomalies | **Dens evaginatus**: tubercle wears, pulp exposed (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) |
| Pathological (formed apex opened) | Apical inflammatory root resorption | Blunderbuss terminus (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) |
| | Internal resorption, apex wider than ISO 50-60 | Apical plug indications (Tonini and Garo, 2024) |
| | Orthodontic apical resorption | Mature apex opened (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002) |
| Iatrogenic | Over-instrumentation beyond the apex | Creates open apices (Shabahang, 2019) |
| | Incorrect working length | Zipping or "blowing out" of the foramen (Nosrat, Terauchi and Torabinejad, 2021) |
| | Root-end resection | Orthograde plug indicated (Tonini and Garo, 2024) |

**Types by canal shape:** (1) **divergent (blunderbuss)**; (2) **parallel**; (3) **slightly convergent** with a wide foramen; the shape follows the stage of root formation (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002; Tonini and Garo, 2024). The buccolingual dimension is greater than the mesiodistal and is the last to converge (Shabahang, 2019), so a canal that looks parallel on the radiograph may be divergent buccolingually.

## 4. Considerations: why the open apex is difficult

1. **Pulp diagnosis is unreliable.** Pulp tests are less reliable in children; **cold** is the most reliable test and the electric pulp test is less accurate in immature apices (Shabahang, 2019; Sikri, 2019). Tests assess nerves, not circulation; after trauma blood flow may take up to **9 months** to return (Kahler, 2021). Laser Doppler flowmetry measures flow (Shabahang, 2019). Treat the pulp as vital until **at least two signs** of necrosis and infection are present (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).
2. **Radiographic interpretation.** **CBCT** shows the buccolingual anatomy (Shabahang, 2019). A lesion resembles the normal papilla (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010), but the normal zone has a **corticated margin**; compare the contralateral tooth (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).
3. **Thin, short roots.** Thin cervical walls fracture after later trauma (Shabahang, 2019; Cvek, 2007).
4. **Disinfection without weakening.** Debridement is chiefly **chemical** (Shabahang, 2019); brush the walls with a cotton-wrapped file (Bakland, 2007) and keep vital apical tissue (Cvek, 2007). **Irrigant strength differs:** 0.5% NaOCl in volume, because of extrusion through the apex (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021), vs 1.5% NaOCl for 15-20 minutes (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) vs 5% NaOCl (Sikri, 2019).
5. **Working length.** Apex locators are inaccurate; use radiographs (Shabahang, 2019). End-points differ: at least **2 mm short** of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs plug 1 mm from the radiographic apex (Tonini and Garo, 2024) vs the level where bleeding is first met (Bakland, 2007).
6. **No apical stop:** material extrudes (Kahler, 2021).
7. **Patient factors:** cooperation and drop-out over many visits (Shabahang, 2019).

## 5. Treatment planning

A vital pulp must be maintained so the apex can complete (Shabahang, 2019; Kahler, 2021); otherwise the choice depends on the **level of apical development** (Shabahang, 2019).

| Pulp status | Root stage | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Vital, reversible (exposure) | Immature | Apexogenesis: pulp capping, partial (Cvek) pulpotomy, cervical pulpotomy |
| Heavily inflamed coronal pulp, radicular pulp bleeding normally | Immature | Cervical pulpotomy to fresh bleeding tissue |
| Necrotic | Cvek stages 1-3, apex at least 1 mm, no post needed | Regenerative endodontic procedure, or barrier |
| Necrotic | Stage 4 onward or near complete | MTA/bioceramic apical plug; calcium hydroxide apexification |
| Necrotic | Mature root opened by resorption or iatrogenically | Apical plug and obturation; surgery if this fails |

**Indications and contraindications** (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002). *Vital pulp therapy*: radicular pulp presumed healthy and a restorable crown; not for avulsed or severely luxated teeth, crown-root fractures needing a post, unfavourable horizontal root fractures or unrestorable caries. *Root-end closure*: a restorable necrotic immature tooth; not with vertical or most horizontal root fractures, **replacement resorption**, very short roots or marginal periodontal breakdown. Surgery is best avoided in children.

**Diagram to draw:** Decision flowchart. Pulp vital? Yes → capping/partial/cervical pulpotomy. No → stages 1-3: REP or barrier; stage 4 on: plug or apexification.

## 6. Management of the vital pulp: apexogenesis

**Rationale.** After traumatic exposure inflammation stays within the surface **2 mm** (Shabahang, 2019; Kahler, 2021). Goals: keep HERS vital, thicken the walls, form a natural constriction and a dentin bridge (Sikri, 2019).

1. **Pulp capping:** small exposure treated within about 24 hours (Cvek, 2007).
2. **Partial (Cvek) pulpotomy:** Rubber dam; high-speed diamond with copious cooling; amputate about **2 mm below the exposure**; saline; avoid an extrapulpal clot (Cvek, 2007). Profuse persistent bleeding means deeper inflammation (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Cap with calcium hydroxide, MTA or Biodentine (MTA may be placed on slightly bleeding pulp) and bond (Bakland, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021). Success **94-96%** (Kahler, 2021); 95% vs 80% for direct capping (Gopikrishna, 2021).
3. **Cervical (full) pulpotomy:** for deeper inflammation, exposures over 72 hours or carious exposures (Kahler, 2021). Amputate to the cervical third without twisting the stump (Gopikrishna, 2021), to fresh bleeding tissue (Cvek, 2007). Cover with **more than 4 mm** of tricalcium silicate (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), or calcium hydroxide 1-2 mm / MTA at least 2 mm under an RMGIC base (Gopikrishna, 2021). Success about **75%** (Kahler, 2021).

**Diagram to draw:** Partial versus cervical pulpotomy. Two incisors: 2 mm amputation vs cervical-third amputation; label cap, base, composite.

**Indications differ between books.** Partial pulpotomy within 15-18 hours (Gopikrishna, 2021) or within 24 hours (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010) vs neither exposure size nor time is critical when only superficial pulp is removed (Cvek, 2007); Cvek's view rests on his clinical series. Irreversible pulpitis is a contraindication (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs a chamber pulpotomy for a heavily inflamed pulp (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**Medicament.** MTA/calcium silicate is better than calcium hydroxide in healing, seal and biocompatibility (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs calcium hydroxide most predictable (Sikri, 2019); MTA can discolour (Kahler, 2021). Weine (2004) used formocresol, but it is for primary teeth only (Gopikrishna, 2021); it caused internal resorption in primates and is replaced by calcium silicates (Bogen and Chandler, 2019).

**Follow-up and after closure.** Review every 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021); success means **continued root development** (Cvek, 2007) matching the contralateral tooth (Bogen, Dammaschke and Chandler, 2021); recall at least 4 years (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002). If the pulp dies, start apexification (Gopikrishna, 2021). Cvek (2007) regards cervical pulpotomy as temporary, followed by pulpectomy once the root is complete, vs histology does not support routine pulpectomy (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).

## 7. Management of the necrotic pulp (1): calcium hydroxide apexification

**Principle.** Introduced by Frank in 1966 (Shabahang, 2019). Calcium hydroxide (**pH 12.5**) disinfects and, against vital apical tissue, a hard-tissue barrier forms (Cvek, 2007). Pitt Ford and Shabahang (2002), however, call its inductive role a mistaken belief: thorough debridement and a coronal seal are what matter. No true root growth occurs (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**Technique**
1. Rubber dam, access; radiographic length, at least 2 mm short of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021).
2. Light circumferential filing and 0.5% NaOCl; Hedstrom filing is not advocated because thin walls may perforate (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002), although Weine (2004) rasps with a large Hedstrom.
3. Creamy calcium hydroxide by Lentulo for at least 1 week and not more than 1 month (Kahler, 2021).
4. Then thick paste packed to the apex (Kahler, 2021). **Radiopacifier:** barium sulphate 9:1 (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002) vs no radiopaquer, so washout can be seen (Kahler, 2021).
5. Seal the access with ZOE, IRM or RMGIC (Cvek, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021).
6. Review at 4-6 weeks (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002), then every 3 months.
7. When a barrier is felt with a file, fill to it with softened gutta-percha (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).

**Duration varies:** 6-18 months (Cvek, 2007) vs 3-18 months (Kahler, 2021) vs 3 months to 2 years (Shabahang, 2019) vs 9-18 months (Sikri, 2019). Closure is faster when the apical opening is narrower (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).

**Dressing change:** every 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs every 6-8 weeks (Sikri, 2019) vs every 3-6 months (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010) vs changing is counterproductive (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); Shabahang (2019) finds no consensus. Kahler (2021) replaces it only on washout.

**The barrier** is cementum-like or osteocementum tissue at, over or within the apex (Cvek, 2007; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011), porous, **"Swiss cheese"** (Kahler, 2021).

**Outcome and drawbacks.** Success 79-96%; periapical healing in 92% of 589 teeth (Cvek, 2007). Drawbacks: many visits, reinfection and **cervical root fracture** (Shabahang, 2019).

## 8. Management of the necrotic pulp (2): MTA / bioceramic apical plug

**Rationale.** An artificial barrier is placed at once instead of waiting for a calcified one; MTA sets in moisture and blood (Bakland, 2007; Shabahang, 2019).

**Technique**
1. **Calcium hydroxide pre-medication:** for 7 to 14 days because set MTA has little antibacterial effect (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017), or a week to raise periapical pH (Gopikrishna, 2021); a dog study found it unnecessary (Shabahang, 2019).
2. A totally dry canal is not needed (Bakland, 2007).
3. **Matrix:** generally not required (Shabahang, 2019) vs collagen (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), calcium sulphate pushed through the apex (Kahler, 2021) or platelet-rich fibrin (Sikri, 2019).
4. Mix MTA 3:1 (Gopikrishna, 2021); compact with pre-fitted pluggers. **Compaction:** ultrasonic activation on the plugger (Shabahang, 2019) vs manual, to limit extrusion (Kang and Bogen, 2017).
5. **Plug thickness:** 3-4 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs 3-5 mm (Shabahang, 2019) vs 4 mm (Bakland, 2007) vs 4-5 mm (Kahler, 2021) vs at least 5 mm (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Consensus **4 mm**.
6. Moist pellet; wait at least 4-6 hours (Bakland, 2007), or verify the set at 48 hours (Gopikrishna, 2021).
7. Backfill with thermoplasticised gutta-percha, or fill the whole canal with MTA in short roots (Bakland, 2007; Sikri, 2019).

**Outcome.** MTA healing 81-100% (Tonini and Garo, 2024); treatment complete in **0.75 months vs 7 months** for calcium hydroxide, with similar success in meta-analysis (Shabahang, 2019). **Barrier time** of 5-20 months (Bogen, Dammaschke and Chandler, 2021) measures the later biological barrier, not the immediate artificial stop.

**Limitations.** No root growth; cost and handling (Tonini and Garo, 2024). **Extrusion:** overfilling is the major limitation (Tonini and Garo, 2024) and beyond 2 mm may retard healing (Shabahang, 2019), vs overfill does not compromise the outcome (Kang and Bogen, 2017).

**Diagram to draw:** Calcium hydroxide apexification vs MTA plug. Left: calcium hydroxide, barrier, gutta-percha. Right: matrix, 4 mm plug, backfill, resin below CEJ.

## 9. Regenerative endodontic procedures (outline)

The canal is disinfected without instrumentation and bleeding is induced so that apical papilla stem cells enter on a blood clot (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Select Cvek stages 1-3 (Widbiller and Galler, 2024), apex at least 1 mm (Diogenes and Smith, 2019), no post needed (Kim, 2016). Visit 1: 1.5% NaOCl, calcium hydroxide or triple antibiotic paste (minocycline stains). Visit 2: 3% mepivacaine without vasoconstrictor, 17% EDTA, bleeding to the CEJ, calcium silicate cap (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). If it fails, apexification is still possible (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).

## 10. Materials used in the management of open apex

| Use | Material | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Disinfection and apexification | **Calcium hydroxide** | Antibacterial; prolonged use may weaken dentin (Shabahang, 2019) |
| | Triple antibiotic paste | Metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, minocycline; stains (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) |
| Artificial apical barrier | **MTA** | Hydrates in blood (Gopikrishna, 2021); discolours |
| | **Biodentine** | Sets in about 10 minutes (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs 12 minutes (Tonini and Garo, 2024) ; radiopacity "excellent" (Gopikrishna, 2021) vs less than MTA (Tonini and Garo, 2024) |
| | Bioceramic putty, CEM cement | Zirconium oxide radiopacifier (Tonini and Garo, 2024) |
| | Older: tricalcium phosphate, dentin chips, freeze-dried bone | Less effective (Gopikrishna, 2021) |
| Matrix | Collagen, calcium sulphate, PRF | Resorbable |
| Temporary seal | ZOE, IRM, RMGIC, Cavit | Prevents reinfection |
| Obturation and restoration | Thermoplasticised gutta-percha; bonded composite; fibre post | Sections 11-12 |

## 11. Obturation after apexification

Fill **to the barrier, not to the radiographic apex**, with gentle pressure, because the calcium hydroxide barrier is porous and the walls thin (Kahler, 2021; Cvek, 2007). Use thermoplasticised gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021) or custom cones (Weine, 2004).

## 12. Restoration and reinforcement of thin roots

- Remove gutta-percha or MTA to below the CEJ and bond composite resin into the canal (Kahler, 2021; Bakland, 2007).
- Glass ionomer over MTA, then bonded composite or a fibre post; **metal posts are contraindicated** (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Tonini and Garo, 2024).

## 13. Prognosis, comparison and follow-up

| | Calcium hydroxide apexification | MTA apical plug | Regenerative procedure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Healing | 79-96% | 81-100% | nearly 100% (lesion resolution) |
| Time | 6-18 months or longer | 1-2 visits (0.75 months) | 2 visits; months for root change |
| Root length or thickness | No increase | No increase | Increase in 27-55%, variable |
| Fracture risk | High (cervical) | Thin root remains (Kim, 2016) | Lower if walls thicken |

**Cervical root fracture** falls from 77% in the least developed roots to 2% in mature teeth (Cvek, 2007); about 30% in another series (Kahler, 2021).

**Criteria** (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002). Success: no signs and a barrier shown radiographically or by probing. Failure is mainly **bacterial contamination**: no barrier, continual calcium hydroxide loss, bleeding short of the apex. Review at 3-6 months, then yearly for at least 4 years (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002; Sikri, 2019).

## 14. Critical appraisal of the evidence

1. **Does long-term calcium hydroxide cause cervical fracture?** It weakens dentin beyond one month (Shabahang, 2019; Bakland, 2007). However, Cvek (2007) relates fracture chiefly to root stage, with calcium hydroxide one of several factors, Kahler (2021) calls the evidence inconclusive and Gulabivala and Ng (2014) limit the effect to the surface. Weakening data are laboratory; no textbook shows clinically that plugs prevent fracture.
2. **Is the MTA plug more successful?** Individual series favour MTA, but meta-analyses found no significant difference in clinical or radiographic success (Shabahang, 2019; Tonini and Garo, 2024); bioceramics give similar results (Ørstavik, 2020). The correct statement is **equal healing, far shorter treatment**.
3. **How often should calcium hydroxide be changed?** Recommendations conflict (section 7) and rest on old, small studies; review at 3 months and replace only on washout.
4. **Does regeneration succeed in nearly 100%?** That figure is lesion resolution (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021); root growth occurs in only 27-55% (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017), and the tissue is repair, not pulp. Periapical healing and survival do not differ between apexification, plug and revitalisation (Widbiller and Galler, 2024).

## 15. Conclusion

Preserve the pulp whenever possible; when it is lost, disinfect gently, place a barrier (a plug equals calcium hydroxide and is far quicker), consider regeneration in very immature roots, and reinforce the cervical root.
