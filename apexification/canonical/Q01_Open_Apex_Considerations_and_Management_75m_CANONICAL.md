## 1. Introduction and definitions

### Explanation

**What "open apex" means.** Weine defines it by shape: "An open apex refers to the absence of" sufficient root development to give the canal a conical taper; the canal "is wider toward the apex than near the cervical area" and is called a blunderbuss canal (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Sikri opens his apexification section with the same image: "Endodontic management of the pulpless permanent teeth with a wide open blunderbuss apex has always been challenging" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng label the radiograph of such a tooth "Arrested root formation resulting in an open apex" (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. The AAE Glossary gives the same idea for the blunderbuss canal: "Term denoting an incompletely formed root in which the apical diameter of the pulp canal is greater than the coronal diameter" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. So "open apex" is a descriptive clinical term, not a diagnosis: it says nothing about whether the pulp is alive. The two treatments that follow depend entirely on that second question. [Certain]

**Apexogenesis.** The Glossary and Sikri use the same words: "a vital pulp therapy procedure performed to encourage continued physiological development and formation of the root end" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's table defines it as the "treatment of a vital pulp by capping or pulpotomy in order to permit continued growth of the root and closure of the open apex" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri adds that apexogenesis "can be regarded as a very deep pulpotomy" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], which is narrower than Grossman's capping-or-pulpotomy definition. Torabinejad uses "maturogenesis" for continued root maturation after partial pulpotomy (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC].

**Apexification.** The Glossary: "A method to induce a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex or the continued" apical development of an incompletely formed root in teeth with necrotic pulps (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Grossman: "a method to induce a calcific barrier across an open apex of an immature, pulpless tooth" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri stresses mineralised tissue "in the apical pulp region of a nonvital tooth" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. The definitions agree on the necrotic pulp; the Glossary alone keeps "continued apical development" inside apexification, which other authors place under apexogenesis or regeneration. This is a terminology difference, not a factual conflict. [Likely]

**Apical barrier and one-step apexification.** The Glossary defines an apical barrier as "A blockage of the apical foramen; may be an induced hard tissue or artificial" material such as dentin chips, collagen, MTA or calcium hydroxide, and the apical barrier technique as "Placement of a matrix in the apical region to prevent extrusion of" filling materials (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Tonini and Garo call the apical plug, "also known as one-step apexification", a technique for immature necrotic teeth "to create an apical barrier and achieve adequate stopping" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Regenerative endodontics is defined by the Glossary as "Biologically-based procedures designed to physiologically replace" damaged dentin, root structure and cells of the pulp-dentin complex (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** An open apex is a root apex that has not formed or has lost its constriction, so the canal has no apical stop. Weine describes it as the absence of enough root development to give the canal a conical taper; the canal is wider toward the apex than at the cervix, the "blunderbuss" canal (Weine, 2004). The AAE Glossary defines a blunderbuss canal as an incompletely formed root whose apical canal diameter is greater than its coronal diameter (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). Management depends on one question: is the pulp vital?

**Definitions (AAE Glossary, 2012).**
- **Apexogenesis:** a vital pulp therapy procedure performed to encourage continued physiological development and formation of the root end (also Sikri, 2019); Grossman: capping or pulpotomy of a vital pulp to permit continued root growth and apical closure (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Apexification:** a method to induce a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex, or continued apical development, in teeth with necrotic pulps; Grossman: in an immature, pulpless tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- **Apical barrier:** a blockage of the apical foramen by induced hard tissue or an artificial material (dentin chips, collagen, MTA, calcium hydroxide). The MTA apical plug is called one-step apexification (Tonini and Garo, 2024).
- **Regenerative endodontics:** biologically based procedures designed to replace damaged dentin, root structure and cells of the pulp-dentin complex.

## 2. Root development and stages

### Explanation

**How a root forms.** The inner and outer enamel epithelia fuse at the cervical loop and grow apically as Hertwig's epithelial root sheath (HERS), which "acts as the stimulus and template for the di.erentiation of odontoblasts and formation of root dentin" and "it is responsible for increasing root length and closure of the apical foramen" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad describes the same process as requiring "the complex interaction of the epithelial root sheath and mesenchymal cells located in the dental apical papilla" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. HERS outlines the root, but "without a vital pulp, root development cannot continue to completion" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; loss of odontoblasts in the radicular pulp "results in arrested tooth development" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Gutmann adds that odontoblasts "rarely if ever survive pulpal necrosis" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC], which is why apexification produces a barrier, not a root. Cohen's calls teeth treated by apexification "in a state of “arrested development,”" with no further root growth, nociception or immune defence expected (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC].

**How long it takes.** Torabinejad says root development continues 2 to 3 years after eruption (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's says "full radicular maturation occurs up to 3 years after the eruption of a tooth in the oral cavity" (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology places the problem in the mixed dentition, patients of about 5-13 years, where root formation is incomplete for some years after eruption (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Ingle's adds that facial injuries "between the ages of 6 and 14 years" can interrupt, alter or arrest root development (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

**Stages.** Cohen's Fig. 12.1 gives six stages: "Stage 1: One-fourth root formation with "blunderbuss" apex", stage 2 half and stage 3 three-fourth root formation with a blunderbuss apex, "Stage 4: full development with open apex", stage 5 full development with a partially closed apex and "Stage 6: Full development with closed apex" (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek's fracture data are given by stage of root development (Cvek, 2007) [SRC], but the stage definitions are in a figure not reproduced in the extract; that Cvek used a five-stage scale is [RECALL]. Nolla's stages are not described in any book read; if an examiner asks for them, write them from memory and label them as such. [Certain]

**Why the stage matters clinically.** Tonini and Garo describe the canal walls of an immature root as "parallel, convergent, or divergent depending on root development" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC], and Cvek's cervical-fracture risk falls as the stage advances (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. The stage therefore decides both the technique (barrier versus conventional preparation) and the prognosis. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Root formation.** Hertwig's epithelial root sheath (HERS) is the template for odontoblast differentiation and root dentin; it lengthens the root and closes the apical foramen (Shabahang, 2019). Root formation needs HERS plus the mesenchymal cells of the apical papilla (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Root formation continues for up to 3 years after eruption (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021). Without a vital pulp the root cannot complete development; loss of radicular odontoblasts arrests it, leaving a short root with thin walls and an open apex (Shabahang, 2019; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**Stages of root development** (Cohen's, Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021):

| Stage | Root | Apex |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | one-fourth formed | blunderbuss |
| 2 | half formed | blunderbuss |
| 3 | three-fourth formed | blunderbuss |
| 4 | full length | open |
| 5 | full length | partially closed |
| 6 | full length | closed |

The earlier the stage at which the pulp dies, the thinner and shorter the root and the higher the fracture risk (Cvek, 2007).

## 3. Causes and types of open apex

### Explanation

**No book gives a single classification.** The causes below are collected from several books; the grouping into developmental, pathological and iatrogenic is this answer's arrangement, offered because the 2018 ABS wording asks for "the cause". [Likely]

**Developmental (immature apex; the common type).** Weine: "The open apex case occurs when trauma or decay causes pulpal" exposure or periapical involvement before root development is complete (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Cohen's lists "trauma, caries, and developmental dental anomalies such as dens evaginatus" (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad describes dens evaginatus, where necrosis after wear of the tubercle "often occurs in pediatric patients with incomplete root development and open apices", and caries reaching the pulp of "any permanent teeth" at any stage of root development (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology: anterior cases are usually post-traumatic, while in posterior segments first molars fail early from caries or enamel defects such as "molar–incisor hypomineralization [MIH]" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Ingle's points to intrusion: "teeth that have a history of intrusive injuries will most likely require endodontic therapy" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Cvek adds that a concomitant luxation compromises the pulp's supply, though "in luxated immature teeth the chance of pulp survival is considerable" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

**Pathological (a formed apex that has been opened).** Gulabivala and Ng: "When apical root resorption is evident, the canal terminus may be" blunderbuss-shaped and allow extrusion of materials (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Tonini and Garo list among apical-plug indications an apex "greater than 50–60 ISO in diameter", irregular apical morphology, "canal morphology with parallel walls, (5) internal resorptions" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. In a retrospective MTA study reported by Ingle's, some of the open-apex teeth were not immature: "remainder were mature teeth that had open apices" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

**Iatrogenic.** Over-instrumentation produces an open apex: Ingle's describes experiments that "created open apices in incisor teeth of monkeys by over-instrumenting canals" and that "over-instrumented the cuspid teeth to simulate open apices" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Tonini and Garo also list "orthograde root canal filling in case of subsequent apical surgery" as an indication (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC], i.e. a resected root end. Clinical apical zipping and foraminal enlargement during routine treatment as causes are [RECALL], not stated in these chapters; see Procedural Errors for transportation.

**Types by shape.** By wall configuration the books describe the blunderbuss (divergent) canal (Weine, 2004; American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC] and parallel or convergent walls (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Ingle's warns that the radiograph misleads: "the buccal-lingual dimension of the root canal is typically greater than the mesio-distal dimension", and the buccolingual aspect "is the last dimension to become convergent as the root develops" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. So a canal that looks parallel mesiodistally may be divergent buccolingually. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Causes of open apex** (grouped from Weine, 2004; Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Baumann and Beer, 2010; Gulabivala and Ng, 2014; Tonini and Garo, 2024; Shabahang, 2019):

| Group | Cause | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Developmental (pulp dies before root completes) | Trauma: crown fracture with exposure, luxation, intrusion, avulsion | Ages 6-14 years; intrusion most often needs endodontics (Shabahang, 2019) |
| | Caries in young permanent teeth | First molars; MIH (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |
| | Developmental anomalies | Dens evaginatus: tubercle wears, pulp exposed (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) |
| Pathological (formed apex opened) | Apical inflammatory root resorption | Blunderbuss terminus (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) |
| | Internal resorption reaching the apex | An apical plug indication (Tonini and Garo, 2024) |
| Iatrogenic | Over-instrumentation beyond the apex | Used experimentally to create open apices (Shabahang, 2019) |
| | Root-end resection (apical surgery) | Orthograde plug indicated (Tonini and Garo, 2024) |

**Types by canal shape.** (1) Blunderbuss/divergent: apical diameter greater than coronal (American Association of Endodontists, 2012); (2) parallel walls; (3) convergent walls with a wide foramen (Tonini and Garo, 2024). The buccolingual dimension is wider than the mesiodistal and is the last to converge, so a periapical film may show a closed apex that is open buccolingually (Shabahang, 2019).

## 4. Considerations: why the open apex is difficult

### Explanation

**Diagnosis of pulp status.** Ingle's: "Less reliable pulp testing data pose a special challenge in children"; communication and apprehension limit history; the response is less predictable; the "most reliable test may be cold testing" while "electric pulp testing in teeth with immature apices can result in less accurate results"; determination of "pulpal blood" flow (laser Doppler) may be more accurate, with gingival interference reduced by dental dam (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's explains why: thermal and electric tests "are in reality sensitivity tests for nerve function and do not indicate the presence or absence of blood circulation", and after trauma "it may take as long as 9 months for normal blood flow to return to the coronal pulp" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's names carbon dioxide snow or dichlorodifluoromethane at the incisal edge as "the best methods of sensitivity testing, particularly in young" teeth (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri lists "Young teeth with immature apices" among causes of false electric pulp test readings (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's therefore treats the pulp as vital until there are "at least two signs and symptoms indicating pulp necrosis and infection" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. The books agree; they differ only in the preferred cold agent. [Certain]

**Radiographs and CBCT.** Root maturity is judged radiographically, but the buccolingual width problem (section 3) makes the periapical film unreliable, and "A more complete and accurate visualization of anatomic structures is now possible with the use of 3-dimensional CT imaging" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Cvek: "Wide or funnel-shaped root canals make endodontic treatment in immature teeth difficult" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Endodontology speaks of "the inverted funnel-shaped canal lumen and the lack of an apical constriction" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Endodontology adds a reading trap: it is often difficult to tell a periapical radiolucency from the normal radiolucent zone of an incompletely formed root ("Radiographically it is often diffi" cult, Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

**Thin walls and fracture.** Without a vital pulp dentin does not thicken, and thin cervical dentin "in the cervical region teeth is more susceptible to fracture if subsequent traumatic injury occurs" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Cvek: removing the pulp in children "leaves thin dentinal walls, which may increase the risk of later cervical root fractures" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Cohen's: "Loss of vitality in an immature tooth can have catastrophic consequences" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC].

**Disinfection without instrumentation.** Ingle's: "debridement should be achieved primarily through chemical means" and "Minimal mechanical instrumentation is required because the canals are already wide" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Bakland: "Instead of aggressively filing the thin root canal walls, it is preferable to gently brush the walls" with a cotton-wrapped file and plenty of hypochlorite (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]; Weine uses the same cotton-wrapped broach and a large Hedstrom file to rasp the walls (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Cvek warns against removing vital apical tissue: "the vital tissue present in the apical part of the pulpal lumen should not be removed" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Endodontology: "Caution: overinstrumentation may compromise the chances for success!" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Yet disinfection cannot be skipped: "remaining bacteria in the root canal system will lead to continued irritation that can interfere with root-end development" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

**Irrigant concentration: the books differ.** Cvek and Cohen's use 0.5% NaOCl; Cohen's explains "A lower strength of NaOCl is used because of the increased danger of placing the agent through the apex in immature teeth", compensated by volume (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's apexification protocol uses 1.5% NaOCl for 15 to 20 minutes (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri rinses with 5% NaOCl and 17% EDTA before the MTA plug (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's warns that NaOCl exposure over 20 minutes produces structural changes "that may ultimately result in weakening of the root dentin" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. These are different protocols, not contradictory facts: lower concentrations reflect the extrusion risk through a wide foramen. [Likely]

**Working length.** Ingle's: "Electronic apex locators are not accurate in immature teeth or teeth with wide open apices"; radiographic length is recommended, and extrusion "might damage the periapical tissues including the remaining and vital HERS" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman sets the working length "at least 2 mm short of the radiographic apex" for both calcium hydroxide and MTA (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], whereas Ingle's sizes the smallest plugger to about 0.5 mm from the working length and Tonini and Garo place the plug "1 mm from the radiographic apex" (Shabahang, 2019; Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Bakland stops at the level where bleeding is first met (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. The figures differ because the authors define the end-point differently (radiographic apex versus vital tissue). [Likely]

**Extrusion, sealing and the patient.** Cohen's: in the wide apex "no barrier exists to stop this softened material from moving into and traumatizing the apical periodontal tissues" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology mentions limited cooperation in young children and, for molars, the need for orthodontic advice (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Ingle's: long calcium hydroxide therapy "increases the risks of patient attrition" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Diagnosis of pulp status**
- Pulp tests are less reliable in children; cold is the most reliable test, the electric pulp test is less accurate in immature apices (Shabahang, 2019; Sikri, 2019). They test nerve function, not blood flow; after trauma blood flow may take up to 9 months to return (Kahler, 2021). Laser Doppler flowmetry measures blood flow; use dental dam to exclude gingival signal (Shabahang, 2019).
- Consider the pulp vital until at least two signs of necrosis and infection are present (pain, swelling, sinus, mobility, tenderness, radiolucency, discoloration, persistent no response, arrested root development) (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).

**2. Radiographs and CBCT.** The buccolingual width is greater than the mesiodistal; a "closed" apex on a periapical film may be open buccolingually; 3D imaging (CBCT) shows it (Shabahang, 2019). A periapical lesion may be hard to tell from the normal radiolucent papilla (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**3. Thin, short roots.** Dentin does not thicken without a pulp; the cervical root is fracture-prone (Shabahang, 2019; Cvek, 2007).

**4. Disinfection without weakening.** Chemical rather than mechanical debridement; light circumferential filing or a cotton-wrapped file (Shabahang, 2019; Bakland, 2007; Weine, 2004). Do not remove vital apical tissue (Cvek, 2007). NaOCl 0.5% (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021) or 1.5% for 15-20 minutes (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021): low strength because of extrusion through the apex.

**5. Working length.** Apex locators are inaccurate; use radiographic length (Shabahang, 2019); Grossman sets it at least 2 mm short of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**6. No apical stop.** Filling materials extrude into the periapical tissues and may damage HERS (Kahler, 2021; Shabahang, 2019).

**7. Patient factors.** Young age, cooperation, multiple visits and drop-out; growth and space maintenance (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Shabahang, 2019).

## 5. Treatment planning: the decision

### Explanation

Ingle's: when the pulp is vital and the apex unformed, "it is imperative to make every attempt to maintain the vitality of the pulp in order for the apex to complete its formation" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Weine recalls that before apexification the necrotic blunderbuss apex "was treated surgically" by apical curettage and a retrograde filling, a poor option in children (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Ingle's decision algorithm (Fig. 28-9) puts it in two lines: "in immature teeth with vital pulps, pulp vitality must be preserved"; if the pulp is irreversibly inflamed or necrotic, the choice between conventional root canal treatment and root-end closure "is based on the level of apical root development" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Cvek agrees that "the exposed vital pulp should be maintained in young teeth with incomplete root formation" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC], and Cohen's: "In an immature tooth, vital pulp therapy should always be attempted if at all feasible" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's gives the indication for apexification: "Apexification should be performed in teeth with open apices and thin dentinal walls in which standard instrumentation techniques cannot create an apical stop" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. For the necrotic tooth, Torabinejad offers apexification and regenerative procedures; REP suits immature teeth with "adequate coronal tooth structure to be restored with direct restoration" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's adds that teeth "with near or complete root formation may be more suited for conventional endodontic therapy or MTA barrier techniques" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's states the principle for the necrotic immature tooth: "Completion of endodontic therapy should therefore be delayed until root-end closure has been completed by apexi,cation" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Decision** (Shabahang, 2019; Kahler, 2021; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021):

| Pulp status | Root stage | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Vital, reversible (exposure) | Immature | Apexogenesis: pulp capping, partial (Cvek) pulpotomy, cervical pulpotomy |
| Deeper coronal inflammation, radicular pulp bleeding normally | Immature | Cervical (deep) pulpotomy to fresh bleeding tissue (Grossman lists irreversible pulpitis as a contraindication) |
| Necrotic | Immature, enough crown for direct restoration | Regenerative endodontic procedure, or MTA barrier |
| Necrotic | Immature or near-complete | MTA/bioceramic apical plug (one-step apexification); calcium hydroxide apexification |
| Necrotic | Mature root with open apex (resorption, iatrogenic) | Apical plug, then obturation; surgery if this fails |

Rule: preserve vitality whenever possible; Cohen's says vital pulp therapy should always be attempted in an immature tooth if feasible (Kahler, 2021).

## 6. Management of the vital pulp: apexogenesis

### Explanation

**Biology.** In young patients the pulp "has a higher likelihood to recover from injury"; Cvek found traumatically exposed pulps "maintain a vital pulp for up to seven days post injury", with inflammation "limited to the surface 2 mm of the pulp just beneath the exposure level" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's puts it at 48 hours: "the initial reaction of the pulp is proliferative, with no more than a 2-mm depth of pulpal inflammation" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek's account of calcium hydroxide is that it is not the chemical itself "but the low-grade irritation from coagulation necrosis that induces defensive reactions in the pulp", and that "calcium hydroxide as such has no beneficial effect on healing of a chronically inflamed pulp" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; internal resorption and dystrophic calcification after dressing "seem to be related to the presence of an extra-pulpal blood clot" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Sikri lists the goals of apexogenesis: "Sustaining a viable Hertwig’s sheath, thus allowing" a better crown-root ratio, letting "remaining odontoblasts to lay down dentin, producing a thicker root and decreasing the chance of root fracture", natural apical constriction, and a dentin bridge (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Why vitality is worth keeping.** Weine: when the apical pulp stays vital the root end and canal usually assume a near-normal size and shape, but after apexification "the root end often develops in a short blunted condition, whereas the canal remains rather wide" (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Grossman defines pulpotomy as the procedure in which "a portion of the exposed coronal vital pulp is surgically removed as a means of preserving the vitality and function of the remaining radicular portion" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Pulp capping.** Cvek: capping is indicated when a small exposure is treated soon after injury, which "seems to mean within 24 hours" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Endodontology: only for pinpoint exposures with a healthy pulp (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

**Partial (Cvek) pulpotomy.** Glossary: "The removal of a small portion of the" vital coronal pulp to preserve the remaining coronal and radicular pulp (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Grossman: removal of tissue "until normal tissue that is free of inflammation is reached before placing a medicament" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Technique (Cvek): "Pulpotomy should be performed with a diamond bur" at high speed with copious cooling; "The level of amputation should be about 2mm below the exposure site"; saline until bleeding stops, calcium hydroxide, tight seal (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Torabinejad uses an operating microscope ("This assessment is best achieved with the use of the operating microscope"), judges a pulp irreversibly inflamed "if it bleeds profusely despite local hemostasis measures", applies NaOCl then 17% EDTA and a tricalcium silicate cap (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Bakland's MTA pulpotomy removes about 2 mm with a round diamond, accepts slight bleeding because "MTA can be placed on pulp tissue that may still be bleeding slightly", and waits 4-6 hours before restoring (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Advantage, per Cvek: "undisturbed physiologic apposition of dentin, especially in the critical cervical area of the tooth", and sensitivity testing remains possible (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

*When to do it: the books give different time windows.* Grossman recommends partial pulpotomy for pinpoint exposures that "can be treated within 15–18 hours of the accident" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Endodontology for small exposures of 24 hours or less (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; but Cvek states that "neither exposure size nor time interval between injury and treatment are critical for healing when only superficial layers of the pulp are removed" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC], and Cohen's indicates full pulpotomy only after "more than 72 hours" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek's position rests on his own clinical series and is the most data-based; Grossman's and Endodontology's windows are recommendations without data in the chapter. This is a disagreement about indications, with the evidence favouring Cvek. [Likely]

**Cervical (full) pulpotomy.** Grossman: in anterior teeth remove "the bulbous portion up to, but not beyond, the cervical third of the root canal"; "As much pulp tissue as possible should be left in the root" canal; "Twisting of the pulp stump compresses the tissue, with" consequent necrosis (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek: in immature teeth with necrotic tissue or poor vascularity at the exposure, amputate "to a level at which fresh bleeding tissue is found" with a low-speed round bur (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Torabinejad: NaOCl pellet 2-5 minutes, 17% EDTA, "a layer of >4 mm of a bioactive tricalcium silicate material is placed over the pulp stumps", definitive restoration, recall "at 6 and 12 months, followed by annual evaluations for the first 4 years" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman: Ca(OH)2 filled to "a depth of at least 1–2 mm", or MTA with "a minimum thickness of" 2 mm, under an RMGIC or flowable compomer base and a bonded restoration (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman's indications include "Mechanical or carious exposure in permanent teeth with" incomplete roots and states "Even if only the apical 3 or 4 mm of the pulp tissue is still vital, the root apex can complete development"; contraindications include irreversible pulpitis, tenderness from pulpal disease, "Periradicular radiographic changes resulting from exten"sion of pulpal disease, and calcified canals (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Does irreversible pulpitis exclude pulpotomy? The books differ.** Grossman lists "Patients with irreversible pulpitis" first among contraindications (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad: "A complete pulp chamber pulpotomy is often required when the coronal pulp is heavily inflamed or with a questionable vitality status" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Cvek amputates deeper until fresh bleeding tissue is reached (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Gutmann mentions revascularisation for teeth with "immature root formation and either irreversible pulpitis and periapical periodontitis" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. The difference is one of definition: the clinical label "irreversible" describes the coronal pulp, and the later books judge the radicular pulp at the amputation site by bleeding. [Likely]

**Choice of medicament: a real conflict.** Grossman calls MTA/CSC better "than calcium hydroxide in terms of healing, quality of seal provided, and superior biocompatibility" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Bakland adds that MTA does not need re-entry because "it is not necessary to re-enter the site of material placement at a later time to remove the material" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]; Sikri still writes that "the use of calcium hydroxide has been shown to be the most predictable with regard to long-term clinical success" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's notes MTA capping "has been to shown to cause discoloration of the tooth" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Weine's pulpotomy for anterior apexogenesis places "a cotton pellet lightly dampened with formocresol is applied to the pulp stump for 3 minutes" and a formocresol-ZOE mix (Weine, 2004) [SRC], while Grossman restricts formocresol: "It is indicated for pulpotomy of primary teeth only" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Weine's 2004 technique is outdated by the later books; do not write formocresol for a permanent immature tooth. [Likely]

**Outcomes and follow-up.** Grossman: partial pulpotomy 95% versus direct pulp capping 80% in young permanent teeth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's: partial pulpotomy "The prognosis is extremely good (94% to 96%)"; cervical pulpotomy "in the range of 75%, is poorer than for partial pulpotomy" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad cautions that "there are no studies evaluating the long-term outcomes of these procedures in large patient populations" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek's criteria of healing include "Continued root development in immature teeth" and a hard tissue barrier, and all failures in his series came within 26 months, "indicating that a 3-year follow-up should be considered adequate" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Grossman: radiographs and vitality tests every 3 months; "If the apex is immature, apexification therapy should be initiated" if the pulp dies (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri gives one to two years for apexogenesis "depending on the degree of tooth development" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**After the root closes: should the canal be treated?** Cvek: cervical pulpotomy "is therefore regarded as a temporary treatment to be followed by pulpectomy once root formation is complete" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Sikri reports the view that "once root development appears to be completed, the tooth should be re-entered and root canal therapy performed" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad says full pulpotomy "it may be considered definitive treatment in certain cases" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC] and Endodontology that if the pulpotomy succeeds "conventional root canal treatment will not be necessary later on" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. The older calcium hydroxide literature drove routine pulpectomy because of canal obliteration; with tricalcium silicates the later books treat a successful pulpotomy as definitive. This is a change over time. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Rationale.** Young pulps are well vascularised and heal; after a traumatic exposure the inflammation stays within about 2 mm of the surface for days (Shabahang, 2019; Kahler, 2021). Goals: keep HERS vital (root length), let odontoblasts thicken the walls (less fracture), form a natural apical constriction and a dentin bridge (Sikri, 2019).

**1. Pulp capping:** small exposure treated within about 24 hours (Cvek, 2007); pinpoint exposure, healthy pulp (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

**2. Partial (Cvek) pulpotomy:** removal of a small portion of the coronal pulp (American Association of Endodontists, 2012).
- Rubber dam; high-speed diamond with copious water; remove pulp about 2 mm below the exposure (Cvek, 2007).
- Haemostasis with saline or NaOCl pellet; profuse bleeding that does not stop means deeper inflammation (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). Avoid an extrapulpal blood clot (Cvek, 2007).
- Calcium hydroxide, MTA or Biodentine; MTA can be placed on slightly bleeding pulp; bacteria-tight seal (GIC/RMGIC) and bonded composite (Bakland, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Advantages: cervical dentin continues to form; coronal pulp left for sensitivity testing (Cvek, 2007). Success 94-96% (Kahler, 2021); 95% versus 80% for capping (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**3. Cervical (full) pulpotomy:** for deeper inflammation, exposures over 72 hours, carious exposures of immature teeth (Kahler, 2021).
- Amputate to the root orifices (posterior) or cervical third (anterior), to fresh bleeding tissue (Gopikrishna, 2021; Cvek, 2007). Do not twist the stump.
- More than 4 mm tricalcium silicate (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), or calcium hydroxide 1-2 mm / MTA at least 2 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021); base and bonded restoration.
- Success about 75% (Kahler, 2021).

**Medicament.** MTA/CSC is better than calcium hydroxide in healing, seal and biocompatibility (Gopikrishna, 2021) but can discolour (Kahler, 2021). Formocresol, still in Weine's apexogenesis technique (Weine, 2004), is for primary teeth only (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Follow-up.** Clinical and radiographic review every 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021); success: no symptoms, no radiographic pathosis, continued root development, a hard tissue barrier, positive sensitivity (except after cervical pulpotomy); follow for at least 3 years (Cvek, 2007). Apexogenesis takes one to two years (Sikri, 2019). If the pulp dies, start apexification (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**After closure.** Cvek regards cervical pulpotomy as temporary, followed by pulpectomy once the root is complete (Cvek, 2007); Torabinejad accepts a successful pulpotomy as definitive in some cases (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

## 7. Management of the non-vital pulp (1): calcium hydroxide apexification

### Explanation

**Principle and history.** Ingle's: "calcium hydroxide has historically been the material of choice for apexi,cation procedures" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman dates the technique to Frank's three cases of 1966 and states: "Actual root growth does not occur as a result of apexification", and "disinfection of the root canal is indispensable to apical closure" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Ingle's records Frank's thick paste of calcium hydroxide and CMCP and his "follow-up evaluations at three- to six-month intervals until closure of the apex" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad: the calcium hydroxide route is "an indirect approach" because it relies on a hard tissue barrier forming (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; "no further root development is anticipated in teeth treated by apexification", and studies show such teeth "do not undergo changes in the root dimension, such as lengthening or thickening of the root dentin" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek: the purpose is periradicular healing "including arrest of inflammatory root resorption and formation of a hard tissue barrier apically", its effects "all related to its high pH = 12.5", including the "capacity to dissolve necrotic pulp remnants", important where thin walls do not permit reaming (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Ingle's adds experimental evidence that "the pH of calcium hydroxide plays a more signi,cant role in the induction of root-end closure than the presence of exogenous calcium ions" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

**Technique.** Cvek: careful filing with lateral pressure, "flushed with 0.5% sodium hypochlorite", calcium hydroxide by spiral or syringe pressed lightly toward the apex to contact vital tissue, "thereafter the access cavity is sealed with zinc oxide-eugenol cement"; "Calcium hydroxide that is eventually pressed through the apical foramen during filling is readily resorbed by periapical tissues" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Cvek also says "The addition to calcium hydroxide of toxic antibacterial drugs, such as camphorated parachlorophenol, does not seem to be justified" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC], whereas Frank's original paste and Weine's technique used CMCP (Shabahang, 2019; Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Cohen's: creamy mix spun in with a Lentulo, at least 1 week, then a thick packed fill; avoid radiopaquers: "it is prudent to use a calcium hydroxide mixture without the addition of a radiopaquer", because washout cannot be judged (Kahler, 2021) [SRC], while Sikri says "The addition of barium sulfate enhances its radiopacity" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's agrees with Cohen's (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Harty's two-stage method: a thin mix, then after about 3 weeks "a thicker, almost dry mixture of calcium hydroxide and sterile saline is packed" to full length under IRM (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Grossman's box: working length 2 mm short, circumferential filing, thick paste, "The access cavity is sealed with RMGIC (resin-modified glass ionomer cement)", 3-monthly recall, thermoplasticised obturation once a barrier is present; its clinical notes add that long-term intracanal Ca(OH)2 "can cause weakening of" root dentin and that "every effort should be made to preserve any vital apical pulp tissue" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek reviews "the teeth should be controlled at 3- to 6-month intervals" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

**How long? The books disagree widely.** Grossman, in one chapter, gives both "6 months to 4 years to complete" and "Apexification is usually complete in 6 months, or 2 years at most" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; this is an internal inconsistency (range versus usual). Ingle's: "Apical barrier formation may take from 3 months to" 2 years (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's: "takes anywhere from 3 to 18 months with calcium hydroxide" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Cvek: "this may take 6 to 18 months after initial treatment" depending on the foramen and lesion (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Sikri: "may take 9 to 18 months for calcific bridge formation" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Harty's: 6-24 months "with an average of 1 year and 7 months" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Endodontology: "and is generally cited as 1 year on average" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Gutmann: "The mean time to barrier formation in incisor teeth has been shown to be 34.2 weeks" (range 13-67) (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; Tonini and Garo: "approximately six months" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Individual studies quoted by Ingle's range from a "mean duration for apical closure was 10–14 weeks" to "slightly more than 12 months" and a "mean treatment duration of 8.6 months" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. This is genuine variation between case series (age, apex width, infection); Ingle's attributes shorter times to older children with narrower apices and to teeth without periradicular infection (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Write *6-18 months, sometimes up to 2 years*. [Likely]

**How often to change the dressing? The books disagree.** Ingle's: "there has been no consensus on the optimal timing to change the dressing material"; recommendations run "once every month, once every three months, once every six to eight months, or not at all" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman: changed and "recalled every 3 months till evidence of calcific barrier is seen" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri: "is to be repeated every six to eight weeks for better results" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Endodontology: changes "every 3–6 months" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Gutmann: left or changed every 3 months, "with intervals as long as 12 months in later stages", and changing "has been shown to be counterproductive to the formation of hard tissue" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; Cohen's: review at 3 months and "If no washout is evident, the calcium hydroxide can be left intact for another 3 months"; "Excessive calcium hydroxide dressing changes should be avoided if at all possible" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's: "it is not recommended to replace the mixture if it appears to be intact at these recall" visits (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Cvek gives both sides: "Intermediary replacements of calcium hydroxide did not appear in an experimental study in monkeys to advance the apexification", while a clinical study found that frequent change, written as "more than 5 months between shifts compared to less than 5 months", gave faster barrier formation (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. That parenthesis reads the wrong way round: "frequent change" must mean less than 5 months between changes; the book appears to have reversed the comparison. [Likely] Cvek's practical rule is to refill "when the level of calcium hydroxide falls in the cervical part of the root canal" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. The newer books (Cohen's, Harty's, Gutmann) converge on *replace only if washed out*. [Likely]

**The barrier.** It forms where calcium hydroxide meets vital tissue: "An apical barrier can be formed around the apex of the tooth, over the apical foramen or in the root canal" and is "a reparative or cementum-like hard tissue is formed instead of dentin" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Grossman: bone, dentin, osteodentin or cementum (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's (Dylewski's monkeys): osteodentin from proliferating connective tissue (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Gutmann: "a gnarled osteocementum type of material" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. It is porous: "a porous structure116 that may be permeable to microbial irritants" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's: a "Swiss cheese" consistency (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Weine likewise (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Weine and Sikri list the possible outcomes: "One of the following five apical conditions will be found" (Weine, 2004) [SRC] and "There are four clinical possibilities in apexification" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Ingle's gives Frank's four outcomes (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. The lists differ only in whether failure is counted as an outcome (Weine yes, Frank no). [Certain]

**Outcomes and drawbacks.** Cvek: success "has been reported to occur in 79–96% of the treated teeth" and, in his long-term series, "periapical healing was observed in 92% of 589 teeth" with no difference between immature and mature teeth (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Ingle's reports "Forty-nine of 51 teeth developed an apical barrier" in one series (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Torabinejad reports a study that "showed greater than 90% success in terms of resolution of apical periodontitis" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Endodontology gives 74% to 96% (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Drawbacks (Ingle's list): variable time, "multiple visits necessary to replace and refresh the calcium hydroxide paste", coronal leakage and reinfection, variable barrier tissue, cervical fracture (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Bakland adds "increased treatment time, unpredictable results and delays in completing final restoration" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]; Sikri adds that calcium hydroxide "may damage the Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath", and lists "Risk of re-infection; periapical exudate may dissolve" the dressing among its limitations (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** Calcium hydroxide (pH 12.5) disinfects, dissolves necrotic remnants, helps arrest inflammatory resorption and, against vital apical tissue, induces a hard tissue barrier against which the canal is filled (Cvek, 2007). It is an indirect method; no true root growth occurs (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021). Disinfection is indispensable (Gopikrishna, 2021). Introduced by Frank, 1966 (Shabahang, 2019).

**Technique** (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017)
1. Radiograph, LA if needed, rubber dam, straight-line access.
2. Working length from a radiograph (apex locators unreliable), at least 2 mm short of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021).
3. Light circumferential filing; 0.5% NaOCl in volume; do not remove vital apical tissue.
4. Dry; creamy calcium hydroxide by Lentulo for at least 1 week (not more than 1 month) (Kahler, 2021).
5. Then a thick calcium hydroxide paste (with sterile water or saline) packed to the apex; the canal should look as radiodense as dentin. Avoid barium sulphate so washout can be seen (Kahler, 2021).
6. Seal: ZOE, IRM or RMGIC (Cvek, 2007; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017; Gopikrishna, 2021).
7. Review every 3 months; refill only if washed out (Kahler, 2021; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).
8. When a barrier is seen on the radiograph and felt with a file, fill to the barrier with softened gutta-percha.

**Duration:** 6-18 months (Cvek, 2007); 3-18 months (Kahler, 2021); 3 months to 2 years (Shabahang, 2019); 6-24 months, average 1 year 7 months (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).

**Barrier:** cementum-like or osteocementum tissue, at, over or within the apex; porous, "Swiss cheese" (Cvek, 2007; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Kahler, 2021).

**Success:** 79-96% (Cvek, 2007); periapical healing in 92% of 589 teeth (Cvek, 2007).

**Drawbacks:** variable and long treatment; multiple visits; reinfection through the temporary seal; porous barrier; cervical root fracture; no increase in root length or thickness (Shabahang, 2019; Bakland, 2007; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

## 8. Management of the non-vital pulp (2): MTA / bioceramic apical plug (one-step apexification)

### Explanation

**Why a plug.** Ingle's: long therapy "increases the risks of patient attrition", and calcium hydroxide "may be ine.ective in some cases" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Bakland, citing Tittle et al: "the induction of an apical hard tissue barrier was not necessary, since MTA itself used as an apical plug provides the necessary apical stop" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. MTA's relevant properties: pH "reported to be 10.2, rising to 12.5 at three hours and thereafter" and "its ability to induce cementum formation around the repair area" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; hydrophilic and sets "in the presence of any moisture, including blood"; sets in about 3 hours while strength rises over 3 weeks; "MTA does not appear to have a detrimental effect on dentin" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. In monkeys "MTA-treated teeth display less in0ammation and a greater amount of hard tissue formation" than calcium hydroxide (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

**Pre-medication with calcium hydroxide: the books disagree.** Ingle's reports a dog study: "initial use of calcium hydroxide paste was not necessary for apical root formation to occur", and more calcium hydroxide meant more MTA extrusion; but another found "intra-canal medication with calcium hydroxide for one week results in better marginal adaptation of an apical plug of MTA" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman advises a week of calcium hydroxide "to raise the acidic pH of inflamed periradicular tissue before permanently sealing with MTA" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's: because "MTA has minimal bactericidal effect on several bacterial species", follow a two- to three-step approach, "disinfecting and medicating the root canal for 7 to 14 days with a thin calcium hydroxide paste" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Endodontology likewise, "because MTA in its set condition exerts no disinfection effect" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Bakland: "There is good evidence that the use of intracanal calcium hydroxide is very effective if left in place for up to two weeks", limited to "one month or less" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Torabinejad keeps calcium hydroxide for teeth with swelling or a sinus tract (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. On outcome, Witherspoon et al found "Overall success rates were 93.5% in the single visit cases and 90.5% in the two visit cases" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Gutmann notes "what is defined as a one-visit treatment may be ambiguous" since many clinicians place calcium hydroxide for 7-14 days first (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. This is a disagreement of method with no outcome difference shown; a short dressing (1-2 weeks) is the consensus for infected canals. [Likely]

**Technique.** Ingle's: pluggers pre-fitted, the "smallest plugger should ,t loosely at a level ~ 0.5 mm from the working length"; MTA in small moist increments; "Ultrasonic vibration can be used on the pluggers to help advance MTA towards the apical extent of the canal"; excess removed with moist paper points (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Bakland: "a totally dry canal is not required – MTA needs moisture to cure"; plug of at least 4 mm, then gutta-percha after it cures, or "fill the entire canal with MTA"; wait at least 4-6 hours under a moist pellet (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Grossman: 3:1 MTA:water to wet-sand consistency, a 3-4 mm plug, moist pellet, "Patient is recalled after 48 hours and the set MTA/CSC cement is verified" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology recommends filling 24 hours later (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Tonini and Garo: the MAP system carrier, a moist paper point or brush on the MTA, check set after a few days (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Gutmann: MTA "has been difficult to manipulate in its present form" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Hachmeister et al, reported by Ingle's, concluded that leakage depends on "the technique of placing MTA rather than the intrinsic characteristics of the MTA material itself" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC], so operator skill and magnification matter more than the brand. [Likely]

**Plug thickness: the books give different figures.** Endodontology "with a minimum thickness of 3 mm" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Grossman 3-4 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri "A 3.0–4.0 mm apical plug is created" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Ingle's 3-5 mm "to minimize the possibility of future apical leakage" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Bakland "The recommended size of apical MTA plug is 4mm" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]; Tonini and Garo 4 mm (3 mm over a collagen matrix) (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]; Cohen's "packed into the apical 4 to 5 mm" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad "After at least 5 mm of apical plug is established" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. The figures overlap at 4 mm; the differences are recommendations from leakage studies, not conflicting measurements. Write *3-5 mm, usually 4 mm*. [Certain]

**Matrix against extrusion: the books disagree.** Ingle's: "use of a matrix material is generally not required to limit the movement of MTA into the periradicular tissues" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad uses collagen (CollaPlug/CollaTape) in certain cases "to serve as internal matrix to prevent or minimize the extrusion of MTA into the periradicular tissues" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's: "Calcium sulfate is pushed through the apex to provide a resorbable extraradicular barrier" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's: CollaCote "or calcium sulphate sponges can be placed apically beforehand to act as a matrix" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Sikri: platelet-rich fibrin as an internal matrix (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Tonini and Garo: "Overfilling is the major limitation of the apical plug technique" and a collagen or PRF matrix creates "an artificial boundary that ­prevents the apical plug material from penetrating the periapical tissues" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Ingle's also warns that "Extrusion of MTA more than 2 mm beyond the root apex may retard healing and cementum formation" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's position is based on MTA's tolerance by tissue; the others aim to control placement. Both are reasonable; use a matrix in very wide apices. [Likely]

**Outcomes.** Ingle's case series: Pace et al "10 of 11 cases had completely healed"; Sarris et al "A clinical success rate of 94.1% and a radiographic success rate of 76.5% was reported"; a single-appointment prospective study "healing was recorded in 81% of cases"; Moore et al 95.5% success but "coronal discoloration in 22.7% of the teeth that were treated" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Pradhan et al: MTA completed in 0.75 ± 0.5 months "compared to traditional calcium hydroxide therapy (7 ± 2.5 months)"; a meta-analysis: "the authors concluded that the two protocols have similar success rates", although 100% of MTA cases succeeded "in comparison to a 92% success rate in the calcium hydroxide treated cases" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Tonini and Garo: CH 79-96%, MTA "higher healing rates (81–100%)", but the meta-analysis of four studies found "there was no difference in clinical and radiographic success rates or in the ratio of apical barrier formation between CH and MTA"; "The actual difference between the two approaches is due to the time required for apical barrier formation" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Cohen's: "the one-step apexification has been shown to have as high success rate as apexification with calcium hydroxide" (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. The books agree on equal healing and shorter time. [Certain]

**Advantages and limits.** Ingle's: "more predictable closure of the apical opening, reduced treatment time, and fewer appointments" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. But "continued root development ceases once a vital pulp is removed", and failures after apexification come from "fractures in the cervical region of the tooth rather than the root apex" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Discoloration: Harty's says grey and white MTA "have the inconvenience of causing unaesthetic discolouration of teeth, even when used as an apexification material" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Ingle's limits MTA to "the apical 4–5 mm" in anterior teeth (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Tonini and Garo list MTA's "long setting time (from 2 hours and 45 minutes to 4 hours), high cost, difficult handling" and bismuth-oxide discoloration (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Rationale.** An artificial barrier is placed at once instead of waiting for a calcified one; MTA itself provides the apical stop (Bakland, 2007). MTA sets in moisture and blood, has a pH of 10.2-12.5, is biocompatible, induces cementum over the material and does not weaken dentin (Shabahang, 2019; Bakland, 2007).

**Technique** (Shabahang, 2019; Bakland, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017)
1. Access, radiographic length, gentle debridement, low-concentration NaOCl.
2. Calcium hydroxide for 1-2 weeks (not more than 1 month) when the canal is infected; MTA in its set state does not disinfect (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017; Baumann and Beer, 2010).
3. Remove calcium hydroxide; dry with paper points; a totally dry canal is not needed (Bakland, 2007).
4. Optional apical matrix: collagen (CollaCote/CollaPlug), calcium sulphate or PRF (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Kahler, 2021; Sikri, 2019).
5. Mix MTA 3:1 to wet-sand consistency; carry with an MTA carrier/MAP system; compact with pre-fitted pluggers (smallest loose about 0.5 mm from working length), ultrasonics on the plugger.
6. Plug thickness 3-5 mm (Shabahang, 2019), commonly 4 mm (Bakland, 2007); confirm radiographically; clean walls with moist paper points.
7. Moist pellet, temporary seal; check set after 4-6 hours to 48 hours (Bakland, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021).
8. Backfill with thermoplasticised gutta-percha, or fill the whole canal with MTA in short roots; bonded resin below the CEJ.

**Outcomes.** Healing similar to calcium hydroxide (meta-analysis), but treatment time about 0.75 months versus 7 months (Shabahang, 2019); MTA 81-100% healing (Tonini and Garo, 2024); single and two visit 93.5% and 90.5% (Shabahang, 2019).

**Limitations.** No root lengthening or thickening; cervical fracture remains the main failure; discoloration (22.7% in one series); long setting time, cost, difficult handling; extrusion beyond 2 mm may retard healing (Shabahang, 2019; Tonini and Garo, 2024).

## 9. Regenerative endodontic procedures (brief; see the Regenerative Endodontics topic)

### Explanation

Torabinejad: "At present, REP is considered a viable treatment option for immature teeth with pulpal necrosis", with dimensional changes "that result in increased root dentin thickness and length" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Protocol: minimal instrumentation, 1.5% NaOCl, calcium hydroxide or triple antibiotic paste, second visit "typically 2 to 4 weeks after the placement of medicament", "local anesthetic without vasoconstrictor, e.g., 3% mepivacaine", 17% EDTA, bleeding induced with a #10 K-file to the CEJ, collagen, tricalcium silicate cap, restoration (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Trope's group recommended a triple antibiotic paste "composed of metronidazole, cipro0oxacin, and minocycline"; Sikri: "Intracanal medication with triple antibiotic paste for four weeks is an effective approach" for non-vital open-apex teeth (Shabahang, 2019; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Ingle's warns that triple antibiotic pastes "can signi,cantly reduce the survival of these cells in contrast to calcium hydroxide" (stem cells of the apical papilla) and that minocycline stains (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri: "minocycline can be replaced with cephalexin to avoid discoloration" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Cohen's: "Biodentine when used as an intracanal barrier results in less crown discoloration then when MTA is used" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC].

**Success: two different measures.** Torabinejad: "The success/survival rate of REP in the literature is surprisingly high, nearing 100%", defined as resolution of apical periodontitis (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's: the rate at which the root "continues to grow and/or mature" is "27% and 55%, and the survival rate of such teeth much higher" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Cohen's: "In regenerative endodontic procedures there are three outcome measures for success" (healing, root thickening/lengthening, vitality response), and "increases in root width and length are variable" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. These figures are not in conflict; they measure different outcomes. Histologically REP "may represent “tissue repair” rather than de novo regeneration of functional dental pulp", with intracanal calcification "a very common" complication (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Tonini and Garo report pooled success of 94.6% for MTA plugs and 91.3% for REP in one review (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Cohen's adds that "REPs have been shown to be beneficial in cases of root resorption" (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC], and Ingle's sees the aim as "continued root development along the entire root length" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Gutmann asks "If the pulp is necrotic, how can “regeneration” in the truest sense be achieved in a predictable manner?" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Concept.** Disinfect the canal without instrumentation and let stem cells from the apical papilla enter on an induced blood clot, so that the root may lengthen and thicken (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**Protocol (outline).** Visit 1: 1.5% NaOCl, calcium hydroxide or triple antibiotic paste (minocycline stains; cephalexin may replace it). Visit 2 after 2-4 weeks: anaesthetic without vasoconstrictor (3% mepivacaine), 17% EDTA, bleeding induced to the CEJ, collagen matrix, MTA/Biodentine cap (Biodentine discolours less), bonded restoration (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Sikri, 2019; Kahler, 2021).

**Outcome.** Healing of apical periodontitis nearly 100% (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), but continued root development in only 27-55% (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017); the tissue is repair, not true pulp; canal calcification is common (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021). If it fails, apexification is still possible (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).

## 10. Materials used in the management of open apex

### Explanation

**Calcium hydroxide and its vehicles.** Ingle's lists vehicles: CMCP, "iodoform,92 water,93,94 local anesthetic solution, isotonic saline solution, glycerol" and others (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri prefers "calcium hydroxide and methylcellulose (pulpdent), because of better consistency and less solubility" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cvek rejects CMCP and the mixing of calcium hydroxide with corticosteroids or antibiotics (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

**Historical and alternative barrier materials.** Ingle's names "Tricresol and Formalin,87 antibiotic pastes,69 tricalcium phosphate,88 and calcium hydroxide" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; dentin plugs ("Formation of a calci,ed tissue barrier is more rapid, however, when using dentin plugs" than with calcium hydroxide); "Tricalcium phosphate has also been proposed as an apical barrier in a single-appointment technique"; hydroxyapatite and dentin chips closed some apices in monkeys; collagen-calcium phosphate gel gave conflicting results; OP-1 matched calcium hydroxide (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's one-visit list: "tricalcium phosphate, calcium hydroxide, freeze dried bone and mineral trioxide aggregate in one-visit apexification" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman: none of the alternatives "is as effective in promoting a calcific barrier as calcium hydroxide or MTA" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Calcium silicate cements.** MTA: Glossary "as a root-end barrier in teeth with an open apex" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; setting by "Hydration (in the presence of water or blood)" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Biodentine: Grossman lists it for "used for repair of perforations and resorption, apexification, and root-end fillings", says "Biodentine sets in approximately 10 minutes", "It does not stain the tooth" and "It has excellent radiopacity" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Tonini and Garo give "with setting time of approximately 12 minutes" and say Biodentine "has less radiopacity compared to MTA", and that "the current literature is inconclusive regarding the efficacy of Biodentine when used as an apical plug" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. The radiopacity statements conflict directly; Grossman's "excellent" is a manufacturer-style claim, while Tonini and Garo compare it with MTA; Biodentine is radiopaque enough to see but less so than MTA. [Likely] Bioceramic putties replace bismuth oxide: "replacement of bismuth oxide with zirconium oxide" improves colour stability (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. CEM cement is "a hydrophilic tooth-­colored cement that releases CH during and after setting" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC].

**Matrices.** Collagen, calcium sulphate, PRF (section 8).

**Obturation materials.** Gutta-percha with sealer, thermoplasticised (Gopikrishna, 2021; Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; custom/inverted cones (Weine, 2004; Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; MTA as the entire fill in short roots (Sikri, 2019; Bakland, 2007) [SRC].

**Temporary and coronal materials.** ZOE (Cvek, 2007), IRM (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017), RMGIC (Gopikrishna, 2021), Cavit then GIC (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; final: bonded composite resin into the canal below the bone crest, fibre posts, glass ionomer over MTA (section 12).

## 11. Obturation after apexification (short; see Obturation Q10)

### Explanation

Cohen's: "a softened filling technique is indicated in these teeth"; avoid lateral force on thin walls; because the calcium hydroxide barrier is porous, sealer may be pushed through, and "Filling should be completed to the level of the hard-tissue barrier and not forced toward the radiographic apex" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek: "A gentle obturation technique is therefore recommended"; warm only the tip of the master point ("only the tip of the gutta-percha point should be softened") and press it against the barrier, then lateral condensation (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Grossman: "Obturation using a thermoplasticized technique" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Weine: with a wide canal "it may be necessary to invert the master cone" or customise it (Weine, 2004) [SRC]; Sikri: "e.g. inverted point technique and roll-cone technique" if apexification fails (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. After an MTA plug, Ingle's backfills with gutta-percha and sealer once the MTA has set (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. The methods agree. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

Fill to the barrier, not to the radiographic apex, with gentle pressure, because the calcium hydroxide barrier is porous and the walls thin (Kahler, 2021; Cvek, 2007). Options: thermoplasticised (injectable) gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021); a master cone with only its tip softened, then lateral condensation (Cvek, 2007); custom, inverted or roll cones for very wide canals (Weine, 2004; Sikri, 2019); after an MTA plug, backfill once it has set (Shabahang, 2019). Details: Obturation Q10.

## 12. Restoration and reinforcement of thin roots

### Explanation

Ingle's: "Extension of the core layer into the coronal third of the canal may enhance fracture resistance of the tooth" and "Placement of bonded resin below the cemento-enamel junction may also increase the fracture resistance of the tooth" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Bakland: "the cervical area can be strengthened both before and after apexification procedures by bonding resin materials", and after an MTA plug "the resin can be placed into the canal to a level well below the crest of alveolar bone after the MTA has cured" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Cohen's: after filling, "the material should be removed to below the marginal bone level and a bonded resin filling placed" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Gutmann: "the use of preformed, intraradicular metallic posts in the tooth are not indicated"; "a glass ionomer can be placed on top of the MTA, followed by a bonded composite or bonded post"; "Bonding the canal walls in these cases is a wise choice to prevent fracture" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Sikri: "Newer dentin bonding techniques have found to significantly increase the resistance to fracture of these teeth", a clear curing post technique to keep a channel for calcium hydroxide changes, and "If the canal walls are thin, the canal space can be completely filled with MTA to strengthen the tooth against fracture" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Tonini and Garo: "The dual-­flow composite and fiber posts placed only 3 mm deep significantly strengthen the root of the immature tooth" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Cvek's pragmatic point: "much is gained if a young, immature incisor can be kept as space maintainer during the periods of maxillary growth" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. The books agree on bonded resin below the CEJ; the evidence is laboratory, and Bakland notes long-term results are lacking (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. [Certain]

#### ✎ Exam core

- Remove gutta-percha/MTA to below the CEJ/marginal bone level and place bonded composite resin into the canal (Kahler, 2021; Bakland, 2007; Shabahang, 2019).
- Glass ionomer over MTA, then bonded composite or a bonded (fibre) post; fibre posts with dual-cure composite 3 mm deep strengthen the root (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011; Tonini and Garo, 2024).
- Metal posts are contraindicated (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- Short roots with thin walls: fill the whole canal with MTA (Sikri, 2019).
- Evidence is laboratory; long-term clinical data are lacking (Bakland, 2007). Even so, keeping the tooth as a space maintainer during growth is a gain (Cvek, 2007).

## 13. Prognosis, outcome comparisons and follow-up

### Explanation

**Cervical root fracture and calcium hydroxide: the books disagree.** Cvek: "Frequency of cervical fractures fell gradually from 77% in 26 teeth with the lowest degree of root development, to 2% in 362 mature teeth"; laboratory data suggest long-term calcium hydroxide may "increase the brittleness of dentin and risk of cervical root fractures", and "the high frequency of fractures could be the result of several, concomitantly involved factors" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Ingle's: "exposure to calcium hydroxide for periods exceeding one month may result in structural changes in dentin" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Bakland: calcium hydroxide "weakens dentin’s resistance to fracture" and "Younger teeth were more likely to fracture than teeth from older animals" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.12: one retrospective study "resulted in the fracture of 23% of the teeth treated during the follow-up period of up to 18 months" (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's Ch.21: "approximately 30% of these teeth will fracture during or after endodontic treatment", but the weakening effect is not conclusive and "fracture may be more related to the stage of root development rather than calcium hydroxide use" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Gulabivala and Ng: the weakening effect is real "although the effect appears to be limited to the surface" (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC]. Torabinejad: fracture incidence "depended on the stage of root development" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. So the two chapters of Cohen's take different sides; the laboratory evidence of weakening is in sheep, dog and extracted teeth, and the clinical fracture rates are confounded by root stage. Both factors probably act; limiting calcium hydroxide to a few weeks removes the one that can be controlled. [Likely]

**Other prognostic points.** Thin walls left after early necrosis are the dominant risk; Torabinejad: "there is increased probability of root fracture in immature teeth treated by apexification" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's: reinfection in 7.1% of one series, "highlighting the importance of a good root canal seal" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri's follow-up criteria: no symptoms, no resorption or fracture, and "the alveolus should continue to grow in conjunction with the adjacent teeth" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Cvek: re-treatment with calcium hydroxide often resolves reappearing lesions (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Outcome comparison** (Cvek, 2007; Shabahang, 2019; Tonini and Garo, 2024; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017; Kahler, 2021):

| | Calcium hydroxide apexification | MTA apical plug | Regenerative procedure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Healing | 79-96% | 81-100% | nearly 100% (healing of lesion) |
| Time | 6-18 months, up to 2 years | 1-2 visits (0.75 months) | 2 visits, months to see root change |

**Cervical root fracture.** Cvek: 77% in the least developed roots to 2% in mature teeth (Cvek, 2007); Cohen's: about 30% (Kahler, 2021) and 23% within 18 months in one study (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021). Cause: thin walls (root stage) plus possible weakening of dentin by calcium hydroxide used for more than a month (Shabahang, 2019; Bakland, 2007); Cohen's trauma chapter calls the calcium hydroxide effect inconclusive (Kahler, 2021).

**Follow-up.** Clinical and radiographic review at 3-6 months, then yearly: symptoms, healing of the lesion, barrier, resorption, fracture, continued alveolar growth (Cvek, 2007; Sikri, 2019; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**Conclusion.** Preserve the pulp whenever possible (apexogenesis); when it is lost, disinfect gently, create a barrier (an MTA/bioceramic plug is quicker and as successful as calcium hydroxide), consider regeneration in very immature roots, fill to the barrier and reinforce the cervical root with bonded resin.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Long-term calcium hydroxide weakens immature roots and causes cervical fracture; therefore MTA plugs reduce fractures."**
*Textbooks:* Ingle's (more than one month alters dentin) (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Bakland (weakens dentin; younger teeth fracture more; limit to one month) (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]; Cohen's Ch.12 (23% fractures within 18 months) (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]; Endodontology (MTA's rapid closure diminishes the fracture danger compared with long dressings) (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Cvek's own clinical series relates fracture to stage of root development (77% to 2%) and to resorption defects, with calcium hydroxide one of "several" factors (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Cohen's Ch.21 cites a study showing no weakening and calls the effect inconclusive (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Gulabivala and Ng limit the effect to the surface (Gulabivala and Ng, 2014) [SRC].
*Evidence vs interpretation:* the weakening evidence is from animal and extracted-tooth fracture tests (laboratory); the clinical fracture rates are from case series without controls for root stage. Ingle's notes that apical plugs address only the apical opening and "continued root development ceases" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].
*Conclusion:* it is reasonable to keep calcium hydroxide to 1-4 weeks, but no book shows clinically that MTA plugs reduce cervical fractures; the thin root remains the main cause. [Likely]

**Claim 2: "The MTA apical plug is more successful than calcium hydroxide apexification."**
*Textbooks:* individual MTA series 81-100% (Tonini and Garo, 2024; Shabahang, 2019) [SRC] against 79-96% for calcium hydroxide (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].
*Refinement:* the meta-analyses reported by Ingle's and Tonini and Garo found no significant difference in clinical or radiographic success or barrier formation; the real difference is time (0.75 versus 7 months) (Shabahang, 2019; Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]; Cohen's agrees (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC].
*Strength:* few, small trials; the meta-analysis in Tonini and Garo pooled only four studies. [Certain]
*Conclusion:* write "equal healing, much shorter treatment, fewer visits"; do not write "more successful". The choice rests on time, cost, cooperation and discoloration.

**Claim 3: "The calcium hydroxide dressing should be changed every 3 months."**
*Textbooks:* Grossman every 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri every 6-8 weeks (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Endodontology 3-6 months (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].
*Challenge:* Ingle's finds no consensus (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Cvek reports a monkey study showing no benefit of changes and a clinical study showing faster closure with frequent changes (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Gutmann calls changing counterproductive (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; Cohen's and Harty's replace only if washed out (Kahler, 2021; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC].
*Conclusion:* the evidence is conflicting and mostly old; review at 3 months and replace only when washout is seen radiographically. [Likely]

**Claim 4: "Regenerative endodontics succeeds in nearly 100% of cases."**
*Textbooks:* Torabinejad: nearing 100% resolution of apical periodontitis (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's: root growth in 27-55% (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Cohen's: three outcome levels, root changes variable (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; tissue is repair, not pulp (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC].
*Conclusion:* the figures measure different end-points. REP heals lesions as reliably as apexification, but the extra benefit (a stronger, longer root) occurs in a minority and is unpredictable. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Open apex = no apical stop; blunderbuss canal: apical diameter greater than coronal (AAE Glossary).
- Vital pulp: apexogenesis (capping, partial Cvek pulpotomy 2 mm, cervical pulpotomy); non-vital: apexification, MTA plug or REP.
- Pulp tests unreliable in immature teeth; cold best; laser Doppler measures flow; buccolingual apex wider than seen; CBCT.
- Chemical not mechanical debridement; low-strength NaOCl; apex locators unreliable.
- Calcium hydroxide apexification: 6-18 months, porous "Swiss cheese" barrier, 79-96% healing, cervical fracture 77% in the least developed roots.
- MTA plug 3-5 mm (usually 4 mm); same healing as calcium hydroxide, far shorter time.
- Fill to the barrier, gently; bond resin below the CEJ; no metal posts.
- REP heals lesions in nearly all cases but thickens/lengthens the root in only 27-55%.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling apexification "root formation": apexification makes a barrier; only apexogenesis (or sometimes REP) continues root growth.
- Instrumenting an immature canal like a mature one, or using an apex locator for working length.
- Writing that the MTA plug is "more successful" than calcium hydroxide; the meta-analyses show equal healing and shorter time.
- Recommending formocresol pulpotomy for an immature permanent tooth (Weine's old technique); it is for primary teeth.
- Forgetting the coronal end: thin cervical walls fracture; bond resin below the CEJ and avoid metal posts.

## MNEMONIC
**"VITAL first, then BARRIER"**: **V**itality preserved if possible (apexogenesis), **I**maging (CBCT for the buccolingual apex), **T**ests unreliable, **A**void instrumentation (chemical debridement), **L**ength radiographic. Then **BARRIER**: **B**rief Ca(OH)2 (1-2 weeks), **A**pical plug 3-5 mm, **R**esorbable matrix, **R**adiograph check, **I**njectable gutta-percha backfill, **E**nhance with bonded resin, **R**eview for fracture and healing.
