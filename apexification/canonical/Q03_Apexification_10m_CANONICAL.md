## Explanation

**Definition: two wordings.** The AAE glossary defines apexification as "A method to induce a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex or the continued apical development of an incompletely formed root in teeth with necrotic pulps" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Grossman keeps only the first half: a method to induce a calcific barrier across the open apex of an immature, pulpless tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Diogenes, Botero and Kang add that no vital pulp is preserved, because obturation goes to the most apical extent of the root (2021) [SRC], and that apexified teeth do not lengthen or thicken their root dentine (2021) [SRC]; Grossman agrees that actual root growth does not occur (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The glossary's "continued apical development" therefore describes the occasional case in which surviving apical tissue keeps forming root (Frank's first two outcomes, below), not a goal of the procedure [Likely]. This is a difference of wording, not of fact. Pitt Ford and Shabahang use "root-end closure", which was "previously referred to as apexification" (2002) [SRC].

**Aims.** To close the open apical third or to form an apical calcific barrier against which obturation can be done (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri lists a hard-tissue barrier allowing proper condensation (2019) [SRC], one that stops irrigant and filling material extruding (2019) [SRC], and that the procedure should not lead to periapical pathology (2019) [SRC].

**Indications.** Teeth with open apices and thin dentinal walls in which ordinary instrumentation cannot create an apical stop (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; such canals are often wider apically than coronally (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. For an artificial plug, Tonini and Garo give an apex wider than ISO 50-60 among the indications (2024) [SRC].

**Contraindications (audit addition).** Only Pitt Ford and Shabahang list them: all vertical and most horizontal root fractures (2002) [SRC], replacement resorption (2002) [SRC], very short roots (2002) [SRC], and marginal periodontal breakdown or a vital pulp (2002) [SRC]. The last point is the line between this answer and apexogenesis (Q04).

**History and materials.** Long-term calcium hydroxide, for up to 18 months (Paranjpe and Noblett, 2021) [SRC], "was first introduced by Kaiser in 1964 and made popular by Frank in 1966" (Paranjpe and Noblett, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman credits the impetus to Frank's three cases of 1966 (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Frank used a thick paste of calcium hydroxide and camphorated monochlorophenol (CMCP) with reviews every three to six months until the apex closed (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Heithersay used Pulpdent, calcium hydroxide in methylcellulose (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC], and saw a fine apical canal on the radiograph rather than a well-defined barrier (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Other agents tried include tricresol and formalin, antibiotic pastes and tricalcium phosphate (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC], with calcium hydroxide mixed in iodoform, water, local anaesthetic, saline or glycerol (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; collagen-calcium phosphate gel and tricalcium phosphate have been used, but none is as effective as calcium hydroxide or MTA (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri's list is calcium hydroxide, triple antibiotic paste and MTA (2019) [SRC], and she prefers Pulpdent for its consistency and low solubility (2019) [SRC]. The vehicle has moved on since Frank: Cvek finds adding CMCP unjustified (2007) [SRC], and Pitt Ford and Shabahang found no benefit from adding other medicaments (2002) [SRC]. **Barium sulphate: the books conflict.** Pitt Ford and Shabahang add it 9:1 for radiopacity (2002) [SRC]; Kahler advises against it because it hides washout on the radiograph (2021) [SRC]. Kahler gives a reason tied to judging the dressing at review, and is the newer book; a plain paste is the safer choice when washout decides the recall [Likely]. Single-visit MTA apexification was reported by Witherspoon and Ham in 2001 (Paranjpe and Noblett, 2021) [SRC]; Biodentine can be used in one visit without discolouration (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC], and calcium-enriched mixed (CEM) cement is a further option (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC].

**Technique with calcium hydroxide.** Working length at least 2 mm short of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; light filing with copious 0.5% sodium hypochlorite (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; a creamy mix spun in with a Lentulo spiral for disinfection (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; then calcium hydroxide packed against the apical soft tissue with a plugger to start hard-tissue formation (Kahler, 2021) [SRC], in contact with the periapical tissues (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and the access sealed with RMGIC (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The dressing is changed and the patient recalled every 3 months until a barrier shows (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Kahler leaves the dressing for another 3 months if there is no washout (2021) [SRC]. When the barrier shows on the radiograph and can be probed with a file, the canal is filled (Kahler, 2021) [SRC], with thermoplasticised gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and gently, because a thin barrier can break and gutta-percha be expelled (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

**Replacement interval: the books differ.** Grossman changes the dressing every 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri repeats it every six to eight weeks (2019) [SRC]; Cvek reviews at 3-6 months (2007) [SRC] and cites a clinical study in which changes less than 5 months apart gave faster barrier formation (2007) [SRC]; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon do not replace an intact dressing (2017) [SRC], nor does Kahler (2021) [SRC]; Weine recalls at 6 months (2004) [SRC]. Pitt Ford and Shabahang recall at 4 to 6 weeks (2002) [SRC], leave a dense dressing in place (2002) [SRC], and remove it once healing is good at 1 year (2002) [SRC]; Baumann and Beer change it every 3-6 months (2010) [SRC]; Gutmann and Lovdahl change it every 3 months, with intervals up to 12 months later on (2011) [SRC], and report that changing it has been shown to be counterproductive to hard-tissue formation (2011) [SRC]. That last statement contradicts Cvek's faster barriers with changes less than 5 months apart; the two rest on different studies, and Gutmann and Lovdahl's supporting reference is, as printed, a study of MTA renewal (see slips) [Likely]. Shabahang says there is no consensus (2019) [SRC]. This is a protocol disagreement on thin evidence, not a factual error by any one book [Likely].

**Time to barrier: the books differ.** Grossman gives 6 months to 4 years (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and, in a clinical note on the same page, "usually complete in 6 months, or 2 years at most" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]: the two statements in one chapter do not match [Certain]. Shabahang gives 3 months to 2 years (2019) [SRC]; Diogenes, Botero and Kang 3-9 months (2021) [SRC]; Sikri 9-18 months (2019) [SRC]; Cvek 6-18 months (2007) [SRC]; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon 6-24 months, mean 1 year 7 months (2017) [SRC]; Kahler 3-18 months (2021) [SRC]; Paranjpe and Noblett up to 18 months (2021) [SRC]; Gutmann and Lovdahl a mean of 34.2 weeks, range 13 to 67, in incisors (2011) [SRC]; Baumann and Beer about 1 year on average (2010) [SRC]. Cvek ties the time to the width of the foramen and the size of the lesion (2007) [SRC], so the ranges reflect different case mixes rather than contradiction [Likely]; Pitt Ford and Shabahang agree that the barrier forms faster when the apical opening is narrower (2002) [SRC].

**Mechanism.** Cvek relates the effects of calcium hydroxide to its pH of 12.5 (2007) [SRC]: killing bacteria makes healing of the surviving apical tissue and barrier formation possible (2007) [SRC]. The barrier is cementum-like rather than dentine, which shows that periapical tissue forms it (Cvek, 2007) [SRC], and its position depends on where the paste meets vital tissue (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Kahler likens it to vital pulp therapy: a mild inflammatory stimulus in a bacteria-free canal (2021) [SRC]. Grossman adds the speculation that surviving pulp and odontoblasts resume work guided by a reactivated sheath of Hertwig (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and stresses that disinfection is indispensable (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Shabahang reports that high pH may activate alkaline phosphatase (2019) [SRC], that pH matters more than added calcium ions (2019) [SRC], and that in Dylewski's monkeys the apex repaired by connective-tissue proliferation rather than true root growth (2019) [SRC]. Gutmann and Lovdahl note that odontoblasts rarely if ever survive pulpal necrosis (2011) [SRC], which weakens Grossman's odontoblast speculation. **Does calcium hydroxide stimulate the barrier? The books conflict.** Baumann and Beer describe a stimulating effect leading to proliferation of periodontal ligament cells (2010) [SRC]; Pitt Ford and Shabahang say it has been used "in the mistaken belief that it stimulates hard tissue formation" (2002) [SRC] and that debridement and a complete coronal seal matter most (2002) [SRC]. Cvek's account (bacterial kill lets surviving tissue heal) sits between the two. These are hypotheses that sit side by side, not one settled mechanism [Likely].

**Types of barrier.** Frank's four outcomes (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]: the apex closes with slight recession of the canal; the apex is obliterated with no change in the canal; no radiographic change but an instrument meets a definite stop; or a calcific bridge forms just coronal to the apex. Weine adds a fifth, no change with a persisting or enlarging lesion, which is failure and calls for a repeat (2004) [SRC]. The barrier can form around the apex, over the foramen or inside the canal (Cvek, 2007) [SRC], sometimes short of the radiographic apex (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Histologically it is bone, dentine, osteodentine or cementum (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], with soft-tissue inclusions giving a "Swiss cheese" consistency (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; it is porous and may let irritants through (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Gutmann and Lovdahl call it a gnarled osteocementum (2011) [SRC].

**Evaluation of success.** Pitt Ford and Shabahang require absence of signs or symptoms of periradicular pathosis (2002) [SRC] and a calcific barrier shown on radiographs or, more often, by tactile probing (2002) [SRC]; failure is usually bacterial contamination (2002) [SRC]. A barrier seen on the radiograph and felt with a file (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; no external resorption, root fracture or periradicular breakdown (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; the tooth keeps erupting with the alveolus (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; review at 6 and 12 months (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Success with calcium hydroxide is reported in 79-96% of teeth (Cvek, 2007) [SRC], with healing in 92% of 589 teeth at 4 years (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; a 98-tooth study showed over 90% (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Baumann and Beer give 74% to 96%, citing Cvek 1993 and Andreasen 2000 (2010) [SRC]: the lower bound differs from Cvek's own chapter (79%), probably a different series or a slip; the source paper was not opened [Likely].

**Drawbacks.** Variable time to closure (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC], multiple visits to refresh the paste (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC], coronal leakage and reinfection (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC] and patient attrition (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; exudate can dissolve the paste (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and calcium hydroxide may damage Hertwig's sheath (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Exposure beyond one month may change dentine structure (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Cvek found cervical fractures in 77% of the least developed incisors against 2% of mature teeth (2007) [SRC] and accepts that long-term calcium hydroxide may make dentine more brittle (2007) [SRC]; Kahler reports about 30% fracturing during or after treatment (2021) [SRC] but cites a newer study suggesting the stage of root development, not calcium hydroxide, explains the fractures (2021) [SRC]. That is a disagreement between studies (laboratory dentine tests versus a controlled comparison), and neither book claims it is settled [Likely]. Kim lists the same two drawbacks of calcium hydroxide apexification, a long treatment and an increased risk of fracture (Kim, 2016) [SRC].

**The MTA plug in brief (P1 gives the comparison).** Plug thickness differs: 3-4 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], 3-5 mm (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC], 4 mm (Bakland, 2007) [SRC], at least 5 mm (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; these are overlapping recommendations, with 4 mm common to all but the last [Likely]. Grossman medicates the canal with calcium hydroxide for a week first (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC] and checks the set cement at 48 hours (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. MTA does not harm dentine (Bakland, 2007) [SRC] and is covered by a band of cementum (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; it discoloured 22.7% of teeth in one series (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC] and costs more and handles poorly (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** "A method to induce a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex or the continued apical development of an incompletely formed root in teeth with necrotic pulps" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012). The root does not lengthen or thicken (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**Aims.** A hard-tissue apical barrier against which filling material is condensed without extrusion (Sikri, 2019).

**Indications.** Non-vital immature tooth, open apex, thin walls, no apical stop (Kahler, 2021).
**Contraindications.** Vertical root fracture, replacement resorption, very short root, marginal periodontal breakdown, vital pulp (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).

**Materials**
- Calcium hydroxide: introduced by Kaiser (1964), popularised by Frank (1966) with CMCP (Paranjpe and Noblett, 2021); Heithersay used Pulpdent (methylcellulose) (Shabahang, 2019).
- One-visit: MTA (Witherspoon and Ham, 2001), Biodentine, CEM (Tonini and Garo, 2024).

**Technique (calcium hydroxide)**
1. Rubber dam, access, length 2 mm short of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021).
2. Light filing, copious 0.5% NaOCl (Kahler, 2021).
3. Paste packed against apical tissue, canal backfilled, access sealed.
4. Review 3-monthly; refill if washed out.
5. Barrier seen radiographically and felt with a file: gentle thermoplasticised obturation (Kahler, 2021; Cvek, 2007).

**Mechanism.** pH 12.5 kills bacteria; vital apical tissue lays down cementum-like hard tissue where it meets the paste (Cvek, 2007). Debridement and coronal seal matter more than the material (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002).

**Types of barrier (Frank)** (Shabahang, 2019)
1. Apex closes, canal recedes slightly.
2. Apex obliterated, canal unchanged.
3. No radiographic change; file meets a stop.
4. Calcific bridge just coronal to the apex.

Barrier: porous, cementum-like, with soft-tissue islands ("Swiss cheese") (Kahler, 2021).

**Time to barrier: books differ**
| Book | Time |
|---|---|
| Grossman | 6 months-4 years |
| Ingle's | 3 months-2 years |
| Torabinejad | 3-9 months |
| Sikri | 9-18 months |
| Andreasen | 6-18 months |
| Harty's | 6-24 months |

Replacement: every 3 months (Gopikrishna, 2021), 6-8 weeks (Sikri, 2019), or only when washed out (Kahler, 2021); changing may even hinder the barrier (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).

**Success.** No signs or symptoms, barrier present (Pitt Ford and Shabahang, 2002); no resorption or fracture (Sikri, 2019); 79-96% (Cvek, 2007).

**Drawbacks.** Long, many visits, compliance, reinfection, porous barrier; dentine may weaken after a month of calcium hydroxide (Shabahang, 2019); cervical fracture 77% in the least developed roots against 2% in mature (Cvek, 2007).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Kaiser 1964, Frank 1966 (calcium hydroxide + CMCP); Witherspoon and Ham 2001 (one-visit MTA).
- Length 2 mm short; paste must touch vital apical tissue; review 3-monthly.
- Barrier is cementum-like, porous ("Swiss cheese"), may sit short of the apex.
- Frank's four outcomes; Weine's fifth = failure.
- Success 79-96%; cervical fracture 77% (least developed) to 2% (mature).
- Apexification closes the apex; it never thickens or lengthens the root.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing that apexification makes the root grow; only apexogenesis or regeneration does that.
- Leaving out disinfection; infection prevents closure whatever the paste.
- Giving one fixed "time to barrier" as fact; the books range from 3 months to 4 years.
- Forcing gutta-percha to the radiographic apex when the barrier has formed short of it.
- Adding barium sulphate to the paste and then being unable to judge washout.

## MNEMONIC
- Frank's outcomes **C-O-S-B**: **C**loses with recession, **O**bliterated apex, **S**top felt only, **B**ridge coronal to apex.
