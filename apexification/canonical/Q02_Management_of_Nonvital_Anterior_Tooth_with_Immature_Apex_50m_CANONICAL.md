# Discuss various methods to manage a nonvital anterior tooth with an immature apex.

**Marks:** 50 · Essay

## 1. Introduction and definitions

When the pulp of an immature incisor dies, usually after trauma, root development stops, leaving thin walls and an open apex (Bakland, 2007).

- **Apexification:** a method to induce a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex, or continued apical development, in teeth with necrotic pulps (American Association of Endodontists, 2012).
- **Apical barrier:** blockage of the apical foramen by induced hard tissue or artificial material; an MTA plug is "one-step apexification" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).
- **Regenerative endodontic procedure (REP):** biologically based replacement of damaged root structure and pulp-dentine cells, also called revascularisation (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

Apexification closes the apex but does not lengthen or thicken the root (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 2. Problems of the tooth

| Problem | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Blunderbuss canal**: apical diameter greater than coronal (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Weine, 2004) | Walls hard to clean; canal hard to seal (Cvek, 2007) |
| No apical constriction (Kahler, 2021) | Irrigant, medicament and filling extrude (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) |
| Thin dentine walls (Shabahang, 2019) | **Cervical root fracture** (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) |
| Short root; no further growth once the pulp is lost (Weine, 2004; Shabahang, 2019) | Weak tooth for life |
| Infection, periapical lesion, resorption (Bakland, 2007) | Disinfection comes first |

**Diagram to draw:** immature non-vital incisor — blunderbuss canal, thin cervical walls, open apex, periapical lesion; label each problem.

## 3. Assessment and choice of method

- **History:** trauma type, swelling, sinus, discoloration, mobility, remaining crown (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).
- **Pulp tests** are unreliable in immature teeth; cold is better than EPT (Shabahang, 2019). Diagnose necrosis on at least two signs (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).
- **Radiographs:** root stage, wall thickness, apex width, lesion, resorption; CBCT if in doubt (Shabahang, 2019). A normal developing apex can look radiolucent; compare the contralateral tooth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- **Contraindications to root-end closure:** vertical and most horizontal root fractures, ankylosis, very short roots, marginal periodontal breakdown (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Choice.** REP suits a very immature root (stages 1-3), an apex about **1 mm** or wider, no post needed and a compliant patient (Kim, 2016; Widbiller and Galler, 2024), although the minimum apex size has not been established (Diogenes and Smith, 2019). An MTA/bioceramic plug suits an apex wider than size **50-60**, a tooth needing a post, poor compliance or a failed REP (Tonini and Garo, 2024; Kang and Bogen, 2017). Calcium hydroxide suits settings without calcium silicate cements.

**Diagram to draw:** decision flowchart — very immature, no post, compliant → REP; post needed or poor compliance → MTA plug; REP fails → MTA plug; unrestorable → extraction.

## 4. Access and disinfection (common to all methods)

Disinfection is indispensable to apical closure (Gopikrishna, 2021).

1. Rubber dam and access (Cvek, 2007).
2. Working length radiographically, at least **2 mm** short of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021); apex locators are unreliable (Shabahang, 2019).
3. **Minimal instrumentation:** brush the walls with a cotton-wrapped file or gentle circumferential filing, preserving apical vital tissue (Bakland, 2007; Cvek, 2007). Weine (2004) rasps with a Hedstrom file, whereas Walton and Torabinejad (2002) reject it because it may perforate the thin walls. Debridement is mainly chemical (Shabahang, 2019).
4. **Irrigation** slowly, needle about 1 mm short (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021). Strength differs: Cvek (2007) and Kahler (2021) use **0.5% NaOCl** to limit extrusion; Kang and Bogen (2017) 1.5% for 15-20 minutes; Sikri (2019) 5% NaOCl with 17% EDTA. NaOCl exposure beyond 20 minutes alters dentine (Shabahang, 2019).
5. **Medicament.** Calcium hydroxide (pH 12.5) kills 99.9% of canal bacteria in vitro (Cvek, 2007); it is effective after 1 week and should not exceed 1 month (Kahler, 2021; Bakland, 2007). Triple antibiotic paste (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, minocycline) stains the crown and is harsher on apical stem cells (Shabahang, 2019; Sikri, 2019).

## 5. Calcium hydroxide apexification (multiple-visit)

Popularised by Frank in 1966 (Gopikrishna, 2021); the aim is a hard-tissue barrier to fill against (Cvek, 2007).

**Mechanism (disputed).** Shabahang (2019) gives high pH an active role, possibly activating alkaline phosphatase, and more important than calcium ions; Cvek (2007) and Walton and Torabinejad (2002) argue that bacterial elimination and a coronal seal, not induction, allow the tissues to heal.

**Technique**
1. Disinfect and dress (Section 4).
2. Pack a thick paste (with sterile water, saline or methylcellulose) to the apex with a carrier and pluggers, in contact with vital tissue (Gopikrishna, 2021; Cvek, 2007; Sikri, 2019).
3. **Barium sulphate:** Kahler (2021) and Sigurdsson and Bourguignon (2017) omit it so that washout can be judged, whereas Sikri (2019) and Walton and Torabinejad (2002, 9:1) add it for radiopacity.
4. Seal with RMGIC or ZOE (Gopikrishna, 2021; Cvek, 2007).
5. Recall; when a barrier is seen radiographically and felt with a file, obturate (Kahler, 2021).

**Replacement interval — no consensus (Shabahang, 2019):**

| Author | Advice |
|---|---|
| Gopikrishna (2021) | Change and recall every 3 months |
| Sikri (2019) | Repeat every 6-8 weeks |
| Kahler (2021); Sigurdsson and Bourguignon (2017); Walton and Torabinejad (2002) | Review 3-monthly; replace only if washed out |
| Gutmann and Lovdahl (2011) | Changing may be counterproductive to hard-tissue formation |

**Duration** ranges from 3-9 months (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) to 6 months-4 years (Gopikrishna, 2021), and 6-24 months in Sigurdsson and Bourguignon (2017); it is shorter with a narrow apex and no infection (Shabahang, 2019).

**Barrier:** irregular, porous ("Swiss cheese") cementum-like tissue with soft-tissue inclusions, at, over or inside the apex (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021).

**Drawbacks:** long, unpredictable treatment, many visits, reinfection, cervical fracture (Shabahang, 2019; Bakland, 2007).

## 6. MTA / bioceramic apical barrier (one-step apexification)

An artificial barrier is packed apically, so the canal can be filled without waiting for a biological barrier (Bakland, 2007). Cementum forms over it (Shabahang, 2019) and it does not weaken dentine (Bakland, 2007).

**Technique**
1. Disinfect. **One or two visits:** Sigurdsson and Bourguignon (2017) and Gopikrishna (2021) dress with calcium hydroxide for 1-2 weeks, since set MTA does not disinfect (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010) and the periapical pH needs raising; Kang and Bogen (2017) place the barrier at the first visit.
2. Optional collagen or calcium sulphate matrix (Kahler, 2021).
3. Mix MTA 3:1 to wet sand (Gopikrishna, 2021); place increments with a carrier and pre-fitted pluggers (Shabahang, 2019).
4. **Plug thickness** differs: 3-4 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); 4 mm (Bakland, 2007); 4-5 mm (Kahler, 2021; Kang and Bogen, 2017); at least 5 mm (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021); 3-6 mm (Azim and Aksel, 2022). **4-5 mm** satisfies most books.
5. Moist pellet; confirm the set (4-6 hours to 48 hours; Bakland, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021); backfill and bond.

**Materials:** MTA (staining, slow set, cost); Biodentine (sets in about 12 minutes, no staining, evidence inconclusive) (Tonini and Garo, 2024); BC putty, easier handling (Azim and Aksel, 2022).

**Problems:** extrusion (over 2 mm may retard healing), discoloration (22.7% in one series) and thin walls that remain (Shabahang, 2019).

**Diagram to draw:** MTA plug — matrix beyond apex, 4-5 mm MTA, gutta-percha backfill, composite below the crest.

## 7. Regenerative endodontic procedure

**Concept.** After disinfection, stem cells of the apical papilla, which survive necrosis, and a blood-clot scaffold repopulate the canal, so the root may lengthen and thicken (Kang and Bogen, 2017; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**AAE goals:** primary, no symptoms and bony healing; secondary, thicker and/or longer root; tertiary, a positive vitality response (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019, Ch.38).

**Protocol**
- *Visit 1:* minimal instrumentation; 1.5% NaOCl 20 mL per canal, then saline; calcium hydroxide or antibiotic paste; temporary seal. **TAP strength** differs: Kim (2016) and Diogenes and Smith (2019) keep it at 1 mg/mL or less because stronger pastes kill apical stem cells, whereas Diogenes, Simon and Law (2021) give 1-10 mg/mL.
- *Visit 2 (2-4 weeks):* 3% mepivacaine without vasoconstrictor; 17% EDTA; bleeding induced past the apex up to the CEJ; collagen matrix; 2-3 mm tricalcium silicate (Biodentine or BC putty rather than MTA, for colour); GIC and bonded composite (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021).

**Outcomes:** healing nearly 100%, but the tissue is repair tissue, calcified in 62% of one series (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021); root growth occurs in only 27-55%, and apexification remains the fallback (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).

**Diagram to draw:** REP — clot to CEJ, collagen, 2-3 mm tricalcium silicate, GIC, composite; arrows from the apical papilla.

## 8. Other methods

- **Apical surgery:** traumatic in children (Weine, 2004); salvage only, with MTA root-end filling (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Cvek, 2007).
- **Extraction:** last resort (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002). Implants are contraindicated until growth is complete, so keeping the tooth as a space maintainer is valuable (Kahler, 2021; Cvek, 2007).
- **Autotransplantation:** a premolar transplanted at 10-13 years preserves the alveolus; 5-year survival 98-99% (Andreasen, Andersson and Tsukiboshi, 2007).

## 9. Comparison of the main methods

| Feature | Calcium hydroxide | MTA/bioceramic plug | REP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visits | Many | 1-2 | 2-3 plus long recall |
| Time to obturation | Months to years | Weeks (0.75 vs 7 months; Shabahang, 2019) | No obturation |
| Root length/thickness | No change | No change | May increase (variable) |
| Dentine weakening | After long exposure | No (Bakland, 2007) | Short medicament only |
| Discoloration | Slight grey possible (Buerkle and Meissner, 2010) | MTA stains; Biodentine less | Minocycline, MTA cap |
| Healing | 79-96% (Cvek, 2007) | 81-100% (Tonini and Garo, 2024) | About 91% (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) |
| Main drawback | Time, compliance, fracture | Cost, placement, thin walls | Unpredictable root growth; no post |

## 10. Obturation and restoration

- Fill to the barrier, not the radiographic apex (Kahler, 2021), gently, because a porous barrier can break and pass sealer (Cvek, 2007).
- Custom tip-softened cone (Cvek, 2007) or thermoplasticised gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021); in short roots, MTA throughout (Sikri, 2019).
- Most failures are **cervical root fractures** (Shabahang, 2019): place bonded composite in the cervical canal to below the alveolar crest (Kahler, 2021; Bakland, 2007); avoid metal posts and use a bonded fibre post if one is needed (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011); coronal seal with composite over GIC (Kang and Bogen, 2017).

## 11. Follow-up and outcome

- Review at 6 and 12 months, then yearly (Kang and Bogen, 2017), for 4 years (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- **Success:** no symptoms, periapical healing, no resorption or fracture (Sikri, 2019); the barrier is often detected only by probing (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- **Failure:** persistent symptoms or radiolucency, no barrier; usually coronal leakage (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- **Survival** is limited by cervical fracture: 77% in the least developed roots, falling to 2% in mature teeth (Cvek, 2007); about 30% overall (Kahler, 2021). At 18 months, survival was 77% after calcium hydroxide, 95% after an MTA plug and 100% after REP (Diogenes and Smith, 2019).

## 12. Critical appraisal of the evidence

- **Does calcium hydroxide cause the fractures?** Gopikrishna (2021), Bakland (2007) and Shabahang (2019) attribute fracture to long-term calcium hydroxide weakening dentine, but this rests on laboratory and animal data. Cvek's clinical series relates fracture to root stage, and Kahler (2021) calls the weakening not conclusive. Thin walls are the established cause; calcium hydroxide is a plausible added factor.
- **Is MTA more successful?** Tonini and Garo (2024) report higher success with MTA, but the meta-analyses cited by Shabahang (2019) find similar success; the gain is speed and fewer visits, not healing. One meta-analysis had only four studies (Tonini and Garo, 2024). Single-visit bioceramic barriers give results similar to calcium hydroxide (Ørstavik, 2020).
- **Is REP better?** It can increase root dimensions, but healing and survival match apexification in case series and retrospective studies (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021); traumatised teeth may respond less well (Ørstavik, 2020).

## Conclusion

All methods heal well; survival depends on root maturity, cervical reinforcement and coronal seal. Choose REP where root growth is wanted and no post is needed, otherwise an MTA/bioceramic plug; calcium hydroxide apexification is valid but slow.
