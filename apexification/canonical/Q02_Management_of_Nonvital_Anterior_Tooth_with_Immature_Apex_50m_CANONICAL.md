## 1. Introduction and definitions

### Explanation

**The clinical situation.** An anterior tooth whose pulp dies before the root is complete (usually after trauma in a child) stops developing: Bakland describes the result as a tooth with thin, fragile canal walls and an open apex whose necrotic pulp often becomes infected and, untreated, is lost (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Kang and Bogen note that a newly erupted tooth may need one to five years to complete its root, so the window of vulnerability is long: "these teeth may require one to 5 years to complete maturation and root-end closure" (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC].

**Terms.** The AAE Glossary defines apexification as "A method to induce a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex or the continued apical development of an incompletely formed root in teeth with necrotic pulps" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Grossman's narrower wording is a method to induce a calcific barrier across the open apex of an immature, pulpless tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri speaks of inducing apical closure by mineralised tissue in the apical pulp region of a nonvital tooth (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. An apical barrier is "A blockage of the apical foramen; may be an induced hard tissue or artificial materials" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC], which is why the MTA plug is also called one-step apexification (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Regenerative endodontics is defined as biologically based procedures designed to physiologically replace damaged tooth structures (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter adds that the regenerative endodontic procedure (REP) is "also known as pulp revascularization or pulp revitalization" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC].

**Apexification is not root growth.** Grossman stresses that "Actual root growth does not occur as a result of apexification" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and Torabinejad's chapter that apexified immature teeth "do not undergo changes in the root dimension" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. The Glossary's definition, which still allows "continued apical development", reflects the observation that continued root development or "formation of a separate part of the root may occasionally occur" when pulp tissue or the root sheath survives (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; the two are not in conflict, they describe the usual result and the occasional bonus. [Likely]

**The question's scope.** The options the books give are: (1) calcium hydroxide apexification, (2) an artificial apical barrier of MTA or another calcium silicate cement, (3) regenerative endodontic procedures, and older or last-resort options. Harty's lists the first three as "Several approaches exist: classical apexification, MTA apexification, or the ‘revascularization’ procedures" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Kang and Bogen list the traditional ones as "extraction, conventional root canal treatment, or apexification procedures" (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** When the pulp of an immature incisor dies (usually after trauma), root development stops, leaving thin, fragile walls and an open apex; the infected canal, untreated, loses the tooth (Bakland, 2007).

**Definitions** (American Association of Endodontists, 2012):
- **Apexification:** inducing a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex, or continued apical development, in teeth with necrotic pulps.
- **Apical barrier:** blockage of the apical foramen by induced hard tissue or artificial material (MTA: "one-step apexification"; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).
- **Regenerative endodontic procedure (REP):** biologically based replacement of root structure and pulp-dentine cells; also called revascularisation.

Apexification closes the apex but does not lengthen or thicken the root (Gopikrishna, 2021).

## 2. Problems of the non-vital immature anterior tooth

### Explanation

**Canal shape.** Weine's definition: an open apex "refers to the absence of sufficient root development to provide a conical taper to the canal and is referred to as a blunderbuss canal" (Weine, 2004) [SRC]; the Glossary says "the apical diameter of the pulp canal is greater than the coronal diameter" (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. Cvek: "Wide or funnel-shaped root canals make endodontic treatment in immature teeth difficult", because necrotic tissue is hard to remove from the walls and the canal hard to obturate (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Kahler adds the consequence for obturation: "The canal is often wider apically than coronally", so softened material is needed and, with no barrier, it moves into the periapical tissues (Kahler, 2021) [SRC].

**No apical stop.** Apexification is indicated where "standard instrumentation techniques cannot create an apical stop to facilitate effective root canal filling" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; "the open apex increases the risk of extruding material into the periradicular tissues" (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC].

**Thin, fracture-prone walls.** Ingle's names both problems together: "not only because of a blunderbuss root apex, but also because of thin and fragile dentin walls" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Harty's: these teeth are "especially at risk of root fracture at the neck of the tooth" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Once the pulp is lost, "continued root development ceases" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC], so the crown-root ratio stays unfavourable and the cervical dentine stays thin. Weine describes the root shape after apexification: "The root may be shorter, the canal wider with thin lateral walls, or even inversely tapered" (Weine, 2004) [SRC].

**Working length.** "Electronic apex locators are not accurate in immature teeth or teeth with wide open apices", so Ingle's recommends radiographic length (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

**Patient factors.** The patients are young; long treatments test compliance, which Ingle's lists among the drawbacks of long-term calcium hydroxide (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Weine recalls the psychological cost of surgery in children: "Flapping teeth on youngsters is not easy" (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Immature teeth also have large tubules and greater dentine permeability (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC].

| Problem | Consequence |

## 3. Case assessment and diagnosis

### Explanation

**Is the pulp really dead?** Harty's advises treating the pulp of an immature tooth as vital "until there is clear evidence" and asks for "at least two signs and symptoms indicating pulp necrosis and infection" before endodontic treatment (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Its list includes pain, swelling, sinus tract, excessive mobility, tenderness, periapical radiolucency, colour change, persistent unresponsiveness to sensibility tests and failure of root development (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Ingle's: pulp testing in children is less predictable; "most reliable test may be cold testing" while electric pulp tests are less accurate in immature teeth, and laser Doppler blood-flow measurement may be more accurate; after intrusion, "intrusive injuries consistently lead to diminished PBF", so necrosis is likely (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Endodontology warns that transient apical breakdown after trauma must not be mistaken for an inflammatory lesion (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

**Root development.** Determined "primarily achieved radiographically" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; but "the buccal-lingual dimension of the root canal is typically greater than the mesio-distal dimension", so a periapical film can show a convergent canal that is still divergent buccolingually; CBCT gives the full picture (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

**Choice of option.** Ingle's decision chart sends an immature tooth with irreversible pulpitis or necrosis to a root-end closure procedure (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Bioceramics lists indications for an apical plug: "apex greater than 50–60 ISO in diameter", immature apex, irregular apex, parallel walls, internal resorption (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Cohen's REP case selection is the immature tooth with an open apex that is negative to pulp testing; teeth needing a post are poor candidates (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Kang and Bogen call the one-visit barrier "a valid option for challenging behavioral management cases in pediatric patients" (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. So the choice turns on how much root has formed, whether a post will be needed, how much crown is left, compliance and aesthetics. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**History and clinical:** trauma type (intrusion carries most risk of necrosis), sinus, swelling, discoloration, mobility, remaining crown (Shabahang, 2019).
**Pulp tests:** less reliable in immature teeth; cold better than EPT; laser Doppler measures blood flow (Shabahang, 2019). Diagnose necrosis on at least two signs (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).
**Radiograph:** root stage, wall thickness, apex width, lesion, resorption; buccolingual width is underestimated, so CBCT if in doubt (Shabahang, 2019).

**Choosing the option:**
- Very immature root, no post needed, cooperative patient: REP (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021).
- Apex over size 50-60, post needed, poor compliance, failed REP: MTA/bioceramic plug (Tonini and Garo, 2024; Kang and Bogen, 2017).
- Calcium silicate unavailable: calcium hydroxide apexification. [Likely]

## 4. Access, disinfection and intracanal medicaments (common to all options)

### Explanation

**Disinfection is the precondition.** Grossman: "it appears that disinfection of the root canal is indispensable to apical closure" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's: "remaining bacteria in the root canal system will lead to continued irritation" that interferes with root-end development (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Kang and Bogen and Torabinejad's chapter show that REP outcome too depends on disinfection rather than on protocol details (Kang and Bogen, 2017; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC].

**Instrumentation: minimal.** Ingle's: "debridement should be achieved primarily through chemical means" because the canals are already wide (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Cvek: "Only moderate pressure should be used, in order to avoid weakening of the root canal walls in the cervical area", and vital tissue in the apical lumen "should not be removed" because it may improve barrier quality or root growth (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Bakland: "it is preferable to gently brush the walls and use a liberal amount of irrigation with sodium hypochlorite"; a cotton-wrapped file makes the brush (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Weine uses "a large Hedstrom file may be used to rasp the walls of the canal with heavy irrigation" and a cotton-wrapped broach (Weine, 2004) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter, larger K-files used "primarily by circumferential filing" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman sets working length "at least 2 mm short of the radiographic apex of the tooth" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Irrigant concentration: the books disagree.** Cvek flushes with 0.5% sodium hypochlorite (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Kahler uses "copious irrigation with 0.5% NaOCl" because of the danger of extrusion through the wide apex, with volume compensating for strength (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter and Kang and Bogen use passive irrigation with 1.5% NaOCl for 15-20 minutes (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC], and 1.5% is the REP standard (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri, before the MTA plug, irrigates "with 5.0% sodium hypochlorite and 17% EDTA" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Weine alternates sodium hypochlorite and hydrogen peroxide (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. This is a difference of protocol and of era: the low concentrations are chosen for safety at the open apex and for stem-cell survival in REP. Ingle's adds a time limit, structural changes when dentine is exposed to NaOCl "for periods exceeding 20 minutes" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC], and Cvek reports in vitro that 3 or 5% NaOCl increases the brittleness of dentine like calcium hydroxide (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. [Certain that the books differ; Likely that 0.5-1.5% is the safer choice in thin roots]

**Calcium hydroxide.** Its actions all relate to its high pH of 12.5: in vitro 99.9% of common canal bacteria are killed within minutes, and it dissolves necrotic pulp remnants, which matters because "the thin dentinal walls do not permit intensive reaming" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Kahler's creamy mix is "spun into the canal with a Lentulo spiral instrument"; it is effective after at least 1 week, and "Further treatment should not be delayed more than 1 month" because it washes out through the open apex (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Harty's starts with a thin mix ("less than toothpaste consistency") for about 3 weeks (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Cvek finds no justification for adding camphorated parachlorophenol, corticosteroids or antibiotics (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. The time limit: Ingle's reports structural change in dentine after calcium hydroxide "for periods exceeding one month" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Bakland limits exposure "to one month or less", noting it is "very effective if left in place for up to two weeks" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC].

**Triple antibiotic paste (TAP).** Metronidazole, ciprofloxacin and minocycline, recommended by Trope's group (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Drawbacks: crown staining from minocycline, hence double antibiotic paste (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri raises bacterial resistance and suggests cephalexin in place of minocycline (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; and the double and triple pastes "reduce the survival of these cells in contrast to calcium hydroxide" (stem cells of the apical papilla) (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri calls four weeks of TAP an effective approach for nonvital open-apex teeth (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle:** disinfection is indispensable to apical closure (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Steps:**
1. Rubber dam; adequate access (Cvek, 2007).
2. Working length radiographically, about 2 mm short of the radiographic apex (Gopikrishna, 2021); apex locators unreliable (Shabahang, 2019).
3. Minimal instrumentation: "brush" the walls with a large Hedstrom or cotton-wrapped file, moderate pressure, preserving apical vital tissue (Bakland, 2007; Weine, 2004; Cvek, 2007); debridement is mainly chemical (Shabahang, 2019).
4. Irrigation: copious, slow, needle short of the apex. Concentration differs: 0.5% NaOCl (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021); 1.5% for 15-20 minutes (Kang and Bogen, 2017); 5% NaOCl + 17% EDTA (Sikri, 2019). NaOCl beyond 20 minutes weakens dentine (Shabahang, 2019).
5. **Intracanal medicament:**
- *Calcium hydroxide:* pH 12.5, kills 99.9% of canal bacteria in vitro, dissolves pulp remnants (Cvek, 2007); effective after 1 week, not more than 1 month before the barrier visit (Kahler, 2021; Bakland, 2007). No CMCP additive (Cvek, 2007).
- *Triple antibiotic paste* (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, minocycline): minocycline stains the crown, risk of resistance, harsher on apical stem cells than calcium hydroxide (Shabahang, 2019; Sikri, 2019).

## 5. Option 1: calcium hydroxide apexification (multiple-visit)

### Explanation

**History and rationale.** Frank's 1966 description popularised the technique (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's reports Frank's proposal of "a thick paste of calcium hydroxide and CMCP" with recall at three- to six-month intervals (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Cvek: its purpose is periapical healing, "including arrest of inflammatory root resorption and formation of a hard tissue barrier apically" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

**Mechanism.** Several explanations are offered. (a) High pH: Ingle's reports a monkey study in which pH, not exogenous calcium, mattered more: the pH plays a more significant "role in the induction of root-end closure than the presence of exogenous calcium ions" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; the high pH "may activate alkaline phosphatase activity" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. (b) Disinfection: Cvek says ectopic bone induction by calcium hydroxide is not convincing, and that elimination of bacteria lets the surrounding vital tissue heal and form the barrier (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. (c) Surviving tissue: Grossman cites speculation that residual pulp and odontoblasts resume matrix formation "guided by the reactivated sheath of Hertwig" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Weine reports both views, sheath survival and an osteogenic effect of high pH, and notes that non-high-pH materials also close apices (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Sikri warns that calcium hydroxide "may damage the Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath" (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. [Likely: barrier formation is mainly a healing response of periapical tissue once infection is removed]

**Nature of the barrier.** Cvek: cementum-like tissue rather than dentine, irregular layers that "often include islands of soft connective tissue"; the barrier forms wherever calcium hydroxide meets vital tissue, "around the apex of the tooth, over the apical foramen or in the root canal" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Grossman: bone, dentine, osteodentin or cementum (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Gutmann: "a gnarled osteocementum type of material", often porous (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; Kahler and Weine: "Swiss cheese" (Kahler, 2021; Weine, 2004) [SRC]; Ingle's: porous, "may be permeable to microbial irritants" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. The books agree on its nature.

**Technique (composite of the books).** After disinfection, a thick paste is packed to the apex. Grossman mixes calcium hydroxide with sterile water to a thick consistency, delivers it with a carrier, condenses it with finger pluggers so it contacts the periapical tissue, and seals with RMGIC (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek fills with a spiral or syringe, presses it towards the apex with a cotton pellet to contact vital tissue, and seals with zinc oxide-eugenol (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Harty's packs "a thicker, almost dry mixture of calcium hydroxide and sterile saline" at the second visit (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Vehicle: Sikri prefers methylcellulose (Pulpdent) for consistency and low solubility (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Radiopacifier: Kahler and Harty's advise against barium sulphate, because washout is judged by radiodensity (Kahler, 2021; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Sikri says barium sulphate enhances radiopacity (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. That is a real difference in advice. [Certain]

**Replacement interval: the books disagree.**
- Grossman: change the dressing and recall every 3 months till the barrier is seen (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].
- Kahler: radiograph every 3 months; "If no washout is evident, the calcium hydroxide can be left intact for another 3 months"; "Excessive calcium hydroxide dressing changes should be avoided" because its initial toxicity delays healing (Kahler, 2021) [SRC].
- Harty's: recall every 3 months; "it is not recommended to replace the mixture if it appears to be intact" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC].
- Cvek: control at 3- to 6-month intervals; refill when the level falls in the cervical canal; in monkeys intermediate replacement did not speed apexification, but in a clinical study more frequent changes gave a faster barrier (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].
- Sikri: repeat every six to eight weeks, noting that some authors change monthly, others at 3 and 6 months, others never (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].
- Gutmann: left or changed every 3 months, up to 12 months in later stages; changing has recently been shown "counterproductive to the formation of hard tissue" though it lessened inflammation (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].
- Endodontology: change every 3-6 months under rubber dam (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Weine: recall at 6 months (Weine, 2004) [SRC].
- Ingle's summarises: "there has been no consensus on the optimal timing to change the dressing material"; recommendations range from monthly to "not at all" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].
Kind of disagreement: genuine unresolved evidence (two of Cvek's own sources point opposite ways), not a slip. The modern drift (Kahler, Harty's, Gutmann) is to replace only on washout. [Likely]

**Total duration: the books disagree.** Grossman 6 months to 4 years, yet its clinical note says usually complete in 6 months, or 2 years at most (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter 3-9 months (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's 3 months to 2 years, with clinical series of 3-10 months, 10-14 weeks, within one year, slightly over 12 months and a mean of 8.6 months (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Cvek 6 to 18 months depending on apex width and lesion size (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Kahler 3 to 18 months (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's 6-24 months, average 1 year 7 months (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Sikri 9 to 18 months (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Gutmann a mean of 34.2 weeks, range 13 to 67 weeks (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; Endodontology about 1 year on average (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Bioceramics approximately six months (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. These are different study populations (age, apex width, infection), not contradictory facts; Ingle's notes that older children with narrower apices and teeth without periapical infection closed faster (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman's "6 months to 4 years" beside "2 years at most" in the same chapter is an internal inconsistency. [Certain]

**Verifying the barrier.** Kahler: wash out the paste with NaOCl and probe gently with a file; "When a hard-tissue barrier is indicated radiographically and can be probed with an instrument, the canal is ready for filling" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Weine lists five possible findings at recall: a blockage felt with an instrument, calcified material at or near the apex, closure without canal change, closure with canal narrowing, or failure (no change plus symptoms or a growing lesion), the last requiring repeat treatment (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Sikri gives "four clinical possibilities in apexification" including failure (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Drawbacks.** Ingle's lists: variable time; multiple visits to "replace and refresh the calcium hydroxide paste"; "potential for coronal leakage and canal re-infection"; undefined barrier tissue; and coronal fracture if follow-up is not strict (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Bakland: "increased treatment time, unpredictable results and delays in completing final restoration", with a high rate of cervical root fracture in the years after treatment (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Grossman: "Long-term use of intracanal Ca(OH)2 can cause weakening of" root dentine (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Fracture figures and their cause are discussed in section 12 and the Critical Evidence Synthesis.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Rationale.** Popularised by Frank (1966) with calcium hydroxide-CMCP paste (Shabahang, 2019). Aim: periapical healing, arrest of resorption and a hard-tissue barrier to fill against (Cvek, 2007).

**Mechanism.** High pH (12.5): antibacterial, may activate alkaline phosphatase; pH matters more than calcium ions (Shabahang, 2019). Removing bacteria lets periapical tissue form the barrier (Cvek, 2007); surviving Hertwig's sheath may contribute (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Technique.**
1. Disinfect and dress (section 4).
2. Pack a thick paste (calcium hydroxide with sterile water/saline, or methylcellulose paste) to the apex with carrier and pluggers, in contact with apical vital tissue (Gopikrishna, 2021; Cvek, 2007; Sikri, 2019); no barium sulphate, so washout can be judged (Kahler, 2021).
3. Seal with RMGIC or ZOE (Gopikrishna, 2021; Cvek, 2007).
4. Recall every 3 months; when a barrier is seen and felt with a file, obturate (Kahler, 2021).

**Replacement interval (no consensus; Shabahang, 2019):**

| Book | Advice |
|---|---|
| Grossman | Change every 3 months |
| Kahler; Sigurdsson and Bourguignon | Review 3-monthly; replace only if washed out |
| Cvek | Control 3-6 monthly; refill when level falls |
| Sikri | Every 6-8 weeks |
| Gutmann | 3 up to 12 months; changing may be counterproductive |

**Duration:** 3-9 months (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021); 6-18 months (Cvek, 2007); 6-24 months, mean 1 year 7 months (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017); 6 months to 4 years (Gopikrishna, 2021); shorter with narrow apex and no infection (Shabahang, 2019).

**Barrier:** irregular, porous ("Swiss cheese") cementum-like tissue with soft-tissue inclusions, at, over or inside the apex (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021).

**Drawbacks:** long, unpredictable treatment; many visits; coronal leakage and reinfection; dentine weakening and cervical fracture (Shabahang, 2019; Bakland, 2007).

## 6. Option 2: MTA / bioceramic apical barrier (one-step apexification)

### Explanation

**Rationale.** Bakland: MTA "provides an excellent means of generating an apical plug" over which gutta-percha or resin can be placed, and Tittle et al showed that "the induction of an apical hard tissue barrier was not necessary", since the plug itself is the stop (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Ingle's: the plug gives "more predictable closure of the apical opening, reduced treatment time, and fewer appointments" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Grossman: MTA/CSCs have "a better ability to seal and produce a superior barrier" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

**Properties that matter here.** pH "reported to be 10.2, rising to 12.5 at three hours and thereafter" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Bakland gives the same range, 10.2-12.5 (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. It sets with moisture, "a totally dry canal is not required" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Ingle's: "its ability to induce cementum formation around the repair area", a biological seal (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Bakland: "MTA does not appear to have a detrimental effect on dentin", and roots filled with MTA had fracture resistance equal to controls (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. But it is not a disinfectant: Harty's says it has "minimal bactericidal effect on several bacterial species" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC] and Endodontology that "MTA in its set condition exerts no disinfection effect" (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Drawbacks of MTA: "long setting time (from 2 hours and 45 minutes to 4 hours), high cost, difficult handling" and discoloration (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]; coronal discoloration in 22.7% of teeth in one series (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

**Other materials.** Biodentine sets in about 12 minutes, "does not cause discoloration" and can be used in a single visit, but "the current literature is inconclusive" on it as an apical plug (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. CEM cement is a further option (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Cohen's REP chapter notes Biodentine and EndoSequence putty have much less discoloration potential than MTA (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Older barrier materials: tricalcium phosphate, dentine chips, hydroxyapatite, collagen-calcium phosphate gel and OP-1, largely experimental (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; the Glossary lists dentine chips, collagen, MTA or calcium hydroxide as barrier materials (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC].

**One visit or two? The books disagree.**
- Two (or three) steps: Harty's "strongly" advises a two- to three-step approach when infection is likely, medicating "for 7 to 14 days with a thin calcium hydroxide paste" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Grossman medicates 1 week "to raise the acidic pH of inflamed periradicular tissue" (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Bioceramics uses a two-session protocol for the same reason (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]; Endodontology: infection treatment and an interim calcium hydroxide dressing should precede the MTA (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Sikri: at least one week of calcium hydroxide (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Bakland: calcium hydroxide for one month or less before the MTA visit (Bakland, 2007) [SRC].
- One step: Kang and Bogen place the barrier directly after 1.5% NaOCl (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter dresses with calcium hydroxide only for swelling or a sinus tract (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's reports a prospective single-appointment series with 81% healing, and a retrospective series with 93.5% success in single-visit and 90.5% in two-visit cases (96.5% versus 89% in immature teeth) (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Gutmann notes outcomes favouring one visit but that "what is defined as a one-visit treatment may be ambiguous" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].
- Animal and lab evidence pulls both ways: in dogs prior calcium hydroxide "was not necessary" and increased MTA extrusion, while in another study one week of it gave better marginal adaptation (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].
Kind of disagreement: different risk judgements on the same evidence; no book reports a trial showing either is better clinically. [Likely]

**Plug thickness: the books disagree.** Grossman 3-4 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri 3.0-4.0 mm (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Endodontology minimum 3 mm (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; Ingle's approximately 3-5 mm, or limited to the apical 4-5 mm in anterior teeth to reduce staining (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Bakland 4 mm, "at least 4mm" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]; Bioceramics 4 mm placed 1 mm from the radiographic apex (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]; Kahler 4 to 5 mm (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Kang and Bogen 4-5 mm (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter "at least 5 mm" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri adds that in short roots the whole canal can be filled with MTA (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. This is a range of recommendations, 3 to at least 5 mm, rather than a contradiction; 4-5 mm satisfies every book except Torabinejad's lower limit. [Likely]

**Technique details.** Ingle's: pre-fit pluggers, the smallest loose about 0.5 mm from working length; place small increments; ultrasonic vibration on the plugger "to help advance MTA"; "use of a matrix material is generally not required"; extrusion over 2 mm may retard healing (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Kang and Bogen disagree on ultrasound: "manual compaction of MTA provides dense compaction and may be preferable to using ultrasonic condensation techniques", and "CSC overfill does not compromise the outcome" (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]; Bioceramics calls overfilling "the major limitation of the apical plug technique" (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Matrices: Torabinejad's chapter uses a collagen plug "to serve as internal matrix to prevent or minimize the extrusion of MTA" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Harty's CollaCote or calcium sulphate (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Kahler pushes calcium sulphate through the apex as "a resorbable extraradicular barrier" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Sikri, platelet-rich fibrin (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Setting: Grossman places a moist pellet and checks the set cement after 48 hours (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Bakland waits "a minimum of 4–6 hours" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]; Gutmann at least 2 to 4 hours before bonding (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]; Endodontology 24 hours (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]. Harty's says "An operating microscope is needed" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]. Ingle's adds the point that technique of placement, rather than the material, has "the greatest impact" on leakage (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** An artificial MTA/calcium silicate barrier is packed apically; no biological barrier is needed, so the canal can be filled at once (Bakland, 2007). Advantages: predictable closure, fewer visits (Shabahang, 2019); better seal (Gopikrishna, 2021); cementum forms over it (Shabahang, 2019); no dentine weakening (Bakland, 2007).

**Technique.**
1. Disinfect; calcium hydroxide for 1-2 weeks, since set MTA does not disinfect (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017; Baumann and Beer, 2010) and to raise periapical pH (Gopikrishna, 2021); single-visit placement is also used (Kang and Bogen, 2017).
2. Optional matrix: collagen, calcium sulphate or PRF (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Kahler, 2021; Sikri, 2019).
3. Mix MTA 3:1 to wet sand (Gopikrishna, 2021); place small increments with a carrier; compact with pre-fitted pluggers; check radiographically (Shabahang, 2019).
4. Plug thickness differs: 3-4 mm (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019); at least 3 mm (Baumann and Beer, 2010); 3-5 mm (Shabahang, 2019); 4 mm (Bakland, 2007); 4-5 mm (Kahler, 2021; Kang and Bogen, 2017); at least 5 mm (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

5. Moist pellet; confirm set (4-6 hours to 48 hours; Bakland, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021); backfill and bond.

**Materials:** MTA (staining, slow set, cost); Biodentine (sets in about 12 minutes, no staining, evidence inconclusive); BC putty, CEM cement (Tonini and Garo, 2024).

**Problems:** extrusion (over 2 mm may retard healing), discoloration (22.7% in one series), thin walls remain (Shabahang, 2019).

## 7. Option 3: regenerative endodontic procedure (revascularisation)

### Explanation

**Concept.** Kang and Bogen: "The apical papilla is a potent source of stem cells that contribute to pulp and root maturation and can survive after pulpal necrosis" (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter: REP aims to address apexification's main shortcomings, arrest of root development and loss of vitality, because teeth undergo dimensional changes "that result in increased root dentin thickness and length" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Cohen's: apexification does not generally result in further root development, whereas REP is likely to increase root length and wall thickness and may restore vitality responses (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC].

**Case selection.** Cohen's: "the incompletely developed permanent tooth that has an open apex and is negative to pulpal responsiveness testing"; teeth needing a post "are not good candidates for REPs"; alternatives to discuss are "MTA apexification, no treatment, or extraction" (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman: necrotic immature teeth in young patients, good compliance, no antibiotic allergy, no bleeding disorder; contraindicated when the tooth needs a post, cannot be isolated, the patient is medically compromised, or in a freshly replanted avulsed tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter: teeth with "adequate coronal tooth structure to be restored with direct restoration" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC].

**Protocol (AAE/ESE type, as the books give it).** First visit: minimal instrumentation, 1.5% NaOCl 20 mL per canal for 5 minutes then saline (Cohen's) or saline and 17% EDTA (Grossman), dry, medicament of calcium hydroxide or antibiotic paste, temporary seal (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Second visit after 2-4 weeks (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's text says the patient is discharged "for 3 to 4 weeks" though its box says 2-4 weeks (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. If signs of infection persist, the first visit is repeated. Anaesthesia without vasoconstrictor ("3% mepivacaine"), irrigation with 17% EDTA, bleeding induced past the apex (a precurved #25 K-file 2 mm past the foramen in Cohen's; small #10 K-files in Torabinejad's; a Hedström file in Grossman's) to fill the canal to the CEJ; a collagen matrix over the clot; a tricalcium silicate cap; glass ionomer and bonded composite (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sikri describes the older form with clot stopped "3.0 mm apical to cementoenamel junction" and MTA over it (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Protocol details on which the books disagree.**
- *NaOCl:* 1.5% in Cohen's, Grossman's, Torabinejad's and Kang and Bogen, because higher concentrations harm stem cells (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Gutmann's older description rinses with NaOCl and chlorhexidine (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC], while Cohen's says chlorhexidine "should be limited or avoided" (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Kind: superseded protocol.
- *TAP concentration:* Grossman 0.1-1.0 mg/mL (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's 1 to 10 mg/mL (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Both are low-concentration pastes; the ranges overlap only at 1 mg/mL. Kind: different guideline versions; the books do not say which. [Guessing which is current]
- *Cap:* Cohen's about 2 mm of Biodentine or BC putty and 3 mm glass ionomer, MTA being "unsuitable" because of severe discoloration (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman MTA or CSC and 3-4 mm GIC (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter and Kang and Bogen still name MTA (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC].
- *Follow-up:* Cohen's at 3 months, 6 months and yearly for 4 years (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]; Grossman at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18 and 24 months, then annually for 5 years (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter 6 months then yearly (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC].

**Outcomes (moderate).** Healing of apical periodontitis is high: "nearing 100%" (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; nearly 100% success and apical closure in the case literature (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. But root development is less predictable: Harty's gives 27% to 55% for continued root growth or maturation, with survival much higher (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Kahler says increases in width and length "are variable" in traumatised teeth (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; vitality responses in about 50% of cases (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Histology shows ectopic bone, PDL and cementum rather than pulp-dentine (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC], so REP "may represent “tissue repair” rather than de novo regeneration"; intracanal calcification occurred in 62% of one series (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Disadvantages: crown staining from minocycline and more appointments than a one-visit MTA plug (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. If REP fails, apexification remains possible (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Gutmann asks whether that reversion is always possible (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Clinicians must know that the radiograph of a REP tooth will look as if the obturation "is missing" to avoid unnecessary retreatment (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]. Full detail belongs to the regenerative endodontics topic.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Concept.** After disinfection, stem cells of the apical papilla (which survive necrosis) and a blood-clot scaffold repopulate the canal, so the root may lengthen and thicken (Kang and Bogen, 2017; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**Case selection:** immature necrotic tooth; crown restorable directly; no post needed; compliant, medically fit patient without antibiotic allergy (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021; Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Protocol (AAE/ESE type):**
- *Visit 1:* minimal instrumentation; 1.5% NaOCl 20 mL/canal, saline; calcium hydroxide or antibiotic paste (TAP 0.1-1.0 mg/mL in Gopikrishna, 2021; 1-10 mg/mL in Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021); temporary seal.
- *Visit 2 (2-4 weeks):* 3% mepivacaine without vasoconstrictor; 17% EDTA; bleeding induced past the apex to the CEJ; collagen matrix; 2-3 mm tricalcium silicate (Biodentine/BC putty, not MTA, for colour); GIC and bonded composite (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021).
- *Follow-up:* 3 and 6 months, then yearly for 4 years (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021).

**Outcomes:** healing of apical periodontitis nearly 100% (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), but root growth only 27-55% (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) and vitality responses in about 50% (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021); the tissue is repair tissue, with intracanal calcification in 62% of one series (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021).

**Drawbacks:** staining, several visits, unpredictable root development; apexification remains the fallback (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017).

## 8. Older and other options

### Explanation

**Surgery.** Before apexification, Weine says the blunderbuss apex with a necrotic pulp and lesion "was treated surgically": the canal was packed with gutta-percha, then the apex flapped, curetted and sealed with an apical filling, which was difficult and often frightening for children (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Surgery still has a place: Cvek says that if bacteria have settled in the newly formed apical hard tissue, retreatment "may be difficult and apical surgery may be necessary" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Gutmann notes it may be wise to place MTA surgically in some open-apex cases with other complications (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].

**Custom (rolled or softened) gutta-percha cone.** Weine customises the master cone and, when the canal stays wide, inverts it "so the normally apical portion is to the incisal edge" (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Cvek softens only the tip of the largest cone that reaches the barrier, by one pass through a flame, and presses it against the barrier, keeping "the rest of it should remain stiff" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Gutmann uses "a custom fit guttapercha cone" as a plugger for MTA (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. A custom cone alone without a barrier is not offered by the books as a definitive treatment for a wide-open necrotic apex; it is a filling technique after a barrier exists. [Likely]

**Conventional root canal treatment.** Kang and Bogen list it among the traditional strategies (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]; it is feasible only once the apex is narrow enough to give a stop. [Likely]

**Extraction, implant, autotransplantation.** Cohen's lists extraction (and no treatment) among the alternatives to discuss before REP (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Kahler: because of the fracture rate some clinicians opt for extraction and implants, but "this approach cannot be considered in children or young adults because facial growth has not been completed" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Cvek: "much is gained if a young, immature incisor can be kept as space maintainer during the periods of maxillary growth" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Autotransplantation is defined in the Glossary (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]; its use to replace a lost immature incisor (for example with a premolar in an orthodontic case) is [RECALL] here and is covered in Traumatic Dental Injuries T18.

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Apical surgery:** historically gutta-percha plus apical curettage and filling, traumatic in children (Weine, 2004); now salvage when bacteria persist in the barrier (Cvek, 2007) or for surgical MTA placement (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- **Custom (inverted or tip-softened) gutta-percha cone:** a filling technique once a stop exists (Weine, 2004; Cvek, 2007).
- **Conventional RCT:** only when the apex is narrow enough (Kang and Bogen, 2017).
- **Extraction:** last resort (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021). Implants are contraindicated until growth is complete; keeping the tooth as a space maintainer is valuable (Kahler, 2021; Cvek, 2007).
- **Autotransplantation:** a possible replacement in a growing patient [RECALL]; see Traumatic Dental Injuries T18.

## 9. Comparison of the main options

### Explanation

The comparison table draws on sections 5-7. Time: calcium hydroxide 3-9 months (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC] or longer (section 5); MTA plug 0.75 ± 0.5 months against 7 ± 2.5 months for calcium hydroxide in Pradhan et al (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Success: Ingle's reports a meta-analysis concluding "the two protocols have similar success rates", with 100% MTA against 92% calcium hydroxide success at final follow-up (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]; Bioceramics gives 79-96% for calcium hydroxide and 81-100% for MTA, and says a meta-analysis of "only four studies" found no difference in success, the real difference being time (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. REP versus apexification: approximately 91% and 95% healing, 98% and 97% survival, no significant difference, mostly from case series (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]; Bioceramics reports a pooled survival of 97.1% and success of 94.6% for the MTA plug against 97.8% and 91.3% for REP (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. Root growth occurs with REP but not apexification (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontology's own table contrasts MTA (one to two sittings, artificial barrier, high cost, difficult, high breakage resistance) with calcium hydroxide (multiple applications, induced barrier, low cost, simple, 74%-96% success, low breakage resistance after long dressing) (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Feature | Calcium hydroxide apexification | MTA/bioceramic apical plug | Regenerative endodontic procedure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Principle | Induced biological hard-tissue barrier | Artificial barrier, immediate | Repopulation of canal with vital tissue |
| Visits | Many, every 3-6 months | 1-2 | 2-3 + long recall |
| Time to obturation | 3-24 months | Weeks (0.75 vs 7 months; Shabahang, 2019) | No obturation |
| Barrier | Porous, irregular cementum/osteocementum | Set cement + cementum over it | Apical closure if root matures |
| Root length/wall thickness | No change | No change | May increase (variable) |
| Dentine weakening | Yes, after long exposure | No (Bakland, 2007) | Short medicament only |
| Discoloration | Slight grey possible (Baumann and Beer, 2010) | MTA stains; Biodentine less | Minocycline, MTA cap |
| Healing/success | 79-96% (Cvek, 2007) | 81-100% (Tonini and Garo, 2024) | About 91% healing (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) |

Meta-analyses find no significant difference in success between calcium hydroxide and MTA (Shabahang, 2019) or between apexification and REP (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021); the options differ in time, visits and root development.

## 10. Obturation after the barrier (brief; see Obturation Q10)

### Explanation

Cvek: the calcium hydroxide barrier is "quite resistant to pressure" but a thin one can break, so "A gentle obturation technique is therefore recommended"; his method is the tip-softened master cone followed by lateral condensation, with thermoplastic and vertical methods as alternatives (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Kahler: "a softened filling technique is indicated", without excess lateral force; because the barrier forms where calcium hydroxide meets vital tissue, it may lie short of the radiographic apex, and "Filling should be completed to the level of the hard-tissue barrier and not forced toward the radiographic apex" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Grossman obturates with a thermoplasticised technique after either barrier (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; Kang and Bogen backfill with warm vertically compacted gutta-percha (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC]. After an MTA plug the canal may also be filled entirely with MTA (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]; Ingle's suggests gutta-percha only in the coronal part of long roots, with bonded resin in shorter roots (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- Fill to the barrier, not to the radiographic apex; the barrier may lie short of it (Kahler, 2021).
- Gentle technique: a porous barrier can break and sealer passes through it (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021).
- Methods: tip-softened custom master cone + lateral condensation (Cvek, 2007); thermoplasticised gutta-percha (Gopikrishna, 2021); in short roots, MTA or bonded resin throughout (Sikri, 2019; Shabahang, 2019).

## 11. Restoration and intracanal reinforcement of thin roots

### Explanation

Failure after apexification is usually cervical fracture, not apical: Ingle's says failure "has been due to fractures in the cervical region of the tooth rather than the root apex" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Hence reinforcement. Kahler: after root filling, remove material "to below the marginal bone level and a bonded resin filling placed" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Bakland: "the resin can be placed into the canal to a level well below the crest of alveolar bone after the MTA has cured", and with an MTA plug "there does not appear to be any advantage in placing a resin in the cervical area prior to apexification" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC], though in long calcium hydroxide treatment the cervical area can be bonded before and after (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]. Ingle's: bonded resin below the CEJ "may also increase the fracture resistance of the tooth" (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. Sikri: newer dentine bonding increases fracture resistance; a clear curing post can be cured in soft composite and removed, leaving a channel for calcium hydroxide changes and later obturation (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Posts: Harty's: "Metallic posts should be avoided"; a fibre post, if it fails, "is more likely to become de-cemented rather than cause a root fracture" (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC]; Gutmann: metallic posts "are not indicated" (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC]. Kang and Bogen: "Superior outcomes are achieved when the access orifice is restored and sealed with a bonded composite material" (Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Most failures are cervical root fractures (Shabahang, 2019), so:
- Bonded composite in the cervical canal to below the alveolar crest (Kahler, 2021; Bakland, 2007); during long calcium hydroxide courses, a clear curing post leaves a channel for dressing changes (Sikri, 2019).
- Avoid metal posts; if a post is needed, use a bonded fibre post (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017; Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011).
- Seal the access with bonded composite over GIC; coronal seal prevents reinfection (Kang and Bogen, 2017).

## 12. Follow-up, outcome and prognosis

### Explanation

**Follow-up.** For apexification and plugs: radiographs at 6 and 12 months (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021; Kang and Bogen, 2017) [SRC], and then yearly; for REP see section 7. Sikri gives the criteria: no symptoms, no external resorption, fracture or periradicular breakdown, and continued eruption with the alveolus (Sikri, 2019) [SRC].

**Healing.** Calcium hydroxide: successful healing with barrier "in 79–96% of the treated teeth" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC], the same range in Kahler (Kahler, 2021) [SRC] and Bioceramics (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]; Endodontology 74%-96% (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC]; periapical healing in 92% of 589 teeth 4 years after obturation, with no difference between immature and mature teeth (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter greater than 90% in 98 teeth (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; re-infection in 7.1% of one series (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC]. MTA plugs: 81-100% (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]. The success figures differ mainly by criteria and follow-up, not by contradiction. [Likely]

**Survival and fracture: the books disagree on the rate and the cause.**
- Rate: Cvek's cervical fractures fell "from 77% in 26 teeth with the lowest degree of root development, to 2% in 362 mature teeth" (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Kahler: approximately 30% fracture during or after treatment (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; Cohen's REP chapter: a retrospective study with fracture of 23% of calcium hydroxide-treated teeth within up to 18 months (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]. Kind: different populations; Cvek's figure is stratified by root stage, the others are overall rates.
- Cause: Cvek attributes fractures mainly to thin walls related to stage of development, while noting in vitro data that long-term calcium hydroxide "increase the brittleness of dentin" and that NaOCl does the same (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Bakland, Grossman, Ingle's and Torabinejad's chapter accept calcium hydroxide weakening (Bakland, 2007; Gopikrishna, 2021; Shabahang, 2019; Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; Kahler says the weakening study "is not conclusive", citing a study in which calcium hydroxide did not weaken teeth, so that "fracture may be more related to the stage of root development rather than calcium hydroxide use" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]. Kind: genuine disagreement on interpretation of laboratory data; see the Critical Evidence Synthesis.

**Late failure.** Weine finds the cases that break down are those whose apical canal remains wide despite a stop, because they are hard to clean and fill and the barrier is porous (Weine, 2004) [SRC]. Re-appearing periapical lesions after obturation can often be resolved by retreatment with calcium hydroxide (Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Follow-up:** review at 6 and 12 months, then yearly (Kang and Bogen, 2017). Success: no symptoms, periapical healing, no resorption or fracture, continued eruption (Sikri, 2019).

**Healing:** calcium hydroxide 79-96% (Cvek, 2007; Kahler, 2021), 92% of 589 teeth at 4 years (Cvek, 2007); MTA plug 81-100% (Tonini and Garo, 2024); REP about 91% (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021).

**Survival is limited by cervical fracture:** 77% in the least developed roots falling to 2% in mature teeth (Cvek, 2007); about 30% (Kahler, 2021); 23% within 18 months after calcium hydroxide (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021).

Cause: thin walls plus long-term calcium hydroxide weakening dentine (Bakland, 2007; Shabahang, 2019); Kahler considers the calcium hydroxide effect unproven and relates fracture to root stage.

**Conclusion.** Healing is good with every option; survival depends on root maturity, cervical reinforcement and coronal seal. REP where root growth is wanted and no post is needed, otherwise an MTA/bioceramic plug; calcium hydroxide apexification remains valid but slow.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: "Long-term calcium hydroxide weakens dentine and causes the cervical fractures of apexified teeth."**
*Textbooks:* Grossman (Gopikrishna, 2021), Torabinejad's chapter (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021), Bakland (Bakland, 2007) and Ingle's (Shabahang, 2019) state it [SRC]; Bakland's support is an immature sheep-tooth study and an MTA study with fracture resistance "equal to controls" (Bakland, 2007) [SRC]; Ingle's cites structural change after more than one month (Shabahang, 2019) [SRC].
*Refinement/challenge:* Cvek's clinical series relates fractures to the stage of root development (77% to 2%), and he presents the brittleness data as in vitro, adding that NaOCl has a similar effect (Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; Kahler calls the weakening "not conclusive" (Kahler, 2021) [SRC].
*Evidence vs interpretation:* the clinical association with root stage is from patients; the weakening effect is from laboratory and animal dentine. Laboratory data cannot by itself establish the clinical cause. [Certain]
*Conclusion:* thin walls are the established cause; calcium hydroxide weakening is a plausible added factor, which is why books now limit dressings to about a month and favour an MTA plug. [Likely]

**Claim 2: "The MTA apical plug is more successful than calcium hydroxide apexification."**
*Textbooks:* Bioceramics says several studies report higher success and survival with MTA (81-100% vs 79-96%) (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]; Grossman calls the MTA barrier superior (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].
*Challenge:* the meta-analyses reported in Ingle's and Bioceramics find similar success, the real difference being time (0.75 vs 7 months) (Shabahang, 2019; Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC]; Endodontology in 2010 called MTA success "(Still) questionable, seems to be good" in its table (Baumann and Beer, 2010) [SRC].
*Strength:* small numbers, "only four studies" in one meta-analysis (Tonini and Garo, 2024) [SRC].
*Conclusion:* equal healing, MTA faster and with fewer visits; claim superiority in convenience and dentine preservation, not in healing. [Likely]

**Claim 3: "Regenerative endodontics is better than apexification because the root keeps growing."**
*Textbooks:* REP increases root length and thickness compared with apexification (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC].
*Challenge:* root growth in only 27-55% (Sigurdsson and Bourguignon, 2017) [SRC] and variable in traumatised teeth (Kahler, 2021) [SRC]; the tissue is repair tissue with frequent calcification (Diogenes, Botero and Kang, 2021) [SRC]; healing and survival are no different from apexification, from case series and retrospective studies (Diogenes, Simon and Law, 2021) [SRC]; Gutmann raises unanswered questions about the tissue formed and reversion if REP fails (Gutmann and Lovdahl, 2011) [SRC].
*Conclusion:* REP offers a chance of root development, not a guarantee; it is a reasonable first choice in very immature teeth that will not need a post, with apexification kept as the fallback. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Problems: blunderbuss canal, no apical stop, thin walls, short root, unreliable apex locator and pulp tests.
- Disinfect chemically; brush, do not cut, the walls; low-concentration NaOCl (0.5-1.5%).
- Calcium hydroxide apexification: barrier in months to years; review every 3 months; books disagree on replacement and duration.
- Barrier is porous ("Swiss cheese") cementum-like tissue; fill to it gently.
- MTA plug: 3-5 mm (books range 3 to at least 5); 1-2 visits; equal healing, faster.
- REP: 1.5% NaOCl, CH or low-dose TAP, 17% EDTA, induced bleeding, collagen, Biodentine cap; healing high, root growth variable.
- Failure is cervical fracture: bonded resin below the bone crest; no metal posts.
- Implants are not for growing children; keep the tooth as a space maintainer.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing that apexification makes the root grow longer and thicker; it only closes the apex.
- Giving one "correct" calcium hydroxide replacement interval or duration; the books disagree, so quote the range and the reason.
- Instrumenting the thin walls to size, or using full-strength NaOCl with force through the open apex.
- Obturating to the radiographic apex instead of the barrier, or with heavy lateral condensation.
- Offering an implant to a child, or a metal post in a thin root.

**"D-A-B-R-R"** for the order of the answer: **D**isinfect gently, **A**pexify (calcium hydroxide), **B**arrier (MTA/bioceramic plug), **R**egenerate (REP), **R**einforce and review.
