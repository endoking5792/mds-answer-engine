## 1. Definition

### Explanation

Cohen's quotes the AAE Glossary: a VRF is a longitudinal fracture "in the root whereby the fractured segments are incompletely separated"; it may run buccolingually or mesiodistally and may cause an isolated periodontal defect or sinus tract (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Older definitions required the fracture to start apically or to follow endodontic treatment. In the latest edition of the Glossary, these stipulations have been removed (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A VRF differs from a split root, in which the segments are completely separated (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Tamse and Berman describe it as a fracture originating in the root that occurs primarily in endodontically treated teeth, but also in intact mesial roots of mandibular molars, mostly in Chinese patients (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition (AAE, quoted by Berman and Tamse, 2021).** A vertical root fracture is a longitudinal fracture in the root whereby the fractured segments are incompletely separated. It may run buccolingually or mesiodistally, may cause an isolated periodontal defect or sinus tract, and may be radiographically evident. When the segments separate completely, it becomes a split root.

**Key points**
- It originates in the root, at any level, and runs coronally or apically (Tamse and Berman, 2019).
- It occurs primarily in endodontically treated teeth; rarely in intact teeth, mostly mesial roots of mandibular molars in Chinese patients (Tamse and Berman, 2019).
- The latest AAE Glossary removed the old requirements that a VRF must start apically or follow root canal treatment (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

The AAE lists five types of longitudinal fracture, from least to most severe: craze lines, fractured cusp, cracked tooth, split tooth and VRF (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. VRF is the only one that starts in the root. Bakland's umbrella of "dental fractures" places VRF as one of three groups, beside crown-originating and trauma-related fractures (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Tamse and Berman classify by extent across the root: one surface is an incomplete VRF, both surfaces a complete VRF (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's classifies by level: coronal, midroot and apical, since VRFs may be initiated at any root level (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. By direction most are buccolingual, though diagonal VRFs occur. Krishnan and Swain's ESE/AAE box separates VRF from the split root and from apical cracks and apical dentinal defects seen after apicectomy.

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Within longitudinal tooth fractures (AAE five types; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002)**
Craze line, fractured cusp, cracked tooth, split tooth, vertical root fracture. The first four start in the crown and run apically; VRF starts in the root.

**2. Ingle's grouping of dental fractures (Bakland, 2019)**
Crown-originating fractures, vertical root fractures and trauma-related fractures (horizontal root fractures belong to the last group).

**3. By extent across the root (Tamse and Berman, 2019)**
- Incomplete VRF: one root surface involved.
- Complete VRF: both buccal and lingual surfaces involved.
- Split root: complete separation of the segments (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**4. By level of origin (Berman and Tamse, 2021)**
- Coronal (cervical) VRF extending apically.
- Midroot VRF.
- Apical VRF extending coronally.

**5. By direction:** buccolingual (most common), mesiodistal, diagonal.

**6. By endodontic status:** in endodontically treated teeth (most) and in non-endodontically treated teeth (rare).

**7. Related ESE/AAE terms (Krishnan and Swain, 2025):** apical cracks and apical dentinal defects found after root-end resection.

## 3. Incidence and susceptible teeth

### Explanation

Seo and co-workers found VRF in 13% of 107 extracted root-filled teeth, and Fuss and Coppens reported 11% and 20% (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's lists the most susceptible sites as maxillary and mandibular premolars, mesial roots of mandibular molars, mesiobuccal roots of maxillary molars and mandibular incisors (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Tamse found these root shapes in 79% of fractured roots (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Round or bulky roots, such as maxillary central incisors, palatal roots of maxillary molars and canines, resist fracture (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- 11-20% of extracted endodontically treated teeth (Fuss 11%, Seo 13%, Coppens 20%), higher in teeth referred for extraction (Tamse and Berman, 2019).
- Susceptible roots: maxillary and mandibular premolars, mesial roots of mandibular molars, mesiobuccal roots of maxillary molars, mandibular incisors (Berman and Tamse, 2021); these shapes made up 79% of fractured roots (Tamse and Berman, 2019).
- Resistant roots: maxillary central incisors, palatal roots of maxillary molars, canines (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

## 4. Etiology

### Explanation

**Natural predisposing factors.** Roots with an oval cross-section, buccolingually wider than mesiodistally, are most at risk (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Finite element analysis shows strain concentration on the inner side of the dentin wall at the highest convexity (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], which is why the fracture runs buccolingually. Less load propagated a crack to the surface in oval canals than in round ones (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Other risky cross-sections are triangular, kidney-, ribbon- and hourglass-shaped (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Root concavities create "danger zones" where dentin thins (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Barreto found preexisting microcracks in 40% of intact maxillary incisors and canines (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Cracks grow faster in older dentin (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Occlusal prematurities and heavy loads add to the risk.

**Is root canal treatment itself the cause?** The etiology is multifactorial (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; Ingle's divides it into predisposing and contributing factors (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Endodontic treatment is the most common dental procedure contributing to VRF (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], but the fracture usually appears long after obturation (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Dentin as a material is unchanged after endodontic treatment (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; it is the root as a structure that is weakened by the combined effect of procedures (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

**Iatrogenic factors.** Excessive canal preparation (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. NiTi rotary and reciprocating files often produce microcracks in vitro (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], whereas hand files and the self-adjusting file did not (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; the clinical link is not yet confirmed (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Lateral compaction applies internal pressure through a spreader (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A rigid stainless-steel spreader increases strain (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], while NiTi finger spreaders need less force (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter adds the use of many accessory cones with repeated spreader insertions (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Irrigants and medicaments such as NaOCl, chelators and calcium hydroxide may alter dentin (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Ultrasonic root-end preparation creates dentinal cracks (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Long or thick posts are a predisposing factor (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], as is post expansion from corrosion (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC].

**Disagreement.** Torabinejad's chapter (2002) states that only two causes have been demonstrated, post placement and condensation during obturation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and that post placement exerts greater wedging force than lateral condensation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. The later chapters add instrumentation, irrigants, root shape and microcracks, mostly on in-vitro and FEA evidence. Ingle's also notes that residual dentin thickness alone did not increase fracture susceptibility (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC], against the common teaching.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Etiology is multifactorial: predisposing factors plus repeated functional or parafunctional loads over months or years (Berman and Tamse, 2021).**

**A. Natural (predisposing) factors**
- Root shape: oval, buccolingually wide roots (also triangular, kidney-, ribbon- and hourglass-shaped); strain concentrates at the inner wall of the buccal and lingual convexity (finite element analysis), so fractures run buccolingually (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Tamse and Berman, 2019).
- Root concavities ("danger zones"): distal concavity of the mandibular molar mesial root; palatal groove of the buccal root of the maxillary first premolar.
- Preexisting microcracks in 40% of intact incisors and canines (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Age: cracks propagate faster in older dentin (Tamse and Berman, 2019).
- Loss of tooth structure from caries or trauma; occlusal prematurities and heavy loads.

**B. Iatrogenic (contributing) factors**
1. Access and canal preparation: excessive dentin removal, large tapers; NiTi rotary and reciprocating files produce microcracks in vitro (hand files and the self-adjusting file did not) (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
2. Irrigants and medicaments (NaOCl, chelators, calcium hydroxide) may weaken dentin (Tamse and Berman, 2019).
3. Obturation: lateral compaction with rigid stainless-steel spreaders; many accessory cones and repeated spreader insertions (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); NiTi finger spreaders reduce strain.
4. Posts: long, thick, tapered, threaded posts; wedging during cementation; corrosion and expansion of metal posts. Post placement exerts more wedging force than lateral condensation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
5. Restoration: no ferrule, no cuspal coverage.
6. Ultrasonic root-end preparation (Tamse and Berman, 2019).

**Root canal treatment does not alter dentin as a material; it weakens the root as a structure** through cumulative procedures (Berman and Tamse, 2021). The fracture appears long after treatment, not during obturation.

## 5. Pathogenesis

### Explanation

It is commonly believed that VRFs begin as microcracks at the canal surface and grow outward (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], but other studies show microcracks starting at the outer root surface and growing inward (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter explains that forces applied during treatment are stored in root dentin, and the fracture may appear months or years later (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Bacteria, sealer and debris occupy the fracture, which therefore acts like a very long apical foramen communicating with an infected canal (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. The PDL along the fracture is destroyed, producing the narrow pocket and the buccal dehiscence.

#### ✎ Exam core

1. Wedging forces from posts or compaction, or repeated occlusal loads, are stored in root dentin and exceed its strength at the thinnest, most strained site (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
2. A microcrack, usually at the canal wall (sometimes at the root surface), propagates to one root surface (incomplete VRF) and then the other (complete VRF) (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
3. Bacteria from the canal and the oral cavity colonise the fracture, which behaves like a very long apical foramen.
4. The PDL along the fracture becomes inflamed and destroyed, giving a narrow isolated pocket, a buccal dehiscence and a coronally placed sinus tract.
5. Bone loss increases with time, and the segments may separate into a split root.

## 6. Clinical features

### Explanation

Pain is usually dull, unlike the sharp pain of a cracked cusp or tooth (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter says a VRF is seldom painful (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Patients often have a long history of undiagnosed discomfort (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A sinus tract associated with VRF lies more coronally than one from a chronic apical abscess (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A deep, narrow, isolated pocket is typical (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Bakland notes that VRF patients describe gradual-onset soreness (Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**History.** A root-filled tooth, often with a post or serving as a bridge abutment; treatment done months or years earlier; a long history of unexplained discomfort, retreatment or surgery (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**Symptoms.** Mild, dull pain or discomfort on chewing, often minimal (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); occasional swelling, often a periodontal-type abscess.

**Signs**
- Deep, narrow, isolated probing defect, usually buccal or lingual at the root convexity.
- Sinus tract located coronally, near the gingival margin, rather than apically.
- Mobility in some teeth; percussion and palpation are not particularly helpful.

**Early versus late.** Early: tight pocket, little radiographic change. Late: wide pocket, J-shaped radiolucency, segment separation.

## 7. Diagnosis

### Explanation

**Pathognomonic combination.** The AAE (2008) considers a sinus tract with a narrow, isolated probing defect in a root-filled tooth pathognomonic for VRF (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Tamse found this combination in only 23.9% of VRFs, and another study in 43.8% (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Deep probing defects occurred in 64-93% (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC], and coronal sinus tracts in 13-35% (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], or 14-42% in Ingle's figures (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC].

**The VRF pocket.** Periodontal pockets are wide coronally and easy to probe (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A VRF pocket lies at the buccal or lingual convexity (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], is tight, and probing may blanch the gingiva (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A flexible probe should be used instead of a rigid one (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Tamse found a typical VRF pocket in 67% of cases with metal probes (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A furcation pocket may be a VRF or a sinus tract from an apical abscess (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

**Radiographs.** The root filling may obstruct the fracture line (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. The typical sign is a J-shaped or halo radiolucency (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], studied in 102 maxillary premolars (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. In mandibular molar mesial roots, halo and periodontal radiolucencies, furcation involvement and an amalgam dowel predicted fracture in 78% (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. The halo involved the furcation in 63.3% (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. The lesion represents cortical bone destruction and should not be read as PDL widening (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Two or three angled radiographs are advised (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Rud and Omnell claimed to see fracture lines in 35.7% of cases, but many were not true VRFs (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Removing the root filling and repeating angled radiographs can show the line (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter notes that some lesions resemble a failed RCT with an apical "hanging drop" appearance (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**CBCT.** At a 0.3 mm voxel, detecting early unseparated VRFs is not reliable (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Even at the smallest voxel, a fracture narrower than 0.15 mm cannot be seen (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Gutta-percha and posts cause artifacts (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], including streak artifacts (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. CBCT's value is in showing early bone loss along the fracture in cancellous bone (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Claims that CBCT is superior have been disputed by a systematic review and meta-analysis (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Neither the AAE/AAOMR nor the ESE recommends CBCT for definitive VRF diagnosis (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

**Exploratory surgery.** This is indicated when the clinical and radiographic findings are equivocal (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter calls flap reflection the only reliable diagnostic approach (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Bone loss is most often a dehiscence (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]: buccal dehiscence along the full root length in 90% of extracted cases, and fenestration in only 10% (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. The lingual defect is a shallow U-shape (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter describes a "punched-out" oblong defect filled with granulation tissue (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Radiographic evidence alone cannot make the diagnosis (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. General practitioners often misdiagnose VRF as endodontic failure or refractory periodontal disease (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Diagnosis is difficult because the signs mimic failed RCT and periodontitis; early diagnosis matters because bone loss grows with time and compromises implant placement (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Tamse and Berman, 2019).**

**1. "Pathognomonic combination" (AAE 2008):** a sinus tract plus a narrow, isolated probing defect in an endodontically treated tooth (Berman and Tamse, 2021). Present in only 23.9-43.8% of VRFs (Tamse and Berman, 2019).

**2. Periodontal probing (flexible probe):** a deep, narrow, tight pocket at the buccal or lingual convexity; probing may blanch the gingiva. Found in 64-93% of cases. Periodontitis pockets are wide coronally, proximal and involve several teeth (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Tamse and Berman, 2019).

**3. Sinus tract:** coronal, several millimetres from the gingival margin, in 13-42% of cases.

**4. Radiographs (two or three horizontal angulations)**
- J-shaped or halo radiolucency (periapical plus lateral), the commonest sign.
- Isolated lateral ("periodontal") radiolucency; angular crestal loss.
- Furcation involvement in mandibular molars (63.3%).
- Direct fracture line or separated segments, only rarely and late.
- The lesion is cortical bone destruction, not PDL widening.
- Removing the root filling and re-imaging may show the line (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**5. CBCT (limited, selective)**
- Unseparated fractures under 0.15 mm cannot be seen; at 0.3 mm voxels detection is unreliable; gutta-percha and posts cause streak artifacts.
- Useful for showing early bone loss along the root in the axial view.
- Not recommended for definitive diagnosis by the AAE/AAOMR or ESE (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**6. Exploratory flap (most reliable):** an oblong, "punched-out" dehiscence filled with granulation tissue; buccal dehiscence along the root in 90%, fenestration in 10%; the fracture is shown with methylene blue, magnification and transillumination (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Tamse and Berman, 2019).

**7. Other aids:** dye and microscope after removing the restoration and filling; the patient's history of a post or retreatment.

Radiographs alone cannot confirm a VRF (Tamse and Berman, 2019).

## 8. Differential diagnosis

### Explanation

The signs of VRF mimic non-healing root canal treatment, reinfected canals and primary periodontal disease (Tamse and Berman, 2019). A furcation pocket in a molar may be a draining apical abscess; the response to treating the infection may separate the two (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Condition | Distinguishing features |
|---|---|
| Failed RCT / persistent apical periodontitis | Apical radiolucency, no isolated pocket; heals after retreatment |
| Primary periodontal disease | Wide pockets, proximal, several teeth, horizontal bone loss |
| Endo-perio lesion | Furcation or apical drainage; responds to infection control |
| Cracked tooth / split tooth | Starts in crown, mesiodistal, vital pulp early, sharp pain (Bakland, 2019) |
| Periodontal abscess | Generalised periodontitis, no root filling |

A furcation pocket in a molar may be a VRF or a sinus tract from an apical abscess; healing after treating the infection rules out VRF (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

## 9. Management

### Explanation

Torabinejad's chapter sums it up: the treatment plan is easy, but the diagnosis is tricky (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Bone loss correlates with time without treatment (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]: in 72% of patients with chronic signs, bone loss was greater than in those diagnosed early (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Extraction of the tooth or root is recommended as soon as possible (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. In multirooted teeth this can be root amputation or hemisection (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and a new coronal restoration is often advised after root amputation (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. If the VRF is limited to the apex, partial root removal is possible (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]: in Ingle's example the root was shaved until the fracture disappeared (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Repairs with resins, glass ionomer or lasers, including extraction, repair and replantation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], are not reliable long-term solutions (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** The fracture is a permanent bacterial pathway to the periodontium, so the fractured root must be removed; early removal preserves bone for an implant (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Tamse and Berman, 2019).

**1. Single-rooted teeth:** extraction as soon as the diagnosis is made; socket preservation and implant, bridge or removable prosthesis.

**2. Multirooted teeth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Tamse and Berman, 2019)**
- Root amputation (resection) of the fractured root, e.g. the mesiobuccal root of a maxillary molar.
- Hemisection of a mandibular molar, removing the fractured half.
- Followed by a new coronal restoration that protects the remaining tooth.
- Requires good periodontal support, a restorable remaining root and no furcation fracture.

**3. Apical VRF:** surgical removal of the fractured apical part, root-end resection beyond the fracture and root-end filling (e.g. MTA) (Tamse and Berman, 2019).

**4. Experimental options (not reliable):** bonding the segments with resin or glass ionomer, calcium hydroxide, CO2/Nd:YAG laser fusion, intentional replantation after extraoral repair (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**5. Informed consent:** when a VRF is suspected, inform the patient of the reduced prognosis before any endodontic treatment (Tamse and Berman, 2019).

## 10. Prognosis and prevention

### Explanation

The prognosis is virtually hopeless for a vertically fractured root (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Prevention is the key to managing VRF (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's cardinal rules are to avoid excessive removal of intraradicular dentin and to minimise internal wedging forces (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Posts are used only when essential for core retention (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; any post should be small, passive and unthreaded (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and cemented with an escape vent (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Crowns with a ferrule distribute strain better (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], with a ferrule of 1.5-2 mm on sound dentin (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC], and Ferrari advises preserving at least one coronal wall (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prognosis:** virtually hopeless for the fractured root (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); the rest of a multirooted tooth can survive after resection.

**Prevention (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Berman and Tamse, 2021; Tamse and Berman, 2019)**
1. Conservative access; minimally invasive canal preparation; avoid overenlarging oval roots and danger zones.
2. Flexible NiTi instruments; avoid excessive tapers.
3. Gentle obturation: NiTi finger spreaders, fewer accessory cones, or thermoplastic techniques.
4. Avoid posts unless needed for core retention; if used, a short, narrow, passive, parallel-sided or fibre post, never threaded; cement slowly with a vent.
5. Cuspal coverage with a ferrule of 1.5-2 mm on sound dentin; preserve coronal walls.
6. Control occlusal prematurities and parafunction.
7. Examine every tooth before treatment or retreatment with a flexible probe.

## 11. Conclusion

### Explanation

The sources agree [Certain]: a VRF is easy to treat once found and hard to find early. The clinician's real contribution is prevention and early recognition.

#### ✎ Exam core

Vertical root fracture is a root-originating, usually buccolingual fracture of endodontically treated teeth, caused by wedging forces acting on vulnerable oval roots. Diagnosis rests on a narrow isolated pocket, a coronal sinus tract, a J-shaped radiolucency and, when needed, an exploratory flap. Treatment is early extraction or root resection, so prevention through conservative preparation, gentle obturation, passive posts and ferrule-based crowns is the clinician's main responsibility.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1. CBCT is the investigation of choice for VRF.**
- Textbook: Ingle's notes several publications claiming that CBCT has greater sensitivity, specificity and accuracy (Tamse and Berman, 2019).
- Evidence: the same chapter reports this was disputed by a systematic review and meta-analysis. Cohen's explains why: unseparated fractures under 0.15 mm are below the resolution of any current scanner, detection at 0.3 mm voxels is unreliable, and gutta-percha and posts cause artifacts (Berman and Tamse, 2021). Neither the AAE/AAOMR nor the ESE recommends CBCT for definitive diagnosis.
- Appraisal: the positive studies were largely in vitro, on unfilled roots. They do not transfer to filled roots with posts.
- Conclusion: CBCT shows the pattern of bone loss, not the fracture. It supports, but cannot replace, probing and exploratory surgery. [Likely]

**Claim 2. NiTi rotary instrumentation causes VRF.**
- Textbook: Cohen's reports frequent microcracks after rotary and reciprocating preparation, not after hand or self-adjusting files (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Evidence and limitation: these are in-vitro sectioning studies, and Cohen's itself states the link to clinical VRF "has yet to be confirmed". Torabinejad's older chapter accepts only two demonstrated causes, posts and condensation (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002). Ingle's adds that residual dentin thickness alone did not increase susceptibility (Tamse and Berman, 2019).
- Conclusion: microcracks are a plausible predisposing factor, not a proven clinical cause. The demonstrated, preventable causes remain wedging posts, forceful compaction and excessive dentin removal. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. A VRF starts in the root, runs buccolingually and is incomplete or complete; a split root has separated segments.
2. Susceptible roots: premolars, mesial roots of mandibular molars, mesiobuccal roots of maxillary molars, mandibular incisors (oval roots).
3. Demonstrated causes: post placement and lateral condensation (wedging); prevention means avoiding dentin removal and wedging.
4. AAE pathognomonic combination: sinus tract plus narrow isolated pocket in a root-filled tooth (present in only 24-44%).
5. J-shaped/halo radiolucency is the commonest radiographic sign; the fracture line itself is rarely seen.
6. CBCT cannot see unseparated fractures under 0.15 mm; it shows bone loss only.
7. Exploratory flap: oblong buccal dehiscence filled with granulation tissue, the most reliable diagnosis.
8. Treatment: extract early, or resect the root (amputation, hemisection) in multirooted teeth.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW

1. Coronal, midroot and apical VRF; incomplete versus complete in cross-section (section 2).
2. Oval root cross-section with strain at the buccal and lingual inner walls (section 4).
3. Periodontal pocket versus VRF pocket profiles (section 7).
4. J-shaped/halo radiolucency on a premolar (section 7).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Writing that VRF starts at the crown; that describes a cracked or split tooth.
2. Claiming the fracture line is usually visible on radiographs or on CBCT.
3. Calling the VRF lesion "PDL widening"; it is cortical bone destruction.
4. Proposing bonding or "repair" as standard treatment; extraction or root resection is the treatment.
5. Blaming dehydration of root-filled dentin; dentin as a material is unchanged, the root structure is weakened.
