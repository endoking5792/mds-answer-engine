## 1. Definition, incidence and mechanism

### Explanation

Andreasen defines root fractures as fractures involving dentin, cementum and pulp (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; the IADT adds that the line may be horizontal, oblique or a combination of both (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. They make up 0.5-7% of injuries to the permanent dentition (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], or 1-7% in Sikri's figure (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]. A frontal impact creates compression zones labially and lingually, and the resulting shear zone sets the fracture plane (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Maxillary central incisors aged 11-20 years are typical (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Immature teeth tend to luxate instead, probably because the young alveolar socket is elastic (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** A root fracture is a fracture involving dentin, cementum and pulp, confined to the root (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007). The line may be horizontal, oblique or a combination of both (Bourguignon et al, 2020).

**Incidence and cause**
- 0.5-7% of injuries to the permanent dentition; maxillary central incisors aged 11-20 years (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007).
- Mechanism: a frontal blow creates labial and lingual compression zones; the shear zone between them is the fracture plane.
- Immature teeth more often luxate than fracture because the young socket is elastic.

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

Sikri lists the Ellis classes, with Class 6 a fracture of the root with or without loss of crown (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]; Ellis and Davey added Class 7 and Class 8, and Hargreaves and Craig added Class 9 for primary teeth (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]. Heithersay and Morile classified root fractures by the level of the fracture relative to the periodontium (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC], with Class 3 in the coronal third below the crest (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]. Yeh recognised four patterns: vertical, oblique, horizontal and laminar (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC], laminar being a piece of root not involving the canal (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]. Andreasen notes the middle third is now the most frequent site (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], and immature roots may show a partial (greenstick) root fracture (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Ellis classification (Sikri, n.d.):** Class 1 enamel or little dentin; Class 2 considerable dentin; Class 3 pulp exposed; Class 4 non-vital tooth; Class 5 tooth lost; **Class 6 root fracture with or without crown loss**; Class 7 displacement without fracture and Class 8 crown fracture en masse (Ellis and Davey); Class 9 primary teeth (Hargreaves and Craig).

**WHO/Andreasen:** root fracture is one of the injuries to hard tissues and pulp, beside crown and crown-root fractures.

**By level (Heithersay and Morile, in Sikri, n.d.)**
1. Not below the attached gingiva.
2. Below the attached gingiva, not below the crest.
3. Coronal third, below the crest.
4. Middle third.
5. Apical third.

**By pattern (Yeh, in Sikri, n.d.):** vertical, oblique, horizontal, laminar (a root piece not involving the canal).

**By extent:** complete; partial (greenstick) in immature roots (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007).

**By site:** cervical, middle (now most frequent) and apical thirds.

## 3. Clinical and radiographic diagnosis

### Explanation

The tooth is usually slightly extruded and displaced orally (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Clinically it cannot be told from a luxation, so diagnosis depends entirely on radiographs (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. The fracture is visible only when the beam lies within 15-20° of the fracture plane (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Andreasen therefore adds exposures at +15° and -15° (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], and a steep occlusal film best shows apical-third fractures (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. The IADT recommends a parallel periapical, two additional angulations and an occlusal film (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], since root fractures may be undetected without additional imaging (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. A fracture can escape detection at first and appear later as haemorrhage or resorption separates the fragments (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. A negative sensibility test straight after injury does not mean necrosis (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Clinical**
- The coronal fragment is mobile, slightly extruded and displaced lingually; tender to percussion; sulcular bleeding (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007; Bourguignon et al, 2020).
- Mobility depends on the level: cervical fractures are most mobile.
- Sensibility tests are often negative initially (transient neural damage); this is not necrosis.
- Clinically indistinguishable from luxation, so radiographs decide.

**Radiographic methods**
1. Parallel periapical plus two more at different vertical or horizontal angulations (±15°), because a fracture shows only within 15-20° of the beam.
2. Steep occlusal film for apical-third fractures.
3. Repeat films at follow-up: a fracture missed at first appears later as the fragments separate.
4. CBCT when plain films are insufficient, to show location, extent and direction (Bourguignon et al, 2020).

**Differential:** luxation without fracture, crown-root fracture, alveolar fracture.

## 4. Healing

### Explanation

If the pulp is intact at the fracture, odontoblast progenitors create a small dentin bridge that unites the fragments (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Hard-tissue union cannot be seen radiographically before 3 months (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. If the pulp is severed, a revascularisation process in the coronal pulp begins, and PDL-derived tissue dominates healing (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Bacteria entering cause infection, and three entry points are discussed (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. The four outcomes: a uniting hard-tissue callus (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; connective tissue, after moderate pulpal injury (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; bone and connective tissue, when trauma occurs before alveolar growth is complete (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; and granulation tissue, with a necrotic coronal pulp and a vital apical pulp (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Surface resorption was seen in 60% of root-fractured incisors (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC] and was self-limiting (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Andreasen notes that this healing model is only weakly supported by experimental research (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Four healing types (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007)**
1. **Hard tissue (calcified) union:** intact pulp; a dentin callus, then cementum; tooth firm. Cannot be confirmed radiographically before 3 months.
2. **Interposition of connective tissue:** moderate pulp injury; PDL tissue fills the gap; rounded fracture edges; tooth firm or slightly mobile.
3. **Interposition of bone and connective tissue:** injury before alveolar growth ends; the coronal fragment erupts away from the apical one; bone between them.
4. **Interposition of granulation tissue (non-healing):** infected necrotic coronal pulp; the apical pulp stays vital; radiolucency at the fracture line.

**Other changes:** surface resorption in 60% (self-limiting, no treatment); pulp canal obliteration in 69-73%.

## 5. Management

### Explanation

The principles are reduction of the coronal fragment and immobilisation (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Resistance on repositioning suggests a fractured labial socket wall (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Splinting is semi-rigid (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; forced orthodontic bands are contraindicated because they further traumatise the pulp (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Andreasen recommends about 1 month of fixation (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; the IADT gives 4 weeks with a passive, flexible splint (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], and up to 4 months for cervical fractures (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. Rigid splints gave the poorest results (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], 4 weeks was acceptable (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], a delay of a few days did not harm healing (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], and antibiotics did not help (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Immature teeth with incomplete fractures need no fixation (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

**Cervical fractures.** Cervical fractures can heal (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], so a conservative approach is justified (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. With good hygiene, the coronal fragment can be fixed permanently to the neighbours with composite (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Healing is poorest when the line reaches the gingival crevice (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. A very mobile fragment above the crest in a mature tooth is removed, followed by RCT and a post-retained crown (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. If the tooth must be extracted, the marginal socket wall must never be removed (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]; a submerged vital apical fragment preserves the ridge (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

**Pulp necrosis.** No endodontic treatment should start at the emergency visit (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. Necrosis usually affects the coronal fragment only (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], so only the coronal fragment is root filled, with a high healing rate (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Apexification at the fracture line may be needed (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. Intraradicular metal pins failed in about half the cases (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], and endodontic implants have a dubious long-term prognosis (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Emergency (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007; Bourguignon et al, 2020)**
1. Local anaesthesia; reposition the displaced coronal fragment digitally as soon as possible (resistance suggests a labial socket fracture, which is reduced first).
2. Confirm repositioning radiographically.
3. Passive, flexible (semi-rigid) acid-etch composite splint: **4 weeks** for apical and middle-third fractures, **up to 4 months** for cervical fractures. Avoid rigid splints and forced orthodontic bands.
4. No endodontic treatment at the emergency visit; antibiotics do not improve healing.
5. Incomplete fractures in immature teeth need no splint.

**By level**
- **Apical third:** often heals without treatment; splint only if mobile.
- **Middle third:** reposition and splint 4 weeks.
- **Cervical third:** cervical fractures can heal, so do not remove the coronal fragment at the emergency visit; splint up to 4 months; permanent proximal composite fixation with good hygiene. If the fragment is very mobile and the line is above the crest, or communicates with the sulcus: remove the coronal fragment, RCT, post-retained crown, with orthodontic or surgical extrusion or crown lengthening; or extract.

**Pulp necrosis (usually coronal fragment only)**
- Root fill the coronal fragment only, to the fracture line; apexification with calcium hydroxide or an MTA barrier at the fracture level.
- If the apical fragment also becomes necrotic, remove it surgically and root fill the coronal fragment.
- Do not start RCT on a negative sensibility test alone.
- Abandoned: intraradicular metal pins (about 50% failure) and endodontic implants (dubious prognosis).

**Extraction:** preserve the labial socket wall; consider submerging a vital apical fragment to keep the ridge.

## 6. Follow-up and prognosis

### Explanation

The pulp survives more often after root fracture than after luxation without fracture (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Necrosis usually shows within the first 2 months (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. A false-negative sensibility response is possible for several months, so RCT should not start on that basis alone (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. The factors influencing healing are age, root development, mobility, dislocation and diastasis (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. No relationship was found between pulp necrosis and the position of the fracture (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], but in 94 cervical fractures 44% of teeth were lost (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Pulp canal obliteration occurred in 69-73% (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Only external inflammatory resorption needs treatment (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Follow-up continues yearly for at least 5 years (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Review (IADT):** 4 weeks (splint off for apical and middle fractures), 6-8 weeks, 4 months (splint off for cervical fractures), 6 months, 1 year, then yearly for at least 5 years (Bourguignon et al, 2020).

**Favourable signs:** sensibility returns (may take months), firm tooth, signs of repair. **Unfavourable:** extrusion or mobility of the coronal fragment, radiolucency at the fracture line, sinus tract.

**Prognosis (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007)**
- The pulp survives better than after luxation, because the fracture gives a wide revascularisation route.
- Better with an immature root, young age, little displacement and no diastasis.
- Fracture level does not affect pulp necrosis, but cervical fractures are lost more often (44% of 94 teeth) through re-injury and mobility.
- Pulp canal obliteration (69-73%) and surface resorption need only observation; external inflammatory resorption needs RCT; replacement resorption cannot be treated.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim. Cervical-third root fractures have a hopeless prognosis and should be extracted.**
- Textbook (older teaching, reported by Andreasen): cervical fractures were thought to have a very poor long-term prognosis, indicating extraction.
- Evidence: later studies found no relationship between the fracture position and pulp necrosis, and the IADT states that cervical fractures can heal and that the fragment should not be removed at the emergency visit (Bourguignon et al, 2020). Against this, 44% of 94 cervical fractures were eventually lost, mostly through re-injury and mobility (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007).
- Appraisal: these are retrospective clinical series from one centre; the IADT guideline itself was judged methodologically deficient in a later review (noted in the trauma topic, T01).
- Conclusion: the pulp heals as often as at other levels; tooth survival is worse because of mobility. Splint longer (up to 4 months), review, and remove the fragment only when it stays mobile or communicates with the sulcus. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Root fracture involves dentin, cementum and pulp; 0.5-7% of permanent dentition injuries.
2. Radiographs at ±15° and an occlusal film; a fracture shows only within 15-20° of the beam.
3. Four healing types: hard tissue, connective tissue, bone and connective tissue, granulation tissue.
4. Reposition, then a passive flexible splint for 4 weeks (up to 4 months for cervical).
5. Necrosis is usually coronal only: root fill the coronal fragment to the fracture line.
6. No RCT at the emergency visit and none on a negative sensibility test alone.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Root filling both fragments when only the coronal pulp is necrotic.
2. Rigid splinting or orthodontic bands.
3. Extracting every cervical-third fracture.
4. Diagnosing necrosis from a negative pulp test straight after injury.
5. Confusing traumatic root fractures with vertical root fractures.
