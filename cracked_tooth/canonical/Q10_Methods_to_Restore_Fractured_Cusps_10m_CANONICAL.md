## 1. Fractured cusps and assessment

### Explanation

Sturdevant's notes that the non-functional cusps fracture most often (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC], especially in mandibular molars and second premolars (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. In one study, 95% of fractures exposed dentin, 25% extended below the CEJ and 3% exposed the pulp (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. Teeth with weak cusps should be considered for a cusp-protecting restoration (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. A fractured cusp that is not mobile probably does not reach the root surface, and a crown can hold the segments (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; a mobile cusp is removed and the tooth restored (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Facts.** The non-functional cusps fracture most often (mandibular lingual, maxillary premolar buccal); 95% of fractures expose dentin, 25% extend below the CEJ and 3% expose the pulp (Sturdevant's, n.d.).

**Assessment before restoring:** which cusp, depth of the fracture margin (supra- or subgingival), pulp status, remaining dentin support of the other cusps, occlusion and parafunction. A non-mobile fractured cusp is held with a crown; a mobile cusp is removed first (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

## 2. Methods

### Explanation

Sturdevant's "fifth principle" is cusp capping when required (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]: consider reducing a cusp when the outline extends half the distance from a primary groove to the cusp tip (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC], and strongly recommend it at two-thirds (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. Root-filled teeth with lost central dentin need special care (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. Complex amalgams are indicated when structure is lost through cusp fracture (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]; slots and pins add resistance and retention (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. They serve as foundations for crowns (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC] or as control restorations (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. Amalgam with cusp coverage significantly increases fracture resistance (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC], with 72% longevity at 15 years (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC], but it protects less well than full coverage (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. Depth cuts are at least 2 mm for functional and 1.5 mm for non-functional cusps (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC], meeting the unreduced cusp at a 90° cavosurface margin (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. Glass-ceramic needs 2 mm occlusal thickness (Sturdevant's, n.d.) [SRC]. Cuspal-coverage composite gave 90% pulp survival over 7 years in cracked teeth (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; Opdam found no failures with cuspal coverage (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]; indirect composite onlays left 93% symptom-free at 6 years (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]; and crowns were no better than direct cuspal coverage (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Cohen's recommends a full-coverage crown or onlay for cracked cusps (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and Torabinejad's advises onlaying undermined cusps (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. A subgingival segment can be retained under a clamp during RCT and an amalcore onlay, then removed (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], with granulation filling the space (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Self-threading pins are a cause of cracks (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], and bulk composite shrinkage can weaken cusps (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle: cusp capping (Sturdevant's "fifth principle").** Reduce and cover cusps that lack dentin support: consider it when the cavity extends half the distance from the primary groove to the cusp tip; it is strongly recommended at two-thirds (Sturdevant's, n.d.).

**A. Direct methods**
1. **Bonded composite:** small fractures; with cuspal coverage for larger ones (90% pulp survival over 7 years in cracked teeth; no failures with coverage; Krishnan and Swain, 2025; Bresciani et al, 2020). Place it incrementally to limit shrinkage stress.
2. **Complex (cusp-capped) amalgam:** reduce 2 mm (functional) or 1.5 mm (non-functional), with a 90° margin; retention from slots, pins, amalgapins or bonding. 72% survival at 15 years; economical, one visit (Sturdevant's, n.d.).
3. **GIC or RMGI:** interim or for cervical extensions.

**B. Indirect methods**
1. **Onlay** (gold, ceramic at least 2 mm thick, or composite): covers the cusps (indirect composite onlays 93% symptom-free at 6 years; Bresciani et al, 2020).
2. **Three-quarter or full crown:** large loss or root-filled teeth, over an amalgam or composite foundation (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**C. Subgingival fractures:** remove the segment and restore, with crown lengthening if the margin is too deep; or retain the segment under a clamp during RCT and an amalcore onlay, then remove it (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**D. Pulp involvement:** RCT, then cuspal coverage.

**Choice of method.** Small, supragingival, vital: bonded composite. Large loss in a low-cost or interim setting: complex amalgam or a foundation. Aesthetic zone or several cusps: onlay. Root-filled or extensively broken down: crown. A complex amalgam protects less well than full coverage (Sturdevant's, n.d.).

**Avoid:** self-threading pins, wedging inlays and bulk-filled composite (Bakland, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Non-functional cusps fracture most often.
2. Cusp capping: consider at half, strongly recommended at two-thirds of the groove-to-cusp distance.
3. Reduction depth: 2 mm functional, 1.5 mm non-functional.
4. Options: bonded composite, complex amalgam, onlay, crown.
5. Crowns are no better than direct cuspal coverage for vital cracked teeth.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW

1. Molar with the cusp-capping rule (half and two-thirds lines) and reduced cusp outline.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Restoring an undermined cusp with a simple intracoronal filling.
2. Leaving a mobile cusp in place without binding or removing it.
3. Using threaded pins in a cracked tooth.
