## 1. Diagnosis and the order of examination

### Explanation

Grossman's defines diagnosis as the correct determination, discriminative estimation and logical appraisal of conditions found during examination (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Procedures should follow a consistent, logical order: history, radiographs, extraoral and intraoral examination (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. One should not rely on the results of any single test (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Diagnosis is the correct determination, discriminative estimation and logical appraisal of conditions found during examination, from their signs and symptoms (Gopikrishna, 2021).

**Order:** chief complaint and history, then extraoral examination, intraoral examination, clinical tests, radiographs, then special tests. A combination of corroborating tests is needed; never rely on one test.

## 2. Diagnostic aids in endodontics

### Explanation

Visual examination checks the "three Cs": colour, contour and consistency (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; a transilluminator helps detect enamel cracks or crown fractures (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Percussion tests the periodontium (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; palpation is not diagnostic while disease is confined to the pulp (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Mobility tests the attachment apparatus (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and with depressibility the chance of keeping the tooth is poor to hopeless (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The bite test identifies a cracked tooth or fractured cusp (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]; pain on release indicates a cracked tooth (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Radiographs are indispensable diagnostic and prognostic aids (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Sensibility tests stimulate the pulpal nerves (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]: cold with 1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane on a pellet (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], heat, and EPT with an increasing current (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. The test cavity is a last resort (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. True vitality requires a vascularity test (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]: pulse oximetry with 660 nm red and 940 nm infrared light (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], and laser Doppler flowmetry (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC], which is expensive and not routine (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. History:** chief complaint; pain (kind, location, duration, provoking and relieving factors); medical and dental history.

**B. Clinical examination (Gopikrishna, 2021)**
1. **Visual and tactile:** colour, contour, consistency ("three Cs"); swelling, sinus tracts; use an explorer, probe, magnification and a transilluminator.
2. **Percussion:** tests the periodontium (apical periodontitis); start with a finger, then an instrument handle.
3. **Palpation:** swelling, fluctuation, lymph nodes; not diagnostic for disease confined to the pulp.
4. **Mobility and depressibility:** the attachment apparatus (Miller's index); depressibility means a poor to hopeless prognosis.
5. **Periodontal probing:** distinguishes periodontal, endodontic and fracture-related defects.
6. **Bite test:** Tooth Slooth or FracFinder on individual cusps; pain on biting suggests apical periodontitis, pain on release a cracked tooth.

**C. Pulp tests**
- Sensibility (neural): cold (tetrafluoroethane, ice, CO2 snow), heat (gutta-percha, hot water under a rubber dam), electric pulp test.
- Selective anaesthesia; test cavity (last resort).
- Vascularity (true vitality): pulse oximetry (660 nm and 940 nm), laser Doppler flowmetry (accurate but expensive, not routine); dual-wavelength spectrophotometry, thermography.

**D. Imaging:** periapical (parallel, angled), bitewing, digital radiography, CBCT for selected cases.

**E. Others:** transillumination, dyes, magnification (loupes, microscope), staining after restoration removal, exploratory surgery.

## 3. Methods of diagnosis in fractured teeth

### Explanation

For cracks, history is the most important tool (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Bite tests use a Tooth Slooth (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Transillumination works because a crack holds a thin air space that blocks light (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Dyes include methylene blue and iodine (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A microscope at high magnification is best for ruling cracks out, with 92.9% specificity (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Removing restorations raised crack detection from 23.3% to 60% (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Wedging separates a cracked from a split tooth, though it is debatable whether it should be done (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Probing around cracks needs anaesthesia (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Mesiodistal cracks are invisible on radiographs (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Against micro-CT, none of the common tests is highly accurate (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. For VRF, the AAE treats a sinus tract with a narrow isolated pocket as pathognomonic (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A flexible probe is advised (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. The radiographic sign is a J-shaped or halo lesion (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], with two or three angulations (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. CBCT cannot show fractures narrower than 0.15 mm (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and exploratory surgery settles equivocal cases (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. For traumatic root fractures, diagnosis depends on radiographs (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. The beam must be within 15-20° of the fracture (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], an occlusal film shows apical fractures (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], and a fracture may appear only later (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Root fractures may be missed without additional imaging (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**A. Cracked tooth and fractured cusp (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Bakland, 2019; Krishnan and Swain, 2025)**
1. History: sharp pain on chewing and on release, cold sensitivity, poor localisation.
2. Bite test on individual cusps; repeat after anaesthetising one arch.
3. Transillumination: the crack blocks light; craze lines transmit it.
4. Dyes: methylene blue, iodine, sealed under a temporary.
5. Magnification: microscope at 20-25x (high specificity, best for ruling cracks out).
6. Removal of restorations: detection rose from 23.3% to 60%.
7. Wedging: separation means a split tooth.
8. Probing under anaesthesia: a narrow isolated pocket.
9. Radiographs: usually negative for mesiodistal cracks; CBCT shows only angular bone loss.

**B. Vertical root fracture (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Tamse and Berman, 2019)**
1. AAE "pathognomonic" sign: sinus tract plus narrow isolated pocket in a root-filled tooth.
2. Flexible probe for tight buccal or lingual pockets.
3. Radiographs at two or three angulations: J-shaped or halo lesion; a line after removing the root filling.
4. CBCT: bone loss, not the fracture (less than 0.15 mm is invisible).
5. Exploratory flap: oblong dehiscence with granulation tissue; dye.

**C. Traumatic root fracture (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007; Bourguignon et al, 2020)**
1. Mobile, extruded coronal fragment; sulcular bleeding.
2. Periapical at ±15°, and an occlusal film; the beam must lie within 15-20° of the fracture.
3. Repeat radiographs: the fracture may appear later.
4. CBCT when plain films are insufficient.

**Caution:** no single test is accurate; diagnosis rests on combining history, tests and imaging.

## 4. Comparing the findings across fracture types

### Explanation

The three fracture groups give different test patterns, and recognising the pattern is the practical skill. Crown-originating fractures are sharp, poorly localised and radiographically silent. VRFs are dull, localised, root-filled and show a characteristic pocket and radiolucency (Bakland, 2019; Berman and Tamse, 2021). Traumatic root fractures follow a known injury and are shown by angled radiographs (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007).

#### ✎ Exam core

| Finding | Cracked tooth | Vertical root fracture | Traumatic root fracture |
|---|---|---|---|
| History | No injury; chewing hard food; bruxism | Root-filled tooth, often with a post | Recent blow, usually to an upper incisor |
| Pain | Sharp, on biting and release | Dull, mild | Pain on biting; tooth loose |
| Pulp tests | Vital, heightened cold | Not applicable (root filled) | Often negative at first; recovers |
| Percussion | Often negative early | Mild | Tender |
| Probing | Narrow pocket only when advanced | Narrow, deep, isolated, buccal or lingual | Usually normal; sulcular bleeding |
| Sinus tract | Rare | Coronal | Rare; at the fracture level if necrotic |
| Radiograph | Usually nothing | J-shaped or halo; lateral lesion | Fracture line at the correct angulation |
| Best test | Bite test, transillumination, dye | Probing, flap | Angled and occlusal radiographs |

**Interpretation.** Diagnosis depends on matching a pattern. Where findings conflict, remove restorations, re-test, image at different angles and, if needed, explore surgically, while telling the patient that the prognosis may be reduced.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim. Modern aids (CBCT, magnification, transillumination) reliably detect cracks and fractures.**
- Textbook: Grossman's recommends the transilluminator and bite test (Gopikrishna, 2021); Cohen's describes transillumination as sometimes "impressively straightforward" in unrestored teeth (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Evidence: against micro-CT, none of transillumination, DIAGNOcam, the microscope or macrophotography was highly accurate, and 20% of teeth extracted as cracked had no dentin crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025). CBCT cannot resolve fractures narrower than 0.15 mm (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Appraisal: a laboratory reference standard on extracted teeth, and small samples.
- Conclusion: these tools raise suspicion or exclude a crack (the microscope's specificity is high), but none confirms one alone. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Never rely on one test; combine history, clinical tests and imaging.
2. Pulp sensibility tests test nerves; pulse oximetry and laser Doppler test blood supply.
3. Pain on release of bite indicates a crack.
4. Transillumination, dyes and magnification show cracks; radiographs usually do not.
5. VRF: narrow pocket plus coronal sinus tract; J-shaped lesion.
6. Traumatic root fracture: angled and occlusal radiographs.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW

1. Transillumination of a cracked tooth.
2. Radiographic beam angulation for a root fracture.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Calling EPT a vitality test; it tests sensibility.
2. Relying on a periapical radiograph to find a crack.
3. Missing the release-pain clue in the bite test.
