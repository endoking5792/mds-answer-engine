## 1. Basis of the difference

### Explanation

Bakland separates crown-originating fractures from VRFs as distinct categories of dental fracture (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. A crown-originating fracture starts on the pulpal side of the crown and spreads to the DEJ and apically (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; a VRF may begin at any root level (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Cracks run mesiodistally (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; VRFs run buccolingually in oval roots (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. COF pain is sudden and sharp, while VRF patients describe gradual soreness (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; VRF pain is dull (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Cracks favour mandibular second molars and maxillary premolars (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; VRFs favour premolars, mesial roots of mandibular molars, mesiobuccal roots of maxillary molars and mandibular incisors (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Cracks are invisible on radiographs (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; VRF shows a J-shaped lesion (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC] and a narrow isolated pocket (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC] with a coronal sinus tract (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A cracked tooth is banded and crowned (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], while VRF is extracted early (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Both are longitudinal fractures, but a cracked tooth (crown-originating fracture, CTS when symptomatic) starts in the crown of a usually vital tooth, whereas a vertical root fracture starts in the root, usually of an endodontically treated tooth (Bakland, 2019; Berman and Tamse, 2021).

| Feature | Cracked tooth syndrome | Vertical root fracture |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Crown, pulpal side; runs apically | Root, any level; runs coronally or apically |
| Direction | Mesiodistal | Buccolingual (oval roots) |
| Tooth | Vital, minimally restored molars and premolars; mandibular second molars | Root-filled teeth with posts; premolars, mesial roots of mandibular molars |
| Main cause | Fatigue from occlusal loads, wedging cusps, habits | Wedging from posts and compaction; excessive dentin removal |
| Pain | Sharp, on biting and release; cold sensitivity | Dull, mild soreness; often minimal |
| Localisation | Poor | Good |
| Pulp tests | Vital, heightened cold | Not applicable |
| Bite test | Positive, often on release | Not diagnostic |
| Probing | Normal early; narrow pocket late | Narrow, deep, isolated buccal or lingual pocket |
| Sinus tract | Rare | Coronal, near the gingival margin |
| Radiograph | Usually normal | J-shaped or halo lesion; lateral radiolucency |
| Confirmation | Transillumination, dye, restoration removal | Exploratory flap |
| Treatment | Band, cuspal coverage, RCT if needed, crown | Extraction or root resection |
| Prognosis | Questionable but often good | Hopeless for the fractured root |

**Why the clinical pictures differ.** In a cracked tooth the pulp is usually vital, so movement of the crack produces sharp A-delta pain; the PDL is not involved until late, so the patient cannot localise the tooth. In a VRF the pulp has already been removed; symptoms come from bacterial inflammation of the PDL along the fracture, which gives dull, localised discomfort, a pocket and a sinus tract.

**Exception:** VRF can occur in non-root-filled mesial roots of mandibular molars, mainly in Chinese patients (Tamse and Berman, 2019).

**Note:** Cohen's prefers "cracked tooth" to "syndrome" (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Crown versus root origin; mesiodistal versus buccolingual.
2. Sharp release pain versus dull soreness.
3. Radiograph normal versus J-shaped lesion.
4. Crown the cracked tooth; extract the VRF.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW

1. A molar with a mesiodistal crown crack beside a premolar with a buccolingual root fracture.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Describing VRF symptoms as sharp biting pain.
2. Recommending extraction for every cracked tooth.
