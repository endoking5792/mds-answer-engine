## 1. CBCT in fractures

### Explanation

Plain films miss cracks because the crack lies perpendicular to the beam (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], miss VRFs because the root filling obscures the line (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and miss root fractures unless the beam lies within 15-20° of the fracture plane (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. CBCT's axial view can show early bone loss along a VRF (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. At 0.3 mm voxels, detection of unseparated VRFs is unreliable (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and fractures under 0.15 mm cannot be seen at any current voxel size (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Gutta-percha and posts cause artifacts (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], including streaks (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. CBCT's superiority has been disputed by a meta-analysis (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. Neither the AAE/AAOMR nor the ESE recommends it for definitive VRF diagnosis (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. For suspected cracks a 0.2 mm voxel is advised, and only selectively (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; an angular crestal defect is a surrogate for radicular extension (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. In trauma, root fractures may be missed without additional imaging (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], and CBCT shows the path of crown-root fractures (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Why plain radiographs fail.** Cracks run mesiodistally, perpendicular to the beam; VRF lines are hidden by the root filling; root fractures show only within 15-20° of the beam (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007).

**Uses of CBCT**
1. **Traumatic root and crown-root fractures (most valuable):** location, extent, direction and relation to the bone crest; crown-root ratio for extrusion planning (Bourguignon et al, 2020).
2. **Vertical root fracture:** axial views show the pattern of bone loss (buccal dehiscence, bone loss along the root, furcation) before it is visible on periapicals (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
3. **Cracked tooth:** an angular crestal defect suggests radicular extension (Krishnan and Swain, 2025); use small field, 0.2 mm voxel or finer (Bakland, 2019).

**Limitations**
- Resolution: unseparated fractures under 0.15 mm are invisible; at 0.3 mm voxels detection is unreliable (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Artifacts: streaks and beam hardening from gutta-percha and posts.
- Radiation dose and cost; use only when the result will change management.
- Evidence: claims of superiority over periapicals were disputed by a meta-analysis (Tamse and Berman, 2019); the AAE/AAOMR and ESE do not recommend CBCT for definitive VRF diagnosis.

**Practical protocol**
- Take two or three angled periapicals and an occlusal film first; use CBCT only if the findings remain unclear and the result will change the plan.
- Choose the smallest field of view and the smallest voxel available.
- Read the axial slices for bone loss on the buccal and lingual aspects of the root and in the furcation; read the sagittal slices for horizontal fracture lines.
- Correlate with probing, sinus tracts and the history; confirm a suspected VRF with an exploratory flap.
- When retreatment is planned anyway, removing the root filling and re-imaging with angled periapicals may reveal the fracture line (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**Conclusion.** CBCT shows the consequences of a fracture (bone loss, displacement) better than the fracture itself. It supports, but does not replace, clinical tests and exploratory surgery.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Below 0.15 mm, a fracture is invisible on CBCT.
2. Most useful in trauma (root and crown-root fractures).
3. For VRF and cracks it shows bone loss, not the line.
4. Gutta-percha and posts cause streak artifacts.
