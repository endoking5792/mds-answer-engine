## 1. Definition, terminology and history

### Explanation

**Crack versus fracture.** Cohen's Ch.22 quotes the 2016 AAE Glossary: a crack is "a thin surface disruption of enamel and dentin, and possibly cementum, of unknown depth or extension" (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; a fracture is a disruption "that is assumed to be of greater depth than a crack", with separated or unseparated segments (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. The European Society of Endodontology (ESE), as reported by Krishnan and Swain, sets a stricter threshold: a cracked tooth has a crack extending into the dentine, of unknown depth or size, which may extend subcrestally into the root (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. A line confined to enamel is therefore not a cracked tooth. The 2012 AAE Glossary describes "cracked tooth" as a phenomenon of posterior teeth in which fractures usually involve the marginal ridges, mainly in minimally restored mandibular molars, with pain to chewing and thermal sensitivity (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. It defines infraction as an incomplete fracture of enamel without loss of tooth structure (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. In the biomechanical sense used by Cohen's, a crack is a partial discontinuity that may propagate into a complete discontinuity, the fracture (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

**History of the term.** Bakland reports that a 6500-year-old skull had a cracked tooth filled with beeswax, that Gibbs coined "cuspal fracture odontalgia", and that the term cracked tooth syndrome was suggested by Cameron and was accepted at once (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Krishnan and Swain's reference list dates Cameron's paper to J Am Dent Assoc 1964 (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Bresciani and co-workers define the syndrome as a group of clinical signs and symptoms associated with incomplete fractures of enamel and dentin, often extending to the pulp chamber or periodontium (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC].

**Is it a syndrome?** The textbooks disagree, and this is worth one line in any answer. Cohen's argues that the signs and symptoms are too varied to allow an objective definitive diagnosis, so the "terminology of cracked tooth syndrome should be avoided" (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Bakland accepts that the word "syndrome" is questionable, but after about 50 years of use it probably has to be accepted for symptomatic teeth with crown-originating fractures (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. He proposes "dental fractures" as an umbrella term with three groups: crown-originating fractures (COF), vertical root fractures and trauma-related fractures (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. The current trend, in the ESE/AAE material reported by Krishnan and Swain, is to describe the crack (its type and extent) rather than the syndrome [Likely].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Cracked tooth syndrome (CTS) is a group of clinical signs and symptoms associated with incomplete fractures of enamel and dentin, often extending to the pulp chamber or periodontium (Bresciani et al, 2020). The AAE describes a cracked tooth as a phenomenon of posterior teeth in which fractures usually involve the marginal ridges, mainly in minimally restored mandibular molars, with pain on chewing and thermal sensitivity (American Association of Endodontists, 2012).

**Crack and fracture (AAE 2016, quoted by Berman and Tamse, 2021)**
- Crack: a thin surface disruption of enamel and dentin, and possibly cementum, of unknown depth or extension.
- Fracture: a break assumed to be deeper than a crack, with separated or unseparated segments.
- The ESE requires the crack to extend into dentine for a tooth to be called "cracked"; it may extend subcrestally into the root (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
- Biomechanically, a crack is a partial discontinuity that may propagate into a complete one, a fracture (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**History**
- A 6500-year-old skull showed a cracked tooth sealed with beeswax (Bakland, 2019).
- Gibbs: "cuspal fracture odontalgia". Cameron (1964): "cracked tooth syndrome" (Bakland, 2019; Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
- Synonyms: infraction, incomplete tooth fracture, greenstick fracture, hairline fracture (Bakland, 2019).

**Terminology debate.** Cohen's recommends avoiding the word "syndrome" because the signs are too varied for an objective diagnosis (Berman and Tamse, 2021); Ingle's accepts it through long use but places it under "crown-originating fractures" within an umbrella of "dental fractures" (Bakland, 2019). In the answer book, describe the crack type and its extent, and use CTS only for the symptomatic presentation.

## 2. Classification

### Explanation

**The AAE five types.** Torabinejad's chapter lists five categories of longitudinal fracture from least to most severe: craze lines, fractured cusp, cracked tooth, split tooth and vertical root fracture (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Bakland traces this to the AAE's identification of five types of cracks (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Craze lines are confined to enamel (Bakland, 2019) [SRC] and are unimportant except as a common source of confusion with cracked teeth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. On transillumination, light passes through a craze line instead of being blocked (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. A cracked cusp allows microscopic flexure on chewing (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A fractured cusp is an oblique shearing fracture from the occlusal surface that does not extend beyond the cervical third of the root (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. A cracked tooth is an incomplete, greenstick fracture (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. A split tooth is the evolution of a cracked tooth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002), with the segments completely separated longitudinally (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

**Direction of propagation.** Fractured cusp, cracked tooth and split tooth begin occlusally and run apically. Bakland adds that crown-originating fractures start on the pulpal side of the crown and spread towards the DEJ and then apically (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Vertical root fracture begins in the root and runs coronally. Cohen's groups these into three clinical categories: crown-originating fractures, split teeth and VRFs (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

**Extended terms (ESE/AAE, Krishnan and Swain).** Their Box 9.1 adds the corono-radicular crack, which extends into the pulp chamber floor (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; the marginal ridge crack, with a high risk of propagation where a proximal contact is missing (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; body cracks in the pulpal floor of a cavity preparation (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; the split root; and apical cracks and apical dentinal defects seen after apicectomy.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification of longitudinal tooth fractures (AAE, five types, least to most severe; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Bakland, 2019)**

| Type | Extent | Origin and direction | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Craze line | Enamel only | Crown | Not blocked on transillumination; no treatment except aesthetics |
| Fractured cusp | Cusp, crossing marginal ridge and down a buccal or lingual groove | Occlusal, oblique | Usually no pulp exposure; ends no deeper than the cervical third of the root |
| Cracked tooth | Incomplete (greenstick), centred, mesiodistal | Occlusal, running apically | May reach the pulp; segments do not separate |
| Split tooth | Complete; segments separate | Evolution of a cracked tooth | Wedging separates the segments |
| Vertical root fracture | Root, buccolingual | Root, often apical, running coronally | Mostly in endodontically treated teeth |

**Additional terms (ESE/AAE, reported by Krishnan and Swain, 2025)**
- Cracked cusp: a crack under a cusp allowing microscopic flexure on chewing; may progress to a fractured cusp (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Corono-radicular crack: a coronal crack extending into the pulp chamber floor, with or without the root canal.
- Marginal ridge crack: high risk of propagation when the proximal contact is missing.
- Body crack: in the pulpal floor of a cavity preparation, found after the restoration is removed.
- Split root: a complete VRF with visible separation.

**Ingle's grouping (Bakland, 2019).** "Dental fractures" divide into (1) crown-originating fractures (COF), which arise spontaneously in the crown and progress apically, (2) vertical root fractures and (3) trauma-related fractures. CTS is a symptomatic COF.

## 3. Incidence and teeth affected

### Explanation

Torabinejad's chapter states that longitudinal fractures are apparently increasing, partly because patients keep teeth longer and partly because of better recognition (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Bakland reports that 2-3% of dental patients have infractions in molars and premolars, most asymptomatic, and estimates five fractured teeth per 100 adults per year (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. A US general-practice network found 55% of cracked teeth asymptomatic (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Among patients with crack-like symptoms, about 10% actually have cracked teeth (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Krishnan and Swain report a prevalence of 10-70% depending on setting and period, call cracks the third most common reason for tooth loss in industrialized countries (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], and note they are more common over 40 years of age (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC].

**Teeth.** Pooling 12 studies, 48% of cracked teeth were mandibular molars, 28% maxillary molars, 16% maxillary premolars and 6% mandibular premolars (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's names mandibular second molars and maxillary premolars as particularly prone (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Cracks run overwhelmingly mesiodistally; in Brynjulfsen's series, 70% of maxillary fracture lines lay towards the buccal and 70% of mandibular ones towards the lingual (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Restored teeth are generally more prone, but Class I restored teeth fracture as often as Class II (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and cracks occur in unrestored teeth too.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Incidence**
- Increasing, because teeth are retained longer and recognition is better (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- 2-3% of patients have infractions in molars and premolars, most asymptomatic; about five fractured teeth per 100 adults per year (Bakland, 2019).
- 55% of cracked teeth in a US general-practice network were asymptomatic (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
- Cracks are reported as the third most common cause of tooth loss in industrialized countries; they are more common over 40 years (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**Teeth and direction**
- Mandibular molars 48%, maxillary molars 28%, maxillary premolars 16%, mandibular premolars 6% (pooled 12 studies; Bakland, 2019). Mandibular second molars and maxillary premolars are particularly prone (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Mostly mesiodistal: maxillary cracks lie buccally and mandibular cracks lingually (70% each; Bakland, 2019).
- Occur in restored and unrestored teeth; Class I restored teeth crack as often as Class II (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

## 4. Biomechanics and pathogenesis

### Explanation

**Fracture mechanics.** Cohen's defines fracture mechanics as the part of biomechanics concerned with the propagation of cracks until catastrophic fracture (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Most of these cracks are not caused by one impact. They follow an accumulating, unobserved trauma from normal or excessive occlusal forces applied repeatedly (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Molars can generate forces of 90 kg (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and Yeh's term "fatigue root fracture" covers this cumulative loading (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter adds that forces are greatest near the fulcrum of the mandible, the "nutcracker" effect (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Wedging and loading geometry.** Bresciani and co-workers describe a wedging effect: the mesiopalatal cusp of the maxillary molar loads the mandibular fossa and fatigues it (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Maxillary molars may resist cracks because the oblique ridge stabilises them (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Krishnan and Swain explain why: steep cuspal inclines create significant wedging force at the base of occlusal fissures (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Loss of the marginal ridge lets a crack start at much lower load (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. A small contact area gives very high stress even with low bite force (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. The contact position sets the moment arm from the pivot point at the base of the fissure. In restored teeth, occlusal load and thermal expansion of the restoration increase cuspal flexure and concentrate stress at sharp internal line angles (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC].

**Material resistance.** Dentin's collagen network resists crack formation and growth (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. As dentin loses its capacity for plastic deformation with age, infractions become more common. Unlike bone, enamel and dentin fractures do not repair (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], so every crack can only stay the same or grow [Certain].

**Path of the crack and the pulp.** The more centred the fracture on the occlusal surface, the deeper it tends to run before shearing to a root surface (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Pulp involvement is more frequent with centrally located cracks (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], which cross the chamber roof; cuspal cracks skirt the pulp. Bacteria colonise the crack, inflame the pulp and, if the crack reaches the PDL, the periodontium.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Concept.** Most cracks are fatigue failures, not single-impact injuries: an accumulating, unobserved trauma from normal or excessive occlusal forces applied repeatedly (Berman and Tamse, 2021). Fracture mechanics is the study of crack propagation until catastrophic fracture; a crack is a partial discontinuity that grows into a complete one.

**Forces**
- First molars generate occlusal forces of up to 90 kg (Berman and Tamse, 2021); Yeh's "fatigue root fracture" describes cumulative loading.
- "Nutcracker effect": forces are highest near the fulcrum of the mandible, so molars crack most (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Wedging effect: the maxillary mesiopalatal cusp acting on the mandibular fossa fatigues the lower molar; the maxillary oblique ridge protects the upper molar (Bresciani et al, 2020).

**Geometry that concentrates stress (Krishnan and Swain, 2025)**
- Steep cuspal inclines create wedging force at the base of the occlusal fissure.
- Loss of the marginal ridge lowers the load at which a crack starts.
- A small contact area produces very high stress even with low bite force.
- The contact position sets the moment arm about the pivot at the fissure base or tooth-restoration interface.

**Restored teeth.** Occlusal load and thermal expansion of a restoration increase cuspal flexure and concentrate stress at sharp internal line angles, producing microcracks (Bresciani et al, 2020).

**Tooth tissue**
- Dentin's collagen resists crack initiation; with age this plastic deformation decreases (Bakland, 2019).
- Enamel and dentin do not repair once fractured (Bakland, 2019); a crack can only stay the same or progress.

**Progression**
- Centred cracks run deeper before shearing to a root surface and are more likely to reach the pulp (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Sequence: crack, bacterial colonisation, pulpitis, necrosis, periodontal defect, split tooth.

## 5. Etiology and predisposing factors

### Explanation

Cohen's states plainly that masticatory forces cause cracked teeth (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Contributors include chewing hard, brittle substances such as ice, unpopped popcorn kernels and hard candy (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], bruxism and clenching, and occlusal prematurities. Krishnan and Swain structure the risk assessment as three questions: is there an environmental issue, a loading issue or a structural issue in this dentition? (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Environmental issues include occlusal interferences such as plunger cusps and tilted molars (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Bruxism has a global prevalence of about 22% (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], and SSRIs may be associated with it (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Bilateral masseteric hypertrophy and a brachyfacial pattern signal high bite force (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC].

**Iatrogenic factors.** Bakland lists large and poorly designed restorations. Non-bonded inlay materials such as gold and amalgam increased longitudinal fractures (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], and Bresciani's chapter agrees that teeth with non-bonded restorations are more often affected than those with bonded restorations (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Other factors are self-threading and friction-lock pins (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], coarse diamond burs that produce subsurface cracks from enamel into dentin (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], and thermal cycling, which is suspected of starting infractions near the DEJ (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Wide Class II cavities tend to produce cusp fractures rather than true cracked teeth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter adds that bonded composite may shrink excessively on polymerisation and weaken cusps (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Two disputes.** First, occlusal anatomy: Torabinejad's chapter (2002) calls deep fissures and prominent cusps only speculative, with no demonstrated relationship (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], whereas Krishnan and Swain (2025) treat steep inclines and marginal ridge loss as structural risk factors. The newer source draws on more recent laboratory work, but the clinical link is still not proven by a trial [Likely]. Second, root canal treatment: Torabinejad's chapter says split teeth are more common in root-filled teeth, but "not because the treatment per se weakens the tooth"; the cause is tooth structure already lost (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Natural (non-iatrogenic) factors**
- Masticatory forces are the basic cause (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Habits: chewing ice, unpopped popcorn kernels, hard candy, coarse food; unilateral chewing (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Bresciani et al, 2020).
- Bruxism and clenching: global prevalence of bruxism about 22%; a possible link with SSRIs (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
- High bite force: bilateral masseteric hypertrophy, brachyfacial (short, broad) face, large build.
- Occlusal interferences: plunger cusps, tilted molars, uneven marginal ridges, lone-standing teeth with missing contacts.
- Structure: steep cusps, deep fissures, undermined cusps, lost marginal ridges; age-related brittleness of dentin.

**Risk framework (Krishnan and Swain, 2025):** environmental, loading and structural issues. These overlap, and together they raise the risk that a crack starts and grows.

**Iatrogenic factors (Bakland, 2019)**
- Large, poorly designed intracoronal restorations; non-bonded inlays (gold, amalgam), which wedge cusps apart.
- Self-threading and friction-lock pins.
- Coarse diamond burs and vibrating high-speed handpieces, which create subsurface cracks.
- Thermal cycling of restorations, which starts cracks near the DEJ.
- Polymerisation shrinkage of bulk-placed composite (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Deep access cavities without cuspal protection.

**Acute causes:** biting unexpectedly on a hard object; an upward blow to the mandible (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**Points of dispute**
- Torabinejad's chapter calls the role of occlusal anatomy "only speculative" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); Krishnan and Swain (2025) treat steep inclines and lost marginal ridges as structural risks.
- Root canal treatment itself does not weaken dentin; the loss of tooth structure does (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

## 6. Mechanism of pain

### Explanation

**Biting and rebound pain.** Brännström proposed that biting pain comes from a sudden movement of dentinal fluid when the cracked portions move independently, stimulating A-delta fibres (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. No pulpal inflammation is needed, which explains why chewing pain appears early. Bresciani and co-workers attribute the fast, intense pain to Type A myelinated fibres (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Pain on release, "rebound" or "relief" pain, occurs as the crack closes rapidly. With a moist cotton roll, rebound pain on release suggests that one of the two opposing teeth is cracked (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter also notes pain more distinct on masticatory release (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Thermal and sweet pain.** Bacteria in the crack inflame the pulp. Inflammation sensitises the pulpal C-fibre receptors (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], giving cold sensitivity and later heat sensitivity. Sweets can also provoke pain (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], as can sugary or grainy food (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC].

**Poor localisation.** Until the fracture reaches the PDL, percussion does not localise the tooth (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], because the pain is pulpal and has no proprioception, as no periodontal ligament is involved (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Pain from mandibular teeth is often referred to maxillary teeth, the ear and the TMJ (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. One series recorded pain histories of 3 months to 11 years in 32 patients (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Krishnan and Swain attribute the diffuse pain of long-standing cases to central sensitisation in the trigeminal subnucleus caudalis (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. CTS can therefore mimic earache, sinusitis, TMD and trigeminal neuralgia (Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Sharp pain on biting (early, before inflammation).** When the cracked segments move independently, dentinal fluid moves suddenly and stimulates A-delta (Type A myelinated) fibres (Brännström's hydrodynamic explanation; Bakland, 2019; Bresciani et al, 2020). Pain can therefore precede any pulpitis.

**2. Rebound (relief) pain.** Pain on releasing the bite as the crack closes rapidly; characteristic of crack (Bakland, 2019; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**3. Thermal and sweet sensitivity (later).** Bacteria colonise the crack and inflame the pulp; inflammation sensitises C-fibre receptors, giving cold, then heat, then sweet sensitivity (Bakland, 2019).

**4. Poor localisation and referral**
- The pain is pulpal, without PDL proprioception, so the patient often cannot name the tooth; percussion is often negative until the crack reaches the PDL (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Bakland, 2019).
- Mandibular teeth refer pain to maxillary teeth, ear, neck and TMJ; maxillary teeth rarely refer to the mandible (Bakland, 2019).
- Histories of 3 months to 11 years are reported; long-standing pain becomes diffuse through central sensitisation in the trigeminal subnucleus caudalis (Bakland, 2019; Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
- CTS can mimic earache, sinusitis, TMD and trigeminal neuralgia (Bakland, 2019).

## 7. Clinical features

### Explanation

**Cracked cusp, early.** The patient history is the most important diagnostic tool (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]: sharp pain on chewing that stops the patient chewing on that side, for a long time, with no radiographic finding. Cracked cusps are often associated with extensive occlusal restorations (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. The pulp is vital and may test like reversible pulpitis.

**Fractured cusp, late.** The earlier pulpal pain typically resolves once the cusp fractures completely (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; Torabinejad's chapter notes the symptoms are often relieved when the cusp finally breaks off (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Cracked tooth.** Such cracks typically run mesiodistally, splitting the crown into buccal and lingual fragments (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. They are invisible radiographically because the crack is microscopic and runs perpendicular to the X-ray beam (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Over time the patient may report that the sharp pain has given way to cold sensitivity and then to less pain, as the pulp becomes inflamed and then necrotic. Berman and Kuttler examined 27 non-vital molars and premolars with minimal or no restorations or caries and found a crack reaching the pulp in every one (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], which Cohen's calls "fracture necrosis" (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Narrow, isolated deep pockets may develop later (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

**General-practice presentation differs from specialist presentation.** Krishnan and Swain note that cold sensitivity can be an early sign and may be mistaken for dentin hypersensitivity (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Pain on biting formed only 33% of symptomatic cracked-tooth presentations to general practice (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], so the absence of biting pain does not exclude a crack. Rarely, bacteria in the crack cause an internal resorptive lesion (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. A split tooth may present as a periodontal abscess and be misdiagnosed (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Symptoms (the "CTS" picture)**
- Sharp, brief pain on chewing, especially grainy or tough food; the patient avoids that side (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Rebound pain on release of biting.
- Sensitivity to cold, sometimes to sweets; heat sensitivity is unusual early (Bakland, 2019).
- Long history; the patient cannot locate the tooth; earlier dentists "found nothing".
- In general practice, cold sensitivity may be the only symptom and is mistaken for dentin hypersensitivity; biting pain is present in only about one-third (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**Signs by stage**

| Stage | Pulp | Percussion | Radiograph | Periodontium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cracked cusp | Vital, normal or reversible pulpitis | Often negative | Nothing | Normal |
| Fractured cusp | Pulpal pain resolves; dentin exposed | Fragment tender | Nothing | Fragment held by gingival fibres |
| Cracked tooth (early) | Vital; heightened cold | Variable | Nothing (crack perpendicular to beam) | Normal |
| Cracked tooth (late) | Irreversible pulpitis or necrosis ("fracture necrosis") | Positive | Apical radiolucency | Narrow isolated deep pocket |
| Split tooth | Usually necrotic | Positive; segments mobile | Diffuse or furcal bone loss | Deep pockets; may mimic a periodontal abscess |

**Key observations**
- A tooth with no caries, restoration or trauma that becomes non-vital should be suspected of a crack: in 27 such teeth a crack reached the pulp in every case (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Teeth with large intracoronal restorations tend to develop cracked cusps; minimally restored mandibular molars tend to develop central cracks.
- Bacteria in the crack can rarely cause internal resorption (Bakland, 2019).

## 8. Diagnosis

### Explanation

**Bite tests.** The AAE defines the bite test as loading individual cusps with differential occlusal force to reproduce the complaint (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC]. The Tooth Slooth is a small pyramid with a flattened top placed on one cusp at a time (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; a positive test reproduces the chief complaint (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Alternatives include Burlew wheels (Bakland, 2019) [SRC] and a cotton roll or cotton-tip applicator (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Placing the Tooth Slooth first on the filling and then on the cusps separates a leaking restoration from a cracked cusp (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Specific instruments were more reliable than sticks or cotton rolls (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Repeatability is unpredictable, however, because the load must be aligned with the crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Anaesthetising the suspected tooth and repeating the cotton-roll test separates a maxillary from a mandibular source (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

**Seeing the crack.** In transillumination, light stops at the crack and the part beyond appears dark (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], because the crack holds a thin air space (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Fissures and enamel infractions also block light, so false positives are common (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Dyes include methylene blue and tincture of iodine (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and the dye can be sealed in under a temporary for a few days (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Magnification alone is not a predictable detector (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. At 20-25x, however, its specificity of 92.9% makes it best for ruling cracks out (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Light-induced fluorescence is another option (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC].

**Accuracy.** Against micro-CT, none of transillumination, DIAGNOcam, microscope or macrophotography was highly accurate (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], and 20% of teeth extracted as "cracked" had no crack reaching dentin (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. No single test should decide the diagnosis [Certain].

**Removing restorations.** In 245 restored teeth, cracks were seen in 23.3% before the restorations were removed and in 60% afterwards (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Brynjulfsen and co-workers removed restorations one tooth at a time in patients with undiagnosed orofacial pain (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Once a crack is found, wedging decides between cracked and split. Torabinejad's chapter notes it is debatable whether wedging should be done, because it may split the tooth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Probing, percussion and imaging.** A sharp explorer drawn around the cervical margin may "click" on the fracture (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Directional percussion that opens the crack may cause pain (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Probing below the alveolar crest means the tooth is not restorable (Bakland, 2019); the pockets are so narrow that probing usually needs local anaesthesia (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. A normal probing depth does not exclude a crack, because subgingival fractures often do not create a defect (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Probing depths over 4 mm are more likely with pulp necrosis (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. On radiographs, bone loss increases with the severity of the crack (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. On small-field CBCT, an angular crestal defect is a surrogate marker for radicular extension (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; if CBCT is used, a 0.2 mm voxel is advised (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Ultrasound has imaged cracks in simulated tooth structure (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], but none of the newer methods has been tested to STARD standards.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Diagnosis rests on the history, then on tests that load the crack or make it visible; no single test is reliable.**

**1. History.** Pain on chewing and on release, cold sensitivity, long duration, poor localisation, previous unsuccessful treatment. Suspect a crack whenever pulpitis or necrosis has no obvious cause (no caries, restoration or trauma) (Berman and Tamse, 2021). Record habits, bruxism and medication.

**2. Extraoral and intraoral examination.** Masseteric hypertrophy, brachyfacial pattern, cheek ridging and tongue scalloping (parafunction); wear facets, plunger cusps, steep cusps, missing contacts, uneven marginal ridges, occlusal cant, large amalgams with undermined cusps (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**3. Bite test (most useful).** Load cusps one at a time with a Tooth Slooth or FracFinder, Burlew wheel, cotton-tip applicator or moist cotton roll; a positive test reproduces the complaint, often on release (American Association of Endodontists, 2012; Berman and Tamse, 2021). Instruments are more reliable than cotton rolls (Bresciani et al, 2020). Loading the filling, then the cusps, separates a leaking restoration from a crack (Bakland, 2019). Limitation: the load must line up with the crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025). Repeat after anaesthetising one arch to tell upper from lower.

**4. Transillumination.** The crack's air space blocks light, so the far side is dark (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); light passes through a craze line. Works best without restorations; fissures give false positives (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**5. Dyes.** Methylene blue or tincture of iodine, applied or sealed under a temporary for a few days (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**6. Magnification.** Microscope at 20-25x with a dry, lit field; specificity 92.9%, so best for excluding a crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**7. Removal of the restoration.** Cracks were seen in 23.3% of restored teeth before removal and in 60% after (Berman and Tamse, 2021). Inspect the base of the cusps, gingival seats and pulpal floor. Wedge gently with an instrument: no movement means cracked tooth, separation means split tooth (wedging may itself split the tooth; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**8. Periodontal probing (under anaesthesia).** A narrow, isolated deep pocket shows radicular extension. Probing below the alveolar crest means the tooth is not restorable (Bakland, 2019). A normal depth does not exclude a crack (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**9. Other tests.** A sharp explorer at the cervical margin may "click" (Bakland, 2019). Directional percussion may hurt when it opens the crack (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002). Cold and EPT show the pulp status, often with a heightened cold response.

**10. Radiographs.** Usually negative, because the mesiodistal crack runs perpendicular to the beam (Berman and Tamse, 2021). Look for angular crestal loss, furcal or apical radiolucency. Small-field CBCT (0.2 mm voxel) only when radicular extension is suspected; an angular defect is a surrogate sign (Bakland, 2019; Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**11. Newer methods.** Light-induced fluorescence, OCT, ultrasound, quantitative percussion, MRI; promising but unvalidated (Bresciani et al, 2020; Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**Accuracy caution.** Against micro-CT, none of transillumination, DIAGNOcam, microscope or macrophotography was highly accurate, and 20% of teeth extracted as "cracked" had no dentin crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

## 9. Differential diagnosis

### Explanation

Bakland sets crown-originating fractures against VRFs. COF patients describe sudden, sharp pain on chewing, while patients with VRFs are more likely to describe gradual-onset soreness (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. VRFs are easier to localise and often visible radiographically because they run buccolingually. Ingle's chapter contradicts itself on percussion: it says percussion is uncommon in infractions until the PDL is reached, yet its comparison paragraph calls percussion "more pronounced" in COFs than VRFs. This answer follows the more detailed explanation, that percussion is often negative early [Likely]. Bakland adds earache, sinusitis, TMD and trigeminal neuralgia as mimics (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Krishnan and Swain add myofascial pain referred from the masseter or temporalis in bruxists. **Cracked tooth (COF) versus vertical root fracture (Bakland, 2019; Berman and Tamse, 2021)**

| Feature | Cracked tooth | Vertical root fracture |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Crown, runs apically | Root, often apical, runs coronally |

**Other conditions to exclude**
- Dentin hypersensitivity, postoperative sensitivity, a leaking or fractured restoration, secondary caries.
- Occlusal trauma, parafunction, myofascial pain from the masseter or temporalis.
- Reversible or irreversible pulpitis from other causes; apical periodontitis; a periodontal abscess.
- Non-odontogenic pain: sinusitis, earache, TMD, trigeminal neuralgia (Bakland, 2019).

## 10. Management

### Explanation

**Principles.** Ingle's chapter is blunt: enamel and dentin cannot be permanently reunited once fractured (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Treatment aims to stop the segments separating, protect the pulp and seal out bacteria. The patient should be told that the prognosis is reduced and sometimes questionable (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], or "always questionable at best" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Cracked cusp and fractured cusp.** A full-coverage crown or onlay is recommended for a cracked cusp (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Endodontic treatment is indicated only if there are signs of pulpal disease (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. If a fractured cusp is not mobile, the fracture probably does not reach the root surface and a crown can hold the segments (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. A mobile cusp is removed and the tooth restored as appropriate (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

**Cracked tooth with reversible pulpitis.** An orthodontic band or a provisional crown stabilises the tooth at once (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Bakland bands the tooth for about 2 weeks to see whether the symptoms settle (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Krishnan and Swain's protocol: remove any amalgam, assess the crack, place direct composite with an RMGI liner, with or without cuspal coverage, and secure the tooth with a stainless-steel band luted with RMGI at the first visit (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. In their studies 92% were asymptomatic at 2 months (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], and endodontic treatment should be considered if symptoms do not resolve within 3 months (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC].

**Crown alone versus elective RCT.** Krell and Rivera crowned cracked teeth with reversible pulpitis; about 20% later needed RCT (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Cohen's gives the detail: of 127 teeth, 20% became irreversible within 6 months, and none of the rest needed RCT over 6 years (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Bresciani's chapter reports the same 20% figure from Abbott and Leow and from Krell and Rivera (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. After bonded resin restorations, only 7% needed RCT or extraction (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

**Choice of restoration.** Cuspal-coverage direct composite gave 90% pulp survival over 7 years (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Without cuspal coverage, the annual failure rate was 6% (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; Opdam found no failures with cuspal coverage (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Indirect composite onlays left 93% symptom-free at 6 years (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. A meta-analysis found full crowns no better than direct cuspal-coverage restorations for vital cracked teeth (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Wu and co-workers found crowned teeth did better than those left in bands (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Bresciani's chapter concludes that the superiority of crowns over onlays or composites "is not proven yet" (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Direct composite splints are a short-term option (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC].

**Monitoring.** In the US practice network, 2858 vital cracked teeth were seen by 209 general dentists (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; 65% were monitored, and only 20% of these needed treatment within 3 years (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Krishnan and Swain warn that the enrolment definition was loose (any visible external crack), so monitoring should follow a structured risk assessment.

**Remove the crack?** Chasing a crack with burs "is not rational", because its far end is invisible (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Pulp survival is similar with or without crack removal, 76-80% (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC].

**Irreversible pulpitis or necrosis.** After access, examine the crack systematically, including whether it crosses the pulp chamber floor (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. A crack running from the mesial wall through the floor to the distal wall means a poor prognosis, and extraction should be considered (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A maxillary molar may be hemisected along the fracture (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Banding during RCT tests whether symptoms settle; Torabinejad's chapter calls this rationale "unsupported" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. The final restoration binds the segments (the "barrel-stave effect"; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and posts and internally wedging foundations are avoided (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Restoring a root-filled cracked tooth with only an intracoronal filling is a temptation that "should be resisted by all means" (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. If the crack enters the canal, obturate to 2-3 mm apical to the deepest extension of the crack and use an extended orifice barrier (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; this gave 96% survival over 4 years (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Without a crown, the relative risk of extraction is 11 times higher (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Chewing pain may persist after RCT, because PDL inflammation from bacteria in the crack continues (Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

**Split tooth.** Extraction is typically the only option (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and keeping the tooth intact is impossible (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. If the small segment shears off no deeper than the middle-to-cervical third, it may be removed. One technique retains the fragment temporarily under a strong clamp, completes RCT and an amalcore onlay, and then removes the fragment (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]; granulation tissue fills the space (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Hemisection or root amputation may be possible in multi-rooted teeth (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principles**
- Enamel and dentin cannot be reunited once fractured (Bakland, 2019). Treatment binds the segments, protects the pulp and seals out bacteria.
- Inform the patient that the prognosis is reduced, sometimes questionable, and that other teeth may also be cracked (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Control the cause: occlusal adjustment of plunger cusps, a night guard for bruxism, advice on habits.
- Management depends on (1) crack type and extent, (2) pulp status, (3) periodontal probing.

**A. Craze line:** no treatment; aesthetics only.

**B. Cracked or fractured cusp**
- Cracked cusp: protect it with an onlay or full-coverage crown, or bonded cuspal-coverage composite (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Fractured cusp, not mobile: crown to hold the segments. Mobile: remove the cusp and restore with a bonded composite, onlay or crown (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- RCT only if pulpal disease is present (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**C. Cracked tooth, vital, reversible pulpitis (crack confined to the crown)**
1. Immediate stabilisation: orthodontic band, stainless-steel band luted with RMGI, provisional crown, or direct composite splint (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Krishnan and Swain, 2025; Bresciani et al, 2020).
2. Remove any amalgam, inspect the crack, restore with RMGI liner and bonded composite with cuspal coverage (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
3. Review at 2 weeks to 3 months: 92% are asymptomatic by 2 months; if symptoms persist beyond 3 months, start RCT (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
4. Definitive cuspal coverage: direct composite with cuspal coverage, an indirect onlay or a full crown.
5. Warn that about 20% of crowned cracked teeth later need RCT (Krell and Rivera, reported by Berman and Tamse, 2021; Bakland, 2019).

**D. Cracked tooth with irreversible pulpitis or necrosis**
1. Band the tooth, reduce the occlusion and gain access.
2. Map the crack: entry into the chamber; number of cracks; whether they cross a marginal ridge, the pulp chamber floor or the canals; whether an explorer catches; probing depth (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
3. A crack from the mesial wall through the floor to the distal wall: poor prognosis, extract (Berman and Tamse, 2021); a maxillary molar may be hemisected (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
4. If retainable, complete RCT. If the crack enters a canal, keep the gutta-percha 2-3 mm apical to the deepest extent of the crack and seal the coronal part with a resin orifice barrier (96% survival at 4 years; Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
5. Bonded core without posts or wedging foundations; full crown as early as possible, because without one the risk of extraction is 11 times higher (Krishnan and Swain, 2025). Never finish with an intracoronal filling alone (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
6. Chewing pain may persist after RCT, because PDL inflammation from the crack continues (Bakland, 2019).

**E. Split tooth**
- Usually extraction (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- If the fragment ends in the cervical or middle third: remove it (or retain it temporarily under a clamp, complete RCT and an amalcore onlay, then remove it; the space granulates in) or use crown lengthening (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Multi-rooted teeth: hemisection or root amputation (Bresciani et al, 2020).

**F. Asymptomatic crack:** monitor only after a structured assessment of environmental, loading and structural risk; in a practice network only 20% of monitored cracked teeth needed treatment in 3 years (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**Do not chase the crack.** Removing dentin until the line vanishes is "not rational" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002); pulp survival is the same with or without crack removal, 76-80% (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**Experimental:** CO2 and Nd:YAG lasers have not given promising results (Bakland, 2019).

## 11. Prognosis

### Explanation

Cameron reported 75% success at 10 years after crowns (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; Tan and co-workers reported 85% survival 2 years after protective crowns (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; and Brynjulfsen's group achieved pain relief in 90% of patients (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Wu found about 70% of cracked teeth with reversible pulpitis healthy at 3 years (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. After RCT, survival was 92% over 5 years (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC], and 84-88% over 1-5 years overall (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Probing depths over 6 mm worsened the prognosis (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC], and Krishnan and Swain put the threshold at over 5 mm, together with terminal tooth and multiple cracks (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Cracks reaching the pulpal floor raised the risk of extraction (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Ingle's chapter names terminal teeth, teeth with crack-related periodontal involvement and teeth with multiple cracks as having a poor prognosis (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Necrosis alone is not an indicator of poor prognosis (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Patients should be told that other teeth may also be cracked (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Reported outcomes**
- Crowns: 75% success at 10 years (Cameron); 85% survival at 2 years (Tan); pain relief in 90% of patients after protective restorations (Brynjulfsen) (all reported by Bakland, 2019).
- Reversible pulpitis: about 80% stay vital over 5-6 years, and about 20% need RCT within 3-6 months (Bresciani et al, 2020); about 70% healthy at 3 years (Wu).
- After RCT: 92% survival at 5 years (Bresciani et al, 2020); 84-88% over 1-5 years (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

**Poor prognostic factors**
- Crack crossing the pulp chamber floor or reaching the canals.
- Probing depth over 5-6 mm; isolated deep pocket.
- Terminal tooth (no distal neighbour); multiple cracks; crack detectable with an explorer.
- Centred, deep cracks; split tooth.
- No cuspal coverage or crown.

**Not a poor factor on its own:** pulp necrosis (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

## 12. Prevention

### Explanation

Ingle's chapter lists conservative cavity size and design, proper design of intracoronal cast restorations, avoiding self-threading and friction-lock pins, avoiding coarse diamonds and vibrating handpieces, removing working interferences, and advice on habits such as pipes, pencils and fingernails. Until evidence is available, it recommends advising against chewing ice (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Torabinejad's chapter recommends reducing and onlaying undermined cusps (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], but says altering the occlusal anatomy or occlusal relationships "is not useful" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. This sits uneasily with Krishnan and Swain's emphasis on loading geometry. The two can be reconciled: removing a specific plunger-cusp interference differs from reshaping the occlusion as a general preventive measure [Guessing].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Restorative**
- Conservative cavities; preserve marginal ridges; rounded internal line angles.
- Onlay undermined cusps; cuspal coverage after RCT and for large MOD cavities (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Prefer bonded to non-bonded intracoronal restorations; avoid wedging inlays, self-threading and friction-lock pins, and posts that wedge.
- Avoid coarse diamonds and vibrating high-speed handpieces; place composite incrementally (Bakland, 2019).

**Occlusal and patient**
- Remove working-side interferences and plunger cusps; a night guard for bruxism.
- Advise against chewing ice, hard candy, pens and fingernails (Bakland, 2019).
- Restore missing posterior contacts to avoid lone-standing, overloaded teeth.
- Torabinejad's chapter considers general reshaping of the occlusion not useful (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

**Early detection:** examine high-risk teeth (mandibular molars, maxillary premolars) at recall with magnification and bite testing.

## 13. Conclusion

### Explanation

All sources share the same core messages [Certain]: CTS is a fatigue fracture that is diagnosed mainly from the history; tests make the crack visible but none is conclusive; and treatment binds the tooth without ever healing it. The clinician must decide the type, extent and pulp status. The disputes are about how to restore the tooth (crown versus cuspal-coverage composite) and whether to monitor. They are not about whether cuspal protection is needed.

#### ✎ Exam core

Cracked tooth syndrome is a crown-originating fatigue fracture that never heals and tends to grow. The history leads the diagnosis; tests confirm it. Vital teeth are banded and given cuspal coverage, non-vital teeth receive RCT and an early crown, and split teeth are usually extracted. The prognosis is guarded, so prevention matters most.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1. A full crown is the definitive treatment for a vital cracked tooth.**
- Textbook: Cohen's recommends a full-coverage crown or onlay (Berman and Tamse, 2021); Torabinejad's chapter prefers a full crown (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Evidence: cuspal-coverage direct composite gave 90% pulp survival over 7 years (Krishnan and Swain, 2025), and Opdam found no failures with cuspal coverage against 6% a year without it (Bresciani et al, 2020). A meta-analysis found crowns no better than direct cuspal coverage (Krishnan and Swain, 2025). Wu found crowns better than bands alone (Bresciani et al, 2020).
- Appraisal: these are cohort and practice-based studies, not head-to-head randomised trials; Bresciani's chapter says crown superiority "is not proven yet".
- Conclusion: cuspal coverage is what matters; its form (crown, onlay or composite) is not settled. Leaving the tooth in a band, or restoring it intracoronally without coverage, is the inferior option. [Likely]
- Clinical implication: a bonded cuspal-coverage composite is a reasonable first definitive restoration for a vital cracked tooth; reserve crowns for root-filled teeth and extensive breakdown.

**Claim 2. A cracked tooth with reversible pulpitis needs elective RCT before the crown.**
- Textbook: Torabinejad's chapter says many cracked teeth require RCT, "preferably before restoration" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- Evidence: about 20% of crowned teeth with reversible pulpitis converted within 6 months, and none of the rest needed RCT over 6 years (Krell and Rivera, in Berman and Tamse, 2021). Only 7% needed RCT after bonded resin restorations (Berman and Tamse, 2021). 92% were asymptomatic at 2 months after banding and bonding (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
- Appraisal: these are single-operator or specialist cohorts with short follow-up for the bonded group.
- Conclusion: elective RCT would overtreat about 80% of teeth. Stabilise, wait up to 3 months, and treat endodontically only if symptoms persist. [Likely]

**Claim 3. Every cracked tooth needs treatment.**
- Evidence: in a practice network of 2858 vital cracked teeth, only 20% of monitored teeth needed treatment within 3 years (Krishnan and Swain, 2025). Against this, a cuspal crack progressed to a corono-radicular fracture within 3 months in a case report from the same chapter.
- Appraisal: the network counted any visible external crack, including enamel-only lines, so it overstates how benign dentin cracks are.
- Conclusion: monitoring is defensible only for asymptomatic teeth with low environmental, loading and structural risk. [Likely]

**Limitation.** The diagnostic tests themselves have never been validated against a reference standard to STARD quality, and 20% of teeth extracted as "cracked" had no dentin crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025). Every outcome figure above therefore rests on an uncertain diagnosis.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. AAE five types: craze line, fractured cusp, cracked tooth, split tooth, VRF; the first four start in the crown, VRF starts in the root.
2. Cameron 1964 named CTS; Cohen's says avoid "syndrome"; Ingle's calls it a crown-originating fracture.
3. Most common in mandibular molars (48%) and maxillary premolars; usually mesiodistal; often invisible on radiographs.
4. Rebound pain on release is characteristic (dentinal fluid movement, A-delta fibres).
5. Bite test on individual cusps (Tooth Slooth), then transillumination, dye, magnification and restoration removal; no test is conclusive.
6. Vital tooth: band, bonded cuspal coverage; about 20% later need RCT.
7. Crack across the pulp chamber floor from mesial to distal: extract.
8. A root-filled cracked tooth needs a crown early (11 times the extraction risk without one).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Calling buccal and lingual craze lines on molars "cracks"; true cracked teeth run mesiodistally and craze lines transmit light.
2. Expecting the radiograph to show the crack; the mesiodistal crack lies perpendicular to the beam.
3. Treating every cracked tooth with RCT; vital teeth with reversible pulpitis are banded and given cuspal coverage first.
4. Finishing a root-filled cracked tooth with an intracoronal filling or a post; it needs early cuspal coverage and no wedging post.
5. Mixing up cracked tooth and VRF; they differ in origin, direction, pulp status and prognosis.
