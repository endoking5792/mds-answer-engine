## 1. Role of biomechanics in crack progression

### Explanation

Fracture mechanics is the branch of biomechanics that studies how cracks grow until a catastrophic fracture (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A cracked tooth is usually a fatigue failure: an accumulating, unobserved trauma from repeated normal or excessive occlusal forces (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. First molars carry up to 90 kg (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and forces are highest near the fulcrum of the mandible, the "nutcracker" effect (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

Geometry turns that load into stress at a crack tip. The maxillary mesiopalatal cusp wedges into the mandibular fossa and fatigues the lower molar (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Steep cuspal inclines create significant wedging force at the base of the fissure (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Loss of the marginal ridge lets a crack start at much lower load (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], and a small contact area gives very high stress even when bite force is low (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. In restored teeth, occlusal load and thermal expansion increase cuspal flexure and concentrate stress at sharp internal line angles (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. Non-bonded inlays and amalgam increase longitudinal fractures (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], as do self-threading and friction-lock pins (Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

Dentin's collagen resists crack initiation and growth (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], but enamel and dentin never repair (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], so a crack can only stay the same or grow [Certain]. The more centred the crack, the deeper it runs before shearing to the root surface (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and the more likely it is to reach the pulp (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Concept.** CTS is a fatigue fracture: repeated occlusal loads, not one impact, propagate a crack into a complete fracture (Berman and Tamse, 2021).

**Biomechanical factors**
- **Force:** molars carry up to 90 kg, and forces are highest near the mandibular fulcrum ("nutcracker effect") (Berman and Tamse, 2021; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).
- **Wedging:** a plunger cusp in the opposing fossa, especially with steep cuspal inclines, opens the fissure base like a wedge (Bresciani et al, 2020; Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
- **Marginal ridge loss and small contacts:** a crack starts at lower load, and the stress rises (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
- **Restorations:** cuspal flexure, stress at sharp line angles, wedging inlays and pins (Bresciani et al, 2020; Bakland, 2019).
- **Tissue:** dentin's collagen resists cracking less with age; cracks never heal (Bakland, 2019).

**Progression.** Craze line, cracked cusp or cracked tooth, pulpitis, necrosis, then split tooth. Central cracks run deeper and reach the pulp (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002).

## 2. Diagnostic challenges

### Explanation

Most cracks show nothing on radiographs in the early stages (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], because the crack runs perpendicular to the X-ray beam (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. The pain is pulpal, with no proprioception, as no periodontal ligament is involved (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; percussion is unhelpful until the crack reaches the PDL (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. Bite tests are unpredictable because the load must align with the crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], and transillumination gives many false positives (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Against micro-CT, none of the common tests was highly accurate (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; 20% of teeth extracted as cracked had no crack reaching dentin (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Restorations hide cracks: 23.3% were visible before removal and 60% after (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. 55% of cracked teeth are asymptomatic (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], and a subgingival crack may not create a probing defect (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Diagnostic challenges**
1. Radiographs are usually negative: the mesiodistal crack lies perpendicular to the beam (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
2. Poor localisation and referred pain: pulpal pain has no PDL proprioception, and percussion is negative until the crack reaches the PDL (Bakland, 2019).
3. Tests are unreliable: bite tests need aligned load, transillumination gives false positives, and 20% of teeth extracted as cracked had no dentin crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
4. Restorations hide cracks (seen in 23.3% before removal versus 60% after; Berman and Tamse, 2021).
5. The extent cannot be measured; 55% of cracked teeth are asymptomatic (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

## 3. Restorative challenges

### Explanation

Enamel and dentin cannot be permanently reunited once fractured (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], so restoration can only bind the segments. Removing dentin until the crack disappears "is not rational" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. A full crown was no better than direct cuspal-coverage restoration in a meta-analysis (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], and the superiority of crowns "is not proven yet" (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC]. About 20% of crowned teeth with reversible pulpitis later needed RCT (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Restorations should bind the tooth (the "barrel-stave effect"; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC] without posts or wedging foundations (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Wedging to test for a split may itself split the tooth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. After RCT, a missing crown raises the extraction risk 11 times (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Where a crack enters a canal, the obturation is kept apical to the deepest extension of the crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Restorative challenges**
1. The crack cannot be healed or removed, only bound. Chasing it with burs is "not rational" (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Bakland, 2019).
2. The best form of cuspal coverage is unsettled: crowns are no better than bonded cuspal-coverage composite (Krishnan and Swain, 2025; Bresciani et al, 2020).
3. The pulp outcome is unpredictable: about 20% of crowned teeth later need RCT (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
4. Restorations must bind the tooth without posts; after RCT, crown early (11 times the extraction risk without; Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
5. Cracks entering a canal: obturate apical to the crack and seal the coronal part.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. CTS is a fatigue fracture; molars carry up to 90 kg near the mandibular fulcrum.
2. Wedging cusps, steep inclines, lost marginal ridges and small contacts concentrate stress.
3. Cracks never heal; central cracks run deeper and reach the pulp.
4. Radiographs miss mesiodistal cracks; no clinical test is accurate against micro-CT.
5. Restoration binds the segments; cuspal coverage matters more than its form.

## DIAGRAMS TO DRAW

1. Plunger cusp wedging into a fossa with the crack path towards the pulp.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Describing CTS as a single-impact injury instead of fatigue.
2. Saying the crack can be removed with burs.
3. Assuming a crown is proven superior to bonded cuspal coverage.
