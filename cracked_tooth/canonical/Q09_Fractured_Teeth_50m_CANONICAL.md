## 1. Introduction and classification

### Explanation

Bakland suggests "dental fractures" as an umbrella term for every fracture entity in a tooth (Bakland, 2019) [SRC], divided into crown-originating fractures, VRFs and trauma-related fractures. Within the longitudinal group, the AAE lists five types from least to most severe (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. Sikri sets out the Ellis classes, from Class 1 (enamel or little dentin) (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC] through Class 2 (considerable dentin) (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC] and Class 3 (pulp exposed) (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC] to Class 6 (root fracture) (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC], with Classes 7 and 8 (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC] and Class 9 for primary teeth (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]. He also lists Luebke's three classes of crack (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC], from Class 1, incomplete and supraosseous with no periodontal defect (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC], to Class 3, intraosseous with a major periodontal defect (Sikri, n.d.) [SRC]. The AAE defines infraction as an incomplete enamel fracture without loss of tooth structure (American Association of Endodontists, 2012) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Fractured teeth are a broad group: from an acute blow that breaks an incisor to a slow fatigue crack in a molar or a root fracture in a root-filled premolar. Their diagnosis, treatment and prognosis differ widely, so the first step is to classify the fracture correctly.

**1. "Dental fractures" (Bakland, 2019)**
- Trauma-related fractures: acute; crown, crown-root and root (horizontal) fractures.
- Crown-originating fractures: fatigue; cracked cusp, cracked tooth, split tooth.
- Vertical root fractures: root-originating, mostly endodontically treated teeth.

**2. Traumatic (WHO/IADT; Bourguignon et al, 2020):** enamel infraction; enamel fracture; enamel-dentin (uncomplicated crown) fracture; complicated crown fracture; uncomplicated and complicated crown-root fracture; root fracture.

**3. Ellis (Sikri, n.d.):** Class 1 enamel or little dentin; 2 considerable dentin; 3 pulp exposed; 4 non-vital; 5 avulsed; 6 root fracture; 7 displacement without fracture; 8 crown fracture en masse; 9 primary teeth.

**4. Longitudinal (AAE five types; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002):** craze line, fractured cusp, cracked tooth, split tooth, vertical root fracture.

**5. Luebke's crack classes (Sikri, n.d.):** Class 1 incomplete, supraosseous, no periodontal defect; Class 2 incomplete, intraosseous, minor defect; Class 3 complete or incomplete, intraosseous, major defect.

## 2. Mechanisms

### Explanation

Traumatic root fractures follow a frontal impact that creates labial and lingual compression zones (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Crown-originating cracks follow accumulated, unobserved occlusal trauma (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Enamel and dentin never repair (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]. A crown-root fracture communicates with the mouth, so it cannot heal (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Root fractures inside the alveolus can heal with hard tissue (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Acute trauma:** a frontal blow produces labial and lingual compression zones; the shear plane between them fractures crown or root (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007). Mainly maxillary incisors in young people.
- **Fatigue:** repeated normal or excessive occlusal loads, wedging cusps and large restorations propagate cracks in posterior teeth (Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- **Iatrogenic wedging:** posts, lateral compaction and excessive dentin removal cause VRF.
- **Healing potential:** cracks and crown-root fractures never heal, because they open to the mouth (Bakland, 2019; Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007). Intra-alveolar root fractures can unite with hard tissue (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007).

## 3. Crown fractures (traumatic)

### Explanation

The IADT recommends etching and sealing severe enamel infractions (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. Enamel fractures are smoothed or restored (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. For enamel-dentin fractures the fragment is rehydrated for 20 min and bonded (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], and dentin within 0.5 mm of the pulp gets a calcium hydroxide lining (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. For complicated crown fractures, partial pulpotomy or capping preserves the pulp in immature teeth (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], and partial pulpotomy is also preferred in mature teeth (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], unless a post is needed, when RCT is preferred (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Clinical features.** Usually maxillary central incisors in children and adolescents after falls, sports or fights. Enamel and enamel-dentin fractures: normal mobility, no tenderness, positive sensibility tests; sensitivity to air and cold if dentin is exposed. Complicated fractures: a bleeding or pink pulp exposure sensitive to air, cold and sweets. Tenderness or mobility suggests an associated luxation or root fracture.

**IADT 2020 (Bourguignon et al, 2020)**

| Injury | Management |
|---|---|
| Enamel infraction | No treatment; etch and seal severe infractions with bonding resin |
| Enamel fracture | Smooth edges or composite; bond the fragment |
| Uncomplicated (enamel-dentin) | Rehydrate the fragment 20 min and bond it, or cover dentin with GIC or bonded composite; calcium hydroxide lining if within 0.5 mm of the pulp |
| Complicated (pulp exposed) | Immature: partial pulpotomy or capping (calcium hydroxide or non-staining calcium silicate). Mature: partial pulpotomy still preferred; RCT if a post is needed. Bond the fragment |

Follow-up at 6-8 weeks and 1 year (longer if luxation is associated).

**Points to remember.** Examine every crown fracture for an associated luxation or root fracture, which then sets the follow-up schedule. Account for missing fragments. Bonded fragments may re-fracture and darken. Teeth with exposed dentin need immediate coverage to protect the pulp from bacteria. Unfavourable outcomes are pulp necrosis, apical periodontitis, arrested root development in immature teeth, and loss or breakdown of the restoration.

## 4. Crown-root fractures

### Explanation

A crown-root fracture involves enamel, dentin and cementum (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Andreasen gives four definitive conservative options (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Orthodontic extrusion was introduced by Heithersay in 1973 (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC], within 2-4 mm for a central and 4-6 mm for a lateral incisor (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Surgical extrusion needs a complete root long enough for a post crown (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. In mature teeth the IADT usually indicates pulp removal (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- Definition: a fracture of enamel, dentin and cementum, uncomplicated or complicated (pulp exposed); about 5% of permanent-tooth injuries; often overlooked in posterior teeth because displacement is minimal.
- Oblique line from above the labial gingiva to below the palatal crevice; mobile coronal fragment; radiographs miss the palatal extent; CBCT helps (Bourguignon et al, 2020).
- Emergency: splint the fragment; partial pulpotomy (immature) or pulpectomy (mature).
- No fracture healing is possible, because the line opens into the gingival sulcus.
- Definitive (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007): (1) fragment removal and supragingival restoration; (2) surgical exposure by gingivectomy and osteotomy (palatal only); (3) orthodontic extrusion (Heithersay, 1973; central 2-4 mm, lateral 4-6 mm); (4) surgical extrusion or intentional replantation with rotation; plus root submergence or extraction, with an implant or autotransplantation.

## 5. Root fractures (horizontal, traumatic)

### Explanation

Root fractures involve dentin, cementum and pulp (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC], and diagnosis depends entirely on radiographs (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Healing by hard tissue union is possible (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC]. Splint 4 weeks (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], up to 4 months if cervical (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. Necrosis is usually coronal only (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC], so only the coronal fragment is root filled (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- Definition: a fracture of dentin, cementum and pulp within the root; horizontal, oblique or both; 0.5-7% of permanent-tooth injuries; middle third now most frequent.
- Clinical: mobile, slightly extruded coronal fragment displaced lingually; tender; sulcular bleeding; sensibility often negative at first.
- Diagnosis by radiographs at ±15° and an occlusal film.
- Healing: hard tissue, connective tissue, bone and connective tissue, or granulation tissue (non-healing).
- Treatment: reposition; passive flexible splint 4 weeks (cervical up to 4 months); no RCT at the emergency visit.
- Necrosis: root fill the coronal fragment only, with an apexification barrier at the fracture line.
- Cervical fractures: may heal; if very mobile, remove the coronal fragment, RCT, post crown, with extrusion (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007; Bourguignon et al, 2020).
- Follow-up for at least 5 years; pulp canal obliteration (69-73%) and surface resorption need only observation.
- Abandoned techniques: intraradicular metal pins and endodontic implants.
- Prognosis: the pulp survives more often than after luxation, because the fracture gives a wide route for revascularisation; healing is better with immature roots, young age and little displacement.

## 6. Longitudinal (crown-originating) fractures

### Explanation

Craze lines matter only as a source of confusion (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC]. A cracked cusp flexes microscopically on chewing (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC] and needs an onlay or crown (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A cracked tooth is stabilised with a band or provisional crown (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], or a stainless-steel band luted with RMGI (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. Cuspal-coverage composite gave 90% pulp survival over 7 years (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], and crowns were not better than direct cuspal coverage (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. A crack across the pulp floor from mesial to distal means a poor prognosis (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. After RCT, keep gutta-percha apical to the crack (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC] and crown early, because the extraction risk is 11 times higher without one (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. An intracoronal filling alone "should be resisted by all means" (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. A split tooth usually means extraction (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Features of the cracked tooth.** Posterior teeth (mandibular molars, maxillary premolars) of adults over 40; sharp pain on chewing and on release; cold sensitivity; poor localisation; long history; usually no radiographic sign because the mesiodistal crack lies perpendicular to the beam. Later, pulpitis, "fracture necrosis" of an apparently sound tooth, a narrow isolated pocket and finally a split tooth. Pain on release comes from fluid movement in the crack stimulating A-delta fibres.

**Biomechanics.** Repeated loads up to 90 kg on molars, wedging plunger cusps, steep cusps, lost marginal ridges and large non-bonded restorations concentrate stress at the fissure base; central cracks run deeper and reach the pulp.

| Type | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Craze line | None (aesthetics) |
| Cracked or fractured cusp | Remove a mobile cusp; onlay, crown or bonded cuspal-coverage composite; RCT only if the pulp is involved |
| Cracked tooth, vital | Band or provisional crown; RMGI and composite with cuspal coverage; crown or onlay; review for 3 months |
| Cracked tooth, necrotic | RCT (gutta-percha apical to the crack); full crown early; extract if the crack crosses the pulp floor mesiodistally |
| Split tooth | Extraction; or remove the small segment if it ends in the cervical or middle third |

(Berman and Tamse, 2021; Krishnan and Swain, 2025; Walton and Torabinejad, 2002)

## 7. Vertical root fractures

### Explanation

A VRF is a longitudinal root fracture with incompletely separated segments (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. The AAE's pathognomonic sign is a sinus tract with a narrow isolated pocket (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and the typical radiograph shows a J-shaped lesion (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Treatment is removal of the fractured root, by amputation or hemisection in multirooted teeth (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], as early as possible (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]. Prevention follows two cardinal rules (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

- Root-originating, buccolingual, mostly in root-filled teeth with posts; premolars and mesial roots of mandibular molars.
- Incidence: 11-20% of extracted root-filled teeth.
- Signs: narrow isolated pocket (64-93%), coronal sinus tract (13-42%), J-shaped or halo radiolucency; the AAE pathognomonic pair (sinus tract plus narrow pocket) is present in only about a quarter to two-fifths of cases; exploratory flap confirms with a buccal dehiscence.
- CBCT cannot show unseparated fractures under 0.15 mm.
- Treatment: extract early; root amputation or hemisection in molars.
- Prevention: avoid excessive dentin removal and wedging forces (posts, spreaders); passive, small posts; ferrule crowns (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002; Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- Etiology: oval roots (strain at the buccal and lingual convexity), danger-zone concavities, excessive canal enlargement, rigid stainless-steel spreaders, tapered or threaded posts, and loss of coronal structure.
- Differential: failed RCT, periodontitis and endo-perio lesions; a single radiograph cannot confirm a VRF.

## 8. Diagnosis of fractured teeth

### Explanation

Q8 gives the full diagnostic answer. In outline: cracks are found by history, bite test, transillumination, dyes, magnification and restoration removal (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; VRF by probing, sinus tract, J-shaped radiolucency and exploratory flap (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC]; traumatic root fractures by angled and occlusal radiographs (Andreasen, Andreasen and Cvek, 2007) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**History.** Trauma (when, how, where; tetanus status; lost fragments) versus chewing pain without injury (crack) versus a root-filled tooth with a post (VRF).

**Clinical examination**
- Inspection with magnification: fracture lines, exposed dentin or pulp, mobility of fragments, displacement, soft-tissue wounds (fragments may be embedded in the lip).
- Bite test on individual cusps: pain on release suggests a crack.
- Transillumination and dyes: a crack blocks light and takes up stain; craze lines transmit light.
- Removal of restorations and wedging: separation means a split tooth.
- Periodontal probing (flexible probe, under anaesthesia): a narrow isolated pocket signals radicular extension or VRF.
- Mobility and percussion: a mobile coronal fragment suggests a root fracture.
- Pulp sensibility tests: cold and EPT; after trauma a negative response may be temporary.

**Imaging**
- Periapical at two or three angulations, and an occlusal film for root fractures.
- Lip or cheek radiographs for missing fragments.
- CBCT for crown-root and root fractures and for the bone loss pattern of VRF.

**Surgical exploration** when a VRF is suspected and other findings are equivocal.

## 9. Recent advances in diagnosis

### Explanation

Krishnan and Swain report that none of the modern crack-detection tools was highly accurate against micro-CT (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC], although a microscope's specificity reaches 92.9% (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]. For suspected crown fractures CBCT at 0.2 mm voxel is advised (Bakland, 2019) [SRC]; for VRF it cannot show fractures under 0.15 mm (Berman and Tamse, 2021) [SRC], and its superiority is disputed (Tamse and Berman, 2019) [SRC]. The IADT uses CBCT for crown-root and root fractures (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC] that plain films may miss (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]. Ultrasound imaging (Bakland, 2019) [SRC] and light-induced fluorescence (Bresciani et al, 2020) [SRC] are in development.

#### ✎ Exam core

- **Magnification:** operating microscope at 20-25x; high specificity for excluding cracks (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
- **CBCT:** essential in trauma for the path and extent of crown-root and root fractures (Bourguignon et al, 2020); for cracks and VRF it shows bone loss, not the fracture; small field, 0.2 mm voxel or finer (Bakland, 2019; Berman and Tamse, 2021).
- **Optical methods:** light-induced fluorescence, DIAGNOcam, optical coherence tomography.
- **Ultrasound and thermography:** experimental crack imaging (Bakland, 2019).
- **Limitation:** no tool is accurate alone against micro-CT (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).

## 10. Recent advances in treatment

### Explanation

Advances include fragment reattachment with rehydration (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]; partial pulpotomy with calcium silicate cements in mature teeth (Bourguignon et al, 2020) [SRC]; bonded cuspal-coverage composite for cracked teeth (Krishnan and Swain, 2025) [SRC]; and orthodontic and surgical extrusion of crown-root fractures (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007) [SRC]. Lasers have not given promising results (Bakland, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

1. **Fragment reattachment:** rehydrate 20 minutes and bond; the most conservative and aesthetic option (Bourguignon et al, 2020).
2. **Vital pulp therapy:** partial pulpotomy with non-staining calcium silicate (MTA, Biodentine) is now preferred even in mature teeth with complicated crown fractures (Bourguignon et al, 2020).
3. **Adhesive cuspal coverage:** bonded composite or indirect onlays for cracked teeth, as effective as crowns (Krishnan and Swain, 2025).
4. **Extrusion and replantation:** orthodontic (magnets, masked appliances) and surgical extrusion with rotation; intentional replantation for crown-root fractures (Andreasen, Andreasen and Tsukiboshi, 2007).
5. **Root submergence:** keeps the ridge for a future implant.
6. **Minimally invasive endodontics:** conservative access, flexible NiTi, thermoplastic obturation and fibre posts to prevent VRF.
7. **Not effective:** lasers to fuse cracks; bonding a VRF (Bakland, 2019).

## 11. Prevention and conclusion

### Explanation

Prevention follows the mechanism: mouthguards for trauma [RECALL], cuspal protection and habit control for fatigue cracks (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC], and avoidance of wedging for VRF (Walton and Torabinejad, 2002) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Prevention**
- Trauma: mouthguards in contact sports; early correction of proclined incisors.
- Cracks: onlay undermined cusps; bonded rather than wedging restorations; control bruxism and hard-food habits.
- VRF: conservative preparation; gentle obturation; avoid posts or use passive ones; ferrule crowns.

**Conclusion.** Fractured teeth range from a harmless craze line to a hopeless vertical root fracture. Correct classification (trauma-related, crown-originating or root-originating) drives the diagnosis and the treatment. Modern adhesive dentistry, calcium silicate pulp therapy and extrusion techniques now save many teeth that were once extracted. Cracks and VRFs, however, never heal, so prevention and early diagnosis matter most.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1. Mature teeth with pulp exposure from a fracture need root canal treatment.**
- Textbook (traditional): pulpectomy for exposed pulps in mature teeth.
- Evidence: the IADT now prefers partial pulpotomy even in mature teeth with complicated crown fractures, but advises pulp removal in mature crown-root fractures (Bourguignon et al, 2020).
- Appraisal: an internal IADT distinction based on access and contamination, not on a trial comparing the two.
- Conclusion: for a crown fracture, preserve the pulp; for a crown-root fracture, remove it, mainly because the fracture extends subgingivally. [Likely]

**Claim 2. Full crowns are mandatory for cracked teeth.**
- Evidence: cuspal-coverage composite gave 90% pulp survival over 7 years, and a meta-analysis found crowns no better than direct cuspal coverage (Krishnan and Swain, 2025). Root-filled cracked teeth without crowns had an 11-fold extraction risk.
- Conclusion: cuspal coverage is essential; a crown is essential after RCT; for vital teeth its form is open. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

1. Three groups: trauma-related, crown-originating, vertical root fractures.
2. Ellis Class 1-9; WHO/IADT; AAE five longitudinal types; Luebke crack classes.
3. Crown fractures: rehydrate and bond the fragment; partial pulpotomy preferred.
4. Crown-root: four options (removal, surgical exposure, orthodontic extrusion, surgical extrusion).
5. Root fracture: flexible splint 4 weeks; coronal-only RCT if necrotic.
6. Cracked tooth: band, cuspal coverage; split tooth: extract.
7. VRF: extract or resect; prevent by avoiding wedging.
8. Cracks never heal; root fractures within the alveolus can.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Writing only about traumatic incisor fractures and omitting cracked teeth and VRF.
2. Mixing up Ellis Class 4 (non-vital) with a fracture pattern.
3. Expecting cracks or crown-root fractures to heal.
4. Root filling both fragments of a horizontal root fracture.
5. Calling a buccolingual molar craze line a cracked tooth.
