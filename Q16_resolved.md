## STUDY MODE

### S1. The release pattern
GIC shows a high early release that falls over two to three months and then stabilises at a low steady level that has been monitored for at least five years (Mount, 1994). Fluoride is not part of the matrix, so release does not damage properties (Mount, 1994). The time to a steady level differs by author: about 3 weeks, followed by 0.5 to 1.0 micrograms per mL in the second year (Forsten, quoted by Hilton et al, 2013); a fall over the first two months (Mount et al, 2016); 2-3 months (Mount, 1994); a fall-away 8-10 weeks after placement, with later release called of subclinical value (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); and, after the first few days, a very low rate (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Three further textbooks give different time courses: most ions are released in the first 2 days (5 to 155 ppm in different studies), followed by slow, constant release that can last up to 3 years (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021); the release peaks in the first 24 hours, falls over many weeks and stabilises at a constant level in 3-4 months (Garg and Garg, 2011); and the burst occurs mainly in the first 24 hours after mixing, after which release is sustained but likely far below the therapeutic level needed to prevent secondary caries (Sturdevant 2nd South Asia edition, adapted from the 7th edition; Ritter et al, 2019). These are not reconciled here.

### S2. Recharge
The cement acts as a reservoir: fluoride from toothpaste, gels or rinses can return to it when the surrounding gradient is high, and is released again (Mount et al, 2016; Sturdevant 5th ed, Roberson et al, 2006). Summitt calls the ability to be recharged perhaps the most important variable in fluoride release, but adds that the increased release after recharge is short-lived (under a day), so recharge must be daily (Hilton et al, 2013). Sturdevant 5th edition says it is unlikely that recharge significantly improves prevention of secondary caries compared with other restoratives (Roberson et al, 2006). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia edition says glass ionomer materials absorb topical fluoride and slowly re-release it over 24 hours, that a potentially therapeutic level needs exposure to fluoride about three times a day, that a luting-cement film or a base or liner under a sealed restoration would not receive enough fluoride, and that only restorative glass ionomers have legitimate potential for preventing secondary caries (Ritter et al, 2019). McCabe and Walls (2008) call the benefit of long-term release debatable, state that a long-term pattern of recharge and re-release is likely to matter more than the high initial release, and note that materials with the greatest initial release also have the greatest ability to be recharged. The recharge order differs within Summitt: RMGIs and conventional GICs (Fuji IX, Ketac Molar) show the greatest capacity, compomers lie between RMGIs and composites, composites least; Xu and Burgess, quoted by Summitt, found RMGIs greatest, then compomers, with traditional GICs intermediate and composites least (Hilton et al, 2013). Summitt recommends neutral NaF and says acidulated phosphate fluoride and other acidified products degrade GIC and should be avoided; gels recharge more than low-fluoride toothpastes (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S3. Other materials
Compomers show no initial burst and a low sustained release (McCabe and Walls, 2008), or no burst and a more constant level (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021), or fluoride at a very low level, less than 10% of the release of glass ionomers (Ritter et al, 2019); giomer release is comparable to compomers (McCabe and Walls, 2008); resin-modified GIC releases slightly more than conventional GIC, mostly in the first 10-15 days (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013), while resin-modified glass ionomers feature a lower fluoride release than conventional glass ionomers (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021), and McCabe and Walls (2008) state that typical resin-modified GICs behave in a similar fashion to conventional GICs in pattern and daily amount, and that compomer release becomes equivalent to conventional GIC after about 40 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Summitt, quoting Hsu et al, reports Vitremer releasing more than Fuji IX over 8 hours (Hilton et al, 2013).

### S4. Does it work?
Fluoride gives fluorapatite and enhances remineralisation (Hilton et al, 2013); Summitt recommends a long-term release of at least 2-3 micrograms per mL per day, and adds that with present materials this rate can be maintained only when supplemental fluorides are used (Hilton et al, 2013). The clinical benefit against recurrent caries is contested; laboratory release is highest in GIC (Justen et al, 2024), and in an RCT meta-analysis GIC had less secondary caries than amalgam but not than composite (Ge et al, 2023). Braga (2021) qualifies the claim: fluoride does not prevent caries lesion development but slows its progression; clinical evidence for the remineralising and antibacterial effects of fluoride-releasing materials is scant and most often shows no significant effect on lesion development; a freshly mixed cement is antibacterial because of high initial fluoride and low pH, but this effect is lost with time; and higher fluoride in enamel next to a restoration reflects de- and remineralisation rather than a strengthening effect of the material (Braga, 2021). Goldstein et al (2018) say the anticariogenic effect promoted for fluoride-releasing glass ionomer luting cement has not been documented clinically (Goldstein et al, 2018). The term "secondary caries" is better written caries adjacent to a restoration or sealant (CARS) (Longbottom and Ferreira Zandona, 2020).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 8 minutes.)*

### 1. Materials that release fluoride
Glass ionomer (conventional, resin-modified, high-viscosity), compomer and giomer (McCabe and Walls, 2008), and fluoride-releasing resin composites (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 2. Pattern in GIC
- Initial burst, declining over 2-3 months (Mount, 1994); plateau 3 weeks (Forsten, in Hilton et al, 2013) to 8-10 weeks (Banerjee and Watson, 2015); burst in 24 h (Ritter et al, 2019) or 2 days (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021).
- Low steady level for years; at least five years measured (Mount, 1994).
- Higher release at low pH (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 3. Recharge (reservoir effect)
Uptake from toothpaste, gel or rinse and later release; greatest in GIC and resin-modified GIC, least in composites; the order among GIC, resin-modified GIC and compomer varies by study (Hilton et al, 2013). Use neutral fluoride, not acidulated (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 4. Mechanism of anticaries action
Fluorapatite, remineralisation, reduced bacterial metabolism and plaque (Hilton et al, 2013; Mount, 1994).

### 5. Comparison
| Material | Release |
|---|---|
| Conventional GIC | high burst, then low steady (Mount, 1994) |
| Resin-modified GIC | slightly higher, mostly first 10-15 days (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); similar (McCabe and Walls, 2008); lower (Brambilla and Ionescu, 2021) |
| Compomer | no burst, low sustained; GIC-equal at 40 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Composite | little; poor recharge (Hilton et al, 2013) |

### 6. Clinical significance
Evidence of benefit is contested: GIC had fewer secondary caries than amalgam but not than composite (Ge et al, 2023). Use for high-risk patients, with recharge by topical fluoride; the 2-3 micrograms per mL per day Summitt recommends needs it (Hilton et al, 2013). Luting cements release too little at the margin to be relied on for remineralisation (Mount, 1994).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Burst, then low steady release for years (Mount, 1994).
- Recharge: GIC and resin-modified GIC best, composite least (Hilton et al, 2013); order varies by study.
- Compomer: no burst, low release (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Benefit shown against amalgam, not composite (Ge et al, 2023).
