## Explanation

Galvanism is the clinical face of electrochemical corrosion, which "requires the presence of water or some other fluid electrolyte"; "An electrochemical cell is composed of three essential components: an anode, a cathode, and an electrolyte", and "The more negative the electrode potential, the easier it is for any particular metal to ionize and oxidize" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. The classic case is "a dental amalgam restoration placed on the occlusal surface of a tooth directly opposing a gold inlay": both are wet with saliva, and when they touch the circuit is short-circuited — "This can result in a sharp pain, called galvanic shock"; out of contact, currents between a gold crown and an amalgam "appear to be approximately 0.5 to 1 microampere" with a "potential difference of approximately 500 millivolts"; currents also occur between similar alloys, and "A current is present even in a single isolated metallic restoration", between saliva and tissue fluid — "Because the chloride ion concentration is seven times higher than that of saliva", the inner surface is more active; "Coating with a varnish helps to eliminate galvanic shock"; although the current diminishes somewhat with age, "it remains indefinitely at the approximate value cited" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Clinical significance: pain is uncommon, and "such postoperative pain usually occurs immediately after insertion of a new restoration and generally subsides and disappears in a few days", as the pulp recovers — "the physiological condition of the tooth is the primary factor responsible for the pain resulting from this current flow"; "the most practical method for reducing or eliminating galvanic shock is a varnish coating", and "By the time the varnish has worn away, the pulp has usually healed sufficiently that no pain persists"; amalgam should not be placed against a gold crown because "Mercury released from the corroding amalgam (the anode) may interact with the gold alloy (the cathode) and weaken it", both may discolour, and "often a metallic taste develops that can persist indefinitely" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Where dissimilar metals must coexist, a non-conductive film helps "if it is applied to the more noble metal", because it reduces the cathode area (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Overall, "The balance of evidence shows that galvanic currents are deleterious only from the standpoint of patient discomfort, and even then only rarely" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Ion release adds the toxic and allergic dimension: "The most important electrochemical effects are toxic and allergic responses to metal ions released by corrosion" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Galvanism (electrogalvanism) is the flow of electric current between metallic restorations, or between parts of one restoration, in the electrolyte of the mouth. It is a form of electrochemical corrosion (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Mechanism.** Two metals with different electrode potentials in saliva form a cell: the more active metal (for example amalgam) is the anode and corrodes; the more noble metal (gold) is the cathode. Saliva is the electrolyte, and the tooth tissues and fluids complete the circuit.

**Situations**
1. **Dissimilar restorations in contact:** for example amalgam opposing a gold inlay. On contact the cell is short-circuited, giving a sudden sharp pain: galvanic shock.
2. **Dissimilar restorations not in contact:** a continuous current of about 0.5-1 µA at about 500 mV. It diminishes slightly with age but persists indefinitely.
3. **Similar alloys:** their surfaces are never identical, so currents still flow.
4. **A single restoration:** a cell between saliva and dentinal/tissue fluid, whose chloride concentration is seven times higher (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Clinical effects**
- Postoperative pain, usually immediately after insertion, subsiding in a few days. The physiological state of the recently prepared tooth is the main factor; once the pulp recovers, the same current causes no pain.
- Accelerated corrosion of the anode.
- Mercury released from corroding amalgam weakens and discolours a gold restoration in contact with it.
- Persistent metallic taste.
- Ion release with toxic or allergic potential.

**Prevention and management**
- Avoid placing dissimilar metals in contact (amalgam against a gold crown).
- Varnish the restoration: it insulates it from saliva, so no cell forms. By the time the varnish wears away the pulp has healed. This is the most practical method.
- If dissimilar metals must coexist, coat the more noble metal.
- Polish restorations (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Significance.** The balance of evidence is that galvanic currents are harmful mainly as patient discomfort, and even then only rarely (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Saying gold corrodes in an amalgam-gold couple (amalgam is the anode).
- Claiming galvanism needs two metals (a single restoration also generates a current).

## MNEMONIC
**"PACT"** effects: **P**ain, **A**ccelerated corrosion, **C**ontamination of gold by mercury, **T**aste.
