## Explanation

Galvanism is the clinical face of electrochemical corrosion, which "requires the presence of water or some other fluid electrolyte"; "An electrochemical cell is composed of three essential components: an anode, a cathode, and an electrolyte", and "The more negative the electrode potential, the easier it is for any particular metal to ionize and oxidize" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. The classic case is "a dental amalgam restoration placed on the occlusal surface of a tooth directly opposing a gold inlay": both are wet with saliva, and when they touch the circuit is short-circuited — "This can result in a sharp pain, called galvanic shock"; out of contact, currents between a gold crown and an amalgam "appear to be approximately 0.5 to 1 microampere" with a "potential difference of approximately 500 millivolts"; currents also occur between similar alloys, and "A current is present even in a single isolated metallic restoration", between saliva and tissue fluid — "Because the chloride ion concentration is seven times higher than that of saliva", the inner surface is more active; "Coating with a varnish helps to eliminate galvanic shock"; although the current diminishes somewhat with age, "it remains indefinitely at the approximate value cited" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Clinical significance: pain is uncommon, and "such postoperative pain usually occurs immediately after insertion of a new restoration and generally subsides and disappears in a few days", as the pulp recovers — "the physiological condition of the tooth is the primary factor responsible for the pain resulting from this current flow"; "the most practical method for reducing or eliminating galvanic shock is a varnish coating", and "By the time the varnish has worn away, the pulp has usually healed sufficiently that no pain persists"; amalgam should not be placed against a gold crown because "Mercury released from the corroding amalgam (the anode) may interact with the gold alloy (the cathode) and weaken it", both may discolour, and "often a metallic taste develops that can persist indefinitely" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Where dissimilar metals must coexist, a non-conductive film helps "if it is applied to the more noble metal", because it reduces the cathode area (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Overall, "The balance of evidence shows that galvanic currents are deleterious only from the standpoint of patient discomfort, and even then only rarely" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's adds quantitative detail: "most patients feel pain at a value between 20 and 50" µA, some at 10 µA, "whereas others do not experience it until 110" µA, so "the sensitivity of the patient to the current has a greater influence on whether pain is felt" than the voltage; "The current rapidly falls off if the fillings are maintained in contact, probably as a result of polarization of the cell"; "An alloy of stainless steel develops a higher current density than either gold or cobalt-chromium alloys when in contact with an amalgam restoration"; "The larger cathode, likewise, can enhance the corrosion of the smaller anode"; and non-γ2 (high-copper) "containing amalgams appear to be less than those associated with the" γ2-containing ones in current density (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

**Conflict — insulating bases.** Bhat et al advise to "provide a good insulating base, which protects the pulp from galvanic shock" (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019) [SRC], whereas Craig's reports that "a cement base does not effectively insulate the pulp from the electric current developed in a metallic" restoration (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]; Phillips' relies on surface varnish, which insulates the restoration from saliva rather than the pulp from the restoration. Follow Phillips' and Craig's. [Likely]

**Conflict — amalgam couples.** Phillips': "A high-copper amalgam is cathodic with respect to a conventional amalgam", and "Clinical observations do not indicate accelerated corrosion in such situations" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; Bhat et al say "the high copper becomes the anode and undergoes corrosion" (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019) [SRC]. Craig's galvanic series in saliva agrees with Phillips'. [Likely]

Ion release adds the toxic and allergic dimension: "The most important electrochemical effects are toxic and allergic responses to metal ions released by corrosion" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Galvanism (electrogalvanism) is the flow of electric current between metallic restorations, or between parts of one restoration, in the electrolyte of the mouth. It is a form of electrochemical corrosion (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Mechanism.** Two metals with different electrode potentials in saliva form a cell: the more active metal (for example amalgam) is the anode and corrodes; the more noble metal (gold) is the cathode. Saliva is the electrolyte, and the tooth tissues and fluids complete the circuit.

**Situations**
1. **Dissimilar restorations in contact:** for example amalgam opposing a gold inlay. On contact the cell is short-circuited, giving a sudden sharp pain: galvanic shock. Most patients feel pain at 20-50 µA (range 10-110 µA), so patient sensitivity matters more than voltage; the current falls rapidly if contact is maintained (polarization) (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
2. **Dissimilar restorations not in contact:** a continuous current of about 0.5-1 µA at about 500 mV. It diminishes slightly with age but persists indefinitely.
3. **Similar alloys:** their surfaces are never identical, so currents still flow.
4. **A single restoration:** a cell between saliva and dentinal/tissue fluid, whose chloride concentration is seven times higher (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Clinical effects**
- Postoperative pain, usually immediately after insertion, subsiding in a few days. The physiological state of the recently prepared tooth is the main factor; once the pulp recovers, the same current causes no pain.
- Accelerated corrosion of the anode, greater with a large cathode against a small anode, or stainless steel against amalgam.
- Mercury released from corroding amalgam weakens and discolours a gold restoration in contact with it.
- Persistent metallic taste.
- Ion release with toxic or allergic potential.

**Prevention and management**
- Avoid placing dissimilar metals in contact (amalgam against a gold crown).
- Varnish the restoration: it insulates it from saliva, so no cell forms. By the time the varnish wears away the pulp has healed. This is the most practical method. A cement base does not reliably insulate the pulp (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
- If dissimilar metals must coexist, coat the more noble metal.
- Polish restorations (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Significance.** The balance of evidence is that galvanic currents are harmful mainly as patient discomfort, and even then only rarely (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- 0.5-1 µA, about 500 mV (gold crown vs amalgam, not in contact).
- Tissue fluid has 7 times the chloride of saliva.
- Varnish = most practical prevention.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Relying on an insulating base alone (Craig's: a cement base does not effectively insulate the pulp; Bhat et al recommend one).
- Saying gold corrodes in an amalgam-gold couple (amalgam is the anode).
- Claiming galvanism needs two metals (a single restoration also generates a current).

## MNEMONIC
**"PACT"** effects: **P**ain, **A**ccelerated corrosion, **C**ontamination of gold by mercury, **T**aste.
