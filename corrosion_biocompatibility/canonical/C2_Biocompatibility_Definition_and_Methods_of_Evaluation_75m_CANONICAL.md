## 1. Definition and concept of biocompatibility

### Explanation

Craig's defines biocompatibility as "the ability of a material to elicit an appropriate biological response in a given application in the body"; the definition carries the idea that one material may be acceptable in one use and not in another — "a material that is acceptable as a full cast crown may not be acceptable as a dental implant" — and that the "appropriate" response differs (osseointegration for an implant; no pulpal or periodontal inflammation for a crown) (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Craig's compares it with colour: "biocompatibility is much like color", a property of the material interacting with its environment, so it changes if the host, the application or the material changes (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips' gives Williams' (2008) general definition: "the ability of a biomaterial to perform its desired function with respect to a medical (or dental) therapy", without undesirable local or systemic effects but generating the most appropriate beneficial cellular or tissue response (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. The two books agree in substance; Phillips' adds the functional and "beneficial response" element, Craig's stresses dependence on the application.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Every dental material is placed against living tissue: enamel and dentin, pulp, gingiva, mucosa, periapical tissue or bone. Its success therefore depends not only on strength and aesthetics but on how the tissues respond to it.

**Definition.** Biocompatibility is "the ability of a material to elicit an appropriate biological response in a given application in the body" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019). Williams (2008), quoted by Anusavice, Shen and Rawls (2013), defines it as the ability of a biomaterial to perform its desired function in a medical or dental therapy without eliciting undesirable local or systemic effects, while generating the most appropriate beneficial tissue response.

**Implications of the definition**
- Biocompatibility is not a fixed property of a material; it depends on the application. A material acceptable as a full cast crown may not be acceptable as a dental implant (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
- The "appropriate" response differs: for an implant it is osseointegration; for a crown it is the absence of pulpal and periodontal inflammation.
- Like colour, it is a property of the material interacting with its environment; it changes if the host, the use or the material changes (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

## 2. Factors that determine biocompatibility and why it is tested

### Explanation

Phillips' lists six factors: the chemical nature of the components, the physical nature of the components, the types and locations of tissues exposed, the duration of exposure, the surface characteristics of the material, and "the amount and nature of substances eluted from the material" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "The primary purpose of biocompatibility tests is to protect dental patients" and also office staff and laboratory technicians; because no material is free of risk, "the testing of biocompatibility is related to risk assessment", and the dentist chooses products whose benefits outweigh their risks (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Phillips' cites Paracelsus: "only the dose of a substance differentiates a toxic agent from a remedy" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Adverse reactions to dental materials include "allergic reaction, chemical burn, pulp irritation, pulp damage, thermal injury, tissue irritation, and toxic reaction"; diagnosis is difficult: "There are no direct tests for the diagnosis of toxicity reactions", and a patch test by an allergist adds evidence but "the results of such testing are not 100% conclusive" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "The use of a material plays a crucial role in its biocompatibility", and "very small changes in composition or processing of a material can alter its biocompatibility" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Factors that determine the biological response** (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013)
1. Chemical nature of the components.
2. Physical nature of the components.
3. Type and location of the tissues exposed (pulp, gingiva, bone, mucosa).
4. Duration of exposure.
5. Surface characteristics of the material.
6. Amount and nature of substances eluted (released) from the material.

A small change in composition or processing can alter biocompatibility, so data for one product cannot be assumed for a modified one (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Purpose of testing.** The primary purpose is to protect patients, and also dental staff and technicians who handle the materials. No material is free of risk, so testing is a form of risk assessment: the aim is to select materials whose benefits clearly outweigh their risks (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013). As Paracelsus said, only the dose separates a toxin from a remedy.

**Adverse reactions the tests try to predict:** allergic reaction, chemical burn, pulp irritation, pulp damage, thermal injury, tissue irritation and toxic reaction (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013). These are hard to diagnose in a patient: there is no direct test for toxicity, and even a patch test is not 100% conclusive. This is why materials must be screened before clinical use.

## 3. Requirements of a biocompatibility test and classification of methods

### Explanation

Phillips' states three major requirements: "the test should be performed under conditions that simulate the actual use of the material in the body", the test conditions should reflect the material's time in the body, and the stresses on the material in function should be considered; conditions should reflect whether the material contacts soft or mineralised tissue, lies outside the epithelium, is an endosseous implant, is exposed to bone, tissue fluid, blood or saliva, or is separated from cells by a barrier such as dentin (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "Three types of tests are used to analyze the biocompatibility of dental materials": in vitro, animal and usage tests; "No single test can accurately estimate the biological response to a material" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's uses the same three classes: tests "are classified as in vitro, animal, and usage tests", the last including clinical trials (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Requirements of a test** (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013)
1. Simulate the actual use of the material in the body.
2. Reflect the time the material will spend in the body.
3. Consider the stresses (physical, chemical, thermal) on the material in function.
4. Reflect the tissue contacted: soft or mineralised tissue, bone, blood, saliva, or separation by a barrier such as dentin.

**Classification of methods** (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013)

| Group | Main tests |
|---|---|
| A. In vitro tests | Cytotoxicity (membrane permeability), cell metabolism/function (MTT, NBT, XTT, WST, alamarBlue, DNA/protein synthesis), barrier tests (agar overlay, Millipore filter, dentin disk), immune-function assays, mutagenesis assays (Ames test, Styles' cell transformation) |
| B. Animal tests | Mucous membrane irritation, skin sensitization, implantation (subcutaneous, bone, muscle), systemic (acute/chronic) toxicity |
| C. Usage tests (animals or humans = clinical trials) | Dental pulp irritation test, implant-in-bone test, gingival/mucosal usage test |
| D. Combined schemes and standards | Pyramid schemes; ANSI/ADA Specification 41; ISO 10993 and ISO 7405 |

No single test can estimate the biological response; tests are used in combination (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 4. In vitro tests

### Explanation

In vitro tests place a material or a component "in contact with a cell, enzyme, or some other isolated biological system", either directly or indirectly; "Direct tests can be further subdivided into those in which the material is physically present with the cells and those in which some extract from the material contacts the cell system" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]; indirect contact "involves a barrier such as agar, a membrane filter, or dentin" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "Primary cells are those cells taken directly from an animal and cultured", grow for a limited time but keep in vivo characteristics; "Continuously grown cells or cell lines are cells that have been transformed previously" to grow indefinitely, giving consistency and standardisation; Craig's says both should be used (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

Cytotoxicity: "Cytotoxicity tests assess cell death caused by a material by measuring cell number or growth before and after exposure to that material"; "Membrane permeability tests are used to measure cytotoxicity by the ease with which a dye can pass through a cell membrane" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Cell metabolism or function: DNA or protein synthesis; "A commonly used enzymatic test for cytotoxicity is the" MTT test, with NBT, XTT and WST, "all being colorimetric assays based on different tetrazolium salts"; "alamarBlue tests quantitatively measure cell proliferation using a fluorescent indicator" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

Barrier (indirect) tests: "an agar overlay method, which uses agar to form a barrier between the cells and the material" — cells pre-stained with neutral red are covered with agar; a cytotoxic sample releases the dye, "leaving a zone of inhibition"; "the Millipore filter assay, in which a monolayer of cells is grown on a filter" that is turned over so leachables diffuse through it; both "can provide, at best, a qualitative cytotoxic ranking among materials"; dentin disks are used because dentin is a barrier, "with the thickness of the dentin directly correlating with the protection offered to the pulp" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Other assays "measure cytokine production by lymphocytes and macrophages", lymphocyte proliferation, chemotaxis, or T-cell rosetting (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

Mutagenesis: "Genotoxic mutagens directly alter cell DNA"; "Epigenetic mutagens do not alter the DNA themselves" but support tumour growth; "Mutagens may or may not be carcinogens, and carcinogens may or may not be mutagens"; "The Ames test is the most widely used short-term mutagenesis test and the only one that is considered thoroughly validated", testing reversion of "a mutant stock of Salmonella typhimurium back to a native strain"; the Styles' "cell transformation test. This test on mammalian cells offers an alternative to bacterial tests" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips' notes that since the 1980s "testing has focused on primary tests for cytotoxicity, hemolysis", Styles' cell transformation, the Ames test, the dominant lethal response and LD50 tests (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

Advantages and disadvantages: "They are relatively quick, generally cost less than animal or usage tests", can be standardised, suit large-scale screening and are well controlled; "The overriding disadvantage of in vitro tests is their questionable relevance to the final in vivo use of the material", with no inflammatory or tissue-protective mechanisms (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips' example: "a test that measures cytotoxicity using osteoblast cell cultures may be somewhat irrelevant if the material never contacts osteoblasts in vivo" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** The material, or an extract of it, is placed in contact with cells, enzymes or another isolated biological system outside the body (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
- **Direct contact:** the material itself, or an extract of it, contacts the cells.
- **Indirect contact:** a barrier such as agar, a membrane filter or dentin lies between material and cells (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).
- **Cells used:** primary cells (taken directly from an animal; closer to in vivo behaviour but short-lived and variable) and continuous cell lines (transformed cells that grow indefinitely; consistent and easy to standardise). Both are used (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**1. Cytotoxicity tests.** Measure cell death by counting cells or measuring growth before and after exposure. Membrane permeability tests measure how easily a dye passes through the cell membrane; increased permeability is nearly equivalent to cell death (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**2. Tests of cell metabolism or function.** Measure DNA or protein synthesis, or enzyme activity. The MTT test is the common enzymatic test; NBT, XTT and WST are similar colorimetric assays based on tetrazolium salts; alamarBlue uses a fluorescent indicator to follow cell proliferation continuously (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**3. Barrier (indirect) tests** — mimic the in vivo situation where cells are not in direct contact with the material:
- **Agar overlay:** a cell layer stained with neutral red is covered with agar; the sample is placed on the agar. A cytotoxic material injures the cells, the dye is released and a zone of inhibition (decolorisation) forms around the sample.
- **Millipore filter assay:** cells are grown on a filter, which is turned over; the material is placed on the filter and leachables diffuse through it to the cells.
- Both give, at best, a qualitative ranking of cytotoxicity.
- **Dentin disk (dentin barrier) test:** a dentin disk separates the material from the culture medium or cells, so components must diffuse through dentin as they would to reach the pulp. The thickness of dentin correlates directly with the protection it offers (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**4. Other cell-function assays.** Cytokine production by lymphocytes and macrophages, lymphocyte proliferation, chemotaxis, T-cell rosetting, cell cycle and complement activation. Their in vivo meaning is not yet established, but they may reduce animal testing (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**5. Mutagenesis assays.** Genotoxic mutagens alter DNA directly; epigenetic mutagens act without altering DNA. Mutagens are not necessarily carcinogens, and vice versa.
- **Ames test:** the most widely used short-term mutagenesis test and the only one considered thoroughly validated. It measures reversion of a mutant strain of *Salmonella typhimurium* to the native strain; chemicals that increase reversion are likely carcinogens.
- **Styles' cell transformation test:** uses mammalian cells, an alternative to the bacterial Ames test (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
- Hemolysis (red cell membrane lysis) is also a primary in vitro test (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Advantages:** quick, least expensive, standardisable, suitable for large-scale screening, well controlled, good for studying mechanisms.
**Disadvantages:** relevance to in vivo use is questionable; there are no inflammatory, immune or circulatory systems; for example, an osteoblast culture is irrelevant if the material never contacts osteoblasts (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 5. Animal tests

### Explanation

In animal tests "the material is not placed in the animal with regard to its final use", which distinguishes them from usage tests; they allow interactions with a complete biological system and are more comprehensive, but are difficult to interpret and control, expensive, slow and raise ethical concerns (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. "Test animals include baboons, cats, dogs, ferrets, guinea pigs, hamsters, mice, monkeys, pigs, rats, and sheep"; in acute or chronic toxicity tests "the materials are ground and fed to the animals", which is unrealistic, so these tests are being discontinued; guinea pig tests evaluate sensitisation (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "The mucous membrane irritation test determines whether a material causes inflammation to mucous membranes or abraded skin" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]; Phillips' places it "in hamsters' cheek pouches" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. In skin sensitisation "materials are injected intradermally to test for development of skin hypersensitivity reactions", followed by patches; "Implantation tests are used to evaluate materials that will contact subcutaneous tissue or bone" (connective tissue, bone or muscle), mainly for implant, endodontic and periodontal materials (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** The material is placed in a living animal (mouse, rat, hamster, guinea pig and others), but not in the way it will be used clinically (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Tests**
1. **Mucous membrane irritation test:** whether the material inflames mucous membrane or abraded skin, for example in the hamster cheek pouch.
2. **Skin sensitization test:** the material is injected intradermally (classically in guinea pigs), then patches of the test substance are applied to see whether a hypersensitivity reaction develops.
3. **Implantation tests:** the material is implanted in subcutaneous connective tissue, bone or muscle and the tissue reaction is examined histologically at intervals; used for implant, endodontic and periodontal materials.
4. **Systemic toxicity tests:** acute (for example oral LD50) or chronic, with the material ground and fed to the animal. Because such ingestion is unrealistic, these tests are being discontinued (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Advantages:** an intact biological system (immune, inflammatory, circulatory), so responses are more comprehensive and more relevant than in vitro.
**Disadvantages:** expensive, time-consuming, difficult to control and interpret; ethical and legal concerns; relevance to human use, and the choice of species, are often unclear (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

## 6. Usage tests (including clinical trials)

### Explanation

Usage tests "require that the material be placed in a situation identical to its intended clinical use"; their value depends on how faithfully they mimic use, so animal usage tests "usually employ larger animals that have similar oral environments to humans, such as dogs, mini-swine, or monkeys"; "These tests are the gold standard" but are very expensive, long, legally and ethically difficult, hard to control and may harm participants (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. "Usage tests are the most clinically relevant tests"; "When a usage test is performed in humans, it is called a clinical trial" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Pulp: "materials to be tested on the dental pulp are placed in class 5 cavity preparations in intact teeth without caries", then the teeth are extracted and sectioned and necrosis and inflammation graded; concern that inflamed pulps react differently has led to "usage tests on teeth with induced pulpitis" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips' adds that human pulp studies use "teeth that are scheduled for extraction because of orthodontic reasons are normally obtained form young patients" with open apices, who may react differently from older patients; clinical trials need approval "from an institutional review board", and long-term tests "may take 5 years or more" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Implants: success is graded as "early implant success for implants surviving 1 to 3 years", intermediate (3 to 7 years) and long-term (more than 7 years), and predicted by "(1) penetration of a periodontal probe along the side of the implant, (2) mobility of the implant" and (3) radiographs; "Any fibrous capsule formation is a sign of irritation and chronic inflammation" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Gingiva and mucosa: "placement in cavity preparations with subgingival extensions"; "responses are categorized as slight, moderate, or severe" by the number of mononuclear inflammatory cells, confounded by plaque, roughness, overhangs and contour (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Principle.** The material is placed exactly as in its intended clinical use, in larger animals with an oral environment similar to that of humans (dogs, mini-swine, monkeys) or in humans, where it is called a clinical trial (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013). Usage tests are the gold standard and the most clinically relevant.

**1. Dental pulp irritation test.** The material is placed in Class V cavity preparations in intact, caries-free teeth (in humans, often teeth to be extracted for orthodontic reasons). After set periods the teeth are extracted, sectioned and examined microscopically, and necrosis and inflammation are graded by intensity; reparative dentin is also assessed. Tests on teeth with induced pulpitis are being developed, because inflamed pulps may react differently. Orthodontic teeth come from young patients with open apices and may not reflect older or carious teeth (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**2. Dental implants in bone.** Success is graded as early (1-3 years), intermediate (3-7 years) and long-term (more than 7 years). It is judged by:
- probe penetration along the implant;
- implant mobility;
- radiographs showing osseous integration or peri-implant radiolucency.

A fibrous capsule indicates irritation and chronic inflammation and leads to loosening (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**3. Mucosa and gingival usage test.** The material is placed in cavity preparations with subgingival extensions. The gingival response is graded slight, moderate or severe by the number of mononuclear inflammatory cells. It is confounded by pre-existing plaque-induced inflammation, surface roughness, overhangs and contour errors (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**Advantages:** the relevance to clinical use is assured.
**Disadvantages:** very expensive and time-consuming (long-term trials take 5 years or more); major ethical and legal issues; ethics committee (institutional review board) approval is required for humans; difficult to control, interpret and analyse statistically; may harm participants; often no histology in human trials (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 7. Correlation among the tests: the dentin barrier

### Explanation

Craig's explains why in vitro, animal and usage tests disagree: they measure different aspects, and barriers present in use may be absent in vitro; "The dentin barrier, although possibly only a fraction of a millimeter thick, is effective in modulating the toxic effect of a dental material" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. In the classic comparison (Mjör et al, 1977, as reported in Craig's Table 6.2), four cell-culture tests ranked ZOE severe, composite moderate and silicate slight; but in Class V usage tests in monkeys "The silicate was found to have the most severe inflammatory reaction", composite moderate-to-slight, and "the ZOE had little or no effect". The explanation: "The silicate cement released hydrogen ions" that were buffered in culture but not by dentin, plus microleakage; eugenol and zinc from ZOE, and resin monomers, were diluted by the dentin diffusion gradient, and ZOE killed bacteria and reduced microleakage (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips' agrees that there "is the relatively poor correlation among these tests" and that in vitro and animal tests do not "necessarily predict the results of usage tests or successful clinical experience with the material", though correlation improves under simulated clinical conditions (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

In vitro, animal and usage tests often do not agree, because each measures a different aspect of the response and barriers present in the mouth may be absent in the laboratory (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Classic example (Mjör et al, 1977, reported by Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019)**

| Material | Cell culture | Implantation (90 days) | Pulp usage test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silicate cement | Slight | Slight | Severe |
| Resin composite | Moderate | Moderate | Moderate to slight |
| ZOE | Severe | Slight | Little or none |

**Explanation:** silicate releases hydrogen ions, which were buffered in culture but not by dentin, and microleakage added bacterial irritation. Eugenol, zinc and resin monomers were diluted by diffusion through dentin, and ZOE also killed bacteria and sealed the cavity. The dentin barrier, even a fraction of a millimetre thick, modulates toxicity. This is why barrier (dentin disk) tests were developed.

## 8. Using the tests together: testing schemes

### Explanation

Craig's states that "the most accurate and cost-effective means to assess biocompatibility of a new material is a combination of in vitro, animal, and usage tests". "Early combination schemes proposed a pyramid testing protocol": unspecific toxicity tests at the base, specific toxicity tests next, and clinical trials at the top, with materials weeded out at each level; a later pyramid used initial (primary), secondary and usage tests and added immunogenicity, mutagenicity and animal usage tests. Newer schemes keep all tests in use throughout development and after marketing and recognise that "assessing the biocompatibility of a material is an ongoing process" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips' describes "Three test phases have been distinguished in the testing of a new biomaterial: primary, secondary, and usage tests", run as a roughly linear progression (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

No single test is adequate, so a combination of in vitro, animal and usage tests is used. This is the most accurate and cost-effective approach (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

1. **Early pyramid scheme:** unspecific toxicity tests (base) → specific toxicity tests → clinical trials (apex). Materials are "weeded out" at each level, so fewer, safer materials reach expensive clinical trials.
2. **Contemporary pyramid** (used in most standards): **primary** tests (mainly in vitro: cytotoxicity, hemolysis, mutagenicity, plus acute systemic toxicity) → **secondary** tests (in animals: irritation, sensitization, implantation, chronic toxicity) → **usage** tests (animals, then humans) (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).
3. **Newer schemes:** primary and secondary tests continue to be used during, and even after, clinical use; testing goes back and forth between levels, and biocompatibility assessment is an ongoing process (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

## 9. Post-marketing evaluation: adverse-event registries

### Explanation

Phillips' advises the clinician to check adverse-event registries: "A search of the websites in Norway, Sweden, and the United Kingdom" shows the adverse effects reported for each type of material, followed by a PubMed or Web of Science search and enquiry with national agencies; the shorter a product's time on the market, the fewer reports there will be (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Evaluation continues after a material is marketed. National adverse-reaction registries (Norway, Sweden, the United Kingdom), literature searches and reports to national agencies show the reactions recorded with a product. A newer product has had less time to accumulate reports, so it deserves more caution (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 10. Standards that regulate biocompatibility testing

### Explanation

Craig's dates the ADA's first efforts on dental-material guidelines to 1926: they "came in 1926 when scientists at the National Bureau of Standards" developed specifications for amalgam (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Specification No. 41 "was approved in 1972 by the ADA Council on Dental Materials, Instruments, and Equipment", and an update "was published in 1982 to include tests for mutagenicity" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; "it was further updated in 2005" to conform to ISO 10993 (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Its initial tests include in vitro cytotoxicity, "red blood cell membrane lysis (hemolysis), mutagenesis and carcinogenesis at the cellular level", and acute systemic toxicity in vivo; secondary tests in small animals include "dermal irritation, subcutaneous and bony implantation, and hypersensitivity tests"; usage tests follow in larger animals and humans; the 1982 addendum added the Ames and Styles' tests (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. The FDA classifies dental materials as devices, so "Dental devices need to meet only safety test requirements" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

ISO 10993: international groups worked "to develop standard ISO 10993, published in 1992"; its dental revision is ISO 7405:2008, "Preclinical evaluation of biocompatibility of medical devices used in dentistry" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. "ISO 10993 is not restricted to dental materials"; "In 2010, ISO Standard 10993 consisted of 20 parts"; "Part 3 specifies tests for genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and reproductive toxicity", "Part 4 describes tests for materials that interact with blood" and "Part 20 specifies the principles and methods for immunotoxicology testing"; "The standard identifies initial tests for cytotoxicity, sensitization, systemic toxicity, and supplementary tests for chronic toxicity, carcinogenicity, and biodegradation" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Test selection is "based on how long the material will be present; whether it will contact body surface only, blood, or bone", and whether it communicates externally, but "the ultimate selection of tests for a specific material is left up to the manufacturer" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]; "the manufacturer is fully responsible legally for any adverse effects" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. ISO 7405 describes "all usage tests" and dental-specific cell tests "such as the dentin barrier test" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Standards are needed for unbiased comparison, but "they tend to be developed quite slowly", lag behind new science and use thresholds that are not universally accepted (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. The current number of ISO 10993 parts beyond the 2010 figure was not checked in this session.

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. ANSI/ADA Specification No. 41** ("Recommended Standard Practices for Biological Evaluation of Dental Materials")
- Approved in 1972 by the ADA Council on Dental Materials, Instruments and Equipment. An addendum in 1982 added mutagenicity tests (Ames and Styles'). Revised in 2005 to conform to ISO 10993 (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013; Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
- **Initial tests:** in vitro cytotoxicity, hemolysis, mutagenesis and carcinogenesis at the cellular level; acute systemic toxicity in animals.
- **Secondary tests** (small animals): dermal irritation, subcutaneous and bony implantation, hypersensitivity.
- **Usage tests:** in larger animals (often primates), then in humans with FDA approval: pulp and bone response.

**2. ISO 10993** ("Biological evaluation of medical devices")
- Published in 1992; covers all medical devices, not only dental materials. In 2010 it had 20 parts, for example Part 3 (genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, reproductive toxicity), Part 4 (interaction with blood) and Part 20 (immunotoxicology) (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).
- **Initial tests:** cytotoxicity, sensitization, systemic toxicity.
- **Supplementary tests:** chronic toxicity, carcinogenicity, biodegradation (mostly in animals, many in usage situations).
- **Test selection** (Part 1) depends on duration of contact, the tissue contacted (body surface, blood, bone) and whether the device communicates externally. The final choice, and the legal responsibility, rest with the manufacturer (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**3. ISO 7405:2008** ("Preclinical evaluation of biocompatibility of medical devices used in dentistry — test methods for dental materials"): the dental companion to ISO 10993. It describes the usage tests (pulp, implant, mucosa) and dental-specific cell tests such as the dentin barrier test (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Limitations of standards:** they develop slowly by consensus, lag behind new science, and set arbitrary thresholds. Dental materials are regulated as devices, so only safety (not efficacy) testing is required (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 11. Effects of dental cements on the pulp

### Explanation

Microleakage: early studies attributed pulp irritation to materials, but later work suggested "the products of microleakage, not the restorative materials, caused the irritation"; in a classic monkey study (amalgam, composite, zinc phosphate and silicate placed directly on pulp, half surface-sealed with ZOE), after 21 days "the sealed restorations showed less pulpal irritation than those not sealed"; "Only zinc phosphate cement elicited a long-term inflammatory response", sealed teeth showed more dentin bridging and "Only amalgam seemed to prevent bridging" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. For cast restorations "the pulp is more likely to be affected by the cement retaining the restoration" than by the alloy (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

Zinc phosphate: "zinc phosphate cement elicits strong-to-moderate cytotoxic reactions that decrease with time" (zinc ions and low pH); in deep cavities it causes moderate-to-severe pulpal damage within 3 days, "probably because of the initial low pH (4.2 at 3 minutes)"; "the pH of the set cement approaches neutrality after 48 hours"; "By 5 to 8 weeks, only mild chronic inflammation is present, and reparative dentin has usually formed"; therefore "the placement of a protective layer of a dentin-bonding agent, ZOE, varnish, or calcium hydroxide, is recommended" when little dentin remains (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

Polycarboxylate: these cements "were developed as biocompatible and cements chemically adhesive to tooth structure"; in culture, cytotoxicity correlates with zinc and fluoride release and low pH, possibly an artefact because "cell growth inhibition can be reversed if EDTA, which chelates zinc, is added to the culture medium"; "concentrations of polyacrylic acid above 1% appear to be cytotoxic in tissue culture tests", but implantation over a year shows no long-term cytotoxicity; "Polyacrylate cements evoke a pulpal response similar to that caused by ZOE" (slight-to-moderate at 3 days, mild chronic at 5 weeks), with minimal reparative dentin, so "they are recommended only in cavities with intact dentin in the floors" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

ZOE: in vitro "eugenol from ZOE fixes cells, depresses cell respiration, and reduces nerve transmission with direct contact", yet it is relatively innocuous in Class V usage tests because "diffusion through dentin dilutes eugenol by several orders of magnitude" — about 10⁻² M "(bactericidal), the concentration on the pulpal side of the dentin may be" 10⁻⁴ M or less, which suppresses nerve transmission and "inhibits synthesis of prostaglandins and leukotrienes (antiinflammatory)"; it also seals against bacteria; "it has been used as a negative control substance for comparison with restorative procedures in usage tests" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

Glass ionomer: "freshly prepared ionomer is mildly cytotoxic, but this effect is reduced over time" (fluoride implicated in vitro); pulpal compatibility is attributed to the weak, high-molecular-weight polyacrylic acid — "polyacrylic acid is unable to diffuse through dentin due to its large size"; "any inflammatory infiltrate from ionomer is minimal or absent after 1 month"; there are reports of "pulpal hyperalgesia for short periods (days) after placing glass ionomers in cervical cavities" (probably increased permeability after etching); and "glass ionomer has not been shown to be well tolerated when placed directly upon living pulp tissue" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

Calcium hydroxide: pastes have "a very alkaline pH (>12)", are extremely cytotoxic in screening tests, and cause "necrosis to a depth of 1 mm or more" on exposed pulp; neutrophils infiltrate, inflammation is slight after 5-8 weeks, and "the necrotic zone undergoes dystrophic calcification, which appears to be a stimulus for dentin bridge formation"; resin-containing versions form bridges faster — "Significantly, this occurs with no zone of necrosis" — but may break down with time; calcium hydroxide also releases growth factors such as "transforming growth factor-beta-1" from dentin, and "the main soluble component from MTA has been shown to be calcium hydroxide" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Varnishes give no thermal insulation and their seal is unreliable "because of the thinness of the film and formation of pinpoint holes" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**General principle.** Pulpal reaction under a cement depends on the cement's chemistry (pH, released ions), the remaining dentin thickness, and above all microleakage of bacteria. In a classic monkey study, restorations surface-sealed with ZOE caused less pulpal irritation and more dentin bridging than unsealed ones. Only zinc phosphate caused long-term inflammation, and only amalgam prevented bridging (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019). Under cast restorations it is the luting cement, not the alloy, that mainly affects the pulp.

**Conclusion.** Biocompatibility is application-specific and cannot be proved by one test. It is evaluated by a sequence of in vitro, animal and usage tests, interpreted together and continued after marketing, under ANSI/ADA Specification 41, ISO 10993 and ISO 7405. For cements, the dentin barrier and the control of microleakage decide the pulpal outcome as much as the chemistry of the cement.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim:** in vitro cytotoxicity predicts how a material will behave on the pulp.
- **Textbook position:** both books present in vitro tests as primary screening steps (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013; Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
- **Challenge:** the Mjör et al (1977) comparison, as reported by Craig's, reversed the ranking: ZOE was the most cytotoxic in culture and the mildest in pulp usage tests, while silicate was the reverse. Phillips' states the correlation among tests is "relatively poor", improving only under simulated clinical conditions.
- **Refinement:** Craig's argues that disagreement is expected, because each test measures a different aspect and the dentin barrier and microleakage are absent in culture. Barrier tests (dentin disk, now in ISO 7405) were the response.
- **Strength:** one classic study, reported second-hand (the original was not read); a consistent mechanism (buffering, dilution, bacterial leakage). [Likely]
- **Conclusion:** in vitro results identify hazards; they do not predict clinical pulp response unless the test includes a dentin barrier, and usage tests remain the reference. [Likely]

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Definition (Craig's): "appropriate biological response in a given application".
- Three test types: in vitro, animal, usage. Usage tests are the gold standard; in humans they are clinical trials.
- Ames test: the only thoroughly validated short-term mutagenesis test (*Salmonella typhimurium* reversion).
- Agar overlay (neutral red, zone of decolorisation), Millipore filter, dentin disk = barrier tests.
- ADA Specification 41: 1972, addendum 1982 (mutagenicity), revised 2005. ISO 10993: 1992, 20 parts in 2010. ISO 7405:2008 is the dental standard.
- ZOE: the negative control in pulp usage tests. Zinc phosphate: pH 4.2 at 3 minutes. Calcium hydroxide: pH above 12, necrosis 1 mm or more.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Calling a material "biocompatible" in absolute terms, without naming the application.
- Treating animal implantation tests as usage tests (an implantation test does not place the material as it is used clinically).
- Saying in vitro results predict clinical results (the ZOE/silicate reversal shows they do not).
- Confusing ISO 10993 (all medical devices) with ISO 7405 (dental test methods).
- Forgetting microleakage when discussing pulpal response to cements.

## MNEMONIC
**"VAU — Very Accurate Usage"**: in **V**itro → **A**nimal → **U**sage (the pyramid, in order).
**Cements on the pulp, "Phosphate Pains, Eugenol Eases"**: zinc phosphate is acid and initially damaging; ZOE is soothing and the negative control.
