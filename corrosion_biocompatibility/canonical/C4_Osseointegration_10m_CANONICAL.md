## Explanation

Phillips' defines osseointegration as "The process of forming a direct structural and functional interface between live bone and an artificial implant surface without any intervening fibrous connective tissue" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's: "Osseointegration is formally defined as the close approximation of bone to an implant material"; the bone must be viable, "the space between the bone and implant must be less than 10 nm and contain no fibrous tissue", the interface must survive loading, and "osseointegration is an absolute requirement for the" successful implant prosthesis (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha: it "occurs when the bone grows to within 100 A" of titanium, where "there is only extracellular matrix and no collagen or fibrous tissue" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]; Craig's 11th edition states the gap "must be less than 100 A and contain no fibrous tissue" (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC], the same 10 nm. History: a key step "was the clinical research led by P. I. Brånemark in Sweden" — "His studies utilized unalloyed titanium, a root-form design" under controlled surgical conditions; osseointegration "is characterized by direct contact between bone and the surface of a functional implant after one year", the Brånemark group's "direct anchorage to bone" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Biointegration is "The process of forming an interface between bone or other living tissue and an implanted material with no intervening space" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; with ceramic coatings "the interface is called biointegration rather than osseointegration, and there is no intervening space between the bone and the implant" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]; "In both osseointegration and biointegration, the supporting bone must remain vital" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC].

Materials: "commercially pure titanium, titanium-aluminum-vanadium alloy, tantalum, and several ceramic materials such as zirconia" permit it (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. "Titanium alloys are well tolerated by the body because of their passive oxide layers"; "titanium repassivates almost instantaneously", and the inert dissolution products allow bone to osseointegrate, though "even in its passive condition, titanium is not inert"; "in less than a second the surface forms a thin conformal layer of various titanium oxides"; "implants have been maintained in healthy tissue for longer than three decades"; "there is no evidence that released titanium ions are a problem locally or systemically", but "questions remain about the liability of released aluminum and vanadium from alloys" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. The old idea that a fibrous capsule was an acceptable ligament-like attachment was dropped because "in most cases it resembled the wall of a cyst" (Craig and Powers, 2002) [SRC]. Requirements: "The bone must be prepared in a way that does not cause necrosis or inflammation"; "The implant must be allowed to heal for a time without a load"; "not all materials will promote osseointegration"; in dense bone "overheating of the bone while drilling is a major cause of future failure" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]; it is "achieved only if the surgical technique is atraumatic" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Surfaces: around screws it "represents a situation of bone ongrowth"; interfacial shear strength rises in the order "smooth, textured, screw threaded, plasma sprayed, and porous coated, porous body design"; a thin calcium-phosphate "layer of ceramic materials to coat the surface of metallic implants" aids integration, but the ceramic-metal "bond fails before the ceramic-tissue bond and is the weak link in the system" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Clinically "it feels similar to the pathologic condition of natural tooth ankylosis" and "tapping the implant with an instrument elicits a high-pitched, sharp sound"; with no periodontal ligament, "occlusal loads are transmitted to the bone mostly by compressive forces", whereas around teeth "These tensile forces stimulate bone deposition" (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Assessment: "(1) penetration of a periodontal probe along the side of the implant, (2) mobility of the implant", and radiographs; success is graded as "early implant success for implants surviving 1 to 3 years", "intermediate implant success for implants surviving 3 to 7 years, and long-term success for implants surviving more than 7 years"; "Any fibrous capsule formation is a sign of irritation and chronic inflammation"; implants "are successful in about 95% of cases, 5 years after implantation"; failures include "early loosening from a lack of initial osseointegration", and "The most common failure mechanism is alveolar crest resorption due to overloading" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC].

## ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Osseointegration is the formation of a direct structural and functional interface between living bone and an implant surface without any intervening fibrous connective tissue (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013). The bone must be viable, the gap must be less than 10 nm (100 Å) and contain only extracellular matrix with no collagen or fibrous tissue, and the interface must withstand loading (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019; Powers and Wataha, 2013). It is an absolute requirement for a successful implant prosthesis. The concept came from Brånemark's clinical research in Sweden with unalloyed titanium root-form implants: "direct anchorage to bone" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Osseointegration versus biointegration**

| | Osseointegration | Biointegration |
|---|---|---|
| Interface | Close approximation (gap under 10 nm) | Bone fused with the surface, no space |
| Material | Titanium and its alloys | Ceramics (hydroxyapatite-coated implants, zirconia) |

In both, the supporting bone must remain vital.

**Materials that permit it:** commercially pure titanium, Ti-6Al-4V, tantalum and ceramics such as zirconia (Powers and Wataha, 2013). Titanium works because its oxide layer forms within a second, repassivates almost instantaneously and releases nearly inert products. Titanium ions are not known to cause problems, but aluminium and vanadium release from Ti-6Al-4V is still questioned (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**Requirements**
1. A biocompatible material.
2. Atraumatic surgery that causes no necrosis or inflammation; overheating the bone while drilling is a major cause of failure.
3. Unloaded healing period.
4. Suitable surface: osseointegration around threads is bone ongrowth; shear strength rises from smooth to textured, threaded, plasma-sprayed and porous surfaces. Calcium-phosphate coatings aid integration, but the coating-metal bond is the weak link.

**Clinical signs:** no mobility (like ankylosis); a high-pitched sound on tapping; no peri-implant radiolucency; no deep probing. A fibrous capsule resembles a cyst wall: chronic inflammation, then loosening (Craig and Powers, 2002).

**Grading of success:** early (implant surviving 1-3 years), intermediate (3-7 years), long-term (more than 7 years) (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

**Biomechanics:** around a natural tooth, the periodontal ligament turns occlusal load into tension, which stimulates bone deposition. An osseointegrated implant has no ligament, so loads reach bone as compression, which favours resorption; implant prostheses must limit compressive stress (Powers and Wataha, 2013).

**Prognosis:** about 95% success at 5 years. Failure: early loosening (no initial integration), late loss of integration, or crestal resorption from overloading, the commonest (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Gap under 10 nm (100 Å), no fibrous tissue, vital bone, load-bearing.
- Titanium: passive TiO₂ film, repassivates instantly.
- Most common failure: crestal resorption from overload.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Describing a fibrous-tissue interface ("fibro-osseous integration") as acceptable; it is a sign of failure.
- Confusing osseointegration with biointegration.

## MNEMONIC
**"BASIC"**: **B**iocompatible material, **A**traumatic surgery, **S**urface design, **I**mmobile healing (no load), **C**lose contact (under 10 nm).
