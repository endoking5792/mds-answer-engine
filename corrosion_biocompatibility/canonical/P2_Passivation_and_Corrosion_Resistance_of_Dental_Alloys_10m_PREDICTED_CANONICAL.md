## Explanation

"A highly effective protection utilizes certain metals that develop a thin, adherent, highly protective film by reaction with the environment; such a metal is said to be passive"; chromium is the example, and "stainless steel, which contains sufficient amounts of chromium added to iron" is the commonest passivated alloy; but "tensile stress and certain ions, such as chloride ions, can disrupt the protective oxide film", making chromium-passivated alloys liable to stress and pitting corrosion, so "patients should be warned against using household bleaches" on partial denture frameworks and removable orthodontic appliances (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "Titanium (Ti) also forms a passivating titanium oxide film", the basis of its use in cast restorations, wires, endodontic instruments and implants; still, "The combination of stress, corrosion, and bacteria contributes to implant failure" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's: "Titanium alloys are well tolerated by the body because of their passive oxide layers", "titanium repassivates almost instantaneously", yet "even in its passive condition, titanium is not inert" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. van Noort explains why a stable oxide protects: when the oxide is stable, growth slows and "this gives the parabolic weight-gain curve" (self-limiting oxidation), whereas an unstable, cracking oxide keeps growing; "These forms of oxidation are described as dry corrosion"; a local "break in the surface oxide coating, which is known as pitting corrosion" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. Other routes to corrosion resistance (Phillips'): noble metals ("Noble metals include gold, platinum, palladium, iridium, rhodium, osmium, and ruthenium"); "at least half the atoms should be noble metals (gold, platinum, and palladium)"; "Palladium has been found to be effective in reducing the susceptibility to sulfide tarnishing for alloys containing silver"; pure metals "corrode at much slower rates than alloys do"; "any cored structure is less resistant to corrosion than is the homogenized solid solution"; coatings on gold alloys failed because they "were too thin, (2) were incomplete", did not adhere, scratched or were attacked; a non-conductive film is useful only "if it is applied to the more noble metal"; and "all metallic dental restorative materials should be polished"; corrosion-prone metals "may also be electroplated with nickel followed by chromium for corrosion protection and esthetic reasons", but if a gold coating is scratched through, "the base metal will be corroded at a very rapid rate because concentration cells have been created and two dissimilar metals are in direct contact" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's notes, for implants, "the possibility of the ceramic functioning as a barrier to metallic corrosion products" when calcium phosphate is coated onto metal (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. McCabe and Walls explain the chromium paradox: chromium is electrochemically active, yet it protects by "forming a layer of chromic oxide which protects the metal or alloy from further decomposition"; and "many manufacturers of alloys carry out homogenization heat treatments" (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. On titanium, "in less than a second the surface forms a thin conformal layer of various titanium oxides" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Bhat et al agree that "Base metals should not come into contact with hypochlorite solution" (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019) [SRC].

**Conflict — gold electroplating.** Bhat et al: "Electroplating the alloys with gold or noble metals prevents inhomogeneous surface corrosions" (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019) [SRC]. Phillips': coatings on dental alloys have been generally ineffective, and once a gold coating is scratched through, the base metal corrodes very rapidly (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Both can be true: a coating works only while it is intact. Do not present it as reliable protection. [Likely]

## ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Passivation is the formation, by reaction with the environment, of a thin, adherent, highly protective surface film (usually an oxide) that stops further corrosion. Such a metal is said to be passive (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Mechanism.** A stable, adherent oxide slows the diffusion of metal ions, so oxidation becomes self-limiting (a parabolic weight-gain curve). An unstable oxide cracks and keeps growing. A local break in a passive film causes pitting corrosion (van Noort, 2013).

**Examples**
1. **Chromium:** although electrochemically active, chromium forms an adherent chromic oxide film, so stainless steel (iron with enough chromium) is passive (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Iron and steel can also be electroplated with nickel and then chromium for corrosion protection (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).
2. **Titanium and its alloys:** an oxide film forms within a second and repassivates almost instantaneously when damaged. This underlies their use in implants, cast restorations, orthodontic wires and endodontic instruments, and their osseointegration (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019). Titanium is still not inert: ions are released by dissolution of the oxide.

**Breakdown of passivity**
- Chloride ions and tensile stress disrupt the film, causing pitting and stress corrosion.
- Household bleach must not be used to clean chromium-alloyed partial denture frameworks or removable orthodontic appliances.
- Stress, corrosion and bacteria together contribute to implant failure (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Other ways to make alloys corrosion resistant** (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013)
- **Noble metal content:** at least half the atoms gold, platinum or palladium; palladium reduces sulfide tarnish of silver-containing alloys.
- **Homogeneity:** a single-phase, homogenized solid solution resists better than cored or multiphase alloys, hence homogenization heat treatments (McCabe and Walls, 2008); pure metals corrode more slowly than alloys.
- **Coatings:** largely ineffective on gold alloys (too thin, incomplete, poorly adherent, scratched). A scratched gold coating on a base metal is worse than none: the exposed base metal corrodes very rapidly (Bhat et al recommend gold electroplating; it protects only while intact). A non-conductive film helps only on the more noble metal of a couple. On implants, a calcium-phosphate coating may act as a barrier to metallic corrosion products (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019).
- **Surface finish:** polish all metallic restorations.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Passive film = thin, adherent, protective oxide (chromium-rich on stainless steel, titanium oxide on titanium).
- Chloride ions and tensile stress break passivity: no household bleach on chromium-alloyed appliances.
- Titanium repassivates almost instantly but is not inert.

- Saying passive metals do not corrode at all (the film can break down).
- Recommending bleach for cleaning metal-framed dentures.
