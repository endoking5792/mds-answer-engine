## 1. Definitions: tarnish versus corrosion

### Explanation

"Tarnish is a surface discoloration on a metal or a slight loss or alteration of the surface finish or luster"; in the mouth it comes from deposits on the restoration and from thin films: "Tarnish also arises from the formation of thin films, such as oxides, sulfides, or chlorides"; the film may even be protective, but "it is often an early indication and precurser of corrosion" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "Corrosion is a process whereby deterioration of a metal is caused by reaction with its environment", and even highly localised corrosion can cause mechanical failure with little material lost (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha put the difference by depth, for amalgam: "With tarnish, the chemical reaction between the amalgam and the oral cavity is restricted to the amalgam surface", whereas "Corrosion results from chemical reactions that penetrate into the body of the amalgam"; "Although tarnish may be undesirable esthetically, it will not often cause the restoration to fail", while corrosion eventually does (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Phillips' agrees: silver-sulfide tarnish on amalgam "does not affect or change the mechanical properties of the amalgam", while corrosion does (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Metals corrode because "most commonly used metals and alloys are not in their lowest energy state"; gold is the exception, and "Ceramics, on the other hand, do not corrode because they are already fully oxidized" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Tarnish:** a surface discoloration or slight loss of lustre of a metal, caused by surface deposits or thin films of oxides, sulfides or chlorides. It is limited to the surface and is mainly aesthetic, but it is often the forerunner of corrosion (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013; Powers and Wataha, 2013).

**Corrosion:** deterioration of a metal by reaction with its environment. It penetrates into the body of the material, weakens it, releases ions and can cause failure (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Cause:** metals are not in their lowest energy state and revert to oxides or sulfides; ceramics, already oxidized, do not corrode (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 2. Why the mouth promotes tarnish and corrosion

### Explanation

The oral environment "is warm, moist, and subjected to wide fluctuations in temperature and pH"; "eggs and certain other foods contain significant amounts of sulfur", and sulfides tarnish silver, copper, tin and mercury; "water, oxygen, and chlorine ions are present in saliva and contribute to the corrosive attack", and acids "such as phosphoric, acetic, and lactic acids" promote corrosion (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Oral factors:** warmth, moisture, swings of temperature and pH, dietary sulfur (eggs), salivary chlorides and oxygen, food acids, plaque (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 3. Classification and mechanism of corrosion

### Explanation

"Chemical corrosion is the direct combination of metallic and nonmetallic elements to yield a chemical compound", for example "where silver sulfide forms by chemical corrosion"; it is "referred to as dry corrosion, since it occurs in the absence of water" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. van Noort describes surface oxidation in air in the same terms: "These forms of oxidation are described as dry corrosion" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. "Electrochemical corrosion, also known as galvanic corrosion, requires the presence of water or some other fluid electrolyte" and an electron pathway; "It is also referred to as wet corrosion, since it requires a fluid electrolyte"; it is the more important mode in dentistry (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "An electrochemical cell is composed of three essential components: an anode, a cathode, and an electrolyte"; "The loss of electrons by a metal is known as oxidation"; "The anode is the surface or site on a surface where positive ions" form and the metal corrodes, while reduction at the cathode consumes the electrons (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. The tendency to corrode is judged from the "electromotive (or galvanic) series, which arranges metals by their equilibrium values of electrode potential": "The more negative the electrode potential, the easier it is for any particular metal to ionize and oxidize"; "Noble metals include gold, platinum, palladium, iridium, rhodium, osmium, and ruthenium" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. van Noort separates uniform from localised attack: "If the metal ions are removed from the surface evenly, the process is called uniform corrosion"; "Galvanic and crevice corrosion are examples of such localized corrosion" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC].

Types (Phillips' and van Noort):
- Dissimilar metals: for example "a dental amalgam restoration placed on the occlusal surface of a tooth directly opposing a gold inlay"; contact short-circuits the cell — "This can result in a sharp pain, called galvanic shock" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; "Galvanic corrosion occurs when two dissimilar metals are combined" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC].
- Heterogeneous surface composition: "Another type of galvanic corrosion is associated with the heterogeneous composition of the surfaces of dental alloys" — eutectic phases, "any cored structure is less resistant to corrosion than is the homogenized solid solution", "Even a homogenized solid solution is susceptible to corrosion at the grain boundaries", and solder joints "between dental alloys also corrode because of differences in compositions of the alloy and solder" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. In amalgam "the γ2 phase corrodes significantly faster than the γ1 phase" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; Phillips': the γ2 "phase is the weakest and least stable in a corrosive environment" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].
- Stress corrosion: "the deleterious effects of stress and corrosion, called stress corrosion"; cold-worked regions become anodic — "This is one reason why excessive burnishing of the margins of metallic restorations is contraindicated" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].
- Concentration cell corrosion, "which occurs whenever there are variations in the electrolytes or in the composition of the given electrolyte within the system" (food debris interproximally), or in oxygen concentration "with the greatest attack at the areas containing the least oxygen (the anode)" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Pits and crevices: oxygen-poor base becomes anodic — "making the base of the crevice anodic and the surface cathodic"; "there is a break in the surface oxide coating, which is known as pitting corrosion" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; "to protect against such pitting corrosion, all metallic dental restorative materials should be polished"; crevice corrosion is "in which preferential attack occurs at crevices and marginal gaps of crowns, bridges, and cavity restorations" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Localised damage "is totally out of proportion to the amount of material destroyed by the corrosion process" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. "Generally two or more types act simultaneously" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**
1. **Chemical (dry) corrosion:** direct reaction of a metal with a non-metal without an electrolyte, for example silver sulfide formation (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013; van Noort, 2013).
2. **Electrochemical (wet, galvanic) corrosion:** needs an electrolyte (saliva). This is the main type in the mouth.

**Mechanism.** A cell needs an anode, a cathode and an electrolyte. The anode is oxidized (M → M⁺ + e⁻) and dissolves; the cathode consumes the electrons. The more negative a metal's electrode potential in the electromotive series, the more readily it corrodes (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Types of electrochemical corrosion**
- **Dissimilar metals (galvanic):** for example amalgam opposing a gold inlay; contact causes galvanic shock.
- **Heterogeneous surface:** eutectic phases, coring, grain boundaries, solder joints, γ2 of amalgam (van Noort, 2013).
- **Stress corrosion:** cold-worked, burnished or fatigued areas become anodic.
- **Concentration cell:** different electrolytes or oxygen levels; the oxygen-poor area (under debris, base of pits and crevices) is anodic, giving pitting and crevice corrosion, localized and more destructive than uniform corrosion (van Noort, 2013).

## 4. Clinical significance and prevention

### Explanation

"The most important electrochemical effects are toxic and allergic responses to metal ions released by corrosion" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. A limited benefit exists: in amalgam "the corrosion products tend to seal the marginal gap and inhibit the ingress of oral fluids and bacteria" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; "The most common corrosion products found with conventional amalgam alloys are oxides and chlorides of tin" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. With gold and amalgam in contact, "Mercury released from the corroding amalgam (the anode) may interact with the gold alloy (the cathode) and weaken it" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. For implants, "The combination of stress, corrosion, and bacteria contributes to implant failure" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Prevention: noble metal content — "at least half the atoms should be noble metals (gold, platinum, and palladium)"; "Palladium has been found to be effective in reducing the susceptibility to sulfide tarnishing for alloys containing silver"; passivating metals ("such a metal is said to be passive"), as in "stainless steel, which contains sufficient amounts of chromium added to iron"; a non-conductive coating helps only "if it is applied to the more noble metal"; "good oral hygiene helps to prevent the accumulation of surface deposits and is essential for minimizing corrosion"; and "Every effort should be made to produce a smooth, homogeneous surface on a restoration in order to minimize tarnish and corrosion"; in amalgam, "the addition of more than 6% of copper by weight can reduce or eliminate the" γ2 phase (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Effects:** discoloration, marginal breakdown, weakening and fracture; metal-ion release (toxic and allergic reactions); galvanic shock and metallic taste; implant failure; mercury from amalgam weakening adjacent gold. Benefit: amalgam corrosion products seal the margin (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Prevention:** noble alloys (at least half the atoms noble; palladium against sulfide tarnish); passivating alloys (stainless steel, titanium); homogenized alloys; no dissimilar metals in contact; no excessive burnishing; polishing; oral hygiene; amalgam with more than 6% copper (little or no γ2) (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Tarnish = surface only, aesthetic, the forerunner of corrosion. Corrosion = penetrates the body of the material, structural.
- Chemical (dry) versus electrochemical (wet) corrosion. The cell = anode + cathode + electrolyte; the anode corrodes.
- Localized corrosion (pitting, crevice) is worse than uniform corrosion; the oxygen-poor base of a pit is anodic.
- Polish every metallic restoration; avoid excessive burnishing.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Using "tarnish" and "corrosion" as synonyms.
- Saying the cathode corrodes (it is the anode).
- Forgetting that a single metallic restoration and similar alloys also set up cells.
