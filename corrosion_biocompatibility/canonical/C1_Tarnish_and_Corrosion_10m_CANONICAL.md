## 1. Definitions: tarnish versus corrosion

### Explanation

"Tarnish is a surface discoloration on a metal or a slight loss or alteration of the surface finish or luster"; in the mouth it comes from deposits on the restoration and from thin films: "Tarnish also arises from the formation of thin films, such as oxides, sulfides, or chlorides"; the film may even be protective, but "it is often an early indication and precurser of corrosion" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "Corrosion is a process whereby deterioration of a metal is caused by reaction with its environment", and even highly localised corrosion can cause mechanical failure with little material lost (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Powers and Wataha put the difference by depth, for amalgam: "With tarnish, the chemical reaction between the amalgam and the oral cavity is restricted to the amalgam surface", whereas "Corrosion results from chemical reactions that penetrate into the body of the amalgam"; "Although tarnish may be undesirable esthetically, it will not often cause the restoration to fail", while corrosion eventually does (Powers and Wataha, 2013) [SRC]. Phillips' agrees: silver-sulfide tarnish on amalgam "does not affect or change the mechanical properties of the amalgam", while corrosion does (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Metals corrode because "most commonly used metals and alloys are not in their lowest energy state"; gold is the exception, and "Ceramics, on the other hand, do not corrode because they are already fully oxidized" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Craig's: "corrosion is the progressive destruction of a metal by chemical or electrochemical reaction with its environment" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. ISO 10271:2001, quoted by McCabe and Walls, defines corrosion as "physicochemical interaction between a metal or an alloy and its environment that results in a partial or total destruction of the material or in a change of its properties" and tarnish as "surface discolouration due to interaction between a metal and its environment" (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. The books agree on the distinction.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Tarnish:** surface discoloration or loss of lustre from deposits or thin oxide, sulfide or chloride films; surface-limited and aesthetic, but the forerunner of corrosion (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013; Powers and Wataha, 2013).

**Corrosion:** deterioration of a metal by reaction with its environment; it penetrates the material, releases ions and causes failure (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Cause:** metals are not in their lowest energy state and revert to oxides or sulfides; ceramics, already oxidized, do not corrode (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 2. Why the mouth promotes tarnish and corrosion

### Explanation

The oral environment "is warm, moist, and subjected to wide fluctuations in temperature and pH"; "eggs and certain other foods contain significant amounts of sulfur", and sulfides tarnish silver, copper, tin and mercury; "water, oxygen, and chlorine ions are present in saliva and contribute to the corrosive attack", and acids "such as phosphoric, acetic, and lactic acids" promote corrosion (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Bhat et al add calculus and plaque deposits — "Pigment producing bacteria also cause tarnish" — and stains from sulphur- or chloride-containing foods, "drinks like coffee, tea, alcohol, etc. tobacco" and drugs (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Oral factors:** warmth, moisture, swings of temperature and pH, dietary sulfur (eggs), salivary chlorides and oxygen, food acids, plaque (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## 3. Classification and mechanism of corrosion

### Explanation

"Chemical corrosion is the direct combination of metallic and nonmetallic elements to yield a chemical compound", for example "where silver sulfide forms by chemical corrosion"; it is "referred to as dry corrosion, since it occurs in the absence of water" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. van Noort describes surface oxidation in air in the same terms: "These forms of oxidation are described as dry corrosion" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. "Electrochemical corrosion, also known as galvanic corrosion, requires the presence of water or some other fluid electrolyte" and an electron pathway; "It is also referred to as wet corrosion, since it requires a fluid electrolyte"; it is the more important mode in dentistry (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. "An electrochemical cell is composed of three essential components: an anode, a cathode, and an electrolyte"; "The loss of electrons by a metal is known as oxidation"; "The anode is the surface or site on a surface where positive ions" form and the metal corrodes, while reduction at the cathode consumes the electrons (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. The tendency to corrode is judged from the "electromotive (or galvanic) series, which arranges metals by their equilibrium values of electrode potential": "The more negative the electrode potential, the easier it is for any particular metal to ionize and oxidize"; "Noble metals include gold, platinum, palladium, iridium, rhodium, osmium, and ruthenium" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. **Sign convention — books differ.** Phillips' (and McCabe) use reduction potentials, so gold is about +1.50 V and the more negative metal corrodes. Craig's Table 4.16 lists oxidation potentials with the opposite sign (aluminium +1.662, gold −1.498) and states "Those metals with a large negative electrode potential are more resistant to tarnish than those with a high positive electrode potential" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Both describe the same ranking (gold most noble, aluminium/zinc most active); state which convention you use. van Noort separates uniform from localised attack: "If the metal ions are removed from the surface evenly, the process is called uniform corrosion"; "Galvanic and crevice corrosion are examples of such localized corrosion" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC].

Types (Phillips' and van Noort):
- Dissimilar metals: for example "a dental amalgam restoration placed on the occlusal surface of a tooth directly opposing a gold inlay"; contact short-circuits the cell — "This can result in a sharp pain, called galvanic shock" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; "Galvanic corrosion occurs when two dissimilar metals are combined" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC].
- Heterogeneous surface composition: "Another type of galvanic corrosion is associated with the heterogeneous composition of the surfaces of dental alloys" — eutectic phases, "any cored structure is less resistant to corrosion than is the homogenized solid solution", "Even a homogenized solid solution is susceptible to corrosion at the grain boundaries", and solder joints "between dental alloys also corrode because of differences in compositions of the alloy and solder" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. In amalgam "the γ2 phase corrodes significantly faster than the γ1 phase" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; Phillips': the γ2 "phase is the weakest and least stable in a corrosive environment" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].
- Stress corrosion: "the deleterious effects of stress and corrosion, called stress corrosion"; cold-worked regions become anodic — "This is one reason why excessive burnishing of the margins of metallic restorations is contraindicated" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].
- Concentration cell corrosion, "which occurs whenever there are variations in the electrolytes or in the composition of the given electrolyte within the system" (food debris interproximally), or in oxygen concentration "with the greatest attack at the areas containing the least oxygen (the anode)" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Pits and crevices: oxygen-poor base becomes anodic — "making the base of the crevice anodic and the surface cathodic"; "there is a break in the surface oxide coating, which is known as pitting corrosion" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]; "to protect against such pitting corrosion, all metallic dental restorative materials should be polished"; crevice corrosion is "in which preferential attack occurs at crevices and marginal gaps of crowns, bridges, and cavity restorations" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Localised damage "is totally out of proportion to the amount of material destroyed by the corrosion process" (van Noort, 2013) [SRC]. "Generally two or more types act simultaneously" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Homogeneity matters: "many manufacturers of alloys carry out homogenization heat treatments" to reduce electrolytic corrosion (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Bhat et al list three further types: "Corrosion fatigue takes place due to the simultaneous action of cyclic forces and chemical attack"; biocorrosion, where "The metabolic activities of various microorganisms in the food debris" attack the metal; and rough-surface corrosion — "Rough surfaces also collect more food debris and contribute to cell corrosion" (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019) [SRC].

Amalgam (Craig's): corrosion products "include tin oxides, tin hydroxychlorides, copper oxides, copper chlorides"; of the pure phases, the γ1 "phase has the highest corrosion resistance, followed by" γ, Ag3Cu2, ε, η′ and γ2 (the least); "The average depth of corrosion for most amalgam alloys is 100 to 500" µm from the margin; phosphate buffers and pellicles mean "thus saliva may provide some protection of dental amalgams from corrosion"; and copper "has improved the resistance of amalgam to chloride and galvanic corrosion" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. **Conflict — which amalgam is the anode.** Phillips': "A high-copper amalgam is cathodic with respect to a conventional amalgam" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; Craig's galvanic series in artificial saliva (Table 4.17) places conventional amalgam on the more anodic side of high-copper amalgam, in agreement. Bhat et al state the opposite: with high- and low-copper amalgams opposing, "the high copper becomes the anode and undergoes corrosion" (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019) [SRC]. Two books against one, with Craig's measured series: follow Phillips' and Craig's. [Likely]

#### ✎ Exam core

**Classification**
1. **Chemical (dry) corrosion:** direct reaction of a metal with a non-metal without an electrolyte, for example silver sulfide formation (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013; van Noort, 2013).
2. **Electrochemical (wet, galvanic) corrosion:** needs an electrolyte (saliva). This is the main type in the mouth.

**Mechanism.** Anode, cathode and electrolyte form a cell. The anode is oxidized (M → M⁺ + e⁻) and dissolves; the cathode consumes the electrons. The more negative a metal's reduction potential, the more readily it corrodes (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Types of electrochemical corrosion**
- **Dissimilar metals (galvanic):** for example amalgam opposing a gold inlay; contact causes galvanic shock.
- **Heterogeneous surface:** eutectic phases, coring, grain boundaries, solder joints, γ2 of amalgam (van Noort, 2013).
- **Stress corrosion:** cold-worked or burnished areas become anodic; with cyclic load, corrosion fatigue.
- **Biocorrosion:** microbial metabolism in debris (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019).
- **Concentration cell:** different electrolytes or oxygen levels; the oxygen-poor area (under debris, base of pits and crevices) is anodic, giving pitting and crevice corrosion, localized and more destructive than uniform corrosion (van Noort, 2013).

## 4. Testing of tarnish and corrosion

### Explanation

ISO 10271:2001 (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC] specifies three tests. Static immersion: "soaking specimens of the test alloy in an aqueous solution of lactic acid and sodium chloride for 7 days" at 37 °C, then analysing the released ions, especially hazardous ones such as "nickel, cadmium and beryllium". Electrochemical test: the alloy is the working electrode in an "aqueous solution of sodium chloride at a pH of 7.4"; "A potentiodynamic sweep is carried out" and the alloy is characterised "by the presence of active peak potentials and by the breakdown potential". Tarnish: cyclic immersion — "The test sample is dipped into an aqueous solution of sodium sulphide hydrate for 10–15 seconds once every minute over a period of 3 days", then compared visually (McCabe and Walls, 2008) [SRC]. Craig's: tarnish "Testing generally relies on controlled exposure of the alloy to a solution rich in sulfides, chlorides, and phosphates", and discoloration "has been evaluated by spectrophotometric methods" (Sakaguchi, Ferracane and Powers, 2019) [SRC]. Bhat et al add the "Electrochemical linear polarization technique" and the "Coupon test: Specimen of standard size is placed in the corroding medium for a long time" (1–3 months) with weight loss measured (Bhat, Nandish and Jayaprakash, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Testing (ISO 10271:2001):** static immersion in lactic acid–NaCl for 7 days at 37 °C (ions analysed); electrochemical potentiodynamic test in NaCl at pH 7.4 (breakdown potential); tarnish by cyclic dipping in sodium sulphide for 3 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008).

## 5. Clinical significance and prevention

### Explanation

"The most important electrochemical effects are toxic and allergic responses to metal ions released by corrosion" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. A limited benefit exists: in amalgam "the corrosion products tend to seal the marginal gap and inhibit the ingress of oral fluids and bacteria" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]; "The most common corrosion products found with conventional amalgam alloys are oxides and chlorides of tin" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. With gold and amalgam in contact, "Mercury released from the corroding amalgam (the anode) may interact with the gold alloy (the cathode) and weaken it" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. For implants, "The combination of stress, corrosion, and bacteria contributes to implant failure" (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC]. Prevention: noble metal content — "at least half the atoms should be noble metals (gold, platinum, and palladium)"; "Palladium has been found to be effective in reducing the susceptibility to sulfide tarnishing for alloys containing silver"; passivating metals ("such a metal is said to be passive"), as in "stainless steel, which contains sufficient amounts of chromium added to iron"; a non-conductive coating helps only "if it is applied to the more noble metal"; "good oral hygiene helps to prevent the accumulation of surface deposits and is essential for minimizing corrosion"; and "Every effort should be made to produce a smooth, homogeneous surface on a restoration in order to minimize tarnish and corrosion"; in amalgam, "the addition of more than 6% of copper by weight can reduce or eliminate the" γ2 phase (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Effects:** discoloration, marginal breakdown, fracture; metal-ion release (toxic, allergic); galvanic shock, metallic taste; mercury weakening adjacent gold. Benefit: amalgam corrosion products seal the margin (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

**Prevention:** noble alloys (half the atoms noble; palladium against sulfide tarnish); passivating alloys (stainless steel, titanium); homogenized alloys; no dissimilar metals in contact; no excessive burnishing; polishing; hygiene; high-copper amalgam (Anusavice, Shen and Rawls, 2013).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Tarnish = surface only, aesthetic, the forerunner of corrosion. Corrosion = penetrates the body of the material, structural.
- Chemical (dry) versus electrochemical (wet) corrosion. The cell = anode + cathode + electrolyte; the anode corrodes.
- Localized corrosion (pitting, crevice) is worse than uniform corrosion; the oxygen-poor base of a pit is anodic.
- Polish every metallic restoration; avoid excessive burnishing.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Using "tarnish" and "corrosion" as synonyms.
- Mixing sign conventions: Phillips'/McCabe list gold as +1.50 V (reduction potentials); Craig's Table 4.16 lists gold as −1.498 V (oxidation potentials). State the convention.
- Calling high-copper amalgam the anode against conventional amalgam (Bhat et al say so; Phillips' and Craig's show the reverse).
- Saying the cathode corrodes (it is the anode).
- Forgetting that a single metallic restoration and similar alloys also set up cells.
