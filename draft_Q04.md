## STUDY MODE

### S1. What "adhesion" has to explain [basic science]
Most restoratives hold on by mechanical locking (composite into etched enamel and collagen). GIC is different: Summitt calls polyalkenoate cements the only dental materials whose primary adhesion to tooth is a true chemical bond [SRC:C1122]. The question therefore has three parts: how the bond forms, how to prepare the tooth so it forms, and how strong it really is.

### S2. How the bond forms [mechanism]
The polyacid in the freshly mixed cement is a high-molecular-weight chain with many carboxyl (COOH) groups. Hydroxyapatite is the mineral of enamel and dentine, and carries phosphate and calcium. The carboxyl groups displace surface phosphate ions, and each displaced phosphate takes a calcium ion with it, so an ion-enriched layer forms and is bound to enamel and dentine [SRC:A97]. As the cement sets, the mineral released buffers the acid and raises the pH, and calcium and phosphate re-precipitate at the interface as a calcium or strontium phosphate and polyalkenoate layer; this is "diffusion-based adhesion" [SRC:A113]. Wilson's SEM work shows this ion-exchange layer [SRC:A96]. Sikri describes the same mechanism and adds micromechanical self-etching as a second part [SRC:G21] [SRC:G22].

On dentine there is an extra route: hydrogen bonding or metal-ion bridging between carboxyl groups and collagen [SRC:A114]. McCabe lists two competing theories, chelation of calcium to give an interfacial calcium polyalkenoate salt (supported by stronger bonds to enamel than dentine), and dissolution and reprecipitation of apatite; he says the exact mechanism is unclear [SRC:B20].

### S3. Preparing the tooth [clinical decision-making]
- **Why condition at all.** A smear layer of debris, pellicle or polishing paste blocks the surface. Brief 10% polyacrylic acid dissolves the smear layer in 10-15 seconds [SRC:A99]. It is the cement's own acid, so residue does not interfere with setting; it improves wetting and pre-activates calcium and phosphate ions [SRC:A100] [SRC:A118].
- **Time and strength.** Left longer than 20 seconds it is likely to demineralise dentine and open tubules [SRC:A99]; van Noort prefers 20% polyacrylic acid and warns that citric acid should not be used because it opens tubules and may compromise the bond [SRC:B237]. Sikri lists 50% citric acid, 25% tannic acid or 20% polyacrylic acid for 10-20 seconds [SRC:G43].
- **Alternatives.** Causton's ITS (mineralising) solution or 25% tannic acid unites the smear layer with the tooth and seals the tubules [SRC:A103] [SRC:A119]; useful before luting on vital teeth to avoid hydraulic pressure and sensitivity [SRC:A311].
- **Never acid-etch** the tooth before GIC [SRC:A121].
- **Clinical effect.** Conditioned restorations lasted longer, but the difference in longevity was not statistically significant in the evidence Bonsor quotes [SRC:B319].
- **When not needed.** Under amalgam or gold, where chemical adhesion is not required, conditioning is unnecessary [SRC:A102].

### S4. How strong is the bond? [debated]
Measured values are low and vary by source (see synthesis). The reason is that in most tests the cement fails inside itself, because its tensile strength is low (about 7-12 MPa), leaving the ion-exchange layer attached to the tooth [SRC:A116] [SRC:B22] [SRC:B237]. That is why the cement's own strength limits its apparent adhesion, and why a stronger cement gives better adhesion provided the surface is clean [SRC:A98].

### S5. Modified materials [classification]
- **Resin-modified GIC** bonds by the same chelation, with only a small micromechanical contribution because resin is a minor part of the mix [SRC:A115]; fewer free carboxyl groups, polymerisation shrinkage and water uptake limit the bond [SRC:B364]; Phillips says hybrid-layer formation is not conclusively shown [SRC:B524].
- **Compomers and giomers** have no useful intrinsic adhesion and must be used with a dentine bonding system [SRC:B73] [SRC:B531].
- **GIC to composite** (sandwich): two interfaces, cement to dentine by ion exchange and cement to composite mechanically or chemically; auto-cure cement is etched 15 seconds with 37% phosphoric acid, while dual-cure retains resin bonds and does not need etching [SRC:A112] [SRC:A111].
- **To metals:** GIC bonds to stainless steel and to tin-oxide-plated gold alloys [SRC:B112].

### S6. Clinical meaning
Adhesion means no mechanical undercut is needed, erosion lesions need not be cut, and microleakage is reduced [SRC:A108]. Even with low measured values, retention in cervical erosion lesions has been better than with composite [SRC:B155].

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

### Claim. What is the bond strength of GIC to dentine?
- **Textbook position.** Mount says the strength of the ion-exchange layer "has yet to be measured", because tests actually measure the cement's own tensile strength [SRC:A98] [SRC:A116]. Others give numbers: Craig 11th ed. reports a tensile bond to dentine of 1-3 MPa [SRC:B112]; McCabe 1-3 MPa and about 5 MPa cohesive failure at enamel [SRC:B22]; van Noort 2-7 MPa shear [SRC:B237]; Bhat about 12 MPa to enamel and about 1.23 MPa to dentine [SRC:B176]; Sturdevant macroshear 6-12 MPa against 22-35 MPa for modern dentine bonding agents [SRC:C1213]. Summitt says values equal one-step self-etching adhesives on dentine but are hard to interpret because of cohesive failure [SRC:C1126].
- **Retrieved evidence on conditioning (laboratory studies, abstracts only).** The effect of 10% polyacrylic acid on shear bond strength depended on the material and the substrate and was often not significant [LIT:Cai2021]; the best conditioner depended on the type of cement, polyacrylic acid for self-cure high-viscosity GIC and phosphoric acid plus a resin-modified GIC adhesive for light-cure high-viscosity GIC [LIT:Koh2024]; for a resin-modified GIC bonded to caries-affected dentine, polyacrylic acid gave the highest micro-shear bond strength (14.26 MPa) and 37% phosphoric acid gave more microleakage [LIT:Alrefeai2022].
- **Interpretation and limit.** The sources disagree because they use different tests, and because the bond usually outlasts the cement, so the number reflects the cement. The honest statement is a range of about 1-12 MPa depending on test, lower than modern adhesives, and not a true measure of the interface. [Likely] No journal study was retrieved.
- **Conclusion.** Quote the range and the cohesive failure, not a single figure. Clinical retention is good despite the low number. "Never acid-etch" holds for conventional GIC; laboratory work suggests the best conditioner for light-cure high-viscosity materials may differ, but this is laboratory evidence only.

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 43 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Glass ionomer cement is the only dental material whose main adhesion to tooth is a true chemical bond [SRC:C1122]. It bonds to enamel and dentine without acid etching, which gives retention without mechanical undercuts and reduces microleakage [SRC:A108].

### 2. Mechanism of adhesion
- **Step 1, ion exchange.** Carboxyl groups of the polyacid displace phosphate ions from hydroxyapatite; each phosphate takes a calcium ion, forming an ion-enriched layer bound to tooth [SRC:A97].
- **Step 2, diffusion layer.** As the cement sets, released mineral buffers the acid, and calcium or strontium phosphate and polyalkenoate re-precipitate at the interface; this is diffusion-based adhesion [SRC:A113] [SRC:A96].
- **Step 3, dentine extras.** Hydrogen bonds or metal-ion bridges between carboxyl groups and collagen [SRC:A114].
- **Two parts.** Micromechanical self-etching plus chemical ionic bonding [SRC:G22].
- **Failure.** Cohesive within the cement, leaving the ion-exchange layer on the tooth [SRC:A116] [SRC:A125].

### 2A. Chemical bond compared with micromechanical bond
| Feature | Glass ionomer | Composite (adhesive system) |
|---|---|---|
| Main mechanism | chemical (ionic) bond with hydroxyapatite [SRC:C115] | micromechanical, hybrid layer [SRC:C115] |
| Tooth preparation | polyacrylic acid conditioning, 10 seconds [SRC:A120] | acid etch and primer or self-etch [SRC:C1213] |
| Bond to enamel | weaker than a modern composite bond, but performs well clinically [SRC:A122] | highest |
| Moisture | needs a clean surface, no dehydration [SRC:A109] | needs control of wet or dry dentine |

### 3. Tooth preparation (conditioning)
1. Clean with plain pumice slurry; most polishing pastes leave a smear layer [SRC:A101].
2. Condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds (10-15 seconds removes the smear layer; over 20 seconds demineralises and opens tubules) [SRC:A99] [SRC:A120].
3. Wash for 10 seconds; dry lightly without dehydrating [SRC:A120].
4. Never acid-etch before GIC [SRC:A121].
5. Alternatives: Causton's ITS solution or 25% tannic acid for 1-2 minutes [SRC:A119]; avoid citric acid [SRC:B237].
Reasons for polyacrylic acid: it is the cement's own acid, raises wettability, and pre-activates calcium and phosphate [SRC:A100] [SRC:A118].

### 3A. Protocols by situation
- **Class V erosion lesion:** scrub with plain pumice slurry for 5 seconds, condition 10 seconds, wash, dry gently [SRC:A101] [SRC:A109].
- **Luting on a vital tooth:** considerable hydraulic pressure is possible, so do not condition dentine with 10% polyacrylic acid; use a mineralising solution or 25% tannic acid [SRC:A311]; some patients have severe sensitivity on seating from hydraulic displacement of dentinal fluid, so seal tubules with a low-viscosity resin or a remineralising solution [SRC:G53].
- **Fissure sealant:** condition 10 seconds with 10% polyacrylic acid, wash with water only, dry lightly; this also removes biofilm [SRC:A124].
- **Sandwich technique:** condition dentine, place cement, then etch auto-cure GIC for 15 seconds before composite [SRC:A111].

### 3B. Ways to improve or extend adhesion
- New dentine bonding agents containing polyalkenoic acid (especially maleic acid) form a hybrid layer similar to the ion-exchange layer [SRC:A107].
- A 2-5 micrometre layer of tin oxide on the fitting surface gives some adhesion to noble metals [SRC:A106].
- A mineralising solution or tannic acid unites the smear layer with the tooth instead of removing it [SRC:A103].

### 4. Bond strength
| Source | Value |
|---|---|
| Craig 11th ed. | tensile to dentine 1-3 MPa [SRC:B112] |
| McCabe | dentine 1-3 MPa; enamel cohesive failure about 5 MPa [SRC:B22] |
| van Noort | shear 2-7 MPa [SRC:B237] |
| Sturdevant | macroshear 6-12 MPa (modern bonding agents 22-35 MPa) [SRC:C1213] |
Mount: not measurable, as failure is within the cement [SRC:A116]. The values differ because the tests differ.

### 5. Factors affecting adhesion
- Clean surface free of saliva, pellicle, plaque and blood [SRC:A98].
- Conditioning and its time [SRC:A99].
- Moisture control: dehydration and early water contact damage the cement [SRC:G19].
- Cement strength: a stronger cement gives better apparent adhesion [SRC:A98].
- Type of cement: metal-filled cements show less ion-exchange adhesion [SRC:A110].

### 5A. The interface after the bond forms
- The ion-exchange layer seals the dentinal tubules and prevents microleakage [SRC:A125].
- Polyacrylic acid is mild and of high molecular weight, so it does not penetrate tubules; after 10 seconds tubule plugs remain and limit dentinal fluid flow [SRC:A118].
- With a dual-cure cement, separation occurred inside dentine or enamel and not at the ion-exchange layer, because the cement was stronger in tension; this shows the weakest point is normally the cement, not the interface [SRC:A105].
- When a restoration fractures, the cement often leaves an acid-resistant surface layer on the tooth [SRC:G55].
- Adhesion reduces marginal leakage, so harmful micro-organisms cannot enter the tooth-restoration interface [SRC:G22].

### 6. Adhesion of modified materials
- **RMGIC:** same chelation, minor micromechanical contribution; limited by fewer free carboxyl groups and polymerisation shrinkage [SRC:A115] [SRC:B364].
- **Compomer and giomer:** need a bonding agent [SRC:B73].
- **To composite in a sandwich:** etch auto-cure GIC 15 seconds with 37% phosphoric acid; dual-cure needs no etching [SRC:A111].
- **To metal:** stainless steel, tin-oxide-plated gold [SRC:B112].

### 7. Clinical significance
- No cavity undercuts, minimal preparation of erosion lesions [SRC:A108].
- Retention in cervical erosion lesions better than composite despite low bond values [SRC:B155].
- Seals dentine and reduces microleakage [SRC:A125].
- Luting GIC improves seal but not retention; retention must come from the preparation [SRC:A123].

### 8. Conclusion
GIC bonds by ion exchange and diffusion, with conditioning by 10% polyacrylic acid. Measured values are low (about 1-12 MPa by test) because failure is within the cement, but clinical retention is good. The practical rules are a clean surface, brief conditioning, no etching, no dehydration, and immediate protection of the setting cement.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Writing "acid-etch with phosphoric acid before GIC".
2. Giving one bond strength value without saying the tests differ and failure is cohesive.
3. Calling the bond "mechanical", when the main bond is chemical.
