## STUDY MODE

### S1. Definitions [basic science]
Mount defines a lining as a thin layer of neutral material that corrects a deficiency in cavity design or gives thermal protection, with no therapeutic effect expected unless there is exposure; a base is a dentine substitute for major dentine loss and also a way of bonding composite to dentine. The only difference between lining and base is the powder:liquid ratio (Mount, 1994). Banerjee and Watson say the terms "cavity lining" and "structural base" belong to the amalgam era and should no longer be used in minimally invasive dentistry (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### S2. Why GIC is used [treatment relevance]
GIC is the only cement liner or base that bonds to dentine (Craig, 2002); Craig gives tensile bond strengths of 2.0 to 4.9 MPa (Craig, 2002). Mount says it gives the most effective long-term seal to dentine, best used as the primary dentine sealant (Mount, 1994). Summitt says its mechanical properties, modulus and support allow it to serve as a base as well as a liner, and that zinc oxide-eugenol and zinc phosphate use has diminished (Hilton et al, 2013). For thermal protection, Craig says glass ionomer bases protect the pulp if used in a sufficiently thick layer (more than 0.5 mm), their conductivity being similar to enamel and dentine (Craig, 2002); Mount says a thin layer (0.5 mm) of lining material may offer a degree of thermal protection in a cavity deeper than halfway to the pulp under a metallic restoration (Mount, 1994).

### S3. Uses by restoration [clinical decision-making]
Under amalgam, minimal cavities need no lining because amalgam seals its own margins by corrosion, while cavities more than halfway to the pulp get a small amount of low powder:liquid (1.5:1) GIC lining (Mount, 1994). Under composite, GIC is a base in the sandwich technique (Mount, 1994); Sikri names glass cermet as a preferred base at the cervical wall of proximal lesions (Sikri, 2020). Under indirect restorations the lining is a convenience for impression and line of withdrawal (Mount, 1994), and Summitt adds that the main use of bases is to block out undercuts (Hilton et al, 2013). Over calcium hydroxide or MTA after pulp capping, GIC or resin-modified GIC gives an adhesive acid-resistant cover (Hilton et al, 2013); Summitt notes calcium hydroxide dissolves with acid etching (Hilton et al, 2013). Torres places calcium hydroxide only over pink dentine, in layers of 0.5-0.7 mm, with GIC over it and over the remaining deep dentine (Torres, 2020).

### S4. Limits
Low powder:liquid lining GIC is not strong enough under composite unless the margin is entirely sound enamel; heavier cement (3:1) is used for major defects (Mount, 1994). Glass ionomer and resin-modified glass ionomer both show cytotoxicity in direct contact with cells, resin-modified formulations tending to show more (see Q8) (Hilton et al, 2013). Ricketts and Bartlett say resin-modified GIC should not be used beneath all-ceramic restorations because of hygroscopic expansion that could fracture the ceramic (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011). Cavity varnish is not placed beneath GIC because it interferes with chemical adhesion; if needed, it goes on only after the cement is placed (Singh, 2020; Garg and Garg, 2011).

### S5. Where authors differ (side by side, not ranked)
- **GIC over a pulp exposure.** Summitt: little human clinical research supports direct pulp capping with GIC or resin-modified GIC, one study of resin-modified GIC showing chronic inflammation and no dentine bridge, and calcium hydroxide or MTA are used for direct capping (Hilton et al, 2013). Mount (1994): if a healthy, uninfected pulp is mechanically exposed, it is only necessary to seal it from bacterial invasion for it to heal (Mount, 1994). Mount et al (2016): GIC can cover and protect a mechanical or traumatic exposure of an otherwise healthy pulp, with dentine bridge formation, but a small amount of calcium hydroxide is placed if there is doubt about infection (Mount et al, 2016).
- **Thickness.** Craig (2002): glass ionomer base more than 0.5 mm for thermal protection (Craig, 2002). Mount (1994): lining 0.5 mm (Mount, 1994); cement over the tubules never less than 1 mm under composite (Mount, 1994). Torres (2020): liner 0.2-1 mm, base more than 1 mm (Torres, 2020). Anusavice et al (2013): cement bases thicker than 0.75 mm (Anusavice et al, 2013). Singh (2020): GIC base 0.5-0.75 mm where remaining dentine is 0.5-2 mm (Singh, 2020). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed (Ritter et al, 2019): liner not thicker than 0.5 mm (Ritter et al, 2019).
- **Calcium hydroxide under GIC.** Mount et al (2016): avoid a calcium hydroxide sub-base in the lamination technique because it reduces the area for adhesion (Mount et al, 2016). Sakaguchi et al (2019): if dentine is less than 1 mm thick, a calcium hydroxide liner is recommended (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Anusavice et al (2013): a calcium hydroxide liner is recommended where residual dentine is less than 0.5 mm (Anusavice et al, 2013). Singh (2020): where remaining dentine is 0.5 mm or less, calcium hydroxide is the liner with a GIC base over it (Singh, 2020). Garg and Garg (2011): nothing special is needed under GIC except in very deep preparations (less than 0.5 mm of dentine), where calcium hydroxide may be used (Garg and Garg, 2011). Sturdevant 2nd South Asia ed (Ritter et al, 2019): calcium hydroxide liners must always be covered with resin-modified GIC (Ritter et al, 2019). Banerjee and Watson (2015): the authors cannot suggest any positive indication for calcium hydroxide beneath adhesive restorations as an indirect pulp cap (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).
- **Type III GIC as the base under composite.** Mount (1994): Type III cements, at 3:1 or more, were developed with the sandwich technique in mind (Mount, 1994). Sikri (2020): the Type III lining cement has limited physical properties and should not be relied upon as the base in the lamination technique; Type II restorative or light-cure cement is preferred (Sikri, 2020).
- **Conditioning.** Mount (1994): 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 seconds (Mount, 1994). Mount et al (2016): 10 seconds (Mount et al, 2016). Torres (2020): 10-25% polyacrylic acid gel for 10-30 seconds before GIC protection, noting some studies disagree on its effectiveness (Torres, 2020).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Type III glass ionomer cements are lining cements and bases; the difference between the two is only the powder:liquid ratio (Mount, 1994).

### 2. Definitions
- **Liner:** thin layer, powder:liquid 1.5:1; corrects a deficiency in design, thermal protection, dentine seal (Mount, 1994).
- **Base:** thicker, powder:liquid 3:1 or more; dentine substitute and a way of bonding composite to dentine (Mount, 1994).
- Thickness: liner 0.2-1 mm, base over 1 mm (Torres, 2020); base over 0.75 mm (Anusavice et al, 2013).

### 3. Why GIC
- Only cement liner or base that bonds to dentine; most effective long-term seal (Craig, 2002; Mount, 1994).
- Fluoride release; radiopaque; thermal properties near enamel and dentine (Craig, 2002).
- Strong enough to be a base as well as a liner (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Biocompatible when sealed and not in direct pulp contact (Hilton et al, 2013).

### 4. Types of material (Sikri's grouping by manufacturer)
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Weak, low acidity | GC lining cement, Shofu liner (Sikri, 2020) |
| Stronger, more acidic | Ketac Bond, Shofu base, GC dentin cement (Sikri, 2020) |
| Strong even in a thin layer (light cure) | Vitrebond (Sikri, 2020) |
| Strength by Powers (24 hours) | GIC base compressive 70-210 MPa; resin-modified 150-200 MPa (Powers and Wataha, 2013) |

### 5. Uses
- Under amalgam: none in minimal cavities; small 1.5:1 lining if deep (Mount, 1994).
- Under composite: sandwich base (Mount, 1994); Sikri prefers Type II or light-cure (Sikri, 2020).
- Under indirect restorations: undercut block-out (Hilton et al, 2013), line of withdrawal, thermal protection (Mount, 1994).
- After pulp capping with calcium hydroxide or MTA: GIC or resin-modified GIC over it (Hilton et al, 2013).
- Dentine substitute in large cavities (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Technique
1. Condition dentine: 10 seconds (Mount et al, 2016) to 10-15 seconds (Mount, 1994).
2. Cover all tubules; at least 1 mm for a base under composite (Mount, 1994).
3. Let it set; cut back to expose enamel margins if composite follows (Mount et al, 2016).
4. Calcium hydroxide sub-lining: not unless exposure (Mount et al, 2016); advised under 1 mm dentine (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) or 0.5 mm (Anusavice et al, 2013).
5. No varnish under GIC (Singh, 2020; Garg and Garg, 2011).

### 7. Limitations
Low powder:liquid liner too weak under composite unless the margin is sound enamel (Mount, 1994); GIC and RMGIC cytotoxic in direct pulp contact, RMGIC more (Hilton et al, 2013); no RMGIC under all-ceramic, hygroscopic expansion (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011). Exposed pulp: calcium hydroxide or MTA first (Hilton et al, 2013); Mount reports GIC sealing a clean exposure (Mount et al, 2016).

### 8. Conclusion
GIC is now the standard adhesive liner and base: it bonds, seals and releases fluoride, and is chosen by powder:liquid ratio for the job.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Liner 1.5:1; base 3:1 or more; the only difference is ratio (Mount, 1994).
- Only cement liner or base that bonds to dentine (Craig, 2002).

- Exposed pulp: calcium hydroxide or MTA first (Hilton et al, 2013); Mount reports GIC sealing a clean mechanical exposure (Mount et al, 2016).
