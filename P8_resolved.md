## STUDY MODE

### S1. Compomer [basic science]
A compomer is a resin-matrix composite with ion-leachable aluminosilicate glass and acid-modified methacrylate monomers. It sets by free-radical polymerisation, not by acid-base reaction, because there is no water in the pack (McCabe and Walls, 2008). Bhat gives the composition as dimethacrylate monomers with two carboxylic groups plus partly silanised glass filler, no water, and says it cannot bond to hard tooth tissue and has significantly lower fluoride release than conventional GIC (Bhat et al, 2019). Sikri says its properties are inferior to hybrid composites, though bond strengths are comparable to GIC (Sikri, 2020).

### S2. Giomer
A giomer is a one-paste, light-cured resin material with pre-reacted glass-ionomer (PRG) filler: the glass is reacted with polyacid before it is blended with the resin (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Sikri, 2020). There are surface-reacted (S-PRG) and fully reacted (F-PRG) fillers (Bhat et al, 2019). McCabe and Walls (2008) describe the same two types: in the surface-reaction type only the glass surface is consumed and reinforcement is given priority over fluoride release, while in the full-reaction type almost the whole glass is broken down, some HEMA is needed to blend it, and fluoride release is given priority over mechanical properties; fully reacted glasses give significantly greater fluoride release than surface-reacted ones (McCabe and Walls, 2008). It needs a bonding agent (Sikri, 2020). Giomers do contain glass-ionomer components but cannot be called compomers because the acid-base reaction has already occurred (Sikri, 2020).

### S3. How they differ from true GIC [classification]
Mount says that the critical difference is that glass ionomers and resin-modified GIC set in the dark by acid-base reaction while compomers do not (Mount et al, 2016). A compomer cannot bond by ion exchange and gives no remineralising ion exchange (Mount et al, 2016). Compared with GIC, compomers have better aesthetics and mechanical properties, stain more, and need water uptake for fluoride release (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).

### S3A. Where the books differ [conflicts shown side by side]
- **Fluoride.** Bhat et al (2019), Bonsor and Pearson (2013), Anusavice et al (2013) and Hilton et al (2013) say compomer fluoride release is lower than that of GIC; Bonsor describes a slow steady release without initial burst. McCabe and Walls (2008) say it becomes equivalent to a conventional GIC after about 40 days; van Noort and Barbour (2013) say modern compomers have a similar profile to GICs over the lifetime of the restoration; Ritter et al (2019, Sturdevant 2nd ed South Asia) say less than 10% of GI release. Recharge: Bonsor says uptake from the surroundings does not occur; van Noort says it can occur, less efficiently than GIC.
- **Etching.** Bonsor and Pearson (2013; most restorative dentists advocate it), Anusavice et al (2013) and Hilton et al (2013) say etch; van Noort and Barbour (2013) recommend omitting it, giving a lower bond strength, for low-stress use only.
- **Shrinkage.** About 2-2.5% by volume (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); between 2% and 3.5% (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013).
- **Class I and II.** Hilton et al (2013) advise against load-bearing Class I and II in permanent teeth; Bonsor and Pearson (2013) say one Class I/II product exists but is not recommended; Anusavice et al (2013) say compomers perform as well as composites for Class I and II; Sikri (2020) says they are also being used in Class II; Ritter et al (2019) say there are likely no indications in contemporary dentistry.

### S4. Clinical performance [prognosis]
Summitt reports poor Class II marginal adaptation of compomers at 6 months and 16% failure at 4 years in posterior teeth, so they should not be used in load-bearing Class I or II areas of permanent teeth, though they can be used in Class V and open-sandwich Class II (Hilton et al, 2013). A meta-analysis of RCTs found no benefit of giomers on secondary caries (RR 1.09) while GICs did (RR 0.36) (Zailai et al, 2026).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Compomers (polyacid-modified composite resins) and giomers are resin-based fluoride-releasing restoratives that borrow glass-ionomer components; neither is a true glass ionomer (Mount et al, 2016; Sikri, 2020).

### 2. Compomer
- **Composition:** dimethacrylate monomers with carboxylic groups, ion-leachable glass (partly silanated), initiators; no water (Bhat et al, 2019; Sikri, 2020).
- **Setting:** free-radical polymerisation (light or chemical); acid-base reaction minimal and delayed until water uptake (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Mount et al, 2016).
- **Adhesion:** none intrinsic; needs a dentine bonding agent; etching advised (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013; Anusavice et al, 2013) or omitted for low stress (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Fluoride:** no initial burst; lower than GIC (Bhat et al, 2019), equal after about 40 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008) or similar over the lifetime (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Shrinkage and water:** about 2-2.5% (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) to 2-3.5% (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); water sorption compensates in days (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).
- **Uses:** low-stress Class III and V, primary teeth, Class II open sandwich; load-bearing Class I or II: not advised in permanent teeth (Hilton et al, 2013; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013), performing as well as composite (Anusavice et al, 2013).
- **Luting:** cast alloy crowns, PFM, gold inlays; all-ceramic generally contraindicated (Craig, 2002).

### 3. Giomer
- **Composition:** surface- (S-PRG) or fully (F-PRG) pre-reacted glass-ionomer filler in a dimethacrylate resin matrix; one paste; Beautifil, Reactmer (McCabe and Walls, 2008; Sikri, 2020) (S-PRG and F-PRG differ: see S2).
- **Setting:** light polymerisation; acid-base reaction completed before blending (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- **Adhesion:** needs bonding agent; single-step bonding agents give bond comparable to compomer (Sikri, 2020).
- **Properties:** smooth finish, good aesthetics, fluoride release and recharge claimed (Sikri, 2020).
- **Disadvantages:** little benefit for recurrent caries risk; expansion, so avoid under thin unsupported enamel (Sikri, 2020).

### 4. Comparison with glass ionomer
| Feature | Glass ionomer | Compomer | Giomer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matrix | water-based polysalt (Mount et al, 2016) | resin (McCabe and Walls, 2008) | resin (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Setting | acid-base, dark set (Mount et al, 2016) | polymerisation (McCabe and Walls, 2008) | polymerisation (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Bond to tooth | chemical (Hilton et al, 2013) | needs adhesive (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) | needs adhesive (Sikri, 2020) |
| Fluoride | high, recharges (Sikri, 2020) | lower than GIC (Bhat et al, 2019) or equal after 40 days (McCabe and Walls, 2008) | comparable to compomer (McCabe and Walls, 2008) |
| Aesthetics | opaque (Sikri, 2020) | better (Bonsor and Pearson, 2013) | good (Sikri, 2020) |

### 5. Evidence
Compomers: poor Class II marginal adaptation at 6 months and 16% failure at 4 years in posterior teeth (Hilton et al, 2013). Giomers: no secondary caries benefit in a meta-analysis (RR 1.09) (Zailai et al, 2026). Mount & Hume note compomers are largely no longer available (Mount et al, 2016).

### 6. Conclusion
Compomers and giomers are composite-like materials that release some fluoride; they lack the ion-exchange bond and the dark set of true glass ionomers, and are suited to low-stress sites.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Compomer = composite plus acid monomers plus glass; no water; sets by polymerisation (McCabe and Walls, 2008).
- Giomer = pre-reacted glass-ionomer filler in resin; needs bonding agent (Sikri, 2020).
- Neither bonds by ion exchange; neither sets in the dark like GIC (Mount et al, 2016).
- Compomer, load-bearing Class I or II: not advised (Hilton et al, 2013; Bonsor and Pearson, 2013); Anusavice et al (2013) and Sikri (2020) report use in Class II.
