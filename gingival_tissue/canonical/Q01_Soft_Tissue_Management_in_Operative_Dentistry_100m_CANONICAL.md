## 1. Introduction and periodontal evaluation before restoration

### Explanation

Soft tissue management covers everything the operative dentist does to the gingiva and the supracrestal attachment: assessing it before treatment, protecting it during preparation, displacing it for access and impressions, surgically repositioning it when tooth structure lies too deep, and restoring in a way the periodontium tolerates afterwards. Sturdevant's closes its periodontal chapter with the principle that, although plaque causes periodontal disease, restorative treatment adds "modifying and significant alterations" that help establish and progress breakdown (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Goldstein's states the precondition plainly: "No attempt at gingival retraction should ever be attempted on inflamed or swollen tissue"; periodontal problems must be treated before the impression (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC]. Where tissue is unhealthy because of old ill-fitting restorations, Goldstein's describes a two-appointment approach: prepare, fit a good provisional, and record the impression later when the tissue has recovered, which also shows where the gingival crest will settle (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC].

Sikri lists the conditions to check before retraction: a sound periodontium around the prepared and adjacent teeth, the free gingival crest at its normal level without recession, hyperplastic or fibrosed tissue treated beforehand, crevicular fluid and bleeding arrested, and retraction done "without detaching the apically located epithelial attachment and periodontal ligament" (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri's contraindications to retraction are poor oral hygiene, gingival recession, bone loss and systemic diseases affecting the gingiva (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** Soft tissue management in operative dentistry is the assessment, protection, displacement, surgical correction and long-term preservation of the gingiva and supracrestal attachment before, during and after restorative treatment. Restorations do not cause periodontitis by themselves, but subgingival margins, overhangs, poor fit and rough materials modify plaque retention and the host response and so contribute to breakdown (Morelli and Neiva, 2019). The aims are:
1. A healthy, non-bleeding field for preparation, bonding and impressions.
2. Access to and visibility of margins, including subgingival ones.
3. Respect for the biologic width so that the restoration does not cause chronic inflammation, recession or bone loss.
4. Stable, esthetic gingival margins and papillae after treatment.

**Periodontal evaluation before restoration**
- Medical history (bleeding disorders, cardiac disease, pacemaker, overgrowth-inducing drugs).
- Plaque and bleeding indices, probing depths, recession, furcations, radiographic bone level.
- Keratinized/attached gingiva width and biotype; bone sounding.
- Existing restorations: overhangs, open or rough margins.

**Rule:** gingival retraction should never be attempted on inflamed or swollen tissue; periodontal disease is treated first (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018). When tissue is inflamed because of faulty restorations, use a two-appointment approach: prepare, place a well-fitting provisional, and make the final impression once the tissue has healed (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018).

**Contraindications to gingival retraction (Sikri, 2017):** poor oral hygiene, gingival recession, bone loss, systemic diseases affecting the gingiva. Retraction must never detach the epithelial attachment.

## 2. Keratinized gingiva and gingival biotype

### Explanation

Summitt's calls the importance of the keratinized zone "somewhat controversial" and presents two positions side by side. Lang and Löe concluded "a minimum width of 2.0 mm of keratinized gingiva is required to prevent chronic gingival inflammation" in unrestored sites; Maynard and Wilson "recommended 5.0 mm of keratinized gingiva (2.0 mm of free and 3.0 mm of attached gingiva)" for predictable response to intracrevicular margins (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Maynard and Wilson also said that if the tissue is "thin enough to see the periodontal probe through the free gingival margin", it may not support intracrevicular procedures. Stetler and Bissada found no difference in inflammation between narrow and wide zones without restorations, but more inflammation where narrow zones had subgingival restorations; the advice is to evaluate width and thickness and to consider "mucogingival therapy (soft tissue grafting)" first in compromised areas (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

Sturdevant's links biotype to the type of damage: "if the periodontium is thin and scalloped, recession will follow the inflammatory process" caused by a margin, whereas a thick flat periodontium carries "a higher likelihood of a moat-like lesion around the tooth" with bleeding and pocketing (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. The two books agree that the keratinized zone matters most when margins go subgingival; they differ only in the minimum width they quote, which reflects different study questions (unrestored versus restored sites), not a contradiction.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Keratinized gingiva.** Two positions are quoted (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013):

| Author | Recommendation | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Lang and Löe | Minimum 2 mm keratinized gingiva | Unrestored sites, good hygiene |
| Maynard and Wilson | 5 mm keratinized (2 mm free + 3 mm attached) | Teeth receiving intracrevicular margins |

Narrow zones without restorations show no extra inflammation, but narrow zones with subgingival restorations show more inflammation, recession and attachment loss. Both width and thickness should therefore be assessed before a subgingival margin, and a soft tissue graft considered first where the zone is deficient (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013). Width of attached gingiva = width of keratinized tissue minus probing depth (Overton et al, 2013).

**Gingival biotype.** Tissue so thin that the probe shows through the free margin may not tolerate intracrevicular procedures (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
- **Thin, scalloped:** responds to margin irritation with recession; high risk after cord packing, electrosurgery and crown lengthening.
- **Thick, flat:** resists recession but tends to form pockets and a moat-like bony lesion around the tooth, with bleeding and sensitivity (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).

Biotype guides margin position and retraction method.

## 3. Biologic width

### Explanation

Sturdevant's defines biologic width as the term that "refers to the combined vertical dimension of the junctional epithelium and the supra-alveolar connective tissue" and notes the seal self-restores: an epithelial attachment separated by probing or flossing "reattaches to the original level in approximately 5 days" (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Its Table 10.1 compares Gargiulo et al (1961) with Vacek and Gher (1994): "The mean biologic width was found to be 1.91 mm" (junctional epithelium 1.14 mm, connective tissue 0.77 mm) and "The mean sulcus depth was found to be 1.32 mm" (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Summitt's gives Gargiulo's study "in which the biologic width averaged 2.04 mm" and the working concept of "3.0 mm (2.0 mm biologic width plus an additional 1.0-mm sulcus depth)" (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

Rouse, in the same book, quotes Vacek for a connective tissue attachment of 0.71 mm, "the least variable of the biologic components", an epithelial attachment whose cells have a "complete turnover every 4 to 6 days", and warns that the averages give a "contrived illusion of mathematical precision" because "clinically, the dimensions vary greatly from 0.43 to 6.03 mm" (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Note the small internal difference: Sturdevant's table gives 0.77 mm connective tissue for Vacek, Rouse gives 0.71 mm; both cite the same study.

Ricketts uses the term differently: sulcus 0.7-1 mm plus junctional epithelium 1 mm plus connective tissue 1 mm, "making up approximately 2.7–3 mm of biological width" (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. This is a terminology disagreement: Ricketts includes the sulcus in "biological width", whereas Morelli and Neiva, Fruits et al and Rouse call that 3 mm the dentogingival complex and reserve "biologic width" for about 2 mm. Write the 2 mm definition and mention Ricketts' usage so the examiner sees you know both.

Consequences of violation: Sturdevant's says margins within or apical to the junctional epithelium disturb the seal, causing inflammation, attachment loss and recession or pocketing, and the width can then only be restored by apical re-establishment of the supracrestal connective tissue (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Ricketts lists the body's reaction as "alveolar bone loss, new connective tissue attachment, apical migration of the junctional epithelium and gingival recession" (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Summitt's balances this: in an animal study, after Class 5 restorations placed at the crest, "a functional biologic width reformed apically", and a review concluded "the evidence is primarily based on opinion and anecdotal cases", though prudence still favours 3 mm from crest to margin (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. The same chapter adds that "Fifteen percent of teeth in the study by Vacek" had margins under 2 mm from bone without attachment loss, and a 2-year clinical study of margins within the connective tissue zone found marked papillary bleeding but "there were no signs of either gingival recession or bone resorption" (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Biologic width is the combined vertical dimension of the junctional epithelium and the supra-alveolar connective tissue attachment (Morelli and Neiva, 2019). With the sulcus above it, it forms the dentogingival complex.

**Dimensions (Morelli and Neiva, 2019)**

| Component | Gargiulo et al 1961 | Vacek et al 1994 |
|---|---|---|
| Sulcus | 0.69 mm | 1.32 mm |
| Junctional epithelium | 0.97 mm | 1.14 mm |
| Connective tissue attachment | 1.07 mm | 0.77 mm |
| Biologic width | 2.04 mm | 1.91 mm |

Working concept: about 2 mm biologic width + 1 mm sulcus = 3 mm from gingival margin to bone facially (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013). The connective tissue attachment is the least variable component; the junctional epithelium and sulcus vary most, and total values range widely between teeth, so each tooth must be measured, not assumed (Rouse, 2013).

**Terminology difference.** Ricketts (2011) uses "biological width" for sulcus + junctional epithelium + connective tissue (about 2.7-3 mm); most authors call this the dentogingival complex and keep biologic width at about 2 mm.

**Effects of violation.** A margin within the junctional epithelium or connective tissue causes chronic inflammation, bleeding, attachment loss, and either recession (thin biotype) or pocketing and bone loss (thick biotype) until a new biologic width forms apically (Morelli and Neiva, 2019; Ricketts, 2011).

**Evidence caveat.** A biologic width re-formed apically in an animal study and a review found the rule largely opinion-based; prudence still favours about 3 mm (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

## 4. Bone sounding and crest relationships

### Explanation

Because the base of the sulcus can only be identified histologically, Rouse says the gingival margin to crest distance is "the only predictable measurement" for intracrevicular margins. Under anaesthesia the probe is pushed through the sulcus, angled to stay on the root and forced to bone; "This process is called bone sounding", measured midfacially and at both facioproximal line angles (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Kois's data in Summitt Ch.1: "In 85% of subjects, a 3.0-mm reading confirms existence of an optimum biologic width", allowing a margin 0.5 mm below the gingiva; proximal CEJ "may be scalloped as much as 3.5 mm incisal to the level of the facial CEJ"; and "At a distance of 5.0 mm, 98% of the dental papillae completely filled the restored gingival embrasure" (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

Rouse's three crest types: normal (3.0 mm facially, 3.0-4.5 mm interproximally; "found on 85% of anterior teeth"; margins 0.5-1.0 mm into the sulcus facially and 0.5-2.5 mm interproximally); low ("the most difficult of all crest positions to manage", 13%; options are an internally bevelled gingivectomy so that the gingival crest is 3 mm coronal to bone, or margins at or above the gingiva with minimal retraction); high ("the least common (2%)", greatest risk of violation; only equigingival or supragingival margins) (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Bone sounding (transgingival probing).** Under local anaesthesia, a periodontal probe is pushed through the sulcus and attachment until it meets bone, measured at midfacial and both facioproximal line angles. The reading (gingival margin to osseous crest) equals biologic width plus sulcus depth (Rouse, 2013). A 3 mm midfacial reading confirms an optimum biologic width in 85% of subjects (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

**Crest relationships (Rouse, 2013)**

| Type | Facial / interproximal reading | Frequency (anterior teeth) | Behaviour | Margin and retraction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | 3.0 mm / 3.0-4.5 mm | 85% | Stable; rebounds after manipulation | 0.5-1.0 mm into sulcus facially, 0.5-2.5 mm interproximally |
| Low | >3.0 mm / >4.5 mm | 13% | Unstable; recession and black triangles likely | Internally bevelled gingivectomy first, or margin at/above gingiva with minimal, gentle retraction |
| High | <3 mm | 2% | Very stable, flat scallop | Equigingival or supragingival only; else crown lengthening |

**Clinical points**
- The preparation must follow the scalloped CEJ and gingiva; the proximal CEJ may lie up to 3.5 mm incisal to the facial CEJ (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
- Papilla fill: when the bone crest to contact point distance is 5 mm, 98% of papillae fill the embrasure (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013); contacts can be positioned to avoid black triangles.
- A reading under 3 mm signals that a subgingival margin will violate biologic width and crown lengthening is needed.

## 5. Margin placement

### Explanation

Rouse: "Margin location is the most important biologic parameter in predictably maintaining gingival health" (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Summitt Ch.1 reports a prospective study of 480 metal-ceramic crowns in which bleeding risk "was twice as high for intrasulcular margins as for supragingival margins" (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's cites a 26-year study that "showed a 0.5-mm increased mean loss of attachment" with subgingival margins after 10 years and says placing a restoration beyond 1 mm into the sulcus is potentially disruptive (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Books differ on the subgingival limit: Ricketts says subgingival margins "should not extend deeper than 0.5 mm from the free gingival margin" (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; Rouse allows 0.5-1.0 mm facially in a normal crest (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; Kois (in Fruits et al) allows 0.5 mm after a 3 mm sounding (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. For non-carious cervical lesions, Overton et al argue "Finishing short is almost certainly superior to placing an overhanging restoration" into a healthy sulcus (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

Margin location is the single most important restorative factor in gingival health (Rouse, 2013).

**Supragingival margins (preferred):** easy to prepare, finish, impress, inspect and clean; no retraction needed; least inflammation. Bleeding risk is about twice as high with intrasulcular margins (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013), and subgingival margins showed 0.5 mm more attachment loss over 10 years (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).

**Equigingival margins:** acceptable with tooth-coloured materials; plaque control is easier than with subgingival margins.

**Subgingival margins: indications.** Short clinical crown needing retention; caries, fracture or old restoration extending subgingivally; ferrule; esthetics with a high lip line or discoloured root.

**How deep? Authors differ:**
- Ricketts (2011): no deeper than 0.5 mm from the free gingival margin.
- Kois, quoted by Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson (2013): 0.5 mm below the gingiva after a 3 mm sounding.
- Rouse (2013): 0.5-1.0 mm facially and up to 2.5 mm interproximally in a normal crest; supragingival or equigingival in low or high crests.
- Morelli and Neiva (2019): beyond 1 mm into the sulcus is potentially disruptive.

The apical limit is really set by the measured distance to bone (about 2.5-3 mm from margin to crest); deeper margins need crown lengthening or orthodontic extrusion.

**Cervical non-carious lesions:** finishing a smooth restoration short of the subgingival portion is better than an overhang in a healthy sulcus (Overton et al, 2013).

## 6. Surgical correction: crown lengthening, gingivectomy, orthodontic extrusion

### Explanation

Sturdevant's: "Crown lengthening is defined as the removal of bone tissue with concomitant removal or repositioning of the soft tissue around the tooth". Biologic width is preserved by "allowing for approximately 2 mm for the connective tissue and junctional epithelium and 1 mm for the sulcus", ideally plus 0.5-1 mm, and "A 3–4-mm distance from gingival margin to the alveolar crest" ensures healing. Wagenberg et al "have suggested to aim for a 5-mm distance from bone to the restorative margin" because surgeons tend to remove too little. For subgingival caries, "at least 3 mm of sound tooth structure are needed from the margin of the final cavity preparation (not the caries lesion) to the alveolar bone crest". Limitations include "position of the furcation", reduced attachment, esthetics, reduced support, unresolved periapical infection, compromise of adjacent teeth and "inadequate crown-to-root ratio after surgery". Ferrule: "1.5–2 mm of ferrule is necessary for adequate retention". Orthodontic extrusion: Camargo stated "forced tooth eruption via orthodontic extrusion is the technique of choice" for isolated teeth in the esthetic zone (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC].

Gingivectomy "does not involve hard tissue but rather just gingival excess removal"; for altered passive eruption it may be done when there is "a distance of more than 3 mm from the CEJ to the alveolar crest", enough keratinized tissue and no bone defect; it "is not advised when the band of keratinized tissue is insufficient" (<3 mm). "Distal wedge is commonly performed on the distal surface of molars" for access and pocket elimination (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications for surgical exposure:** subgingival caries or fracture; inadequate retention or ferrule; altered passive eruption; gingival overgrowth; esthetic gingival discrepancies (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).

**1. Crown lengthening.** Removal of bone with concomitant removal or repositioning of soft tissue, to lengthen the clinical crown and preserve biologic width (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).
- Target: about 2 mm (attachment) + 1 mm (sulcus), ideally plus 0.5-1 mm; a 3-4 mm gingival margin to crest distance after surgery. Wagenberg et al advise aiming for 5 mm from bone to margin so that at least 3 mm is actually achieved.
- At least 3 mm of sound tooth from the margin of the final preparation (not the lesion) to the crest.
- Ferrule of 1.5-2 mm is needed for retention.
- Technique: full-thickness flap, ostectomy, flap repositioned or apically positioned (Overton et al, 2013).
- Limitations: short root trunk with risk of furcation exposure; reduced attachment or support; esthetic zone; unresolved periapical infection; compromise of adjacent teeth; poor crown-root ratio (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).

**2. Gingivectomy / gingivoplasty.** Removes only excess soft tissue, no bone (Morelli and Neiva, 2019). Indicated for overgrowth and altered passive eruption when CEJ to crest is more than 3 mm, keratinized tissue is adequate and no bone defect exists. Not advised when keratinized tissue is less than 3 mm.

**3. Orthodontic (forced) extrusion.** Preferred for isolated teeth in the esthetic zone because it avoids moving adjacent gingival margins and bone; crown lengthening may follow (Morelli and Neiva, 2019). Crown-root ratio still reduces.

**4. Distal wedge.** Removes tuberosity or retromolar tissue distal to molars for access and pocket elimination; a direct restoration can follow immediately if isolation is good (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).

## 7. Soft tissue management during direct restorations

### Explanation

Overton et al: rubber dam is ideal, but many Class 5 lesions can be managed with cord and cotton rolls; for lesions at or below the margin an extra retracting clamp helps and "A no. 212SA clamp is effective for this purpose"; it must be stabilised and "Modeling compound is the traditional stabilizing material used" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Sturdevant's credits Markley for "The use of a No. 212 cervical retainer for restoration of Class V tooth preparations", says the hole is punched facial to the arch form, that the retainer may need stabilising "with a fast-setting rigid material" (PVS bite registration or stick compound), can be modified by "heating each jaw of the retainer in an open flame" and bending it to a more horizontal angle, and that its tips "should be sufficiently dulled to prevent damaging cementum" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Cord in direct work: "retraction cord often may be used for isolation and retraction in direct procedures"; choose a diameter that displaces the gingiva "without causing tissue ischaemia secondary to pressure from the cord"; length to "extend approximately 1 mm beyond the gingival width of the tooth preparation"; the aim is "minimal yet sufficient lateral displacement of the free gingiva and not to force it apically" (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Miniflap: "Each incision is first directed at a right angle to the gingival margin and extended approximately a 1 mm", then turned vertical; "The incision(s) should not be extended past the mucogingival junction"; "Sutures are usually not necessary" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Gingivoplasty: "If gingivoplasty would result in less than 3 mm of attached gingiva adjacent to the restoration", an apically positioned flap or graft may be needed; "An inadequate zone of attached gingiva is an absolute contraindication for gingivoplasty". For two adjacent teeth "a miniflap cannot be used; instead, a mucoperiosteal flap is necessary". RMGI: "both soft and hard tissue attachment to glass ionomer or resin-modified glass ionomer (RMGI) does occur", so RMGI or an open sandwich is favoured under a flap. "A combined surgical-restorative procedure provides better access to the restorative site than a two-step procedure" (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

Disagreement to note: Overton et al phrase the 3 mm limit as attached gingiva for gingivoplasty; Morelli and Neiva phrase it as keratinized tissue for gingivectomy. Attached gingiva is narrower than keratinized gingiva (by the probing depth), so Overton's criterion is the stricter one.

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Rubber dam:** ideal isolation and moisture control for all direct restorations; inverting the dam displaces the gingiva slightly (Overton et al, 2013).

**2. No. 212 cervical retainer (Ritter et al, 2019; Overton et al, 2013)**
- Recommended by Markley for Class V restorations.
- Hole punched facial to the arch form, slightly larger.
- Lingual jaw placed first, facial jaw then retracts dam and gingiva apical to the margin.
- Stabilised with stick compound or PVS bite registration material.
- Modified 212 (jaws heated and bent more horizontal) for deep lesions; lingual jaw may be bent to rest on tooth rather than gingiva.
- Sharp tips dulled to protect cementum.

**3. Retraction cord in direct work (Ritter et al, 2019)**
- Used when rubber dam is impractical, or with it.
- Diameter that opens the sulcus without blanching (ischaemia).
- Length about 1 mm beyond the gingival extent of the preparation.
- Inserted with a thin blunt instrument, stepping slightly backward.
- Lateral, not apical, displacement; placed early to prevent bur abrasion and bleeding.
- Moistened with a non-caustic haemostatic agent.

**4. Miniflap (Overton et al, 2013):** one or two incisions at the mesial and/or distal of the lesion, first at right angles to the margin for about 1 mm, then vertical; within keratinized tissue, not past the mucogingival junction; flap reflected, lesion restored under dam with 212 clamp, flap replaced; sutures usually unnecessary.

**5. Gingivoplasty / gingivectomy:** only where enough attached gingiva remains; less than 3 mm attached gingiva after surgery calls for an apically positioned flap or graft; inadequate attached gingiva is an absolute contraindication (Overton et al, 2013).

**6. Mucoperiosteal flap and crown lengthening:** when a miniflap gives insufficient access, for two adjacent teeth, or when ostectomy is needed. A combined surgical-restorative procedure gives better access than a two-step one (Overton et al, 2013).

**7. Material under the flap:** RMGI or open sandwich with RMGI at the apical part, because soft and hard tissue attachment to glass ionomer has been reported (Overton et al, 2013).

## 8. Gingival displacement for impressions: principles and mechanical methods

### Explanation

Sikri: "Gingival retraction is defined as a process of exposing margins when making impressions of the prepared teeth"; "The main goal of gingival retraction is to reversibly displace the gingival tissues" so that low-viscosity material enters the sulcus (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Rouse's four requirements: horizontal displacement for bulk, vertical displacement to expose the area apical to the finish line, arrest of bleeding, and dry hard and soft tissues: "four requirements must be satisfied for effective gingival retraction" (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's criteria are similar and add that the method be as non-toxic as possible; "The method must eliminate seepage or hemorrhage during the setting of the impression material" (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC]. Laufer's studies (in Rouse): defects were frequent at sulcus widths of 0.08-0.13 mm, with no detectable difference at "sulcular widths of 0.2 mm or greater" (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; Ricketts gives the same 0.2 mm figure (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

Classifications differ by book: Sikri: "Mechanical Chemo-mechanical Surgical/electrosurgical Miscellaneous" (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Sturdevant's: mechanical, chemical or surgical (electrosurgery or laser): "This gingival displacement, or retraction, can be done mechanically, chemically, or surgically" (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein's five: "retraction cord, paste systems, conventional electrosurgery, bipolar technique, and diode lasers" (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC].

Mechanical (Sikri): wooden wedges; cotton twills with "mechanical retraction achieved in 20–30 minutes"; twills with ZOE, which "if kept for longer time, leads to loss of periodontal attachment"; heavy rubber dam; "Copper band (slight oversized band filled with modeling wax" (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri's terms: "Relapse is the tendency of the gingival cuff to revert to its original position"; cord colour code "Black – 000 (extra small) Yellow – 00 (small) Purple – 0 Blue – 1 Green – 2 Red – 3 (extra large)" (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Note this colour code is presented generically by Sikri; it matches one manufacturer's system and other brands differ [RECALL].

Cord technique: "The ends of cords should fall at axial angles of the tooth"; double cord: "After 8–10 minutes, the second cord is soaked in water and removed" with the first left in place (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Rouse: compression cord, aluminium sulfate gel, then dry secondary (deflection) cord; "the secondary cord optimally must remain in the gingival crevice for 4 minutes"; "The secondary cord should be removed wet to prevent renewed hemorrhage" (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein's one-cord technique: cord in haemostatic agent, "leave for about 5–15 min", sometimes a second cord, both removed before the impression (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri for multiple anterior teeth: "Gingival retraction of alternate teeth should be carried out" (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Problems: "there is danger of severing the gingival attachment fibers"; "Leaving the retraction cord in the sulcus too long can create ischemia in the area" (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC]. Low crest: light pressure, small cords, and "Rotary curettage, electrosurgery, and laser troughing are definitely contraindicated in low-crest patients" (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].

Timing disagreement: Rouse 4 min for the secondary cord; Goldstein 5-15 min (one cord) or 4-10 min by tissue; Sikri 8-10 min. These are technique recommendations, not tested equivalents.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition.** Gingival retraction (displacement) is the deflection of the marginal gingiva away from the tooth to expose the margins of a preparation for impression making; the goal is reversible displacement so that low-viscosity impression material enters the sulcus (Sikri, 2017).

**Requirements (Rouse, 2013; Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018)**
1. Horizontal displacement for an adequate bulk of material.
2. Vertical displacement to expose tooth apical to the finish line.
3. Complete arrest of bleeding and seepage.
4. Dry hard and soft tissues.
5. Minimal toxicity and no irreversible damage.

A sulcus width of at least 0.2 mm is needed; narrower sulci produce torn, defective margins (Rouse, 2013; Ricketts, 2011).

**Classification**
- Sikri (2017): mechanical; chemomechanical; surgical/electrosurgical; miscellaneous (newer).
- Morelli and Neiva (2019): mechanical, chemical, surgical (electrosurgery, laser).
- Goldstein, Powers and Lee (2018): cord, paste systems, conventional electrosurgery, bipolar electrosurgery, diode laser.

**Mechanical methods (Sikri, 2017):** wooden wedges (interproximal gingiva); cotton twills (20-30 min); twills with ZOE (prolonged use causes attachment loss); heavy rubber dam (not with elastomers); copper band with modelling wax; plain retraction cord; provisional crowns.

**Retraction cords:** twisted, braided or knitted; plain or impregnated; colour-coded sizes 000 (black) to 3 (red) (Sikri, 2017). Braided cords do not unwind (Ricketts, 2011).

**Technique**
- Cut to length; ends meet at an axial line angle, not facial or lingual (Sikri, 2017).
- Blunt serrated instrument, angled towards the root and towards cord already placed.
- **Single cord:** largest cord that fits, soaked in haemostatic agent, 5-15 min, removed before the impression (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018).
- **Double cord:** thin first cord (000/00) left in place; thicker second cord removed wet just before the impression (Sikri, 2017: 8-10 min; Rouse, 2013: 4 min for 0.2 mm width). The first cord stops tissue rebound. It must lie apical to the finish line (Ricketts, 2011).
- Multiple anterior teeth: retract alternate teeth, impress, then the rest (Sikri, 2017).

**Errors:** excess pressure severs attachment fibres (delayed recession); prolonged placement causes ischaemia (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018). Low crests: small cords, light pressure (Rouse, 2013).

## 9. Chemomechanical retraction and haemostatic agents

### Explanation

Sikri groups the chemicals into vasoconstrictors (epinephrine), biological fluid coagulants (alum, aluminium chloride, tannic acid), surface coagulants (zinc chloride, silver nitrate) and chemical cautery (concentrated zinc chloride, potassium hydroxide) (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Epinephrine: "The retraction is achieved in 10 minutes; however, their use should be limited because of their systemic effects"; contraindicated in "Hypersensitive to epinephrine. Cardiovascular disorders. Hypertension Hyperthyroidism Diabetes" and patients on beta-blockers and antidepressants (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri also states the epinephrine absorbed from 2.5 cm of cord over 5-15 minutes "is 71 pg"; the unit is very probably a print error (picograms would be pharmacologically trivial and contradict the systemic warnings in the same paragraph), so this number is not used in the exam core. Goldstein's cites Timberlake's double-blind study of 100 patients: absorption "produced elevations in pulse rate and blood pressure (as much as 16 mmHg)" (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC]. Ricketts: "The amount of adrenaline absorbed is unpredictable" and "The use of adrenaline-impregnated cord should therefore be avoided or used with caution" (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

Sikri: "10% Alum solution is considered superior to epinephrine"; "Usually, 6–8% zinc chloride is used to coagulate the surface layer" (risk of necrosis); "40% zinc chloride is used to chemically cauterize the local gingival tissues" (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Sikri's table quotes ferric sulphate at 15.5%: "Ferric sulphate is considered better haemostatic agent than epinephrine" but may stain the gums; the text also says "2.0% ferric sulphate has been used to control bleeding" (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. The two concentrations are not reconciled in the book.

Which astringent is gentlest? The books disagree:
- Rouse: "Aluminum sulfate is one of the least irritating of the retraction chemicals"; "Ferric solutions provide minimal tissue injury, and healing is more rapid than with aluminum chloride"; a 12.7% iron solution cauterizes a bleeding site (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].
- Ricketts: "The least damaging to the soft tissues and the astringent of choice is aluminium chloride" (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

Which impression material does ferric sulphate inhibit? Again a disagreement:
- Rouse: ferric compounds "inhibit the setting of polyether impression materials" (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].
- Goldstein et al: "ferric sulfate will interfere with the setting of addition silicone impression" materials (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC].
The safe practical answer that satisfies both is to rinse every astringent off thoroughly before any elastomer. Rouse also notes "Latex gloves may inhibit the set of polyvinyl siloxane" (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Chemomechanical retraction** combines cord displacement with a chemical that controls bleeding and crevicular fluid; it is the most popular method (Rouse, 2013).

**Classification of agents (Sikri, 2017)**

| Group | Examples | Action / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Vasoconstrictors | Epinephrine, nor-epinephrine | Haemostasis in about 10 min; systemic effects |
| Biological fluid coagulants (astringents) | Alum (aluminium potassium sulphate), aluminium chloride, aluminium sulphate, ferric sulphate, tannic acid | Coagulate blood and tissue fluid locally; 10% alum superior to epinephrine |
| Surface tissue coagulants | 6-8% zinc chloride, silver nitrate | Coagulate surface epithelium; necrosis if prolonged |
| Chemical cautery | 40% zinc chloride, potassium hydroxide | Cauterize tissue; rarely justified |

**Epinephrine.** Timberlake's double-blind study in 100 patients showed raised pulse rate and blood pressure (up to 16 mmHg) (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018). Contraindicated in cardiovascular disease, hypertension, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, hypersensitivity, and patients on beta-blockers or antidepressants (Sikri, 2017). Absorption is unpredictable, worse through lacerated tissue; avoid or use with caution (Ricketts, 2011).

**Which astringent is least damaging? Authors differ:**
- Rouse (2013): aluminium sulphate least irritating; ferric solutions cause minimal injury and heal faster than aluminium chloride; 12.7% iron solution for persistent bleeding.
- Ricketts (2011): aluminium chloride is least damaging and the astringent of choice.

**Ferric sulphate:** must be rubbed directly on the cut tissue; may stain gingiva (Sikri, 2017). Impression interaction is stated differently: inhibits polyether (Rouse, 2013); interferes with addition silicone (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018). All agents should be rinsed off before the impression; latex gloves inhibit PVS (Rouse, 2013).

**Clinical sequence (Rouse, 2013):** dry primary cord, aluminium sulphate gel, dry secondary cord packed through it and removed wet; impress only when the field is dry.

## 10. Surgical retraction: electrosurgery, rotary curettage, lasers

### Explanation

Sikri: "Electrosurgical method is defined as the surgical reduction of sulcular epithelium by using electrode of high frequency"; actions are cutting, coagulation, "Fulgeration (involves deeper tissues, accompanied by carbonization)" and desiccation; rules include "Use fully rectified, undamped current with minimum energy output", light rapid strokes "with a five seconds lapse between two strokes", not touching metal restorations, and "Avoid electrosurgery in patients with pacemaker"; the table requires that "Gingival sulcus depth must not exceed 3 mm and there should be adequate keratinized gingivae" (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Goldstein et al: "Avoid removing more than 1 mm apical to the gingival margin"; "electrical retraction procedures can cause an appreciable loss of gingival crest height"; thick fibrous tissue suits electrosurgery or laser; bipolar units cut "by molecular resonance, not by an advancing explosive spark" (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC]. Ricketts: dry field, metal-free instruments, sweeping strokes; heat "can cause necrosis of soft tissues and bone if used incorrectly"; and "Cutting should not be repeated in the same area until the tissues have had time to cool" (5 seconds or more) (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

Rotary curettage: Sikri: "The technique is also known as ‘gingitage’", for adequate gingiva with sulcus under 3 mm (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Ricketts: "such ‘curettage’ is traumatic and associated with considerable bleeding" and rarely used (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

Lasers: Goldstein et al: "An 810 nm diode laser is used specifically for soft tissues", cutting and coagulating with faster healing (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri lists CO2, "Nd:YAG (Neodymium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet): Preferred for retraction of hypertrophied tissues", Er:YAG (poor haemostasis) and argon (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Ricketts is more cautious: "their use in the management of gingival tissues is uncommon" because of cost, lack of tactile feedback and safety (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. This is a genuine difference in emphasis; Goldstein et al themselves say cord is more predictable for multiple anterior units.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Indications for surgical retraction:** hyperplastic or overgrown tissue covering the margin; bleeding granulation tissue; tissue level inconsistent with esthetics; where cord alone cannot expose the margin (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018; Ricketts, 2011).

**1. Electrosurgery** (Sikri, 2017): surgical reduction of sulcular epithelium with a high-frequency electrode (troughing).
- Actions: electrosection (cutting), electrocoagulation, fulguration (deeper, with carbonisation), desiccation (uncontrolled).
- Rules: moist field; fully rectified undamped current at minimum power; light, rapid strokes with 5 seconds between strokes; electrode kept on the inner sulcular wall; avoid metal restorations; clean the tip; avoid in patients with pacemakers.
- Case selection: healthy gingiva, sulcus not deeper than 3 mm, adequate keratinized gingiva; thick fibrous tissue, not thin tissue (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018).
- Remove no more than 1 mm apical to the gingival margin; risk of permanent loss of crest height (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018).
- Advantages: immediate haemostasis, clear margins, less chair time. Disadvantages: technique-sensitive, lateral heat spread, recession, bone necrosis if misused (Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011).
- **Bipolar electrosurgery:** no external ground plate; cuts by molecular resonance without spark or charring (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018).

**2. Rotary curettage (gingitage):** a diamond removes sulcular epithelium while the finish line is prepared; only with adequate gingiva and sulcus under 3 mm (Sikri, 2017); traumatic and bloody, so rarely used (Ricketts, 2011).

**3. Lasers**
- 810 nm diode laser for soft tissue: cuts and coagulates, bloodless troughing, faster healing (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018).
- Nd:YAG for hypertrophied tissue; CO2 good haemostasis but no tactile feedback; Er:YAG poor haemostasis (Sikri, 2017).
- Ricketts (2011) considers laser use uncommon because of cost, no tactile feedback and safety requirements.

All surgical methods are contraindicated in low crest relationships (Rouse, 2013) and should respect biologic width.

## 11. Newer (cordless) displacement systems

### Explanation

Sikri lists Magic Foam, Merocel, Expasyl, Gingitrac, lasers, Stay-put, Comprecap, Racegel, matrix impression system, cryosurgery and decongestants (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Details: "Magic foam is the expanding vinyl polysiloxane material", non-haemostatic, cap for 5 min; "Merocel strip (hydroxylate polyvinyl acetate) is a synthetic material that expands by absorption of oral fluids"; Expasyl (kaolin + aluminium chloride) where one minute's pressure "is sufficient to achieve 0.5 mm of gingival retraction"; Gingitrac (aluminium sulfate) "utilizes patients bite pressure to push material into gingival sulcus"; Stay-put, "softly braided retraction cord and ultra fine copper filaments"; Comprecap, but "Aluminium chloride used in conjunction with comprecap may interfere with setting of polyether/polyvinyl siloxane impression material"; Racegel: "The gel contains 25% aluminium chloride, oxyquinol and excipients"; "Matrix impression system maintains retraction by trapping a highly viscous material in the sulcus"; decongestants such as "0.05% Oxymetazoline hydrochloride (Afrin)", limited use (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Cryosurgery is given as "at 320–340°F using liquid nitrogen"; liquid nitrogen boils at about minus 320°F, so the minus sign has been lost in print [RECALL for the boiling point]; the figure is left out of the exam core.

Comparative evidence (Sturdevant's): "Retraction cords have been shown to cause a reversible gingival displacement that is greater than other types of materials"; in one study all methods caused acute injury healing in a week for Ultrapak and Magic FoamCord, while "The Expasyl group had the highest gingival index of inflammation and showed the slowest healing"; Magic FoamCord was less traumatic but "less effective than the single cord retraction technique in preparations with margins deeper than 2 mm"; Wostmann et al "did not find any significant difference in the quality of the marginal fit of the crowns" between cord and electrosurgery with a two-viscosity impression; and there is no comparison of all methods for gingival health and impression quality; tissue "heals in a few days provided there is adequate marginal adaptation of the temporary restoration" (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. The systematic review by Martins et al (9 studies) found most techniques achieved more than 0.2 mm and cord gave greater displacement than cordless, with risk-of-bias concerns (Martins et al, 2020) [LIT]. Note Ricketts calls Expasyl atraumatic (Ricketts, 2011) while the study in Sturdevant's found it the most inflammatory: manufacturer-style claims versus a clinical comparison.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Rationale:** avoid the trauma, time and skill of cord packing; useful for slightly subgingival margins and multiple preparations (Sikri, 2017).

| System | Composition / principle | Notes (Sikri, 2017 unless stated) |
|---|---|---|
| Magic FoamCord + Comprecap | Expanding vinyl polysiloxane; cap holds pressure about 5 min | Atraumatic, fast; no haemostasis; less effective than cord for margins deeper than 2 mm (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) |
| Expasyl | Kaolin paste with aluminium chloride, syringed into sulcus, 1-2 min | Haemostatic; thick paste hard to inject; highest inflammation and slowest healing in one clinical comparison (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) |
| Gingitrac | Paste with aluminium sulphate; patient's bite pressure on a cap | Under 5 min; single or multiple preparations |
| Merocel strips | Hydroxylated polyvinyl acetate sponge that swells with fluid | No anaesthesia; fibre-free |
| Stay-put | Braided cord with fine copper filaments | Holds shape; can be impregnated |
| Racegel | Gel with 25% aluminium chloride and oxyquinol | With or without cord; rinses off; possible allergy |
| Comprecap with aluminium chloride | Compression cap over cord | Aluminium chloride may inhibit PVS/polyether |
| Matrix impression system | Rigid matrix traps heavy-body material in sulcus | Maintains retraction; more chair time |
| Cryosurgery | Liquid nitrogen-cooled knife | Fibrosed tissue |
| Decongestants | 0.25% phenylephrine, 0.05% oxymetazoline, 0.05% tetrahydrozoline | Less effective than other methods |

**Evidence (Morelli and Neiva, 2019; Martins et al, 2020):**
- Cords produce greater reversible displacement than pastes and foams; a systematic review found cord superior to cordless, although most methods exceeded 0.2 mm and bias risk was high.
- All methods cause temporary injury that heals in about a week with a well-fitting provisional; crown fit did not differ between cord and electrosurgery.
- No study compares all methods for both tissue health and impression quality; cordless systems suit shallow margins rather than replace cord.

## 12. Effect of restorations, materials and provisionals on the periodontium

### Explanation

Overhangs: "Prevalence of ODRs is high, involving at least 25% of restored teeth"; overhangs "have been described as “permanent calculus.”"; Lang et al showed with an overhang "a normally healthy sulcus microflora changed to a gram-negative, anaerobic culture", reversed when proper onlays were refitted (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC]. Marginal fit: in 42 intrasulcular crowns "the mean marginal discrepancy was 160 μm", correlated with gingival index (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013) [SRC].

Materials (Sturdevant's): Geristore "is a dual-cure, hydrophilic resinmodified glass ionomer often recommended when the periodontium is extensively involved"; "MTA is not the material of choice for situations where the material is close to the gingiva" (weak mechanically); RMGI response "was more favourable than the response to amalgam and composite resins in an animal study"; in humans, crevicular inflammatory markers rose for all materials "with amalgam showing the highest levels after 4 weeks" (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Much of the Geristore evidence cited there is cell culture (fibroblasts, PDL cells) plus one clinical study showing no difference from other resin materials; the chapter's "preferred material" statement is about clinician preference, not superior clinical outcomes.

Provisionals: "a well-adapted provisional restoration should always be placed and tissue reaction ideally observed for several weeks" before the final impression; after surgery, early provisionals "should rest on supragingival margins" (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**1. Overhangs.** Present on at least 25% of restored teeth; detect by combined radiographic and tactile examination; associated with more bone loss, attachment loss, pocketing and inflammation in proportion to size; called "permanent calculus". Overhangs shift the sulcus flora to a gram-negative anaerobic periodontitis-type flora, which reverses when the overhang is corrected (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).

**2. Marginal fit.** Intrasulcular crowns averaged a 160 μm discrepancy, and larger discrepancies correlated with gingival index and crevicular fluid flow (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013). Indirect restorations usually have larger gaps than direct ones (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).

**3. Contour and finish.** Any restoration, even a well-fitting one, harbours more plaque and bacteria than enamel, so poorly contoured restorations lead to periodontal breakdown and recurrent caries (Morelli and Neiva, 2019). Overcontoured, rough or voided surfaces close to the gingiva must be avoided.

**4. Materials near the periodontium (Morelli and Neiva, 2019)**
- RMGI: more favourable periodontal response than amalgam and composite (animal study); RMGI or open sandwich preferred under flaps (Overton et al, 2013).
- Geristore (dual-cure hydrophilic RMGI): recommended for subgingival restorations, root caries, resorption and perforation sites; mainly laboratory biocompatibility data.
- MTA: good bone response but not for areas near the gingiva because of poor mechanical properties.
- Amalgam: highest crevicular inflammatory markers at 4 weeks in a human study.

**5. Provisional restorations.**
- Must have accurate margins, correct contour and a smooth surface; a poorly fitting provisional starts inflammation that continues under the final crown.
- Observe tissue response for several weeks before the final impression; healthy tissue under the provisional indicates the biologic width is respected.
- A good provisional also makes retraction easier and helps tissue rebound after the impression.
- After crown lengthening, early provisionals should have supragingival margins (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).

## 13. Healing and timing

### Explanation

Overton et al: posterior "restorations should be placed 4 to 8 weeks after surgery" because of rebound, and Deas et al showed "the gingival crest height was not fully stable at 6 months" in anterior teeth (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Morelli and Neiva: wait "approximately 6–8 weeks before proceeding with final preparation and impression in posterior teeth"; one study found "recession ranging from 2 to 4 mm continued to develop between 6 and 24 weeks", so in esthetic areas "postponing final margin placement and restoration for 6–8 months may be desirable"; direct restorations with supragingival margins can be placed as soon as initial shrinkage occurs (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. The posterior timings overlap (4-8 versus 6-8 weeks) rather than conflict. Rouse: "a normal crest relationship will reestablish itself even if the tissue is completely denuded", although it may take up to 3 years (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

| Situation | Timing |
|---|---|
| Epithelial attachment after probing/flossing | Reattaches in about 5 days (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) |
| Retraction injury (cord, foam, paste) | Heals within days to about a week with a well-fitting provisional (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) |
| Direct restoration, supragingival margin, after crown lengthening | As soon as initial tissue shrinkage occurs; or at surgery in a combined procedure (Morelli and Neiva, 2019; Overton et al, 2013) |
| Posterior teeth, final preparation/impression after crown lengthening | 4-8 weeks (Overton et al, 2013); 6-8 weeks (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) |
| Anterior esthetic zone | 6-8 months, because recession of 2-4 mm continued between 6 and 24 weeks and the crest was not stable at 6 months (Morelli and Neiva, 2019; Overton et al, 2013) |

**Conclusion.** Start with a healthy periodontium and a measured dentogingival complex; keep margins supragingival where possible and correct deficits surgically or orthodontically; use the gentlest method that gives a dry 0.2 mm sulcus; and finish with well-fitting provisionals, tissue-friendly materials and adequate healing time.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: A restoration margin must stay at least 2-3 mm from the alveolar crest (the "biologic width rule").**
- Textbook says: violation causes inflammation, recession or bone loss; keep 3-4 mm from gingival margin to crest, 3 mm from preparation margin to bone (Morelli and Neiva, 2019; Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
- Other evidence: the dimensions are cadaver averages with wide ranges; 15% of Vacek's teeth had margins under 2 mm from bone without attachment loss; an animal study showed a reduced biologic width re-forming; a review called the evidence largely opinion; a 2-year clinical study found bleeding but no recession or bone loss with margins in the connective tissue zone (Fruits, Khajotia and Nicholson, 2013).
- Agreement/challenge: the direction (deeper margins, more inflammation) is consistent; the exact number is not evidence-based.
- Strength: moderate for "subgingival margins increase inflammation" (prospective studies of 480 crowns, 26-year cohort); weak for any specific millimetre threshold.
- Conclusion: measure each tooth by bone sounding and keep margins as far from the crest as practical. [Likely]
- Clinical implication: treat 3 mm as a prudent working minimum and individualise by sounding and biotype.

**Claim 2: Retraction cord is superior to cordless systems.**
- Textbook says: cords give greater reversible displacement than pastes or foams; foam is less effective than cord for margins deeper than 2 mm (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).
- Other evidence: a systematic review of 9 studies found cord gave more displacement than cordless, most techniques reached the 0.2 mm threshold, and risk of bias was a concern (Martins et al, 2020) [LIT, abstract only]. Cord also caused most bleeding but fastest recovery in one comparison, while Expasyl caused most inflammation (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).
- Agreement/challenge: agreement on displacement; trauma comparisons are mixed and small.
- Strength: moderate for displacement; low for tissue-health outcomes; no study compares all methods for both health and impression quality.
- Conclusion: cord remains the reference for deeper margins; cordless systems are reasonable for shallow (under about 2 mm) margins and thin tissues. [Likely]
- Clinical implication: choose by margin depth and biotype, not by marketing claims of atraumatic retraction.

**Claim 3: Epinephrine-impregnated cords are unsafe in medically compromised patients.**
- Textbook says: raised pulse and blood pressure up to 16 mmHg in Timberlake's study; contraindicated in cardiovascular disease, hyperthyroidism, diabetes; absorption unpredictable (Goldstein, Powers and Lee, 2018; Sikri, 2017; Ricketts, 2011).
- Other evidence: none opened in this session. Effective non-epinephrine astringents exist (alum, aluminium chloride, aluminium sulphate, ferric sulphate) and alum is described as superior to epinephrine (Sikri, 2017).
- Strength: one controlled clinical study plus pharmacological reasoning.
- Conclusion: there is little reason to use epinephrine cord routinely. [Likely]
- Clinical implication: use astringent cords by default; reserve vasoconstrictors, if used at all, for healthy patients and intact sulcular tissue.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Biologic width = junctional epithelium + connective tissue attachment, about 2 mm (Gargiulo 2.04, Vacek 1.91); plus 1 mm sulcus = 3 mm dentogingival complex.
- Bone sounding classifies crests: normal 3 mm facial (85%), low over 3 mm (13%, recession-prone), high under 3 mm (2%, violation-prone).
- At least 3 mm from preparation margin to crest; ferrule 1.5-2 mm; aim for 5 mm at surgery to secure 3 mm.
- Retraction requirements: horizontal and vertical displacement, no bleeding, dry field; sulcus width at least 0.2 mm.
- Double cord: thin first cord stays, thicker second cord removed wet.
- Epinephrine: contraindicated in cardiac disease, hypertension, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, beta-blockers.
- Electrosurgery: fully rectified current, 5 s between strokes, no pacemakers, not in thin tissue or low crest.
- Wait 6-8 weeks (posterior) and 6-8 months (anterior esthetic) after crown lengthening.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing "biologic width = 3 mm" without explaining that 3 mm includes the sulcus (or not noting Ricketts' different usage).
- Listing retraction methods without the four requirements and the 0.2 mm sulcus width that justify them.
- Recommending epinephrine cord without contraindications, or electrosurgery without pacemaker, thin tissue and low crest warnings.
- Placing the first cord level with the finish line (margin cannot be recorded) or leaving cord too long (ischaemia, recession).
- Omitting the timing of restoration after crown lengthening and the role of the provisional restoration.
