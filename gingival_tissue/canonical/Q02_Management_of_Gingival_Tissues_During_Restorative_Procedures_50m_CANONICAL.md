## 1. Introduction and pre-restorative periodontal health

### Explanation

Margin location is the most important biologic parameter in predictably maintaining gingival health (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Restorations damage the periodontium in three ways: surface roughness, interproximal overhangs and impingement of the margin on the biologic width (Rose et al, 2013) [SRC]. An overhang may simply be removed and the restoration kept serviceable (Rose et al, 2013) [SRC]. Supragingival margins produce significantly less gingival inflammation than subgingival ones (Rose et al, 2013) [SRC]. Gingival inflammation around a crown can also be an allergic reaction to base-metal alloys, with an incidence around 7% (Rose et al, 2013) [SRC].

Health first: gingival retraction should never be attempted on inflamed or swollen tissue, and periodontal problems must be treated before the impression (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Sikri lists a sound periodontium around the prepared and adjoining teeth as a prerequisite for retraction (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. When tissue is unhealthy (often from poorly fitting restorations), Goldstein uses a two-appointment procedure: prepare, fit a good temporary, and make the impression at a second visit once the tissue has healed (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Ricketts makes the same point the other way round: with periodontal health and correctly placed margins there is little need for retraction at all (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

The phases (pre-restorative, during preparation and isolation, impression, provisional, cementation and maintenance) are a teaching framework, not a scheme printed in these chapters [RECALL]. Initial therapy (oral hygiene instruction, scaling and root planing, removing overhangs and replacing defective restorations before definitive work) is standard periodontal sequencing [RECALL], partly supported by Rose et al's overhang statement above.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Introduction.** The gingiva and restorations interact both ways: restorative procedures (preparation, isolation, retraction, provisionals, cementation) can injure the gingiva, and unhealthy gingiva (bleeding, crevicular fluid, overgrowth) makes accurate restoration impossible. Margin location is the most important biologic factor in keeping the gingiva healthy (Rouse, 2013). Restorations harm the periodontium through **surface roughness, overhangs and biologic width impingement** (Rose et al, 2013); base-metal alloys can also cause allergic gingival reactions (about 7%).

**Phases of management**
1. Pre-restorative: periodontal health, margin planning, surgical access if needed.
2. During preparation and direct restoration: isolation and retraction.
3. Gingival displacement for impressions.
4. Provisional restorations.
5. Cementation, finishing and maintenance.

**Pre-restorative periodontal health**
- Oral hygiene instruction, scaling and root planing; remove overhangs (an overhang can simply be removed: Rose et al, 2013) or replace defective restorations.
- Never retract inflamed or swollen tissue; treat periodontal disease before the impression (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Where tissue is inflamed, use a **two-appointment procedure**: prepare and fit a well-adapted temporary, and take the impression at the next visit, when the tissue has healed and needs little retraction (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- With healthy gingiva and well-placed margins, little retraction is needed (Ricketts, 2011).

## 2. Margin placement and biologic width

### Explanation

Biologic width is the combined vertical dimension of the junctional epithelium and the supra-alveolar connective tissue (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. **The books give different numbers, and some of them cite the same study:**

| Source | Biologic width | Components |
|---|---|---|
| Morelli and Neiva, 2019 (Vacek data) | mean 1.91 mm [SRC] | JE 1.14 mm [SRC], CT 0.77 mm [SRC]; sulcus 1.32 mm [SRC] |
| Rouse, 2013 (Vacek data) | 1.74 mm [SRC] | CT 0.71 mm, the least variable component [SRC] |
| Ricketts, 2011 | about 3 mm, *including* the sulcus [SRC] | sulcus, JE and CT attachment each about 1 mm |

Morelli and Neiva and Rouse both quote Vacek et al but print different means; I cannot tell from these pages which reading of Vacek is correct, so both are shown [Certain that they differ; Guessing about why]. Ricketts's "biological width" includes the sulcus, which the other two exclude; that is a difference in definition, not in anatomy.

Violating it, by placing margins within or apical to the junctional epithelium, causes inflammation, attachment loss and recession or pocket formation (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; the body re-establishes the width by bone loss and recession (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].

**How far subgingival?** Again the books differ: Ricketts says no deeper than 0.5 mm from the free gingival margin (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; Rouse allows 0.5-1.0 mm facially in a normal crest (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; Morelli and Neiva say beyond 1 mm into the sulcus is potentially disruptive (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Rouse argues that average figures cannot be applied to a given tooth and that individual measurement must be used (Rouse, 2013) [SRC], by **bone sounding** (Rouse, 2013) [SRC] under anaesthesia. He classifies anterior crest relationships (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]:
- Normal: gingival margin to crest 3.0 mm facially and 3.0-4.5 mm interproximally [SRC], found on 85% of anterior teeth [SRC].
- Low: the most difficult to manage, 13% of anterior teeth [SRC]; recession-prone, so margins at or coronal to the gingiva with minimal, if any, retraction [SRC].
- High: least common (2%), greatest risk of biologic width violation [SRC]; equigingival or supragingival margins only [SRC].

Safe distances: 3-4 mm from gingival margin to crest ensures healing (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; 3 mm is the minimum (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; Wagenberg suggested aiming for 5 mm from bone to margin (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Subgingival margins, even without overhangs, accumulate more plaque (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. A thin scalloped biotype recedes after margin-induced inflammation (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Q3 and Q4 cover biologic width in depth.

#### ✎ Exam core

**Margin placement.** Supragingival margins are preferred whenever aesthetics, retention or caries extent allow (Rose et al, 2013; Ricketts, 2011).

**Biologic width:** the combined vertical dimension of junctional epithelium and supra-alveolar connective tissue attachment (Morelli and Neiva, 2019). Values differ by source:
- Morelli and Neiva (2019): mean 1.91 mm (JE 1.14 mm, CT 0.77 mm); sulcus 1.32 mm.
- Rouse (2013): 1.74 mm; CT about 0.71 mm, the least variable part.
- Ricketts (2011): about 3 mm, but his figure includes the sulcus.

**Violation** causes inflammation, attachment loss, recession or pocketing (Morelli and Neiva, 2019); the tissues re-establish the width by bone loss and recession (Ricketts, 2011).

**Limit of subgingival extension** (authors differ): not deeper than 0.5 mm (Ricketts, 2011); 0.5-1.0 mm facially in a normal crest (Rouse, 2013); more than 1 mm into the sulcus is disruptive (Morelli and Neiva, 2019). At least 3 mm from the margin to the crest is needed (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).

**Bone sounding** gives the individual measurement (Rouse, 2013):

| Crest type | Facial / interproximal reading | Frequency | Margin and retraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | 3.0 / 3.0-4.5 mm | 85% | 0.5-1.0 mm subgingival facially |
| Low | >3.0 / >4.5 mm | 13% | At or above gingiva; minimal retraction (recession risk) |
| High | <3.0 mm | 2% | Equigingival or supragingival only |

A thin scalloped biotype tends to recede (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).

## 3. Surgical access before or during restoration

### Explanation

Crown lengthening is the removal of bone with removal or repositioning of the soft tissue (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; it is needed when the final cavity margin is less than 3 mm from the crest (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. In the aesthetic zone orthodontic extrusion is the technique of choice for isolated teeth (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Gingivectomy removes only gingival excess (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC] and is not advised when keratinised tissue is insufficient (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Morelli and Neiva list situations where crown lengthening may not be indicated (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]: furcation position, reduced attachment, aesthetics, reduced support, unresolved periapical infection, extensive bone removal compromising neighbours, and poor crown-root ratio. Distal wedge is done on the distal of molars for access (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC].

**Timing (the books differ):** direct restorations can be placed once initial shrinkage occurs if margins are supragingival (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; for indirect work in posterior teeth Morelli and Neiva say wait about 6-8 weeks before final preparation and impression (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC], whereas Overton et al place posterior restorations 4 to 8 weeks after surgery (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. In the aesthetic zone Morelli and Neiva suggest postponing final margins for 6-8 months (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Overton et al note a combined surgical-restorative procedure gives better access than two steps (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**When the margin would invade the biologic width:**
- **Crown lengthening:** removal of bone with removal or repositioning of soft tissue; needed when less than 3 mm of sound tooth remains between the cavity margin and the crest (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).
- **Orthodontic extrusion:** the technique of choice for isolated anterior teeth (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).
- **Gingivectomy / gingivoplasty:** soft tissue only, for overgrowth or altered passive eruption; contraindicated without adequate keratinised gingiva.
- **Distal wedge:** access to distal surfaces of molars.

**Crown lengthening may not be indicated** (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) with a short root trunk (furcation exposure), reduced periodontal support, aesthetic risk in the anterior region, unresolved periapical infection, when bone removal would compromise adjacent teeth, or when the crown-root ratio would become poor; extrusion, a compromised restoration or extraction are then considered.

**Timing:** direct restorations once initial shrinkage occurs; posterior indirect restorations after 6-8 weeks (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) or 4-8 weeks (Overton et al, 2013); aesthetic-zone margins after 6-8 months (Morelli and Neiva, 2019). A combined surgical-restorative procedure gives the best access (Overton et al, 2013).

## 4. Gingival management during direct restorations (isolation and retraction)

### Explanation

Rubber dam is the ideal isolation for all direct restorations (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; a heavier dam retracts tissue better (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. The No. 212 cervical retainer for Class V was recommended by Markley (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; the 212SA is effective (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC] and is stabilised with modelling compound (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Any dam remnant left between teeth causes gingival inflammation (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Retraction cord can be used for isolation and retraction in direct procedures (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], keeps excess restorative material out of the sulcus (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC], and placed early it prevents abrasion of the gingiva by burs (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Technique: choose a cord that opens the sulcus without causing ischaemia (blanching) (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; length about 1 mm beyond the gingival width of the preparation (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; aim for lateral displacement, not apical (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC].

Surgical access: the miniflap incisions should not pass the mucogingival junction (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; gingivoplasty is absolutely contraindicated with an inadequate zone of attached gingiva (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC], and if less than 3 mm attached gingiva would remain, a flap or graft is needed (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]. Larger defects need a mucoperiosteal flap.

Materials near the gingiva: RMGI apically is most conducive to tissue reattachment (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC]; RMGI gave a better periodontal response than amalgam and composite in an *animal* study (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; Geristore is recommended when the periodontium is extensively involved (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; MTA is not suitable next to gingiva (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Finishing short is better than an overhang into a healthy sulcus (Overton et al, 2013) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Isolation and retraction for direct (Class V, subgingival Class II) restorations**
1. **Rubber dam:** the ideal isolation (Overton et al, 2013); heavy dam retracts tissue better; check that no dam fragment remains interproximally, as it causes inflammation (Ritter et al, 2019).
2. **No. 212 / 212SA cervical retainer** (Markley): retracts dam and gingiva for Class V; hole punched slightly facial to the arch; stabilised with modelling compound (Ritter et al, 2019; Overton et al, 2013).
3. **Retraction cord** (Ritter et al, 2019): improves access and visibility, controls seepage, prevents bur abrasion of tissue, keeps material out of the sulcus. Use a cord that opens the sulcus without blanching, about 1 mm longer than the gingival extent, displacing the tissue laterally, not apically.
4. **Surgical access** (Overton et al, 2013):
   - Miniflap: small incisions kept within keratinised gingiva, not beyond the mucogingival junction; usually no sutures.
   - Gingivoplasty: only if at least 3 mm of attached gingiva remains.
   - Mucoperiosteal flap with or without ostectomy for larger or adjacent lesions.
5. **Material choice:** RMGI or open-sandwich RMGI at the apical margin favours tissue attachment (Overton et al, 2013); Geristore for subgingival sites; MTA unsuitable next to the gingiva (Morelli and Neiva, 2019).
6. **Avoid overhangs:** finishing short is better than overhanging a healthy sulcus (Overton et al, 2013).

## 5. Gingival displacement for impressions

### Explanation

**Definition and goal.** Gingival retraction is the process of exposing margins when making impressions of prepared teeth (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; its goal is to displace the gingiva *reversibly* (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Sturdevant's objectives are to widen the sulcus for bulk of material (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC] and to control haemorrhage and moisture. Rouse lists four requirements (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]: horizontal displacement for bulk (Rouse, 2013) [SRC], vertical displacement to expose the area apical to the finish line, arrest of bleeding, and dry tissues. Goldstein adds that the method must be as nontoxic as possible (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. A sulcus width of 0.2 mm or more is needed (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; Rouse quotes Laufer: no difference in distortion at 0.2 mm or greater (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Contraindications include poor hygiene, recession, bone loss and systemic diseases affecting gingiva (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; retraction must cause no irreversible damage (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Classification.** Mechanical, chemical or surgical (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; Sikri lists mechanical, chemomechanical, surgical/electrosurgical and miscellaneous (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Goldstein lists five: cord, paste, electrosurgery, bipolar, diode laser (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].

**Mechanical.** For healthy gingiva with good blood supply (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]: wooden wedges, cotton twills forced into the sulcus (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], rubber dam, copper band with modelling wax (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], plain cord. Braided cord is easier than twisted, which unwinds (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Colour code shows thickness (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], from black 000 extra small [SRC] to red 3 extra large [SRC]. Cord ends should fall at axial line angles (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Thin gingiva needs a very small cord (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]. Rouse warns that the primary cord can flatten the gingival architecture by up to 1.5 mm (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].

**Chemomechanical (double cord).** The most popular technique (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Rouse: dry primary cord, aluminium sulfate gel, dry secondary cord (Rouse, 2013) [SRC], secondary cord for 4 minutes (Rouse, 2013) [SRC], removed wet (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein: 00 then 0 (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], 5 minutes. Sikri: larger cord soaked in water and removed (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] after 8-10 minutes; the first cord stays and stops the cuff recoiling (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; remove in a wet field (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. **The dwell time differs by author: 4 min (Rouse), 5 min (Goldstein), 8-10 min (Sikri).** A cord left in must lie beyond the margin (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. For several anterior teeth retract alternate teeth (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Excess packing pressure severs attachment fibres (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Rotary curettage, electrosurgery and laser troughing are contraindicated in low crests (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].

**Haemostatic agents.**
- Vasoconstrictor (epinephrine): retraction in 10 minutes but systemic effects limit use (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; contraindicated in arrhythmia and severe cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hyperthyroidism, diabetes, beta-blockers, MAO inhibitors, tricyclics (Ritter et al, 2019) [SRC]; Timberlake recorded rises in pulse and blood pressure up to 16 mmHg (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Ricketts advises avoidance or caution (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC].
- Alum / aluminium chloride: 10% alum superior to epinephrine (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], but concentrated alum causes necrosis (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].
- Ferric sulphate: Sikri prints 15.5% (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and notes gum staining (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Rouse uses a 12.7% iron solution (Rouse, 2013) [SRC] (perhaps the same product expressed differently [Guessing]).
- Zinc chloride: ulceration and necrosis if prolonged (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].
- Racegel: 25% aluminium chloride gel (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Which astringent is best? (disagreement):** Ricketts calls aluminium chloride the least damaging and the astringent of choice (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; Rouse calls aluminium sulfate one of the least irritating (Rouse, 2013) [SRC] and says ferric solutions heal faster than aluminium chloride (Rouse, 2013) [SRC].

**Interaction with impression materials (disagreement):** Rouse says ferric compounds inhibit **polyether** setting (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; Goldstein says ferric sulfate interferes with **addition silicone** (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; Sikri says aluminium chloride may interfere with both (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. All agree on rinsing before the impression. Latex gloves inhibit PVS set (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; if the field is not dry, agents and secondary cord are replaced and retraction repeated (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Goldstein lists cord problems: ischaemia from leaving it too long (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC] and injury from oversized instruments, especially on mandibular incisors (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; electrosurgery or laser is reserved for diseased or abundant tissue (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Magic Foam is efficient in multiple preparations (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Surgical.** Electrosurgery is surgical reduction of sulcular epithelium with a high-frequency electrode (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Rules: fully rectified undamped current (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], rapid strokes about five seconds apart (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], avoid metallic restorations (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], not with pacemakers (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; thin gingiva may recede (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Goldstein: no more than 1 mm apical to the gingival margin (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]; it can cause appreciable loss of crest height (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Rotary curettage ("gingitage") only where adequate gingiva and sulcus under 3.0 mm (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; it is traumatic with considerable bleeding (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Lasers produce blood-free incisions (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Goldstein uses an 810 nm diode for soft tissue (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC], whereas Ricketts says lasers are uncommon in gingival management (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]. Even Goldstein prefers cord for a six-unit anterior case (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC]. Cryosurgery is for fibrosed tissue (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Cordless.** Magic Foam, an expanding VPS (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], non-haemostatic (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Expasyl, kaolin paste with aluminium chloride (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], 0.5 mm retraction (Sikri, 2017) [SRC], left up to 2 minutes (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; Merocel, expands with fluids (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Gingitrac uses bite pressure (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; Stay-put, cord with copper filaments (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]; decongestants (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].

**Comparative evidence.** Cords displace more than paste or foam (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; Expasyl showed the most inflammation and slowest healing (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; foam was less effective than cord for margins deeper than 2 mm (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; Wostmann found no fit difference between cord and electrosurgery (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; there is no comparison of all methods for gingival health (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. The meta-analysis by Martins et al (9 studies) found cord gave greater displacement than cordless, most methods exceeded 0.2 mm, with risk-of-bias problems (Martins et al, 2020) [LIT].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Definition:** gingival retraction (displacement) is the process of exposing the finish line for impressions by reversibly deflecting the marginal gingiva away from the tooth (Sikri, 2017).

**Objectives / requirements** (Rouse, 2013; Ritter et al, 2019; Goldstein et al, 2018):
1. Horizontal displacement to give a bulk of material that will not tear.
2. Vertical displacement to expose tooth apical to the finish line.
3. Arrest of bleeding and crevicular fluid.
4. Dry field.
5. No irreversible damage; nontoxic.

A sulcus width of at least **0.2 mm** is needed (Ricketts, 2011; Rouse, 2013).

**Contraindications:** poor oral hygiene, inflamed gingiva, recession, bone loss, systemic disease affecting the gingiva (Sikri, 2017).

**Classification** (Sikri, 2017): mechanical, chemomechanical, surgical, cordless (miscellaneous).

**A. Mechanical:** plain cord, cotton twills, copper band with wax, wooden wedges, rubber dam (Sikri, 2017). Cords: twisted, braided or knitted (braided is easiest to pack: Ricketts, 2011), plain or impregnated; colour-coded from 000 (black, extra small) to 3 (red, extra large) (Sikri, 2017).

**B. Chemomechanical: double-cord technique,** the most popular (Rouse, 2013):
1. Thin dry primary cord (000/00) packed with a blunt instrument, ends meeting at the axial line angles.
2. Haemostatic agent applied; larger secondary cord (0/1) packed over it.
3. Dwell time: authors differ (4 min, Rouse, 2013; 5 min, Goldstein et al, 2018; 8-10 min, Sikri, 2017).
4. Secondary cord wetted and removed; impression with primary cord in place, which must lie apical to the margin (Ricketts, 2011).
5. For multiple anterior teeth, retract alternate teeth (Sikri, 2017).

In low or high crests use thin cords and light pressure; excessive pressure severs attachment fibres and causes recession (Goldstein et al, 2018; Rouse, 2013).

**Haemostatic / astringent agents**

| Agent | Action | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Epinephrine | Vasoconstriction | Systemic effects; avoid in cardiac disease, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, beta-blockers, MAOIs, tricyclics (Ritter et al, 2019; Ricketts, 2011) |
| Aluminium chloride / alum | Fluid coagulant | Least damaging, the agent of choice (Ricketts, 2011); concentrated alum causes necrosis (Sikri, 2017) |
| Aluminium sulfate | Astringent | One of the least irritating (Rouse, 2013) |
| Ferric sulphate | Coagulates blood | Rapid healing (Rouse, 2013); stains tissue (Sikri, 2017); inhibits polyether (Rouse, 2013) or addition silicone (Goldstein et al, 2018) |
| Zinc chloride | Surface coagulant | Ulceration and necrosis (Sikri, 2017) |

**C. Surgical**
- **Electrosurgery:** fully rectified current, light rapid strokes with pauses of about 5 seconds, avoid metal restorations and pacemakers (Sikri, 2017); remove no more than 1 mm apical to the margin; loss of crest height is possible (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- **Rotary curettage (gingitage):** only with adequate gingiva and sulcus under 3 mm (Sikri, 2017); traumatic, bleeds (Ricketts, 2011).
- **Lasers** (diode 810 nm, Nd:YAG, CO2): bloodless troughing (Goldstein et al, 2018; Sikri, 2017), though Ricketts (2011) calls them uncommon.
- All three are contraindicated in low-crest patients (Rouse, 2013).

**D. Cordless:** Magic FoamCord (expanding VPS, no haemostasis), Expasyl (kaolin with aluminium chloride), Merocel strips, GingiTrac, Racegel, Stay-put cord, Comprecap (Sikri, 2017).

**Precautions with cords and agents**
- Wet the cord before removal to avoid tearing the clot and renewed bleeding (Rouse, 2013; Sikri, 2017).
- Cords or agents left too long, or caustic chemicals such as zinc chloride, cause ischaemia and permanent damage (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- An oversized packing instrument injures thin tissue, especially around mandibular incisors (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Rinse off all agents before the impression; latex gloves touching the field inhibit PVS setting (Rouse, 2013).
- If haemostasis fails, replace the agent and secondary cord and repeat before mixing material (Rouse, 2013).

**Choice of method:** cord for healthy tissue and routine margins; electrosurgery or laser where there is overgrowth or excess tissue; thin cords and minimal retraction in thin, low-crest tissue; cordless pastes or foam for shallow margins and multiple preparations (Goldstein et al, 2018; Rouse, 2013).

**Evidence:** cords give greater displacement than paste or foam; Expasyl caused most inflammation; foam fails for margins deeper than 2 mm; all methods cause temporary injury that heals within days (Morelli and Neiva, 2019). A meta-analysis confirmed greater displacement with cord (Martins et al, 2020).

## 6. Provisional restorations

### Explanation

A well-adapted provisional should always be placed and the tissue watched for several weeks before the final impression (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; it also facilitates retraction and impression (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. A poorly contoured provisional starts inflammation that persists after cementation (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. After surgery, early provisionals should rest on supragingival margins (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Retraction injuries heal in a few days if the temporary fits (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Tissue rebound takes up to 3 months (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]. Highly polished, correctly contoured emergence profile and open embrasures are standard provisional requirements [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Provisional restorations** (Morelli and Neiva, 2019):
- Must have accurate margins, physiological contour and emergence profile, smooth polished surfaces, and open embrasures for hygiene.
- A well-fitting provisional allows the tissue to heal and is observed for several weeks before the final impression; healthy tissue indicates no biologic width violation.
- Poor contour or rough margins start inflammation that persists after the definitive restoration, so correct them immediately.
- After crown lengthening, keep provisional margins supragingival.
- It also guides tissue healing after retraction injury, which heals in days with a well-fitting temporary.
- Where tissue is inflamed at preparation, the provisional is cemented and the impression delayed to a second visit; the healed tissue then needs only light retraction, and the gingival crest height can be judged before the final margin is set (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Gingival rebound after preparation takes up to 3 months in normal crests (Rouse, 2013).

## 7. Cementation, finishing and maintenance

### Explanation

Sorensen's data correlate marginal discrepancy with periodontal defects (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]. Retraction also eases cement removal (Sikri, 2017) [SRC] and finishing at the margins (Sikri, 2017) [SRC]. Excess cement is removed only after it has set (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC], with floss or yarn interproximally (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]; cement left subgingivally causes inflammation (Fan et al, 2013) [SRC]. Recall, hygiene instruction and periodic periodontal review are standard [RECALL].

#### ✎ Exam core

**Cementation and finishing**
- Marginal discrepancy correlates with periodontal defects (Morelli and Neiva, 2019); seat completely and verify margins.
- A retraction cord during cementation aids cement removal and margin finishing (Sikri, 2017).
- Remove excess cement after it has set, with explorer or curette and knotted floss interproximally; residual subgingival cement causes inflammation (Fan et al, 2013).
- Finish and polish margins; eliminate overhangs.

**Maintenance:** oral hygiene instruction, recall, and periodontal review of all restored teeth.

**Conclusion.** Gingival management runs through every stage of restorative treatment: healthy tissue before, respect for the biologic width in planning, gentle and reversible retraction during, well-fitting provisionals and clean cementation afterwards.

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

**Claim 1: retraction cord is the most effective displacement method.**
- *Textbooks:* cord gives greater reversible displacement than paste or foam (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC]; Goldstein uses cord whenever it suffices (Goldstein et al, 2018) [SRC].
- *Other evidence:* a meta-analysis of 9 studies found greater displacement with cord, with most techniques achieving 0.2 mm, but risk of bias was high (Martins et al, 2020) [LIT, abstract only].
- *Agreement:* agrees on displacement; for tissue injury, foam was less traumatic in shallow margins (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC].
- *Strength:* moderate for displacement; weak for gingival health, as no study compares all methods (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC].
- *Conclusion:* cord for deep (>2 mm) margins; cordless acceptable for shallow ones [Likely].
- *Clinical implication:* choose by margin depth and tissue biotype, not habit.

**Claim 2: one astringent is "best".**
- *Textbooks:* aluminium chloride is the agent of choice (Ricketts, 2011) [SRC]; aluminium sulfate least irritating and ferric heals faster than aluminium chloride (Rouse, 2013) [SRC]; 10% alum superior to epinephrine (Sikri, 2017) [SRC].
- *Other evidence:* none opened this session.
- *Agreement:* all three avoid epinephrine where possible; they disagree on the first choice.
- *Strength:* the claims are author opinion with cited in-vitro or small studies.
- *Conclusion:* no single best agent is established [Certain]; aluminium salts are favoured for safety.
- *Clinical implication:* use aluminium salts routinely, ferric sulphate for active bleeding, rinse before PVS or polyether.

**Claim 3: margins may go 0.5-1 mm subgingival safely.**
- *Textbooks:* 0.5 mm limit (Ricketts, 2011); 0.5-1.0 mm facially in normal crests (Rouse, 2013); beyond 1 mm disruptive (Morelli and Neiva, 2019); subgingival margins accumulate more plaque even without overhangs (Morelli and Neiva, 2019) [SRC].
- *Challenge:* biologic width numbers vary among individuals and even between books quoting the same study.
- *Strength:* cadaver averages plus clinical observation; no RCT read.
- *Conclusion:* individual bone sounding outweighs any fixed number [Likely].
- *Clinical implication:* sound the crest before deciding margin depth.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Treat periodontal disease first; never retract inflamed tissue (Goldstein et al, 2018).
- Margin location is the key biologic factor; supragingival preferred.
- Keep at least 3 mm from margin to crest; bone-sound to classify normal/low/high crest (Rouse, 2013).
- Four requirements: horizontal and vertical displacement, haemostasis, dryness; sulcus at least 0.2 mm.
- Double-cord chemomechanical technique is the most popular; remove the secondary cord wet.
- Epinephrine cords: avoid in cardiac disease, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, beta-blockers, tricyclics.
- Electrosurgery, curettage and lasers are contraindicated in low crests and thin tissue.
- Well-fitting provisional for several weeks; remove set cement fully from the sulcus.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
- Writing only about retraction cords and omitting pre-restorative health, direct restorations, provisionals and cementation.
- Quoting one biologic width figure as fact without saying sources differ (1.91, 1.74, about 3 mm with sulcus).
- Recommending epinephrine cord without listing its contraindications.
- Using electrosurgery or heavy packing in thin, low-crest tissue.
- Saying ferric sulphate is compatible with all impression materials (rinse first).
