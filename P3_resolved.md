## STUDY MODE

### S1. Why GIC lutes at all [basic science]
Mount's reasons: fine film thickness, low solubility, tensile strength and abrasion resistance equivalent to zinc phosphate, continuing fluoride release, and high pulp and gingival tolerance (Mount et al, 2016). Against zinc phosphate it has similar tensile strength, much lower solubility, thixotropic flow and the same sensitivity potential (Mount, 1994). Sikri lists anticariogenic action, low oral solubility, pulp compatibility, thixotropic flow, low film thickness, adhesion and tooth colour (Sikri, 2020). Craig adds that the powder glass has a maximum particle size of 15 micrometres (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### S2. Limits [treatment relevance]
Mount et al (2016) put the tensile strength of the cements at only 2-4 MPa, so retention comes from preparation design and fit, not from the cement (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994). Other figures: diametral tensile strength about 6 MPa and shear bond to dentine and enamel 2-5 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); tensile strength of traditional restorative GIC 900-960 psi, about 6 MPa (Sikri, 2020, Table 20.1); the tests and materials differ. Adhesion improves marginal seal but not retention (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). GIC luting cements have higher creep than zinc phosphate, and excess is harder to clean off than with zinc phosphate or polycarboxylate (Sikri, 2017). On post-cementation sensitivity, van Noort says there is no significant difference from zinc phosphate (van Noort and Barbour, 2013); Mount et al (2016) say it is no more prevalent than with zinc phosphate or resin cement and is almost invariably tied to removal of the smear layer; Mount (1994) adds that a mix with too little powder stays acidic longer (pH about 1.8 rising to about 4.5 within 30 minutes at the standard ratio); Craig says thin mixes increase the risk and the pain may reflect hydrostatic pressure (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### S2A. Further detail [basic science]
Resin-modified luting cements bond 8-12 MPa to conditioned dentine and enamel, 2-5 MPa to metal and high-strength ceramic, bond to composite, have compressive strength 90-140 MPa and film 9-25 micrometres; some makers advise against them under low-strength silica-based ceramics (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Pulp: more inflammation than ZOE, attenuated by 1 mm of dentine; fluoride release matters less than for restorations (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Brackets: no difference in white spots under fluoride toothpaste (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). Moisture: Craig, McCabe, Phillips and Sikri figures are listed in the exam answer.

### S3. Technique [treatment]
Powder:liquid about 1.5:1, with particles of 10 micrometres or less, give a film that seats (Mount et al, 2016). Mount advises against conditioning dentine on vital teeth because of hydraulic pressure, using a mineralising solution or tannic acid if needed; on non-vital teeth 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 seconds (Mount, 1994). Sikri describes a string test (about 3 cm), painting a thin layer on the prepared tooth, filling the crown, seating with occlusal force, and removing excess at initial set (Sikri, 2020).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Type I glass ionomer cement is a low powder:liquid (about 1.5:1), fine-particle cement used to cement crowns, bridges, inlays, onlays and orthodontic bands (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Indications
- Metal and metal-ceramic crowns, bridges, inlays and onlays (Sikri, 2020).
- Posts and pins; veneers (Sikri, 2020).
- Orthodontic bands (Sikri, 2020; McCabe and Walls, 2008). Brackets: unsuitable, unacceptable debonding (McCabe and Walls, 2008); white spots almost halved against one resin cement (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Temporary acrylic crowns: GIC is the preferred non-eugenol cement (Sikri, 2017).
- Resin-modified GIC cements (self-etching) are popular for luting indirect restorations and posts (Banerjee and Watson, 2015). Craig calls them probably the most popular luting type, not moisture-sensitive early, with HEMA making them less biocompatible (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).

### 3. Advantages
Chemical adhesion, fluoride release, low solubility, thin film, thixotropic flow (no need to hold pressure), thermal expansion near dentine, radiopaque so residue shows on a radiograph (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016). Pulp: high tolerance (Mount et al, 2016); mild irritant over dentine (Sikri, 2020). Fluoride at the small margin cannot be relied on for remineralisation (Mount, 1994).

### 4. Disadvantages
- Tensile strength 2-4 MPa (Mount et al, 2016), diametral about 6 MPa (Sakaguchi et al, 2019); cannot make up for poor fit (Mount, 1994).
- Higher creep than zinc phosphate; excess harder to remove (Sikri, 2017).
- Snap set. Moisture protection: water-resistant within about 5 minutes, no seal needed (Mount, 1994; Sikri, 2020); varnish at once (McCabe and Walls, 2008); saliva off 7-10 minutes (Anusavice et al, 2013); isolate 30 minutes (Sikri, 2020); protect 24 hours (Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Post-cementation sensitivity: no more than zinc phosphate; related to smear-layer removal and low powder content (van Noort and Barbour, 2013; Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- All-ceramic: avoid under porcelain (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011); indicated for high-strength ceramic (Sakaguchi et al, 2019) and reinforced-core ceramics (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### 5. Technique
1. Mix at low powder:liquid ratio (about 1.5:1) on a cool slab (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Prepare the tooth; do not condition vital dentine; use a mineralising solution or tannic acid to seal tubules (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
3. String test: about 3 cm (Sikri, 2020).
4. Paint cement on the prepared surface and fill the crown; seat with occlusal force; vent long parallel walls (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).
5. Remove excess at initial set (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Comparison with other luting cements
| Cement | Compared with luting GIC |
|---|---|
| Zinc phosphate | similar tensile strength; GIC less soluble, thixotropic, fluoride release; phosphate needs positive pressure (Mount, 1994) |
| Zinc polycarboxylate | little to choose; GIC somewhat easier to use (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Resin cement | higher tensile strength and mechanical adhesion; no fluoride; film thickness issues (Mount, 1994) |

### 7. Conclusion
Luting GIC is a reliable low-solubility cement for well-fitting restorations; retention must come from the preparation, and technique must avoid desiccation and over-thin mixes.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Type I: P/L about 1.5:1, particles at or under 10 micrometres (Mount et al, 2016).
- Tensile strength 2-4 MPa; do not rely on it for retention (Mount et al, 2016).
- Vital tooth: do not condition dentine (Mount, 1994).
- No significant sensitivity difference from zinc phosphate (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

2. Conditioning vital dentine before luting.
