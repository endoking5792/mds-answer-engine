## STUDY MODE

### S1. Why GIC lutes at all [basic science]
Mount's reasons: fine film thickness, low solubility, tensile strength and abrasion resistance equivalent to zinc phosphate, continuing fluoride release, and high pulp and gingival tolerance (Mount et al, 2016). Against zinc phosphate it has similar tensile strength, much lower solubility, thixotropic flow and the same sensitivity potential (Mount, 1994). Sikri lists anticariogenic action, low oral solubility, pulp compatibility, thixotropic flow, low film thickness, adhesion and tooth colour (Sikri, 2020).

### S2. Limits [treatment relevance]
Its tensile strength (about 2-4 MPa) is no greater than zinc phosphate, so retention comes from preparation design and fit, not from the cement (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994). Adhesion improves marginal seal but not retention (Sakaguchi et al, 2019). GIC luting cements have higher creep than zinc phosphate, and excess is harder to clean off than with zinc phosphate or polycarboxylate (Sikri, 2017). Early sensitivity worries are now thought unfounded: van Noort says there is no significant difference from zinc phosphate (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

### S3. Technique [treatment]
Powder:liquid about 1.5:1, with particles of 10 micrometres or less, give a film that seats (Mount et al, 2016). Mount advises against conditioning dentine on vital teeth because of hydraulic pressure, using a mineralising solution or tannic acid if needed; on non-vital teeth 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 seconds (Mount, 1994). Sikri describes a string test (about 3 cm), painting a thin layer on the prepared tooth, filling the crown, seating with occlusal force, and removing excess at initial set (Sikri, 2020).

## EXAM MODE
*(Answer-book version. Estimated writing time at 22 words per minute: about 16 minutes.)*

### 1. Introduction
Type I glass ionomer cement is a low powder:liquid (about 1.5:1), fine-particle cement used to cement crowns, bridges, inlays, onlays and orthodontic bands (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).

### 2. Indications
- Metal and metal-ceramic crowns, bridges, inlays and onlays (Sikri, 2020).
- Posts and pins; veneers in some systems (Sikri, 2020).
- Orthodontic bands and brackets; reduced white spot risk almost by half against one resin cement (Sikri, 2020; Sakaguchi et al, 2019).
- Temporary acrylic crowns: GIC is the preferred non-eugenol cement (Sikri, 2017).
- Resin-modified GIC cements (self-etching) are popular for luting indirect restorations and posts (Banerjee and Watson, 2015).

### 3. Advantages
Chemical adhesion, fluoride release, low solubility, thin film, thixotropic flow (no need to hold pressure), thermal expansion near dentine, radiopaque so residue shows on a radiograph, pulp compatible (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).

### 4. Disadvantages
- Tensile strength 2-4 MPa; cannot make up for poor fit or retention (Mount et al, 2016; Mount, 1994).
- Higher creep than zinc phosphate; excess harder to remove (Sikri, 2017).
- Snap set; early moisture sensitivity (protected within about 5 minutes) (Mount, 1994; Sikri, 2020).
- Possible post-cementation sensitivity from hydraulic displacement of dentinal fluid (Sikri, 2020).
- Not for all-ceramic restorations (acid-based cements are avoided under them) (Ricketts and Bartlett, 2011).

### 5. Technique
1. Mix at low powder:liquid ratio (about 1.5:1) on a cool slab (Mount et al, 2016).
2. Prepare the tooth; do not condition vital dentine; use a mineralising solution or tannic acid to seal tubules (Mount, 1994; Mount et al, 2016).
3. String test: about 3 cm (Sikri, 2020).
4. Paint cement on the prepared surface and fill the crown; seat with occlusal force; vent long parallel walls (Sikri, 2020; Mount et al, 2016).
5. Remove excess at initial set (Sikri, 2020).

### 6. Comparison with other luting cements
| Cement | Compared with luting GIC |
|---|---|
| Zinc phosphate | similar tensile strength; GIC less soluble, thixotropic, fluoride release; phosphate needs positive pressure (Mount, 1994) |
| Zinc polycarboxylate | little to choose; GIC somewhat easier to use (van Noort and Barbour, 2013) |
| Resin cement | higher tensile strength and mechanical adhesion; no fluoride; film thickness issues (Mount, 1994) |

### 7. Conclusion
Luting GIC is a reliable low-solubility cement for well-fitting restorations; retention must come from the preparation, and technique must protect against moisture and over-thin mixes.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Type I: P/L about 1.5:1, particles at or under 10 micrometres (Mount et al, 2016).
- Tensile strength 2-4 MPa; do not rely on it for retention (Mount et al, 2016).
- Vital tooth: do not condition dentine (Mount, 1994).
- No significant sensitivity difference from zinc phosphate (van Noort and Barbour, 2013).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Expecting GIC to give retention.
2. Conditioning vital dentine before luting.
3. Using a high powder:liquid ratio that prevents full seating.
